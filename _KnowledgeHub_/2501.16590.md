# Benchmarking Model Predictive Control and Reinforcement Learning Based Control for Legged Robot Locomotion in MuJoCo Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A direct comparison of Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) for legged robot locomotion in MuJoCo simulation reveals RL's superior energy efficiency (CoT 1.768 vs MPC's 2.993) and faster disturbance recovery, while MPC offers balanced control distribution and greater resistance to certain lateral disturbances.

## Problem

- Legged robots require advanced control strategies to navigate complex, unstructured terrains effectively.
- Traditional control methods for legged robots often lack the adaptability needed for dynamic and unpredictable environments.
- There is a distinct lack of direct, standardized comparative analysis between Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) for legged robot locomotion.

## Method

- Conducted a systematic benchmarking study comparing ==MPC== and RL controllers on a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot in the high-fidelity **MuJoCo** simulation environment.
- Implemented RL using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with hyperparameter optimization, and MPC using the predictive sampling algorithm within the MJPC framework.
- Evaluated controllers across standardized metrics including disturbance rejection capabilities, energy efficiency (Cost of Transport), and terrain adaptability (generalization).

## Results

- The RL controller achieved a Cost of Transport (CoT) of **1.768**, which was significantly more energy-efficient than MPC's CoT of **2.993**.
- RL demonstrated superior lateral velocity stability during straight walking with a y-axis variance of **0.0012**, compared to MPC's **0.056**.
- RL showed higher resistance to x-axis disturbances (withstanding up to 950N), while MPC demonstrated better resistance to y-axis disturbances (withstanding up to 340N).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement Learning (RL) excels in energy efficiency and faster transient disturbance rejection, often by concentrating control efforts on specific joints.
> - Model Predictive Control (MPC) offers more balanced joint effort distribution and greater resistance to certain lateral disturbances, maintaining better lateral velocity stability.
> - RL policies exhibit limited generalization, struggling to maintain performance on terrains significantly different from their training environment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has seen significant advancements, enabling robots to navigate complex, unstructured terrains where wheeled robots are often ineffective. This capability is crucial for applications such as disaster response, exploration, and logistics in challenging environments. Historically, control strategies for legged robots evolved from simple open-loop feedforward mechanisms and predefined gaits to more sophisticated closed-loop feedback systems like Proportional-Derivative (PD) controllers. However, these traditional methods often lack the adaptability required for unpredictable and dynamic environments.

The current research landscape is dominated by advanced control strategies, notably Central Pattern Generators (CPGs), Model Predictive Control (MPC), and Reinforcement Learning (RL). CPGs mimic biological rhythmic patterns to generate adaptive limb movements, while MPC optimizes future control actions based on a mathematical model and system constraints. RL, on the other hand, learns control policies through extensive interaction with the environment, making it highly adaptable to various scenarios. Both MPC and RL have demonstrated remarkable success in controlling legged robots, allowing for robust and agile locomotion.

Despite the widespread adoption and extensive research in both MPC and RL for robot locomotion, a critical gap exists: a lack of direct, standardized comparative analysis of their performance. Researchers often develop and evaluate these controllers in isolation or under different experimental conditions, making it challenging to draw objective conclusions about their relative strengths and weaknesses. This paper directly addresses this gap by providing a systematic benchmarking study. Furthermore, an emerging trend in robotics control is the combination of model-based and learning-based approaches, aiming to leverage the strengths of both (e.g., RL optimizing MPC parameters or MPC providing safety guarantees for RL policies). By thoroughly benchmarking standalone MPC and RL, this work provides foundational insights that can inform future hybrid control strategies, highlighting which specific characteristics of each approach might best complement the other. Thus, this study contributes to a more informed selection of control strategies and aids in the development of more robust and adaptable robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a rigorous benchmarking study comparing the performance of Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) controllers for legged robot locomotion. This comparison is specifically performed on a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot within the high-fidelity MuJoCo simulation environment.

The key motivations for undertaking this study are:
- **Addressing a Research Gap:** As identified by the authors, there is a distinct lack of direct comparative analyses between MPC and RL for legged robot locomotion under standardized and consistent experimental conditions. This work aims to fill this void by providing a head-to-head evaluation.
- **Guiding Control Strategy Selection:** By clearly outlining the advantages and limitations of each control paradigm, the paper intends to provide valuable insights for researchers and practitioners in selecting the most appropriate control strategy for specific legged robotic applications. This includes considering factors like desired performance, computational resources, and environmental unpredictability.
- **Enhancing Robot Functionality and Deployment:** A deeper understanding of each method's capabilities can lead to the development of more effective and reliable robotic systems, facilitating their successful deployment in real-world, complex scenarios.
- **Informing Hybrid Control Approaches:** By identifying the specific strengths and weaknesses of standalone MPC and RL, the study lays the groundwork for future research into integrating these methods with other classical control techniques. Such hybrid approaches could potentially overcome the individual limitations of each method, leading to even more robust and adaptive legged locomotion.
- **Standardized Evaluation:** To ensure fairness and objectivity, the benchmarking is focused on a fundamental and standardized locomotion task: straight walking at a constant velocity. This approach minimizes confounding variables and allows for a clear illustration of the inherent differences and characteristics of each control method. Performance is evaluated across critical metrics: disturbance rejection capability, energy efficiency, and terrain adaptability (generalization).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a systematic methodology to compare MPC and RL controllers for legged robot locomotion. The core setup involves a Unitree Go1 quadruped robot operating within the MuJoCo physics simulation environment.

**Robot Platform and Simulation Environment:**
- **Unitree Go1:** Chosen for its recognized reliability and advanced locomotion capabilities. Its structural similarities to other prominent quadrupedal robots (e.g., Boston Dynamics Spot, MIT Cheetah) imply that the developed controllers could be adapted to diverse platforms, increasing the study's relevance.
- **MuJoCo (Multi-Joint Dynamics with Contact):** Selected as the high-performance physics engine. MuJoCo is well-suited for simulating complex dynamic systems in robotics and biomechanics, and its integration with high-level libraries like OpenAI Gym and RL Baselines facilitates end-to-end training pipelines.

**Standardized Task:**
The benchmarking focuses on a fundamental locomotion task: walking in a straight line along the x-axis at a constant velocity of 0.5 m/s. This standardization is crucial for ensuring a fair and unbiased comparison between the two controllers.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL) Algorithm and Setup:**
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an actor-critic type algorithm, is adopted. PPO is known for simplifying the Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) approach by using a clipped objective function, which makes it computationally more efficient while maintaining stability. The objective function ensures policy updates remain within a "proximal" region around the old policy, preventing abrupt changes.
- **Training Environment:** The RL training environment for the Go1 robot is set up using OpenAI Gym and RL Baselines3 zoo (rlzoo3) within MuJoCo.
- **Hyperparameter Optimization:** Optuna, an automatic hyperparameter optimization framework, is used to identify optimal hyperparameters. Key hyperparameters used for training the locomotion task include `MlpPolicy` for the policy, a learning rate of 5.9e-4, 128 steps per update, a total of 1e6 timesteps, and a clip range of 0.2.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC) Algorithm and Setup:**
- **Algorithm:** The predictive sampling algorithm within the MuJoCo MPC (MJPC) framework is utilized. This direct shooting method simplifies real-time predictive control for complex robotic tasks.
- **Optimization Problem:** The core of MPC involves solving an optimization problem that minimizes a cost function over a finite prediction horizon. The cost function is defined as the sum of step-wise values, incorporating terms for desired states and actions (residuals) weighted by their importance.
- **Dynamics Handling:** MJPC leverages MuJoCo's internal physics engine to handle system non-linearity. External disturbances are explicitly modeled within the system dynamics by incorporating additional forces and torques into the robot's equations of motion.
- **Control Strategy:** Predictive sampling generates multiple control action samples, evaluates their costs, and selects the optimal path. This process is repeated continuously for real-time adaptation.
- **Implementation:** The Unitree Go1 robot model is integrated into the pre-existing MJPC package for benchmarking.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Disturbance Rejection:** Assessed by applying perturbation forces to the robot's center of mass (CoM) in various directions (positive x, negative x, positive y, negative y) while it walks at 0.5 m/s. Metrics include recovery performance (overshoot, settling time) and the maximum allowable disturbance force before failure (defined as the robot losing balance and its torso touching the ground).
- **Energy Efficiency:** Quantified using the Cost of Transport (CoT), a standardized metric for locomotion efficiency. CoT is calculated as the ratio of power input to the product of total weight and speed, where power input considers only positive power values (assuming inefficient energy recovery).
- **Terrain Adaptability (Generalization):** Evaluated by testing the trained RL controller on terrains different from its training environment, specifically slippery flat surfaces and uneven terrains, observing changes in distance traveled, velocity stability, and joint activity.

**Data Processing:** All data processing and analysis are performed using MATLAB. The authors have also made their data and simulation videos publicly available on GitHub for reproducibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The benchmarking study yielded significant insights into the comparative performance of MPC and RL controllers for legged robot locomotion.

**Standardized Task Performance (Straight Walking at 0.5 m/s):**
- **Locomotion Achievement:** Both controllers successfully enabled the Go1 robot to walk straight at the target velocity.
- **Velocity Stability:** The RL controller demonstrated superior stability in maintaining the desired forward velocity (0.5 m/s in x-direction) with minimal lateral movement (near 0 m/s in y-direction), exhibiting a very low variance of 0.0012 in the y-axis. MPC also achieved the target x-velocity but showed greater fluctuations in the y-axis, with a higher variance of 0.056.
- **Joint Control Efforts:** RL primarily relied on the knee extension joint for forward locomotion, indicated by its higher amplitude control input. In contrast, MPC achieved locomotion with a more balanced distribution of control efforts across all three major joints (hip abduction, hip flexion, and knee extension).

**Disturbance Rejection Capability:**
The study rigorously evaluated the controllers' ability to handle external perturbations applied to the robot's CoM. A standardized perturbation force of 150N applied for 0.2 seconds was used, along with tests to determine the maximum allowable disturbance.

- **Perturbation in Positive X-direction (Forward):** RL exhibited a smaller position deviation (0.4 m vs. 0.65 m for MPC) and stabilized 0.25 seconds faster. RL primarily compensated using the hip flexion joint, while MPC distributed effort across multiple joints.
- **Perturbation in Negative X-direction (Backward):** RL showed a slightly larger x-axis deviation and took 0.3 seconds longer to recover, nearly reaching a failed state. This again highlighted RL's heavy reliance on the hip flexion joint, which struggled more with backward forces.
- **Perturbation in Y-direction (Lateral):** Despite exhibiting a smaller initial deviation in the y-axis, MPC showed a larger overshoot. However, the RL controller demonstrated a 0.33-second shorter recovery time, indicating its robustness against lateral perturbations. This was consistent for both positive and negative y-axis perturbations due to the robot's symmetry.

- **Maximum Allowable Disturbance:** Failure was defined as the robot's torso touching the ground due to loss of balance.
 - **RL Controller:** Withstood 520N in positive x-axis, 950N in negative x-axis, 210N in positive y-axis, and 250N in negative y-axis.
 - **MPC Controller:** Failed at 260N in positive x-axis, 330N in negative x-axis, 290N in positive y-axis, and 340N in negative y-axis.
 - **Summary:** RL demonstrated superior resistance to disturbances along the x-axis, particularly in the negative direction (attributable to the robot's inherent stability under backward forces due to maintaining ground contact). However, MPC showed better disturbance rejection along the y-axis, which the authors attribute to its balanced multi-joint control strategy compared to RL's concentrated joint usage.

**Energy Efficiency:**
The Cost of Transport (CoT) was used as the metric for energy efficiency.
- **RL CoT:** 1.768
- **MPC CoT:** 2.993
- **Result:** The RL controller proved significantly more energy-efficient, achieving a lower CoT due to its ability to focus control efforts on specific joints that contribute most to forward locomotion.

**RL Generalization Performance (Terrain Adaptability):**
- **Slippery Terrain:** The RL controller, trained on high-friction flat terrain, showed a noticeable decrease in distance traveled and struggled to reach the target velocity of 0.5 m/s, exhibiting slower acceleration and variable control signals due to reduced traction.
- **Uneven Terrain:** Performance further degraded on uneven surfaces, with even shorter distances traveled and significant fluctuations in both x and y positions and velocities. The control signals showed increased activity in hip flexion and knee extension as the controller attempted to compensate, but it struggled to maintain a steady trajectory.
- **Summary:** RL demonstrated significant limitations in generalizing its learned policy to environments that differed substantially from its training conditions, highlighting a major challenge for its real-world applicability without specialized training techniques.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper provides a crucial and much-needed direct comparative analysis between Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) for legged robot locomotion, filling a notable gap in the existing literature. By benchmarking these two prominent control strategies under standardized simulation conditions, the authors offer valuable, unbiased insights into their respective strengths and limitations.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Clearer Understanding of Control Strengths:** The study definitively shows that RL excels in transient disturbance rejection (faster settling times after perturbations) and offers superior energy efficiency, largely due to its ability to learn high-frequency, optimized control inputs and concentrate effort on key joints. Conversely, MPC demonstrates enhanced stability and recovery capabilities from larger perturbations, attributed to its optimization-based approach that allows for a balanced distribution of control efforts across all joints.
- **Informed Design Choices:** The detailed comparison provides practical guidance for robotics engineers and researchers in selecting an appropriate control strategy based on application-specific requirements. For instance, applications prioritizing energy efficiency and rapid recovery from moderate disturbances might favor RL, while those demanding robust recovery from extreme events and predictable, balanced control might lean towards MPC.
- **Foundational for Hybrid Control:** By meticulously detailing the trade-offs, this work lays the groundwork for future research into hybrid control architectures. Combining the adaptability and energy efficiency of RL with the robustness and model-based guarantees of MPC (e.g., using RL to tune MPC parameters, or MPC for high-level supervision) could lead to more versatile and capable legged robots.
- **Reproducible Simulation Environments:** A significant practical contribution is the establishment of robust simulation environments for the Unitree Go1 robot in MuJoCo for both RL and MPC, making the work reproducible and extensible by the wider robotics community. The open-sourcing of data and simulation videos further supports this.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite the promising simulation results, the authors acknowledge critical limitations and outline compelling avenues for future research:
- **Sim-to-Real Gap:** A major challenge for deep reinforcement learning (DRL) is the inherent "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation do not translate perfectly to physical robots due to discrepancies in models, sensor noise, and environmental complexities. Future work must focus on validating these approaches in real-world scenarios and adopting strategies to bridge this gap. Techniques such as domain randomization (varying simulation parameters like mass, friction, and terrain properties during training) and system identification (calibrating simulation models to match physical robot dynamics) are highlighted as crucial for improving generalization.
- **Expanded Task Complexity:** The current study focuses on straight walking. Future research should expand the fundamental locomotion control task to include a broader range of behaviors, such as turning, navigating complex terrains, climbing stairs, and obstacle avoidance. This would further test the adaptability and robustness of the controllers in more realistic scenarios.
- **Exploration of Hybrid Methods:** Building upon the identified strengths and weaknesses, future research could explicitly explore integrated or hierarchical control architectures that combine MPC and RL, aiming to leverage their complementary benefits to overcome individual limitations.

In essence, this paper significantly advances the understanding of contemporary control strategies for legged robots. Its systematic benchmarking provides a vital reference point for the robotics community, informing both current control system design choices and future research directions towards more intelligent, robust, and energy-efficient legged robot locomotion in the real world.
