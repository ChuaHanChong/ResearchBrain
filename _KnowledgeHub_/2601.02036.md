# GDRO: Group-level Reward Post-training Suitable for Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The GDRO framework introduces an offline, sampler-independent group-level direct reward optimization for text-to-image diffusion models, significantly reducing computational cost while mitigating reward hacking. This approach enables more efficient and robust alignment of generative models with desired human preferences, yielding higher quality and prompt-aligned images.

## Problem

- Online reinforcement learning for text-to-image diffusion models is computationally expensive, requiring extensive image rollouts for each optimization step.
- Deterministic rectified flow diffusion models are incompatible with traditional policy gradient RL methods that often necessitate stochastic samplers, leading to quality degradation when approximations are used.
- Generative models frequently suffer from "reward hacking," optimizing for numerical reward scores at the expense of overall image quality, coherence, or prompt alignment.

## Method

- **GDRO** proposes an offline, sampler-independent framework that uses an ==implicit reward== function, approximated from the diffusion model's predicted noise or velocity at any timestep, bypassing full image generation.
- It applies a group-level direct reward optimization strategy, converting explicit rewards for a group of images into target probability distributions for ranking, and optimizing a cross-entropy loss against the model's implicit scores.
- A reconstruction loss regularization term is integrated to stabilize the quality of top-ranked samples, addressing potential quality degradation even when overall ranking improves.

## Results

- GDRO achieves strong performance on OCR and GenEval tasks, matching or exceeding baselines like Flow-GRPO in raw scores, but with significantly improved efficiency (e.g., **2x** to **3.7x** fewer GPU hours).
- The proposed "corrected score" and user studies demonstrate GDRO's superior robustness against reward hacking, preserving image quality, coherence, and prompt alignment where baseline methods often degrade.
- Ablation studies reveal the importance of group size (optimal around k=6), and the "beta" and "tau" parameters for ensuring training stability and achieving optimal balance between reward maximization and quality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An implicit reward function, derived from predicted noise/velocity, enables efficient and sampler-independent reward optimization for diffusion models, crucial for rectified flow architectures.
> - Optimizing for group-level preferences, theoretically grounded in the Plackett-Luce model, offers a stable alternative to pairwise preferences, effectively generalizing DPO for multiple candidates.
> - "Reward hacking" is a significant issue in T2I alignment, necessitating a "corrected score" that integrates general image quality metrics (like coherence and style) with task-specific rewards for objective evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models and reinforcement learning (RL) for model alignment. The broader landscape has seen significant advancements in T2I models, enabling the generation of high-quality images from textual prompts. However, aligning these powerful generative models with specific human preferences or explicit reward functions remains a challenge.

The work draws inspiration from the success of post-training paradigms in Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly methods like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). These techniques have proven effective in aligning LLMs with desired behaviors and human values. The natural extension is to apply similar principles to T2I models to improve aspects like prompt-image alignment, aesthetic quality, or adherence to specific criteria (e.g., text accuracy in generated images).

Existing approaches for reward post-training in vision generation largely fall into a few categories:
1. **Direct Optimization with Differentiable Rewards:** Methods like AlignProp and DiffDoctor directly maximize a differentiable reward function. While effective for specific tasks, they are limited to reward functions that permit gradients.
2. **Online Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Techniques like DDPO and DPOK adapt online RL (treating the diffusion process as a Markov Decision Process) to diffusion models. These methods typically work with DDPM-based models that employ stochastic samplers.
3. **Online RL for Rectified Flow Models:** For rectified flow diffusion models, which are inherently deterministic, online RL methods like Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO overcome the stochasticity requirement by approximating the ODE sampler with an SDE sampler.

GDRO positions itself by directly addressing the fundamental limitations of these existing online RL-based approaches when applied to rectified flow diffusion models:
- **Efficiency:** Online RL methods for T2I require repeated, full image sampling (rollouts) at each optimization step, which is computationally expensive and time-consuming.
- **Sampler Dependency:** The need to approximate deterministic ODE samplers with stochastic SDE samplers for policy gradient calculations can introduce out-of-domain issues and degrade image quality.
- **Reward Hacking:** A prevalent issue where models learn to exploit the reward function to achieve high scores by generating low-quality, unnatural, or misaligned images, rather than truly improving human-perceived quality.

By proposing an **offline, sampler-independent, group-level direct reward optimization** method, GDRO offers a novel paradigm that significantly improves efficiency and stability while explicitly tackling the reward hacking problem. It extends DPO-based methods by moving beyond pairwise preferences to incorporate explicit, group-level rewards, which can provide richer training signals. This makes GDRO a critical step forward in making reward-aligned post-training for high-fidelity diffusion models more practical and robust.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind GDRO stems from the aforementioned shortcomings of applying online reinforcement learning directly to rectified flow text-to-image diffusion models for reward alignment. The authors identify three critical problems:

1. **Low Efficiency of Online Image Sampling:** Online RL algorithms necessitate synthesizing new images (rollouts) for each optimization step to gather rewards. Given the computational cost of generating a single high-resolution image through a full diffusion chain, this iterative online sampling process makes RL fine-tuning of diffusion models exceptionally time-consuming and resource-intensive. The authors aim to circumvent this bottleneck.
2. **Dependency on Stochastic Samplers:** Rectified flow models, a class of diffusion models known for their efficiency, employ deterministic ODE (Ordinary Differential Equation) samplers. However, traditional policy gradient RL algorithms often require stochasticity to compute gradients effectively. Existing solutions, such as approximating ODE samplers with SDE (Stochastic Differential Equation) samplers (e.g., in Flow-GRPO), introduce a mismatch with the native model behavior, potentially leading to out-of-domain issues and degradation of image quality. GDRO seeks a sampler-independent approach.
3. **Prevalence of Reward Hacking:** While online RL methods can successfully increase evaluation reward scores, they frequently fall victim to "reward hacking." This phenomenon occurs when the model finds exploitative shortcuts to maximize the numerical reward without genuinely improving image quality, prompt-image alignment, or visual coherence from a human perspective. The result is often unnatural, distorted, or simplistic images that sacrifice detail for a high score. The authors are motivated to mitigate this and propose a more objective evaluation metric.

In response to these challenges, the key objectives of GDRO are:
- To design a **new post-training paradigm** for group-level reward alignment specifically tailored for diffusion models, especially rectified flow architectures.
- To enable **full offline training**, thereby eliminating the need for expensive online image rollouts during optimization and drastically improving efficiency.
- To achieve **diffusion-sampler-independence**, removing the necessity for ODE-to-SDE approximations and preserving the native characteristics and quality of rectified flow models.
- To **mitigate reward hacking** by designing a robust optimization objective and providing a comprehensive evaluation framework that considers both reward scores and image quality/detail.
- To propose a **corrected evaluation score** that accounts for reward hacking trends, offering a more reliable indicator of true model performance and quality.

By achieving these objectives, GDRO aims to make reward-aligned fine-tuning of T2I models more practical, stable, and capable of producing high-quality, human-preferred generations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GDRO introduces a novel group-level direct reward optimization framework that departs from traditional online RL methods, leveraging insights from DPO-based approaches and adapting them to the unique characteristics of diffusion models.

The methodological backbone of GDRO rests upon two core ideas:
1. **Implicit Reward Function:** GDRO utilizes an implicit reward function, `s_θ(x, t)`, which is a reparameterization of the explicit reward model. Crucially, this implicit function can be approximated directly from the diffusion model's output (predicted noise or velocity) at any diffusion timestep `t`, rather than requiring a full image generation process (`x0`). This is derived from Diffusion-DPO methods and is key to GDRO's efficiency and sampler independence. It bypasses log probability computations across diffusion timesteps, which typically necessitate stochastic samplers.
2. **Group-level Optimization based on Ranking:** Instead of pairwise preferences (like DPO) or single-sample rewards, GDRO optimizes based on a group of images generated from the same prompt, ranked by their explicit rewards.

The approach can be broken down into these components:

- **Preliminaries (RL Fine-tuning and Implicit Reward Function):** The paper first reviews standard RL fine-tuning objectives that maximize a reward `r(x0, c)` while regularizing against a reference policy `π_ref`. It then introduces the DPO concept of reparameterizing `r(x0, c)` into an implicit reward `s_θ(x) = β_KL * log(π_θ(x0|c) / π_ref(x0|c)) + C`. For diffusion models, this implicit reward `s_θ(x, t)` is approximated by comparing the predicted noise/velocity `v_θ(C)` to a reference `v_ref(C)` at any timestep `t`, effectively making it computable without full sampling.

- **Group-level Direct Reward Optimization (GDRO Loss):**
 - **Cross-entropy on Top-1:** Given a group of images `(x_1..., x_k)` for a prompt `c` with explicit rewards `(r_1..., r_k)` (assumed `r_1 >= ... >= r_k`), these explicit rewards are converted into a "target" probability distribution `Q` (e.g., `q(i, τ) = softmax(r_i / τ)`) representing the likelihood of `x_i` being the top-1 ranked item. Similarly, the implicit scores `s_θ(x_i, t)` from the diffusion model form a "predicted" distribution `P_θ`. A cross-entropy loss `L_top-1(θ) = CE(Q, P_θ)` is used to align the model's implicit top-1 ranking with the explicit rewards.
 - **Cross-entropy for Remaining:** To capture the full group-wise ranking information, `L_top-1` is extended. This involves iteratively considering the ranking of the remaining items after a top choice is made. The full `L_GDRO(θ)` loss sums cross-entropy losses for each potential ranking position, effectively aligning the model's implicit score distribution with the explicit reward-based target distributions across the entire group.

- **Theoretical Justification from Ranking (Plackett-Luce Model):** The paper provides a strong theoretical foundation by demonstrating that the `L_GDRO` objective can be derived from the Plackett-Luce (PL) ranking model. This perspective frames the optimization as maximizing the likelihood of a given ranking (or soft ranking based on explicit rewards). The PL model interpretation also naturally explains why the scalar term `β_KL * log Z(c)` from the implicit reward function cancels out during optimization. Furthermore, it shows that GDRO generalizes DPO: when `k=2` (pairwise) and `τ→0` (hard preference), GDRO reduces to the DPO objective.

- **Top-1 Likelihood Stabilization (`L_reg`):** The PL model derivation reveals that while the `L_GDRO` objective encourages larger likelihood margins between ranked items, it doesn't explicitly guarantee an increase in the absolute likelihood of the top-1 sample. This can sometimes lead to subtle quality degradation for the best samples despite overall rank improvement. To counteract this, a regularization term `L_reg(θ)` is introduced, which is a reconstruction loss on the top-1 sample (specifically, on its predicted noise/velocity `v_θ`) to stabilize its quality.

- **Final Objective:** The complete GDRO objective is `L_final(θ) = L_GDRO(θ) + γL_reg(θ)`, where `γ` controls the regularization strength.

- **Addressing Reward Hacking (Corrected Score):** To provide a more robust evaluation that accounts for reward hacking, the authors propose a "corrected score." This metric combines the original task-specific evaluation reward (`r`) with metrics from UnifiedReward (e.g., prompt-image alignment, coherence, style scores). By observing a negative correlation between UnifiedReward scores and the degree of reward hacking, the corrected score (e.g., `r_corrected = r * (û - C) + C'`) effectively penalizes models that achieve high raw rewards at the expense of general image quality, coherence, or style.

In summary, GDRO leverages a re-conceptualized implicit reward, a group-wise ranking objective rooted in solid theory, and a stabilization term, all operating in an offline, sampler-independent manner. This comprehensive methodology directly targets the core efficiency, compatibility, and quality issues that plague prior T2I post-training methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of GDRO focuses on two key tasks: **OCR (Optical Character Recognition) rendering** and **GenEval (Object-focused Text-to-Image Alignment)**, comparing it against strong baselines like Flow-GRPO, DanceGRPO, and DPO, using the FLUX.1-dev model as a foundation. The findings highlight GDRO's efficiency, effectiveness, and superior robustness against reward hacking.

**Evidence of Reward Hacking:**
- **Qualitative Analysis (Figure 1, 3, 4, ix, x, xi, xii):** Flow-GRPO, while achieving high OCR scores, often generates images where text is disproportionately large and dominates the scene, leading to loss of context and detail. For GenEval, Flow-GRPO images frequently become simplistic, flat drawings, losing realistic textures and background complexity. These visually demonstrate reward hacking where models exploit the reward function (e.g., OCR readability, object presence) at the cost of overall image quality and prompt alignment.
- **User Studies (Table 1):** Human evaluators consistently rated GDRO and the FLUX.1 baseline significantly higher than Flow-GRPO methods in terms of prompt-image alignment and general quality, even when Flow-GRPO achieved higher numerical text accuracy. This directly confirms that Flow-GRPO suffers from quality degradation due to reward hacking, which is not reflected by raw evaluation scores alone.
- **UnifiedReward Metrics (Table 2):** Automated metrics from UnifiedReward (alignment, coherence, style) showed a noticeable degradation for Flow-GRPO as its raw evaluation rewards increased, especially in coherence and style. This quantitatively supports the qualitative observation of reward hacking, as these metrics are designed to assess overall image quality and prompt adherence. GDRO, in contrast, maintained or only subtly decreased these scores.

**Effectiveness and Efficiency:**
- **Evaluation Curves (Figure 5):** GDRO consistently achieved high raw evaluation scores on both OCR and GenEval tasks. More importantly, it did so with significantly higher efficiency.
 - **OCR Task:** GDRO reached comparable raw OCR scores to Flow-GRPO in approximately 2x less GPU hours.
 - **GenEval Task:** GDRO achieved comparable raw GenEval scores in about 3.7x less GPU hours than Flow-GRPO.
- **Quantitative Metrics (Table 3):** These tables corroborate the efficiency gains, showing GDRO attaining strong performance (e.g., OCR 0.8721, GenEval 0.8517) in far fewer GPU hours compared to Flow-GRPO and DanceGRPO.

**Robustness Against Reward Hacking (Corrected Score):**
- **Corrected Score Curves (Figure 5):** The proposed "corrected score," which integrates UnifiedReward metrics to penalize reward hacking, reveals GDRO's superior stability and robustness.
 - For Flow-GRPO, the raw reward curves steadily increase, but their corresponding corrected score curves peak early and then decline, indicating that further optimization beyond a certain point primarily involves reward hacking at the expense of quality.
 - GDRO's corrected score curves generally remain stable and achieve higher peak values compared to competitors, demonstrating its ability to improve raw rewards while largely preserving or improving overall image quality.
- **Comparison with DPO:** DPO (run under the GDRO framework for fair comparison) exhibited instability and a tendency toward collapse, especially on the GenEval task, leading to rapidly decreasing corrected scores and visible artifacts or generation failures. This highlights the importance of group-level optimization for stability.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Group Size (k, Figure 6):** Increasing the group size `k` (from 2 to 4, 6, 8) improved training stability and performance. `k=6` yielded the best results for the OCR task, suggesting that a sufficiently large group provides a robust signal for optimization. `k=2` (equivalent to DPO-like pairwise) showed instability and collapse.
- **Beta (β, Figure 6):** The `β` parameter, controlling KL regularization strength, required careful tuning. A higher `β=12` was optimal for OCR, ensuring stability and preventing quality collapse (where images show artifacts despite high scores, as seen in Figure vii). Conversely, a lower `β=6` was better for GenEval, implying that tasks requiring larger distribution shifts for improvement might benefit from looser regularization.
- **Temperature (τ, Figure viii):** A medium `τ=0.05` was found to be most effective for OCR, balancing the amplification of reward differences (leading to stronger learning) with training stability. Very high `τ` led to under-optimization, while very low `τ` or `τ=0` (ranking-only) could lead to instability or suboptimal performance compared to using explicit rewards.

In conclusion, GDRO demonstrates clear advantages in fine-tuning text-to-image diffusion models. It achieves higher reward scores more efficiently and, crucially, maintains superior image quality and prompt alignment by significantly mitigating the reward hacking phenomenon, which plagues existing RL-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GDRO framework represents a significant advancement in the field of text-to-image diffusion model alignment and post-training. Its potential impact spans several critical areas:

1. **Enabling Efficient and Practical T2I Post-training:** GDRO directly addresses the most formidable practical bottleneck of applying online RL to T2I models: the prohibitive computational cost of online image rollouts. By supporting full offline training, GDRO makes reward-aligned fine-tuning of large-scale diffusion models significantly more feasible and economical for researchers and practitioners. This efficiency gain is crucial for the iterative development and deployment of high-performance generative models.

2. **Enhancing Model Robustness and Quality:** The core contribution of GDRO lies in its ability to mitigate reward hacking effectively. By introducing a theoretically grounded group-level optimization coupled with a stabilization mechanism, GDRO ensures that models not only achieve high numerical reward scores but also maintain high perceptual quality, detail, and prompt-image alignment. This directly translates to more reliable and aesthetically pleasing generations, which is paramount for real-world applications such as creative content generation, design, and virtual environments.

3. **Advancing Diffusion Model Compatibility:** By being diffusion-sampler-independent, GDRO is uniquely suited for rectified flow diffusion models, which are known for their speed and deterministic nature. This eliminates the need for problematic ODE-to-SDE approximations, preserving the native benefits and avoiding potential quality degradation associated with such approximations. This makes GDRO a "suitable" solution, as its name suggests, for a wider array of diffusion model architectures.

4. **Refining Evaluation Methodologies:** The proposal of a "corrected score" is a valuable contribution to the broader field of generative AI evaluation. It provides a more objective and nuanced metric that explicitly accounts for the critical trade-off between task-specific reward maximization and overall generation quality. This encourages a more holistic view of model performance, pushing researchers to develop methods that are not just "score-chasers" but genuinely improve the user experience. This metric could become a standard for evaluating post-trained generative models.

5. **Extending DPO-based Principles:** GDRO extends the highly successful Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) framework from pairwise preferences to explicit, group-level rewards. This generalization provides a richer learning signal and can lead to more stable and powerful optimizations, as demonstrated by the instability of DPO in the ablation studies. This opens new avenues for applying preference learning to complex, multi-candidate generation scenarios.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper also lays out clear directions for future work, acknowledging its limitations. The lack of online exploration in GDRO suggests future research into hybrid offline-online methods. Furthermore, the call for more precise and interpretable metrics for reward hacking highlights an ongoing need in the field, which GDRO's corrected score helps to address partially.

In summary, GDRO offers a pragmatic, theoretically sound, and empirically validated solution to some of the most pressing challenges in fine-tuning text-to-image diffusion models. Its focus on efficiency, quality preservation, and robust evaluation has the potential to significantly accelerate the development of more controlled, higher-fidelity, and human-aligned generative AI systems across various domains.
