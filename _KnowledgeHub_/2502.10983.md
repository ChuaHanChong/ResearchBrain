# Learning Quiet Walking for a Small Home Robot

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed a sim-to-real reinforcement learning framework to enable quiet walking for the Sony aibo robot. This approach effectively minimizes footstep noise by reducing foot contact velocity in simulation, leading to lower average sound magnitudes compared to both baseline RL and commercial controllers, thereby making the robot more suitable for domestic environments.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots like the Sony aibo generate distracting footstep noise in home environments, reducing user acceptance and experience.
- Current reinforcement learning approaches for robot locomotion primarily optimize for robustness and energy efficiency, neglecting the acoustic impact of gaits.
- The kinetic energy dissipated during foot contact, a major source of footstep sound, was not explicitly minimized in prior locomotion policies.

## Method

- A sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning framework using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was developed to learn quiet gaits for the aibo robot.
- The framework integrates adaptive PD gains for dynamic joint damping, foot switch contact sensors for direct ground contact feedback, and a two-phase curriculum learning strategy.
- The reward function includes specific penalties for simulated foot contact velocity, joint acceleration, and base angular acceleration, which are correlated with real-world footstep noise.

## Results

- The quiet RL policy achieved superior quietness, producing lower average sound magnitudes than both a baseline RL policy and commercial Sony aibo controllers across various walking speeds.
- In simulation, the proposed policy significantly reduced average foot contact velocity (0.123 m/s vs. 0.417 m/s) along with joint and base angular accelerations compared to the baseline policy.
- A clear trade-off was observed between gait quietness and robustness, which could be effectively adjusted by tuning Domain Randomization parameters, allowing for a balance between these competing objectives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Minimizing specific simulated kinematic parameters (foot contact velocity, joint acceleration, and base angular acceleration) effectively translates to reduced real-world footstep noise in quadruped robots.
> - Adaptive PD gains, which allow for dynamic joint stiffening and damping, are crucial for effective noise reduction during ground contact.
> - A curriculum learning approach that first teaches basic locomotion before enforcing quietness penalties is essential for policy convergence and achieving refined quiet gaits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of home robotics is experiencing growth, driven by advancements in actuators, sensors, and artificial intelligence, leading to robots performing diverse tasks and engaging in human interactions. Quadruped robots, often designed to resemble pets such as the aibo, represent a segment of this trend. However, a significant challenge for their integration into household environments has been the noise generated during locomotion, particularly footstep sounds, which has been identified through user feedback as a concern.

Previous research in reinforcement learning (RL) for robotic locomotion has primarily focused on enhancing robustness across varied terrains and improving energy efficiency. While these advancements have enabled robots to navigate complex outdoor environments, the aspect of noise reduction during walking in domestic settings has received limited attention. For instance, studies on robust or energy-efficient gaits have not typically evaluated or prioritized the acoustic impact of learned policies.

Concurrent developments in sound engineering have demonstrated its importance in human perception and interaction across various products, notably in the automotive industry. More recently, robotics research has begun to explore the role of sound in human-robot interaction, investigating its influence on comfort and perception in the context of robotic arms, bipedal robots, rolling robots, and servomotors. However, the specific acoustic impact of quadruped robot footsteps had not been thoroughly investigated within this body of work.

This research addresses a specific gap in the literature by focusing on minimizing the footstep noise of quadruped robots in home environments. It extends the application of sim-to-real RL methodologies beyond robustness and efficiency to a user-centric quality: quietness. By prioritizing the reduction of a physical parameter (foot contact velocity) directly correlated with real-world noise, this work contributes to the development of more user-friendly and socially acceptable robotic companions, thereby bridging a gap between advanced locomotion control and practical human-robot interaction in domestic settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from practical challenges in deploying quadruped robots in residential settings. As home robotics gains traction and robots become increasingly integrated into daily life, concerns raised by users regarding the operational noise of these devices have become prominent. Specifically, for robotic pets like the Sony aibo, feedback consistently indicates that the footstep sounds generated during walking are perceived as loud, detracting from the user experience and potentially limiting the robot's acceptance as a companion. This highlights a disconnect between the capabilities of modern legged robots—which demonstrate high robustness and adaptability in challenging environments—and their suitability for quiet home use.

Existing research in reinforcement learning-based locomotion has largely focused on optimizing for robustness and efficiency. While these are critical for robot functionality, they do not inherently lead to quieter operation. The kinetic energy dissipated upon foot contact with the ground is a primary source of footstep sound, and current locomotion policies have not been designed to mitigate this effect.

To address these motivations, the study sets forth several key objectives:
1. **Minimize Footstep Noise:** The overarching goal is to significantly reduce the noise generated by a small home quadruped robot (aibo) during walking.
2. **Utilize Sim-to-Real RL:** To achieve noise reduction, the researchers propose a sim-to-real reinforcement learning approach. This involves training a policy in a physics simulator and subsequently transferring it to the real robot.
3. **Reduce Foot Contact Velocity as a Proxy:** Recognizing the difficulty of directly simulating sound, the objective is to minimize foot contact velocity in the simulator, which biomechanical research indicates is highly correlated with real-world footstep sound.
4. **Develop an Integrated RL Framework:** The research aims to design an RL framework that incorporates three specific elements:
 - **Adaptive PD Gains:** Allowing the RL policy to dynamically adjust the proportional-derivative (PD) gains of the robot's actuators to enable active damping and stiffening of joints.
 - **Foot Contact Sensors:** Integrating the use of foot switch contact sensors to provide direct feedback on ground contact, informing the policy when to selectively dampen or stiffen joints.
 - **Curriculum Learning:** Employing a curriculum learning strategy to gradually enforce penalties on foot contact velocity, ensuring the robot first learns basic locomotion before refining it for quietness.
5. **Investigate Robustness-Quietness Trade-off:** An additional objective is to empirically explore and demonstrate the inherent trade-off between the robustness of locomotion and its quietness.
6. **Parameterize the Trade-off:** The study also aims to show that this trade-off can be adjusted by tuning Domain Randomization (DR) parameters during training.

These objectives collectively aim to produce a quieter, more user-friendly robotic companion suitable for integration into domestic environments, while also contributing to the understanding and methodological advancements in RL for robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers developed a sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning framework to enable quiet walking for a small home robot, specifically the Sony aibo. The methodology integrates three primary components: adaptive PD gains, foot contact sensors, and curriculum learning, within a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.

The training network architecture consists of three fully connected layers, each with 128 hidden units and Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) activation functions for both the actor and critic networks, consistent with previous work. The simulation environment, Isaac Gym, operates at a frequency of 400 Hz, with the control loop running at 100 Hz, allowing for parallel training of multiple agents.

**Actions:**
The policy outputs two types of actions for each of the robot's 12 leg joints:
1. **Target Joint Positions ($a^*$):** Desired positions for the joints.
2. **PD Gain Scales ($gain^*$):** A single scaling factor ($x_i$) for both proportional ($P_i$) and derivative ($D_i$) gains for each joint. These gains are computed using nominal values ($P^*, D^*$) and scaling factors ($\alpha=4.0, \beta=0.02$) applied to the sigmoid function of $x_i$: $P_i = P^* + \alpha \sigma(x_i)$ and $D_i = D^* + \beta \sigma(x_i)$. This approach was selected over direct torque control, building on prior research indicating its suitability for sensitive contact tasks and its novelty in quadruped locomotion for quietness.

**Observations:**
The policy receives observations that include:
- Joint positions and velocities.
- The last commanded actions (joint position and PD gain scale).
- **Foot Contact State ($f_c$):** A crucial element is the binary output from foot switch contact sensors. Since the aibo lacks force/torque sensors, these switch sensors provide direct contact information, which is leveraged to inform the policy on when to stiffen or soften joints. This is particularly important for controlling ground impact without relying on complex and often inaccurate model-based force estimation for small, mass-produced robots.
- Gravity orientation.
- Noise is deliberately introduced into these observations to enhance sim-to-real transferability.

**Rewards:**
The reward function is composed of several terms, categorized as task rewards, general penalty rewards, and specific noisy walking penalties.
- **Task Rewards:** Encourage the robot to track desired linear and angular velocities (e.g., $exp(-\frac{1}{0.06} \|v^*_{b,xy} - v_{b,xy}\|^2)$).
- **Penalty Rewards:** Discourage undesirable behaviors such as high joint torques, unstable base linear/angular velocities, foot slippage, self-collisions, excessive foot air time, and abrupt changes in joint targets or PD gain scales.
- **Noisy Walking Penalties:** These are central to achieving quietness and target parameters correlated with footstep sound:
 - **Foot Contact Velocity:** The squared magnitude of the foot velocity upon ground contact ($\|v_{f,xyz}\|^2$) is the primary term, directly linked to kinetic energy dissipation and sound generation.
 - **Joint Acceleration:** Squared joint acceleration ($\|\ddot{\phi}\|^2$) to penalize sudden joint movements often associated with noisy impacts.
 - **Base Angular Acceleration:** Squared base angular acceleration ($\|\ddot{\vartheta}_{b,xy}\|^2$) to penalize jerky body motions that typically follow loud steps.

**Curriculum Learning:**
To overcome issues of non-convergence or the robot learning to remain stationary (a local minimum for noise reduction), a two-phase curriculum learning approach was implemented:
1. **Noisy Walking Phase:** Initially, lower weights are applied to the noise-related penalties. This allows the robot to first learn fundamental locomotion and velocity command tracking, even if it generates significant footstep sounds.
2. **Quiet Walking Phase:** The transition occurs when the sum of episodic rewards for linear and angular velocity tracking exceeds 1.5. In this phase, the weights for noisy walking penalties are significantly increased (foot contact velocity penalty by 5x, joint and base angular acceleration penalties by 2x). This strategy enables the robot to refine its gait for quietness while retaining its ability to execute desired velocity commands.

**Domain Randomization (DR):**
DR is applied to improve the sim-to-real transfer and enhance policy robustness. Various simulation parameters are randomized, including:
- Robot base mass.
- Velocity disturbances.
- External forces and torques applied to the base.
- Terrain height.
- Ground friction coefficient.
The extent of randomization is recognized as influencing the trade-off between robustness and quietness, necessitating careful parameter selection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted hardware experiments on the Sony aibo robot to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, comparing it against a baseline RL policy and two commercial Sony controllers.

**A. Quietness Evaluation:**
To assess quietness, sound magnitude was measured using one of aibo's onboard microphones, focusing on the human audible range of 20 Hz to 20 kHz.
- **Comparison:** The proposed quiet RL walking policy was compared against a baseline RL policy (trained without adaptive PD gains, noise penalties, or curriculum learning) and Sony's "normal" and "quiet" commercial controllers.
- **Result:** The proposed method demonstrated superior quietness. It consistently produced lower average sound magnitudes across a range of measured base velocities compared to the baseline RL policy and both Sony commercial controllers (as depicted in Figure 3). This indicated that the learned policy effectively reduced real-world footstep noise.

**B. Analysis of Noisy Walking Penalties Rewards:**
The study analyzed the average values of the noisy walking penalty terms in the Isaac Gym simulator to validate their correlation with real-world footstep sound.
- **Comparison:** Average foot contact velocity, joint acceleration, and base angular acceleration were compared between the baseline and proposed policies in simulation.
- **Result:** The proposed policy, which yielded quieter real-world walking, exhibited significantly lower average values for foot contact velocity (0.123 m/s vs. 0.417 m/s), joint acceleration (76.7 rad/s² vs. 114.3 rad/s²), and base angular acceleration (23.7 rad/s² vs. 57.2 rad/s²) in the simulator (Table IV). This finding supports the hypothesis that minimizing these simulated parameters effectively translates to reduced footstep sound in the physical robot.

**C. Trade-off Between Robustness and Quietness:**
Robustness was evaluated by measuring the maximum slope angle the robot could traverse within a specified time (0.5 m long slope in 20 seconds).
- **Primary Trade-off:** The baseline RL policy, which was the loudest, demonstrated the highest robustness, successfully climbing a 7-degree slope. Conversely, the proposed quiet policy, while achieving the lowest sound magnitude, exhibited the lowest robustness, struggling with steeper slopes. This established a clear trade-off between quietness and the ability to navigate challenging terrains (Figure 4).
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Curriculum Learning:** Training without curriculum learning, using only the "noisy walking" penalty scale, achieved partial noise reduction but was less quiet than the proposed method. Training with only the "quiet walking" penalty scale without curriculum failed to converge, indicating the necessity of the phased approach.
 - **Foot Contact Sensors:** Removing the foot switch contact sensors resulted in a policy that converged but the robot did not walk. The robot learned to avoid walking to satisfy noise penalties, likely due to a lack of information for proper joint stiffening during contact. This highlights the critical role of these sensors.
 - **Fixed PD Gains:** A policy trained with fixed PD gains, while demonstrating some noise reduction due to the other framework elements, was louder than the proposed method. This confirms the benefit of adaptive PD gain adjustment for sensitive contact tasks like quiet walking.
- **Domain Randomization (DR) for Tuning:** The study showed that DR parameters could be used to adjust the quietness-robustness trade-off.
 - Increasing the range of terrain friction in DR led to slightly increased robustness with a small increase in sound magnitude.
 - Increasing the maximum terrain height in DR resulted in a policy that was even quieter (third-lowest sound magnitude) while maintaining robustness comparable to the baseline RL. This indicates that DR parameters offer a mechanism to balance these competing objectives.

**D. PD Gain Scale Analysis:**
Analysis of the PD gain scales and foot velocity for a foreleg during locomotion revealed how the policy achieved quietness.
- **Adaptive Behavior:** Just before ground contact, the PD gains for all actuators of the right foreleg decreased, actively dampening the joint motion. This was followed by an increase in the shoulder pitch gain to support the body during the stance phase. When the foot was in the air, foot velocity increased, along with PD gains for shoulder roll and ankle pitch, to maintain tracking of the base velocity.
- **Conclusion:** The policy learned to dynamically adjust PD gains, softening joints upon ground contact to reduce impact noise and stiffening them for stability and tracking during other phases of gait (Figure 5).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of robotics by addressing a critical, user-centric aspect often overlooked in locomotion control: noise generation in home environments.

**Enhanced User Experience and Adoption in Home Robotics:** The primary significance lies in its direct impact on the usability and acceptance of robotic companions like the aibo in domestic settings. By demonstrating a method to achieve superior quietness compared to existing commercial and research baselines, the work tackles a key barrier identified by users – loud footstep sounds. This advancement makes quadruped robots more suitable for household integration, fostering greater comfort and improving human-robot interaction in shared living spaces. This could broaden the market and accelerate the adoption of robotic pets and assistance robots.

**Advancement in Sim-to-Real Reinforcement Learning:** The study presents a novel application of sim-to-real RL for optimizing an "interaction aspect" (quietness) rather than just traditional performance metrics like robustness or energy efficiency. The framework's integration of adaptive PD gains, foot contact sensors, and a structured curriculum learning approach provides a template for developing policies for other contact-sensitive tasks where direct real-world parameter simulation is challenging. This methodology could be extended to optimize other non-directly simulable aspects, such as energy consumption by minimizing simulated torque.

**Understanding and Managing Trade-offs:** The explicit identification and quantification of the trade-off between quietness and robustness is a valuable contribution. Furthermore, demonstrating that this balance can be tuned through the careful selection of Domain Randomization parameters offers practical guidance for researchers and developers. This understanding is crucial for designing robots that can adapt their locomotion strategies based on environmental context and user preferences, enabling more versatile and adaptable robotic systems.

**Future Research Directions:** The work opens several avenues for future research.
- **Perception-Aware Locomotion:** Future efforts could integrate perception information (e.g., from vision) to estimate terrain characteristics such as friction, height, and damping. This could enable robots to dynamically select appropriate locomotion policies that balance quietness and robustness based on the perceived environment, allowing for robust walking on varied terrains while maintaining quietness when feasible.
- **Broader Noise Reduction:** While focusing on footstep sound, the paper acknowledges other sources of noise, such as actuator movement and mechanical friction. Future work could investigate these additional noise components to achieve even quieter overall operation, which is essential for truly silent home robots.
- **Commercial Implications:** The findings have direct implications for the commercial development of quadruped robots. Companies like Sony can leverage this research to refine their products, potentially leading to more advanced and consumer-friendly robotic pets.

In summary, this research not only offers a concrete solution for quieter quadruped robot locomotion but also advances the methodological application of RL in robotics and provides fundamental insights into the design considerations for future human-robot cohabitation.
