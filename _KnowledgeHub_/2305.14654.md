# Barkour: Benchmarking Animal-level Agility with Quadruped Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Google DeepMind researchers introduce Barkour, a standardized benchmark inspired by dog agility competitions, designed to evaluate the agile locomotion of quadruped robots. The work establishes baseline performance using specialized and generalist (Locomotion-Transformer) learning-based policies, demonstrating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to a custom robot and achieving a top agility score of 0.91.

## Problem

- The robotics field lacked standardized, intuitive methods for evaluating legged robot agility, making cross-research comparisons difficult.
- Existing locomotion benchmarks often focused on subsets of skills or lacked a unified environment, failing to assess holistic, diverse agile behaviors.
- There was a need for control strategies enabling robots to achieve animal-level agility and generalize across varied terrains and obstacles, beyond robust, moderate-speed locomotion.

## Method

- A comprehensive, ==obstacle-course-based benchmark==, **Barkour**, was designed, inspired by dog agility competitions, featuring varied tasks like weave poles, an A-frame, and broad jumps, with a time-based scoring system and penalties.
- Two learning-based baseline control policies were developed: a ==modular system of specialized policies== and a unified ==generalist policy== (**Locomotion-Transformer**) for diverse agile behaviors.
- Policies were trained using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== in the GPU-accelerated NVidia IsaacGym simulator with extensive ==domain randomization==, enabling zero-shot transfer to a custom-built physical quadruped robot.

## Results

- The specialist policy approach achieved a mean Barkour score of **0.77 ± 0.064** with an average completion time of **24.6 ± 1.1 seconds**, and a best single run of **0.91** in **20.4 seconds**.
- The Locomotion-Transformer, a generalist policy, yielded a mean Barkour score of **0.73 ± 0.062** with an average completion time of **25.8 ± 1.6 seconds**, showcasing smooth transitions and adaptability to varied course layouts.
- Specialist policies demonstrated **100%** success for weave poles and A-frame traversal, while the jumping policy cleared the **0.5m** broad jump in **38%** of trials, with the robot accelerating to **2.3 m/s** before the jump.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Barkour benchmark effectively challenges robot agility, revealing a significant performance gap between current robots (top score 0.91, ~20s completion) and biological animals (dogs with perfect scores in ~9s).
> - While specialized policies achieved higher individual task success rates, the generalist Transformer-based policy provided smoother transitions between behaviors and demonstrated better generalization to modified course configurations without explicit policy switching.
> - Attempts to train a single multi-task reinforcement learning policy to solve the full Barkour course in simulation were unsuccessful on real hardware, underscoring the benchmark's complexity and the value of structured or specialized learning approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen a notable increase in the development of legged robots inspired by the mobility of animals. Examples include the ETH ANYmal, MIT Mini Cheetah, KAIST RaiBo, Unitree A1/Go1, and Boston Dynamics Spot. A central research objective within this domain is to develop control strategies that enable legged robots to achieve animal-level agility and generalize these capabilities across diverse terrains and obstacles.

Prior research has made progress in robust locomotion, enabling robots to traverse a variety of environments such as stairs, bushes, unpaved roads, and rocky or sandy beaches. Notable contributions in this area include work on robust perceptive locomotion using noisy state estimation and heightfields by Miki et al., and rapid controller acquisition using massively parallel simulation by Rudin et al. Agarwal et al. also demonstrated the potential for visual locomotion tasks using raw camera images. The current work builds upon these advancements, particularly leveraging GPU-based simulation environments.

Despite these developments in robot hardware and control, the field has lacked standardized and intuitive methods for evaluating locomotion controllers, especially concerning agility. Existing metrics have often been ad-hoc, complicating performance comparisons across different research groups. While efforts such as the DeepMind Control Suite have standardized evaluation in reinforcement learning, and Eckert & Ijspeert proposed a suite of 13 metrics for measuring various aspects of legged robot agility, a gap remained for a unified, standardized environment with a single, intuitive metric for overall agile performance. Eckert & Ijspeert’s metrics, while comprehensive, did not include a standardized environment, potentially leading researchers to focus on subsets of skills rather than holistic agility.

Concurrently, researchers have explored methods for generating dynamic motions beyond basic robust locomotion, focusing on specific agile skills such as fast running (Kim et al., Margolis et al.) and various forms of jumping (Chignoli et al., Li et al., Nguyen et al.). These works often utilize approaches like Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Whole-Body Control (WBC) or learning-based methods with automated training curricula. However, integrating these diverse agile skills into a single, cohesive evaluation framework has been less explored.

This paper addresses the identified gap by introducing Barkour, a systematic benchmark inspired by dog agility competitions. Barkour aims to provide a standardized environment and a singular metric to evaluate the combination of diverse agile locomotion skills. By emphasizing both agility and generalization across multiple skills simultaneously, Barkour seeks to advance the capabilities of legged robots beyond robust, moderate-speed locomotion to more dynamic and versatile movements. The proposed benchmark complements existing efforts by offering a common evaluation framework that encourages the development of controllers capable of rapid, controllable, and versatile navigation in complex environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce **Barkour**, a comprehensive and systematic benchmark suite designed to quantify the agility of legged robots.

The motivation behind establishing the Barkour benchmark stems from several limitations observed in the current state of legged robotics research:

1. **Lack of Standardized Agility Evaluation:** Despite growing interest in developing legged robots that mimic animal-level agility, the field has lacked standardized and intuitive methods for evaluating the effectiveness of locomotion controllers, particularly in the context of agility. Existing metrics are often ad-hoc, which complicates the comparison of performance across different research efforts and robot platforms.
2. **Need for Diverse Skill Assessment:** A robust benchmark for agile legged locomotion should require a diverse set of primitive behaviors that quadrupeds exhibit in real environments. Such a benchmark needs to be non-trivial and not easily exploitable, encouraging the development of versatile controllers rather than those optimized for narrow, specific tasks.
3. **Encouraging Controllable and Versatile Movement:** The goal is to motivate researchers to develop controllers that not only achieve high speeds but also do so in a controllable and versatile manner, enabling robots to navigate complex environments efficiently and precisely. The benchmark's time-based scoring mechanism, coupled with penalties for errors, is designed to incentivize both speed and accuracy.
4. **Inspiration from Biological Agility:** The authors draw inspiration from dog agility competitions, which provide well-defined rules, diverse obstacles (e.g., weave poles, jumps, A-frame), and a clear time-based scoring system. This biological precedent offers a proven framework for assessing a wide range of locomotion skills. The intention is to translate these principles into a robotics context to quantitatively evaluate small to medium-sized quadruped robots.

A secondary objective of this work is to establish **strong baselines** for the proposed Barkour benchmark. The motivations for providing these baselines include:

1. **Demonstrating Feasibility:** To show that the Barkour tasks can be addressed with current learning-based methods, offering concrete examples of control policies capable of completing the course.
2. **Providing Reference Points:** To offer initial performance metrics for future algorithms and hardware designs to be benchmarked against.
3. **Exploring Different Control Paradigms:** To investigate and present two distinct learning-based approaches: a modular system of specialist policies and a unified generalist policy (Locomotion-Transformer). This exploration aims to understand the trade-offs and capabilities of each approach in achieving agile locomotion and generalization.
4. **Validating Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To demonstrate the effectiveness of simulation-trained policies when deployed zero-shot on custom-built physical quadruped robots in a real-world Barkour setup, addressing a critical challenge in robotics research.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for a standardized, challenging, and biologically inspired framework to accelerate progress towards animal-level agility in legged robots, supported by robust learning-based control solutions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves designing the Barkour benchmark and developing two learning-based baseline solutions—a specialist policy approach and a generalist policy approach—for a custom-built quadruped robot.

#### 3.1. Barkour Benchmark Design

The Barkour benchmark is an obstacle course situated within a 5m × 5m area, drawing inspiration from dog agility competitions. It features four distinct obstacle types:
- **Pause Tables:** Two 1m x 1m x 0.1m solid obstacles (start and end tables). Successful completion involves moving the robot's torso center 0.7m away from the start table and within 0.4m of the end table's center.
- **Weave Poles:** A series of 5 flexible poles spaced 0.5m to 0.8m apart. The robot must navigate between them, maintaining a 0.1m distance from its center to the poles.
- **A-frame:** A ramp with a 30° incline, 1m high at the top, and 1m wide. It uses artificial turf for surface traction. Completion requires traversing across the base, over the top, and across the base on the opposite side. This obstacle challenges friction management and balance.
- **Broad Jump:** A 0.5m long, 1m wide board flush with the floor, which the robot must clear without touching. This tests dynamic jumping capabilities.

**Scoring System:** The agility score, R_agility, is time-based and inspired by dog competition rules. It is calculated as:
R_agility = 1.0 − max(t_run − t_allotted, 0) ∗ 0.01 − penalties
where t_run is the robot’s completion time, t_allotted is the total allotted course time, and "penalties" are deducted for failed or skipped obstacles (0.1 per obstacle). The allotted time is based on nominal obstacle sizes (e.g., 6m for weave poles, 4m for broad jump) and a target average speed of 1.69 m/s.

#### 3.2. Two Baseline Solutions

The research presents two learning-based methods to establish baselines for the Barkour benchmark, leveraging simulation and zero-shot transfer to a physical robot.

##### 3.2.1. Specialist Policy Learning

This approach trains multiple specialized policies in simulation, each designed for a specific task, and then orchestrates them with a high-level navigation controller.
- **Training Framework:** Policies are trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) reinforcement learning algorithm within LeggedGym, which utilizes the GPU-accelerated NVidia IsaacGym simulator.
- **Observation and Action Space:** The observation space includes desired base velocities, robot proprioceptive information (projected gravity vector, joint angles, yaw velocity), terrain heightfield perception, and the last robot action. A history of proprioception for 0.3s is included. Actions are target motor angles.
- **Reward Function:** Reward functions combine task-specific terms (e.g., tracking desired velocities, jump height) and regularization terms (e.g., low energy consumption, stability, low joint acceleration).
- **Specialist Policies:**
 - **Omni-directional Walking Policy (OWP):** Trained for general uneven terrains, including start/end tables and weave poles. It follows randomly sampled velocity commands across all directions.
 - **Slope Climbing Policy (SCP):** Developed for the A-frame task. It is trained with a curriculum of increasing slope angles (5° to 33°) and focuses on agile frontal movement due to the high angle and friction sensitivity.
 - **Jumping Policy (JP):** Designed for the broad jump. It employs a three-stage curriculum: flat ground running, gap training (0.3m to 0.6m gaps), and fine-tuning with additional randomizations. It learns a bounding gait.
- **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer, various parameters are randomized during training, including torso mass and inertia, linear velocity perturbations, ground friction, PD gains, and joint static friction. Additional randomization for torso inertia, motor modeling, and joint static friction were found crucial for agile motions.

##### 3.2.2. Locomotion-Transformer: A Generalist Locomotion Policy

This second approach trains a single, unified generalist policy capable of handling all Barkour obstacles, eliminating the need for explicit policy switching.
- **Policy Distillation:** The generalist policy is developed by distilling the behaviors of the specialist policies using behavioral cloning with a Transformer sequence model.
- **Data Collection:** An offline dataset is collected by rolling out the individual specialist policies in simulated environments corresponding to their training tasks (random steps, stairs, gaps, slopes). The dataset comprises 17,636 simulated episodes, equivalent to 57.58 hours of robot interaction.
- **Transformer Model:** The Locomotion-Transformer is a causal Transformer that processes a history of velocity commands, proprioceptive states, previous actions, and the most recent heightfields (merged from OWP and JP to cover terrain broadly). It predicts the next action using an L2 regression loss. Inputs are tokenized via convolutional encoders (for heightfields) and projection layers (for other states/actions), with a context window of 0.3s.

#### 3.3. High-Level Navigation Controller

A state machine-based navigation controller is designed to guide the robot through the Barkour course.
- **Functionality:** It uses a sequence of predefined waypoints around the obstacles as sub-goals.
- **Command Generation:** At each timestep, it computes linear and angular velocity commands to direct the robot towards the current waypoint.
- **Policy Orchestration:** For the specialist approach, the navigation controller selects the appropriate specialist policy based on the robot's location relative to obstacles and assigns specific velocity commands. For the generalist Locomotion-Transformer, it only provides velocity commands as the policy inherently handles diverse behaviors.
- **Privileged Information:** The controller has access to the full environment state and ground truth robot pose (via an external motion capture system).

#### 3.4. Robot Hardware

The experiments utilize a custom-built quadruped robot weighing 11.5 kg, similar in size to the Unitree A1 and MIT Mini-Cheetah. It features T-Motor AK80-6 actuators controlled by Elmo G-SOLTWIR50/100SE2S motor drivers. Onboard sensors (IMU, joint position/velocity/torque) are complemented by an external Phasespace X2E motion capture system for accurate pose tracking. The robot is controlled by an off-board workstation, with policies sending commands at 50 Hz and a PD control loop running at 1 kHz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the Barkour benchmark and the developed specialist and generalist policies on a custom-built quadruped robot.

#### 4.1. Barkour as an Agility Benchmark

The Barkour benchmark demonstrated effectiveness in several ways:
- **Diverse Skill Requirement:** It necessitates a combination of diverse motion capabilities, effectively challenging the agility and skill discovery of control policies.
- **Maneuverability and Precision:** The complex routes and time-based scoring system require high maneuverability and control precision, especially at high speeds. Penalties for errors (e.g., missing gates, touching jump bars) enforce accuracy.
- **Biological Comparison:** Comparison with two small dogs (Pomeranian/Chihuahua and Dachshund) completing the course in approximately 9 seconds with a perfect score (1.0) highlighted a significant performance gap. This suggests avenues for both hardware (e.g., flexible spines) and algorithmic improvements in robotics.

#### 4.2. Barkour Evaluation using Combined Specialist Policies

The hierarchical controller orchestrating specialist policies was evaluated over 71 trials.
- **Overall Performance:** The mean Barkour score was 0.77 ± 0.064, with an average completion time of 24.6 ± 1.1 seconds. The average base forward velocity was 0.74 ± 0.04 m/s. The best single run achieved an agility score of 0.91, completing the course in 20.4 seconds.
- **Robustness:** The robot completed the course in most runs, though it fell or tipped over 6 times. It touched the jump board in 40 runs (resulting in a score of approx. 0.73). In 25 runs, all 5 obstacles were completed, averaging a score of 0.87.
- **Individual Obstacle Performance:**
 - **Omni-directional Walking Policy (OWP):** Successfully completed weave poles in 100% of trials, averaging 9.27 ± 0.87 seconds (compared to <4 seconds for dogs). The robot's wider body and lack of a flexible spine were noted as limitations. It exhibited stable trotting motions from -1.5 m/s to 1.5 m/s (forward/backward) and -1 m/s to 1 m/s (lateral), and could traverse steps up to 0.1 m.
 - **Slope Climbing Policy (SCP):** Achieved 100% success on the A-frame, maintaining speeds over 0.6 m/s uphill and reaching 1.5 m/s downhill. Performance was consistent across varied initial positions.
 - **Jumping Policy (JP):** Cleared the 0.5m broad jump in 38% of trials. The robot accelerated to 2.3 m/s before the jump. Failures typically involved touching the jump board before lift-off or after landing.

#### 4.3. Barkour Evaluation using Locomotion-Transformer (Generalist Policy)

The generalist policy was evaluated over 19 trials.
- **Overall Performance:** The mean Barkour score was 0.73 ± 0.062, with an average completion time of 25.8 ± 1.6 seconds. The average forward velocity was 0.69 ± 0.04 m/s. This performance was slightly lower than the combined specialist policies.
- **Advantages:** The Locomotion-Transformer demonstrated smoother transitions between obstacles compared to the specialist approach. It also exhibited greater generality and flexibility, as it does not require explicit information about the obstacle type and only needs velocity commands from the navigation controller.
- **Generalization:** The policy successfully adapted to a modified course with altered obstacle order and locations (e.g., climbing the A-frame backward, jumping in a different location) by simply updating the waypoints, demonstrating its capability to generalize beyond the training setup.

#### 4.4. Necessity of Specialist Policies

An attempt to train a single multi-task reinforcement learning policy by mixing terrains from all specialist policy types in IsaacGym was unsuccessful. This policy could handle weave poles but failed to climb the A-frame or perform the broad jump on real hardware. This finding supports the value of specialist training for complex, diverse agile skills and underscores the difficulty of the Barkour benchmark.

#### 4.5. Locomotion-Transformer Ablations (in simulation)

- **Model Architecture:** Transformer-based policies significantly outperformed an MLP-based policy trained on the same dataset.
- **Context Length:** Longer context lengths (up to W=15) improved performance, suggesting the Transformer effectively leverages past state information.
- **Dataset Size:** While 1% of the training data was insufficient, 10% (approximately 1760 episodes) achieved similar performance to 100% of the dataset.
- **Model Size:** Larger model sizes generally led to improved performance.

#### 4.6. Distilling Hardware Data (Appendix D)

A course-specific Locomotion-Transformer, trained on a small dataset of hardware runs (64 episodes) from the Barkour course, achieved consistent completion with a maximum agility score of 0.71 and an average of 0.57. This suggests the potential for the transformer framework to learn higher-level behaviors, including navigation, directly from hardware data and to exhibit robustness to deviations from nominal trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces the Barkour benchmark, which addresses a recognized gap in the systematic evaluation of agility in legged robots. The work has several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of robotics:

1. **Standardized Evaluation Framework:** Barkour provides a standardized, quantifiable, and intuitive benchmark inspired by dog agility competitions. This allows for direct and consistent comparison of different control algorithms, hardware designs, and learning approaches across research groups. By offering a common testbed with clear metrics, Barkour can accelerate progress toward more agile robot locomotion.

2. **Driving Algorithmic Development:** The benchmark's requirement for diverse agile skills—including rapid omni-directional movement, robust slope climbing, and dynamic jumping—encourages the development of more versatile, precise, and robust locomotion controllers. The observed performance gap between robots and biological counterparts within the Barkour framework clearly indicates substantial room for further algorithmic innovation. The failure of a single multi-task RL policy to solve the full course highlights the complexity and value of the benchmark in pushing the boundaries of control methodologies.

3. **Establishment of Strong Baselines:** The paper provides two distinct learning-based baseline solutions: a modular approach using specialist policies and a unified approach with the Locomotion-Transformer. These baselines demonstrate the feasibility of solving complex agile tasks using modern deep reinforcement learning and behavioral cloning techniques. They offer concrete reference points for future work to compare against, aiding in tracking progress in agile robotics.

4. **Advancement in Generalist Control:** The Locomotion-Transformer represents a step toward more generalist robot controllers. Its ability to smoothly transition between diverse agile behaviors and generalize to modified obstacle configurations without explicit instructions on obstacle type signifies progress in developing more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems. This reduces the need for extensive human engineering to define policy switching logic, potentially simplifying deployment in varied real-world scenarios.

5. **Emphasis on Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer of simulation-trained policies to custom physical hardware validates the efficacy of current domain randomization techniques and further underscores the importance of high-fidelity simulation environments (like IsaacGym) for robotics research. The identified challenges in sim-to-real transfer for highly agile motions, such as jumping, will likely spur further research into bridging this gap.

6. **Highlighting Hardware-Software Co-design:** The comparison with real dogs performing the same course illuminates specific limitations of current quadruped robot hardware (e.g., lack of flexible spines). This highlights that achieving true animal-level agility will likely require not only advanced control algorithms but also continued innovation and optimization in robot mechanical design and actuation. Barkour implicitly encourages a co-design approach where hardware capabilities evolve in conjunction with control strategies.

7. **Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines future research avenues, including exploring control strategies solely based on onboard sensors (moving away from privileged information), evaluating the impact of different robot form factors and sensors on performance, and investigating interactive human-robot agility competitions. These directions underscore the long-term impact of Barkour as an enduring testbed for the robotics community.
