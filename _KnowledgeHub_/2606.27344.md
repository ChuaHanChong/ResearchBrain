# VibeAct: Vibration to Actions for Contact-Rich Reactive Robot Dexterity

> [!summary] Summary
> VIBEACT introduces a framework that integrates high-bandwidth vibrotactile sensing into simulation-trained robot policies by mapping real-world microphone signals to a physically grounded contact and slip representation. This approach significantly improved success rates on contact-rich manipulation tasks, for instance, increasing Cube Rotation success by 51 percentage points and demonstrating effective sim-to-real transfer on a physical robot.

## Problem

- Dexterous robot manipulation struggles with precise and rapid responses to contact and slip events, which are crucial but often visually occluded.
- High-bandwidth vibrotactile signals from compact sensors like piezoelectric microphones are too complex and dependent on physical factors to be accurately simulated for end-to-end policy learning in RL.
- Training data-intensive dexterous manipulation policies directly in the real world using complex vibrotactile observations is impractical due to cost, time, and safety.

## Method

- VIBEACT proposes a compact, physically grounded intermediate representation for tactile feedback, comprising contact onset, binary slip, and slip magnitude, which is derivable from real vibro-acoustic signals and computable in simulation.
- A "digital-clone" data labeling pipeline automatically generates ground-truth labels for this tactile representation by replaying real-world robot interactions in a calibrated physics simulator.
- A deep learning tactile estimator maps raw real-world vibro-acoustic signals to this representation, enabling ==reinforcement learning== policies to be trained entirely in simulation using this shared tactile observation channel.

## Results

- The tactile estimator accurately mapped real-world vibro-acoustic signals to the defined representation, achieving a Slip Presence F1 of **0.913** and a Slip Magnitude MAE of **4.736** mm/s.
- Simulation-trained policies using VIBEACT's full tactile representation significantly improved success rates, notably increasing Cube Rotation by 51 percentage points and Peg in Hole by 24 percentage points compared to baselines.
- Policies successfully transferred to a physical robot, enhancing real-world task performance; for instance, Can Climb success rose from 11/20 to 19/20 and Nut Rotation from 1/20 to 8/20.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating complex, difficult-to-simulate vibrotactile sensors into simulation-driven RL is feasible by employing an explicit, physically grounded intermediate representation, decoupling sensing from policy learning.
> - The continuous slip magnitude signal within the tactile representation is critical for enhancing reactive control in contact-rich manipulation, contributing most significantly to performance gains.
> - Optimal tactile estimation from vibro-acoustic signals benefits from dedicated, independent deep learning models for each fingertip, acknowledging unique contact mechanics and vibration characteristics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation, particularly in complex and unstructured environments, fundamentally relies on precise and rapid responses to contact and slip events. These interactions are often brief, localized, and visually occluded, making high-fidelity tactile sensing critical for robotic systems. The broader research landscape for robot tactile sensing encompasses a variety of modalities, each with distinct advantages and challenges for integration into control pipelines.

Vision-based tactile sensors, such as GelSight and Digit, offer high-resolution information regarding contact geometry, shear, and slip. However, their scalability to multi-fingered robot hands can be limited by mechanical bulk, camera bandwidth requirements, illumination constraints, and significant computational overhead. Other tactile sensing approaches, including magnetic, capacitive, or resistive sensors, often provide lower-dimensional contact information that is more straightforward to integrate but may necessitate modifications to fingertip structure or contact surface materials.

Vibrotactile sensing, using sensors like piezoelectric microphones, presents an alternative by leveraging structure-borne vibrations generated by impacts, stick-slip motion, and sliding. These sensors are typically inexpensive, compact, high-bandwidth, and can be embedded within the robot's structure, avoiding alterations to the external contact surface. Despite these advantages, the inherent complexity of vibro-acoustic signals poses a significant challenge: their dependency on numerous physical factors (e.g., finger material, object texture, mounting location, actuator noise) makes them difficult to simulate accurately for end-to-end policy learning in reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks.

This difficulty in faithfully simulating vibro-acoustic signals, combined with the impracticality of large-scale real-world data collection and online RL training for dexterous manipulation, creates a substantial sim-to-real gap. Existing approaches often either train policies directly in the real world using audio-visual signals through behavior cloning, which typically lacks the scalability and safety of simulation, or they rely on other tactile modalities that might have limitations for dexterous, contact-rich tasks.

VIBEACT addresses this critical gap by proposing a framework that bridges real vibrotactile sensing and simulation-based RL through an *explicit, physically grounded intermediate representation* of contact and slip. Instead of attempting to simulate raw audio, VIBEACT focuses on inferring a simplified, task-relevant physical state from real-world vibro-acoustic signals, a state that can also be directly computed within a physics simulator. This approach positions VIBEACT as a method for integrating high-bandwidth, low-cost vibrotactile sensing into scalable, simulation-driven dexterous robot control, thereby enhancing reactive manipulation capabilities in contact-rich scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable dexterous robot manipulation that leverages rapid vibrotactile feedback, particularly for tasks involving frequent and complex contact events. This overarching goal is motivated by several challenges inherent in current robotic manipulation systems and tactile sensing technologies.

A core motivation stems from the limitations of existing sensing modalities for contact-rich tasks. While visual perception provides global scene information, many critical hand-object interactions, such as initial contact, incipient slip, or sustained sliding, are localized, fast, and often visually occluded. Tactile sensors are designed to capture these interactions, but current options each present their own trade-offs, as discussed in the broader research landscape section. Piezoelectric microphones offer a promising balance of compactness, cost-effectiveness, and high bandwidth, allowing them to capture subtle structure-borne vibrations generated during contact.

However, the raw vibro-acoustic signals produced by these microphones are inherently complex. They are influenced by a multitude of factors including the material properties of the robot finger and object, sensor mounting, adhesive, and ambient noise from robot actuators. This complexity makes it exceptionally difficult to simulate these signals with sufficient fidelity to enable end-to-end policy learning directly from simulated audio in a physics engine. Direct simulation would require accurate models of vibration propagation, sensor transfer functions, and material acoustics, which are currently an open research challenge.

Conversely, training dexterous manipulation policies directly in the real world using these complex vibrotactile observations is also not practical. Real-world reinforcement learning for high-dimensional, contact-rich tasks is notoriously data-intensive, prohibitively expensive in terms of time and resources, and potentially unsafe due to exploratory behaviors during early training phases. Collecting large, diverse datasets of contact-rich demonstrations through teleoperation for supervised learning is labor-intensive and challenging, especially for multi-fingered hands with embedded sensors.

These dual challenges—the difficulty of simulating raw vibro-acoustic signals and the impracticality of real-world reinforcement learning—motivated the central insight of VIBEACT: to introduce a compact, physically grounded intermediate representation of contact and slip. The key objectives are therefore:
1. **To define a low-dimensional, task-relevant tactile representation** that encapsulates essential contact-related information (contact onset, binary slip, scalar slip magnitude) without requiring privileged simulator data (e.g., contact location, force vectors, object identity). This representation must be both derivable from real microphone signals and computable directly within a physics simulator.
2. **To develop a robust method for automatically labeling this tactile representation** from real-world vibro-acoustic data. This involves a digital-clone data labeling pipeline that replays real-world robot trajectories in a calibrated simulator to generate ground-truth contact and slip labels without manual annotation.
3. **To train a tactile estimator** that maps real-world, raw vibro-acoustic signals from embedded microphones to this physically grounded contact and slip representation.
4. **To train reinforcement learning policies entirely in simulation** using this same contact and slip representation as a tactile observation channel, alongside proprioception and point cloud data.
5. **To empirically demonstrate that this framework improves performance** on various contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks in simulation and that the learned policies successfully transfer to a physical robot platform. This includes analyzing which specific components of the tactile representation contribute most to performance gains.

By achieving these objectives, VIBEACT aims to decouple the challenging problem of high-fidelity acoustic simulation from the policy learning process, enabling policies to effectively exploit rapid, high-bandwidth tactile feedback for complex manipulation tasks using readily available hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VIBEACT operates as a three-component framework designed to integrate real vibrotactile sensing with simulation-based policy learning through a shared physical representation of contact and slip.

#### 3.1. Real-World Vibrotactile Data Collection and Digital-Clone Labeling

The initial phase involves collecting real-world interaction data and generating corresponding ground-truth labels.
- **Hardware Setup**: The robotic platform consists of an xArm7 collaborative robot arm equipped with a LEAP dexterous hand. Each fingertip of the LEAP hand is embedded with two piezoelectric microphones, resulting in a total of eight microphone channels. These microphones capture structure-borne vibrations within the finger body. All eight audio channels are synchronized with an audio mixer. Object poses are either rigidly fixed to the table with known transforms or tracked using a mocap system calibrated to the robot base.
- **Data Collection Procedure**: Teleoperation is used to control the robot hand to interact with various objects. During these interactions, microphone audio (48 kHz), arm and hand joint positions, joint velocities, and synchronized timestamps are recorded.
- **Digital-Clone Replay and Automatic Labeling**: The recorded robot trajectories and object poses (either fixed or mocap-tracked) are replayed in a calibrated MuJoCo physics simulator, which serves as a "digital clone" of the real-world setup. The simulator's contact solver provides per-finger contact supervision. For each timestep, it determines contact locations, normal forces, and relative tangential velocities for active fingertip contacts. If multiple contacts occur on a single fingertip, the system aggregates them by marking a contact onset if any new contact appears and taking the maximum tangential slip speed. This process automatically generates per-finger contact and slip labels, forming the tactile representation without manual annotation.

#### 3.2. Tactile Representation

The core of VIBEACT is its compact, physically grounded tactile representation, which serves as the interface between sensing and control. For each finger `i` at time `t`, the tactile representation `z_i_t` is defined as a 3-dimensional vector `[b_i_t, m_i_t, e_i_t]`:
- **Contact Onset (`e_i_t`)**: A binary, one-step pulse that is activated when a new contact is established on finger `i`. This event-like nature is designed to align with the acoustic transients often generated by initial impacts.
- **Slip Presence (`b_i_t`)**: A binary state indicating whether slip is occurring. It is determined by a threshold on the tangential relative velocity `∥v_i_t∥` at 5 mm/s.
- **Slip Magnitude (`m_i_t`)**: A scalar value representing the severity of slip. It is defined as the tangential slip speed `v_i_t`, clipped between 0 and a maximum value (`m_max`). This provides a continuous signal of slip intensity.

This representation intentionally avoids simulator-privileged information such as precise contact location, surface normal, tangential direction, object identity, or force vectors. This constraint ensures that the representation remains predictable from real physical microphone signals and can transfer effectively across different objects and tasks in the real world. Despite its compactness, it aims to capture critical feedback for reactive manipulation: initial contact, presence of slip, and the degree of slip.

#### 3.3. Tactile Estimator

The tactile estimator is a deep learning model responsible for mapping real-world vibro-acoustic signals to the defined contact and slip representation.
- **Network Architecture**: The estimator comprises four independent subnetworks, one for each finger. For a given finger `i`, the input consists of multi-channel log-mel spectrograms computed over 200 ms temporal windows from its two synchronized microphones.
 - A learnable microphone-gating layer suppresses noisy channels.
 - Spectrograms are processed by a convolutional encoder, which uses frequency-only pooling to retain temporal resolution, crucial for transient events like contact onset.
 - Temporal convolutions and attention pooling generate per-microphone embeddings, which are then fused to form a per-finger representation.
 - Separate prediction heads are used for each component of the tactile representation: contact-onset probability (sigmoid activation), slip-presence probability (sigmoid activation), and slip magnitude (linear output, additionally conditioned on amplitude statistics).
- **Training Objectives**: The model is trained with a multi-task objective function that combines three loss terms:
 - Class-weighted binary cross-entropy losses for contact onset (`L_onset`) and slip presence (`L_slip`), with weights to compensate for sparse onset events and balance slip-positive frames.
 - A Huber loss for slip magnitude (`L_mag`), applied only to frames where slip is detected, to focus on accurate magnitude prediction during active slipping.
 - The overall loss is the average of these weighted losses across the four fingers.
- **Training Strategy**: The estimator is initially pretrained on a large dataset of interactions with fixed objects (generating diverse slip events), then fine-tuned on a smaller dataset of in-hand manipulation of moving objects (more closely matching deployment scenarios).

#### 3.4. Policy Learning

Reinforcement learning policies are trained entirely in simulation using the PPO algorithm in a MuJoCo environment.
- **Simulation Environment**: The simulator uses the same xArm7 and LEAP hand model as the digital clone. Domain randomization is applied per episode, varying object friction, mass, scale, and initial pose, to encourage policies to rely on tactile feedback rather than memorized geometric properties.
- **Observations**: At each control step `t`, the policy receives an observation tuple `o_t = (p_t, q_t, z_t)`:
 - `p_t`: A fixed-camera point cloud (processed by a PointNet-style encoder).
 - `q_t`: Proprioception data (joint positions and velocities of the hand and arm, processed by an MLP).
 - `z_t`: The 12-dimensional contact-and-slip vector (four fingers × three features per finger), computed directly from the simulator's contact dynamics (processed by an MLP).
- **Policy Architecture**: A symmetric actor-critic architecture is used. The point cloud, proprioception, and tactile features are concatenated and fed into shared MLP branches leading to separate actor and critic heads.
- **Reward Function**: Task-specific dense reward terms (`r_task_t`) are combined with auxiliary terms, which penalize abrupt action changes, discourage object drops, and reward task completion. The auxiliary terms are kept consistent across all tasks and ablation conditions.
- **Deployment**: During physical robot deployment, the trained tactile estimator replaces the simulator-derived tactile channel, providing real-time contact and slip estimates to the simulation-trained policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VIBEACT focused on three aspects: the accuracy of the tactile estimator, the performance of policies in simulation, and the transferability to a physical robot.

#### 4.1. Tactile Estimator Evaluation

The tactile estimator was evaluated on a held-out split of a moving-object dataset, measuring F1 scores for binary contact-onset and slip-presence detection, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for slip magnitude.
- **Performance Metrics**: The full VIBEACT tactile estimator (sequential training, independent encoders, independent heads) achieved a Contact Onset F1 of 0.597 ± 0.101, Slip Presence F1 of 0.913 ± 0.054, and Slip Magnitude MAE of 4.736 ± 0.658 mm/s (averaged across four fingers).
- **Training Strategy Comparison**:
 - Pretraining only on fixed-object data resulted in significantly lower performance (e.g., 35.7% lower Contact Onset F1), indicating a notable domain gap between fixed-object and in-hand slip dynamics.
 - Joint training on both fixed and moving object datasets improved performance over pretraining alone but generally underperformed VIBEACT's sequential pretrain-then-fine-tune strategy. VIBEACT maintained better contact-onset F1 (7.5% higher) and lower slip-magnitude MAE (3.8% lower), suggesting that fine-tuning preserves sensitivity to sparse transient events.
- **Architectural Ablations**: Sharing parameters across fingers (e.g., shared encoder, shared heads) consistently degraded performance. Sharing the encoder and independent heads reduced contact-onset F1 by 2.3% and increased slip-magnitude MAE by 1.9%. Sharing both encoder and heads further worsened performance, suggesting that each fingertip exhibits distinct contact mechanics and vibration propagation characteristics that benefit from dedicated feature extraction and prediction.

#### 4.2. Tasks

VIBEACT was evaluated on five contact-rich manipulation tasks:
- **Box Climb and Can Climb**: The robot arm is fixed, and the hand must "walk" its fingers along a held YCB object (e.g., cracker box, soda can) under randomized gravity orientations.
- **Peg in Hole**: Requires inserting a pre-grasped cylinder sideways into a hole, emphasizing post-contact alignment and force control.
- **Cube Rotation**: Reorients a held cube purely through in-hand manipulation using repeated finger gaiting.
- **Nut Rotation**: Rotates a hex nut through coordinated hand and arm motion.
These tasks were selected to stress various failure modes where reactive tactile feedback is critical, such as grip loss, slip during insertion, or stalled rotation.

#### 4.3. Task Performance

Policies were evaluated in simulation across 100 trials and 3 random seeds, comparing VIBEACT against a proprioception and point cloud (Prop+PC) baseline and ablations of the tactile representation.
- **Overall Performance**: The full VIBEACT (Prop+PC + Contact Onset + Slip Presence + Slip Magnitude) achieved the highest success rates across all five tasks.
- **Gains from Tactile Feedback**:
 - Tasks requiring significant reactive fingertip control showed the largest gains: Cube Rotation and Peg in Hole saw success rates increase by approximately 51 and 24 percentage points, respectively, from near-failing baselines.
 - Can Climb and Nut Rotation improved by about 16 and 15 points.
 - Box Climb showed a modest improvement, suggesting that for larger objects, coarser point-cloud feedback might already be largely sufficient for finger gaiting.
- **Tactile Channel Ablations**:
 - **Slip Magnitude**: This channel was identified as critical, consistently driving the largest performance improvements across all tasks when added. It provided the graded feedback necessary for sustained reactive control.
 - **Contact Onset**: In isolation, contact onset yielded inconsistent gains and sometimes negatively impacted performance. Its sparse, transient nature may limit its utility for continuous reactive control if not combined with other tactile information.
 - **Slip Presence**: Offered an intermediate level of benefit compared to contact onset or slip magnitude.
 - These results indicate that the continuous slip magnitude signal is the primary contributor to VIBEACT's performance advantage.

#### 4.4. Real-World Deployment

Simulation-trained policies were deployed on the physical robot platform for three tasks: Box Climb, Can Climb, and Nut Rotation. During deployment, the simulator-derived tactile channel was replaced by the output of the real-world trained tactile estimator.
- **Transfer Success**: VIBEACT consistently improved success rates over the Prop+PC baseline on all deployed tasks. For example, Can Climb success increased from 11/20 to 19/20, and Nut Rotation from 1/20 to 8/20.
- **Implication**: These real-world results demonstrate that the contact-and-slip representation, learned from real microphone recordings and used as a drop-in observation, retains its effectiveness despite real-world sensing noise and actuation uncertainties, facilitating successful sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VIBEACT introduces a significant methodological advance in the field of dexterous robot manipulation and sim-to-real transfer by effectively integrating high-bandwidth vibrotactile sensing without requiring computationally expensive or physically unrealistic simulation of raw audio signals. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Complex Sensors**: The framework provides a practical solution to a longstanding challenge: leveraging complex, difficult-to-simulate sensor data (like vibro-acoustics) in simulation-driven reinforcement learning. By using a physically grounded intermediate representation, VIBEACT decouples the sensing problem from the control problem, making it feasible to train policies that exploit rich real-world sensory information. This paradigm could be extended to other challenging sensor modalities where direct simulation is impractical.
- **Enhancing Dexterous Manipulation Capabilities**: The empirical results demonstrate that VIBEACT significantly improves success rates on a variety of contact-rich tasks, particularly those requiring sustained reactive control such as in-hand reorientation and precision insertion. This capability is critical for robots operating in unstructured environments, bringing them closer to the dexterity and adaptability of human hands. The identification of continuous slip magnitude as a primary driver for these gains provides insights into essential tactile feedback for such tasks.
- **Cost-Effective and Scalable Tactile Sensing**: The use of inexpensive, compact piezoelectric microphones allows for a cost-effective and scalable integration of high-bandwidth tactile sensing into multi-fingered robot hands. Unlike some vision-based or highly specialized tactile sensors, piezoelectric microphones do not alter the external geometry or material of the fingertip, offering a less intrusive sensing solution.
- **Robustness to Real-World Conditions**: The policies trained with VIBEACT demonstrate improved robustness to domain randomization in simulation and successfully transfer to a physical robot. This indicates that the chosen intermediate representation effectively captures the salient features of contact dynamics necessary for robust control, even in the presence of real-world noise and uncertainties.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: VIBEACT lays groundwork for future advancements in tactile-aware robotics. It encourages exploration into richer intermediate representations that might capture more nuanced interaction dynamics (e.g., contact location, texture, force vectors) while maintaining sim-to-real alignment. It also opens avenues for designing more sophisticated policy architectures that can leverage the spatial and temporal structure of per-finger contact events more effectively. The automatic digital-clone labeling pipeline is a valuable contribution, reducing the manual annotation burden for large-scale tactile data collection.

In summary, VIBEACT presents a pragmatic and effective strategy for harnessing the power of high-bandwidth tactile sensing in robot learning, contributing significantly to the development of more capable and dexterous robotic systems for complex manipulation tasks.
