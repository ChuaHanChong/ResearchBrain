# Reinforcement Learning with Random Delays

> [!summary] Summary
> Delay-Correcting Actor-Critic (DCAC) tackles reinforcement learning under random action and observation delays. The paper formalizes Random-Delay MDPs, augmenting state with an action buffer and the measured delays, then introduces partial trajectory resampling, which rewrites stored action buffers in hindsight to turn off-policy fragments into on-policy ones. This enables unbiased, low-variance multi-step soft value backups, and DCAC clearly outperforms SAC and RTAC on delay-augmented MuJoCo.

## Problem

- Prior delayed-RL work assumes constant, known delays, an unrealistic idealization for real-world remote control where communication delays over WiFi vary randomly step to step.
- Existing methods try to undelay the environment by planning with learned state-prediction models, which is model-based, compounding-error prone, and costly rather than planning-free.
- Off-policy multi-step backups need importance sampling or Retrace corrections that suffer variance explosion, while one-step backups leave delay-induced credit assignment unresolved.

## Method

- ==Random-Delay Markov Decision Process==: state augmented with the last K sent actions plus the observation and action delays themselves, restoring the Markov property and modelling superseded, self-correlated delays.
- ==Partial trajectory resampling operator==: recursively rewrites action buffers of stored sub-trajectories under the current policy, provably on-policy while total delay exceeds the fragment index, needing no importance weights.
- **Delay-Correcting Actor-Critic**: a ==SAC== variant whose critic learns the soft state-value, with both critic and actor losses using variable-length ==n-step resampled targets== backpropagated through the action buffer.

## Results

- On delay-augmented MuJoCo with constant delays of **two** observation and **three** action steps (total **five**), DCAC strongly beats SAC and RTAC using identical augmented observations, **six** seeds, **90%** confidence intervals.
- Under real WiFi delays collected from a flying robot and discretized at **20 ms** timesteps, DCAC dominates baselines and alone escapes HalfCheetah local minima; Ant defeats every tested method.
- Naive SAC on unaugmented delayed observations is near-random even at a **one**-step action delay; DCAC reduces to RTAC when observation delay is **zero** and action delay **one**, with n-step bias scaled by gamma to the n.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Measuring delays and placing them inside the state is cheap instrumentation that converts a broken Markov assumption into exploitable structure for credit assignment.
> - Known partial dynamics can replace importance weights: resample only the history that provably had no effect, gaining off-policy multi-step estimation without variance explosion.
> - Delay tolerance has limits; under long random delays hard high-dimensional tasks stay unsolved, and maximum buffer length plus delay clipping remain engineering design choices.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved remarkable success in various domains, from mastering complex games to controlling robots. However, a significant gap exists between theoretical RL frameworks and their practical application in real-world scenarios. One of the most prevalent and challenging discrepancies is the presence of **delays**—where actions are not applied instantly, and observations are not received instantaneously.

Historically, RL research dealing with delays has largely focused on environments with **constant delays**. Early works, like those by Katsikopoulos & Engelbrecht (2003) and Walsh et al. (2008), introduced the concept of "Constantly Delayed Markov Decision Processes" (CDMDPs). Approaches to handle constant delays typically involve either augmenting the state space with a history of actions or observations (the "augmented approach") or developing model-based planning algorithms to predict the environment's future state. However, the "augmented approach" was often deemed intractable due to the state-space explosion, leading many to pursue model-based solutions. More recently, methods like Real-Time Reinforcement Learning (RTRL) formalized by Ramstedt & Pal (2019) specifically addressed constant action computation/transmission delays (equivalent to α=1, ω=0).

This paper addresses a crucial, yet under-explored, aspect: **random delays**. Real-world systems, especially those relying on networked communication (e.g., controlling a drone over Wi-Fi, as illustrated by the paper's motivating WiFi delay dataset), rarely exhibit perfectly constant delays. Network latency, packet loss, and processing times can introduce significant and variable delays in both action execution and observation reception. Standard RL algorithms, including modern off-policy methods, typically assume instantaneous feedback loops (i.e., a perfectly Markovian environment), leading to deteriorated performance or outright failure when delays are present, especially when they are random and violate the Markov property.

This work specifically positions itself as an **off-policy, planning-free approach** that tackles random delays. Unlike model-based methods, which can be sample-inefficient and computationally expensive, off-policy algorithms are crucial for real-world applications where data collection is costly. The innovation lies in providing a principled way to maintain low-bias and low-variance multi-step value estimation in randomly delayed settings, effectively bridging the gap between theoretical guarantees and practical applicability. By generalizing the RTRL framework and moving beyond constant delays, this paper advances the state-of-the-art in bringing RL closer to robust real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research stem from the fundamental challenges posed by delays in real-world Reinforcement Learning applications:

1. **Address the Real-World Problem of Random Delays:** The authors explicitly highlight that action and observation delays are "commonly encountered in real-world applications," citing examples like remote control scenarios (Mahmood et al., 2018), Gran Turismo racing (Fuchs et al., 2020), and quadrotor control (Hwangbo et al., 2017). They emphasize that prior work predominantly focused on the "unrealistic assumption of constant delays." The paper is motivated by a desire to enable robust RL solutions in more realistic, randomly delayed environments, illustrating this with a real-world WiFi delay dataset.

2. **Overcome the Violation of the Markov Assumption:** When delays are present, particularly if they are longer than one time-step, the immediate observation does not contain all necessary information about the current state of the environment. Specifically, the last sent action, if not yet applied, becomes an unobserved part of the environment's state, violating the Markov property. A key objective is to redefine the state space to restore the Markov property in the presence of random delays.

3. **Improve Credit Assignment in Delayed Environments:** Delays make credit assignment significantly more challenging. An action taken at time `t` might only affect rewards or observations at `t + k`, where `k` is the total delay. This temporal misalignment obscures the causal link between actions and their consequences, making it difficult for agents to learn effective policies. The goal is to develop a mechanism that explicitly compensates for this difficulty.

4. **Develop an Efficient Off-Policy and Planning-Free Algorithm:** Traditional approaches to delays often involve model-based planning, which can be computationally intensive and sample-inefficient. On-policy methods also suffer from sample inefficiency. The authors aim to develop an *off-policy* algorithm that can efficiently reuse old samples, which is critical for real-world applications where data collection is expensive. Furthermore, the objective is to achieve this without relying on complex planning mechanisms.

5. **Enable Low-Bias and Low-Variance Multi-step Value Estimation:** Standard 1-step Q-learning or actor-critic updates can struggle with delayed rewards and observations. Multi-step estimators can reduce bias from function approximation but are often incompatible with off-policy learning due to variance explosion (e.g., with importance sampling). A core objective is to derive a method for multi-step value estimation that maintains low bias *and* low variance in the randomly delayed setting.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between theoretical RL and practical deployment by designing an algorithm, Delay-Correcting Actor-Critic (DCAC), that robustly handles the complexities introduced by random communication delays, thereby making RL more viable for real-world control systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology revolves around two main innovations: a formal definition of environments with random delays and a novel technique for partial trajectory resampling.

#### 3.1. Formalizing Random-Delay Markov Decision Processes (RDMDPs)

To restore the Markov property, the authors propose augmenting the standard MDP state space. A **Random-Delay Markov Decision Process (RDMDP)** is defined as `(E, pω, pα)`, where `E = (S, A, μ, p)` is the underlying undelayed MDP. The augmented state space `X` becomes `S × A^K × N^2`, encompassing:
- `s ∈ S`: The delayed observation.
- `u ∈ A^K`: A buffer storing the last `K` sent actions, where `K` is the combined maximum possible observation and action delay. This buffer is crucial because older actions, along with the delayed observation, describe the true current state of the undelayed environment.
- `ω ∈ N`: The observation delay (time from observation capture to agent use).
- `α ∈ N`: The action delay (time from action computation to environment application, relative to observation capture).

The transition distribution of the RDMDP `˜p` captures how `s, u, ω, α` evolve. It accounts for:
1. **Observation delay dynamics (`pω`):** How observation delays evolve, allowing for repetition or skipping of observations if new ones are not available.
2. **Action buffer dynamics (`pu`):** A simple shift operation, where the new action `a` is prepended, and the oldest action is dropped.
3. **Observation, reward, and action delay evolution (`fΔ`):** A recursive definition that describes how the undelayed environment transitions given a sequence of actions, and how rewards accumulate across skipped transitions. This accounts for the `ω + α` total delay and allows tracking the influence of actions.

This augmented state space ensures that the RDMDP satisfies the Markov property, allowing standard RL algorithms to, in principle, operate within it.

#### 3.2. Partial Trajectory Resampling

Even with an augmented state space, simply applying off-policy algorithms like SAC directly would face issues. When replaying a trajectory fragment from the replay buffer, the actions within its action buffers were generated by an *old policy*. If the current policy `π` is significantly different from the old policy `μ`, these "off-policy" action buffers invalidate the use of on-policy multi-step value estimators, which are crucial for effective credit assignment.

The key insight is that in delayed environments, not all actions in the buffer immediately influence subsequent observations and rewards. Specifically, if a sub-trajectory is short enough, some "recent" actions in the buffer were not yet applied to the undelayed environment when the subsequent observations were captured.

The authors introduce the **partial trajectory resampling operator (`σπn`)**. This operator recursively updates the *most recent* actions of each action buffer in an off-policy sub-trajectory, replacing them with actions sampled from the *current policy* `π`. Critically, this resampling is valid *only if* none of the delayed observations within that sub-trajectory depend on any of the resampled actions. This condition is formally expressed as `ω*t + α*t ≥ t`, where `t` is the index within the sub-trajectory. If this condition holds, the resampled trajectory fragment can be considered "on-policy," even though its earlier parts were sampled by an older policy.

This technique is powerful because it allows for:
- **Off-policy data reuse:** Samples from an old policy can be "corrected" to be compatible with the current policy.
- **Bias reduction without variance explosion:** Unlike importance sampling (e.g., in Retrace), which reweights samples and can suffer from high variance, partial resampling directly transforms the trajectory, avoiding policy ratios.

#### 3.3. Delay-Correcting Actor-Critic (DCAC) Algorithm

The partial trajectory resampling principle is then applied to derive **Delay-Correcting Actor-Critic (DCAC)**, an extension of Soft Actor-Critic (SAC).

- **Value Approximation:** DCAC uses an `n`-step soft state-value estimator (`ˆvsoftn`) as its target for the critic loss. When sampling a trajectory fragment `(x0, τn)` from the replay memory, `n` is chosen as the maximum length for which the condition `ωi + βi < i` (Theorem 1 condition) is *not* met for any `i` within the fragment. This means `n` is the longest possible sub-trajectory that can be partially resampled. The sampled `τn` is then transformed by `σπn` into an on-policy `τ*n`, which is then used to compute the `n`-step value target for the critic. This results in the `LDCACv` loss.
- **Policy Improvement:** The actor loss (`LDCACπ`) is also derived from the `n`-step soft state-value estimate obtained from the partially resampled trajectory. This involves backpropagation through time through the action buffer to update the actor. The authors theoretically show that `LDCACπ` is a "less biased version of the SAC actor loss," with the bias reduced by a factor of `E[γ^n]`.

In essence, DCAC intelligently leverages the known delay dynamics to perform "on-policy-like" multi-step backups from off-policy data, specifically addressing the credit assignment problem introduced by random delays while maintaining sample efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DCAC is conducted on delay-augmented versions of the widely used MuJoCo continuous control benchmark environments, using a custom OpenAI Gym wrapper released alongside the code. This facilitates reproducibility and future research.

**Comparison Baselines:**
- **Naive SAC:** Uses only unaugmented delayed observations, violating the Markov assumption. This baseline consistently performed poorly, essentially yielding near-random results, highlighting the necessity of state augmentation.
- **SAC (with augmented state):** Uses the RDMDP augmented observation space, providing a fair, like-for-like comparison against DCAC, demonstrating the benefit of DCAC's delay-correcting mechanism beyond just state augmentation.
- **RTAC (Real-Time Actor-Critic):** A special case of DCAC where observation transmission is instantaneous (ω=0) and action computation/transmission constantly takes one time-step (α=1). This serves as a strong baseline, as DCAC generalizes this specific scenario.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Superiority in Constant Delays:** In environments with constant delays (e.g., ω=2, α=3), DCAC exhibits a "very strong advantage in performance" compared to both SAC (augmented) and RTAC. This demonstrates that DCAC's multi-step, partial resampling approach effectively handles longer, constant delays, going beyond what simple state augmentation or specific 1-step delay-aware algorithms can achieve. When ω=0 and α=1 (the RTAC setting), DCAC effectively reduces to RTAC and slightly outperforms standard SAC, confirming its robustness in this baseline scenario.

2. **Clear Dominance in Real-World Random Delays:** The most compelling results come from experiments using communication delays sampled from a real-world WiFi dataset (Figure 2). In these challenging scenarios, DCAC "clearly dominates the baselines." For instance, in HalfCheetah, only DCAC manages to escape local minima and achieve meaningful performance, while SAC and RTAC struggle. This highlights DCAC's practical applicability and robustness in the face of stochastic and realistic delay patterns.

3. **Handling of Practical Real-World Nuances:** The authors demonstrate how their approach accommodates real-world complexities:
 - **Superseded Information:** When new actions or observations arrive while older ones are still in transit, only the most recently produced information is kept, mirroring real-world communication protocols.
 - **Clipping Delays:** Extremely long delays (outliers in the WiFi dataset) are clipped, recognizing that in practice, maximum acceptable delays are design choices and often discarded.
 - **Accumulated Rewards:** The reward system is designed to accumulate intermediate rewards in case observations are skipped or repeated, ensuring accurate credit assignment over delayed feedback.

4. **Theoretical Justification for Bias Reduction:** Proposition 1 theoretically demonstrates that the DCAC actor loss is a "less biased version of the SAC actor loss," with the bias reduced by a factor of `E[γ^n]`. This formalizes the advantage of multi-step estimation enabled by partial resampling.

In summary, the results convincingly show that DCAC significantly outperforms existing off-policy methods in both constant and, crucially, random delay settings, demonstrating its effectiveness and practical viability for real-world applications where delays are a non-negotiable factor.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Reinforcement Learning with Random Delays" carries significant implications for both the theoretical understanding and practical deployment of Reinforcement Learning systems.

1. **Bridging the Reality Gap:** The most immediate and profound impact is its contribution to bridging the gap between theoretical RL and real-world applications. By explicitly and effectively addressing random delays, which are ubiquitous in networked control systems, robotics, and remote operations, this work makes deep RL algorithms more robust and deployable in complex, asynchronous environments. This move beyond the "unrealistic assumption of constant delays" is critical for industrial adoption of RL.

2. **Principled Framework for Random Delays:** The formalization of Random-Delay Markov Decision Processes (RDMDPs) is a key theoretical contribution. By augmenting the state space with an action buffer and explicit delay measurements (ω, α), the paper provides a principled way to maintain the Markov property. This robust definition can serve as a foundation for future research in delayed RL, enabling more rigorous analysis and algorithm design.

3. **Novel Partial Trajectory Resampling:** The introduction of the `σπn` operator for partial trajectory resampling is a novel and highly impactful methodological innovation. It elegantly solves the challenge of performing multi-step, off-policy value estimation in delayed environments without suffering from the high variance issues commonly associated with importance sampling. This technique allows for efficient reuse of valuable experience data, making DCAC more sample-efficient than on-policy or model-based alternatives in delayed settings. This innovation could potentially inspire similar "resampling" or "correction" techniques in other areas of off-policy RL where state information might be incomplete or misaligned.

4. **Enhanced Credit Assignment:** DCAC explicitly tackles the notoriously difficult problem of credit assignment in delayed environments. By leveraging delay measurements to "undelay" or "reconstruct" on-policy sub-trajectories, the algorithm can more accurately attribute rewards to the actions that caused them, even across significant temporal gaps. This leads to more effective learning and improved policy performance.

5. **Practical Toolset and Scalability:** The release of the OpenAI Gym wrapper for augmenting environments with custom delays is a significant practical contribution to the RL community. It lowers the barrier for other researchers to experiment with and build upon delay-aware RL algorithms. The paper also discusses practical considerations such as measuring delays, handling superseded information, managing action buffer size (e.g., with run-length encoding), and dealing with delayed rewards, indicating a strong awareness of real-world implementation challenges. Its design with "real-world applications in mind" makes it scalable to various scenarios.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays the groundwork for several promising future directions, including the potential for merging actor and critic networks using PopArt for further simplification and improved performance, and exploring "time-step agnostic RL." This suggests a broader vision for developing RL agents that are more adaptive to varying system latencies and execution rates.

In conclusion, "Reinforcement Learning with Random Delays" represents a significant step forward in making RL viable for a wider range of real-world applications. By providing a theoretically sound and empirically validated method to handle random communication delays, it moves the field closer to robust, autonomous control in dynamic and asynchronous environments.
