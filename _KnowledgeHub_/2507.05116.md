# VOTE: Vision-Language-Action Optimization with Trajectory Ensemble Voting

> [!summary] Summary
> VOTE introduces a Vision-Language-Action framework that employs a special token for efficient action generation and a trajectory ensemble voting mechanism for robust action selection. This approach significantly reduces inference latency by up to 48.8x on A6000 GPUs and 38.6x on edge platforms, while achieving higher task success rates, such as 98.0% on the LIBERO benchmark.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models suffer from high inference latency and computational costs due to extensive token generation for actions.
- Many VLA models inefficiently utilize generated action sequences, often discarding valuable historical predictions and leading to less stable robot trajectories.
- Existing action aggregation methods struggle to effectively combine predictions from different timesteps without introducing inconsistencies or erroneous modes.

## Method

![[2507.05116-method.png]]
*The VOTE framework integrates a Vision-Language Model backbone with a specialized action head and an ensemble voting mechanism to optimize both inference speed and execution robustness.*

- A training framework employs a special `` token to represent entire action chunks, significantly reducing the number of generated tokens for action prediction.
- An MLP Action Head with a bottleneck architecture directly maps the `` token's hidden state to continuous, multi-step action predictions.
- An ensemble voting inference strategy aggregates current and historical action predictions by identifying consistent modes based on cosine similarity to the newest prediction.

## Results

- VOTE achieved significant inference speedups of up to 48.8x on A6000 GPUs and 38.6x on NVIDIA Jetson Orin, with minimal memory overhead compared to existing models.
- The framework demonstrated higher task success rates, notably an average of 98.0% on the LIBERO benchmark, surpassing leading VLA models like CogACT and SpatialVLA.
- Training efficiency was improved, requiring less than 20% of training samples for most tasks compared to OpenVLA-OFT and 15.6% of epochs relative to SpatialVLA for comparable or better performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representing a multi-step, multi-dimensional action sequence with a single special token can drastically reduce computational overhead without sacrificing performance.
> - An intelligent, similarity-based ensemble voting mechanism can effectively leverage historical action predictions to improve trajectory stability and filter out inconsistent actions.
> - A compact bottleneck architecture for the action head can be more efficient and performant than larger, isotropic designs for VLA tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose robotic policies capable of performing diverse tasks across various embodiments and real-world interactions represents a significant challenge in robotics. Recent advancements have leveraged Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which are typically built upon Vision Language Models (VLMs) through continuous training or fine-tuning on extensive robot datasets. These models enable robots to execute complex tasks based on natural language instructions, demonstrating performance in familiar environments and with known objects. Examples of such models include RT-2-X, OpenVLA, CogACT, SpatialVLA, and π$^0$. Their success is attributed to the generalization capabilities of VLMs combined with architectural designs that integrate the VLM backbone with robot action output heads.

Despite these advancements, existing VLA models exhibit certain limitations. Some approaches, such as those employing diffusion action heads (e.g., CogACT), improve generalization but incur high computational costs during both training and inference due to the diffusion process. Other models, like SpatialVLA, aim to enhance generalization by incorporating additional visual modules for 3D position encoding, which can provide richer visual cues. However, this strategy introduces significant pre-processing overhead and increases the number of visual tokens, thereby impacting inference speed and requiring longer input sequences. A general observation across many VLA models is that the VLM decoding phase constitutes a substantial portion of the overall latency in action prediction. Furthermore, the extensive training data requirements and the need to pad numerous empty action embeddings as inputs contribute to high training costs. For instance, diffusion-based action heads have been noted to converge at a slower rate, demanding more gradient steps.

Another identified limitation pertains to the utilization of generated actions. VLA models typically predict a sequence of future actions at each time step. However, common practice involves executing only the action predicted for the immediate current time step, discarding historical predictions for that same time step that were made based on earlier observations. This approach can result in less stable trajectories and a potential degradation in performance due to insufficient leveraging of available temporal information. While prior works have explored combining actions from current and past inferences, these methods have sometimes been characterized as ineffective, either producing meaningless outputs, employing overly simplistic aggregation strategies, or being overly reliant on potentially incorrect current predictions. Specifically, actions predicted at different timesteps might belong to distinct modes, and a simple aggregation without careful consideration could lead to a composite action that does not align with any valid mode. This context motivates the development of methods that can enhance both the computational efficiency and the effective utilization of generated actions in VLA models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The present work is motivated by two primary drawbacks observed in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: the generation of a large number of tokens or reliance on computationally intensive diffusion processes, leading to substantial inference latency and increased training costs; and the insufficient utilization of generated action predictions, which can result in suboptimal performance. The key objectives are designed to address these limitations.

#### 2.1. Massive Computational Overhead

A significant challenge in current VLA models is the high computational burden associated with generating actions.

- **Large Inference Latency:** Typical VLA models are designed to predict multiple tokens corresponding to various action dimensions for each required action. This token generation process, particularly within the VLM backbone's decoding phase, constitutes a major portion of the overall latency for action prediction, often exceeding 50% across different models. Diffusion-based VLA architectures, such as CogACT, further exacerbate this issue by introducing additional latency from multi-step denoising or integration during inference. Models that incorporate multimodal high-level visual representations, such as 3D information (e.g., SpatialVLA), require feeding a large number of additional visual input tokens to the VLM, leading to a substantial increase in latency.
- **Massive Training Cost:** The fine-tuning process for VLA models on new tasks and embodiments typically involves large-scale pretraining datasets and additional downstream data. During training, the need to pad multiple empty action embeddings, corresponding to the numerous output tokens for actions, significantly increases input sequence length and computational cost. This extensive token handling contributes to prolonged training times and higher resource consumption. Furthermore, diffusion action heads have been observed to converge more slowly, requiring an estimated 1.67 to 2 times more gradient steps compared to alternative action heads, which adds to the training overhead.

#### 2.2. Insufficient Utilization of Generated Actions

Beyond computational efficiency, the way VLA models utilize their action predictions presents another area for improvement.

- **Underutilization of Historical Predictions:** At each time step, a VLA model typically predicts a sequence of actions for several subsequent time steps. However, in most implementations, robots only execute the action for the current time step from the most recent inference, effectively discarding the historical predictions for that same current time step that were made by the model in previous inference cycles. This practice overlooks valuable temporal information and past model insights, potentially leading to less stable trajectories and a degradation in task performance.
- **Ineffective Combination Strategies:** Attempts to combine current and historical action predictions have been explored in previous research. However, these methods often suffer from limitations such as producing meaningless aggregated outputs, employing overly simplistic combination strategies (e.g., simple averaging that might mix different action modes), or maintaining a heavy reliance on the most recent prediction even when it might be incorrect. The challenge lies in effectively aggregating predictions made under different temporal contexts while ensuring the resulting action is coherent and aligned with the task.

In response to these identified drawbacks, the objectives of this work are to develop a VLA training framework that generates fewer action-related tokens and an inference optimization technique that employs a novel voting-based ensemble strategy to combine current and previous action predictions more effectively. These innovations aim to collectively reduce inference latency, decrease training costs, and enhance the overall utilization of generated actions, thereby improving performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named VOTE (Vision-Language-Action Optimization with Trajectory Ensemble Voting), introduces a training methodology for efficient token generation and an inference optimization technique based on ensemble voting. The architecture generates actions from an image $I$ and a language instruction $l$, predicting a temporal action sequence $(a_t, a_{t+1}, \dots, a_{t+N})$ for $N$ future steps, where actions are represented by 7 degrees of freedom (DoF) for end-effector pose and gripper state.

#### 3.1. Training Framework for Reduced Token Generation

The training framework aims to reduce the number of tokens generated, thereby decreasing computational overhead.

- **Special Token `<ACT>`:** A unique token, `<ACT>`, is integrated into the tokenizer of the Large Language Model (LLM) used within the VLA architecture. This token explicitly signals the action prediction task. During training, `<ACT>` is appended to the end of each language instruction sequence as the target token label.
- **Action Generation Process:**
 1. The VLA model processes the language instruction $l$ and image $I$ to produce hidden states $h$.
 2. The hidden state specifically corresponding to the `<ACT>` token ($h_{<ACT>}$) is extracted. This design ensures that the model generates only one single `<ACT>` token, rather than multiple tokens for individual action dimensions.
 3. The extracted $h_{<ACT>}$ is then passed through an MLP Action Head. This head is designed to directly predict an entire chunk of consecutive actions.
- **Action Head Architecture:** The Action Head utilizes a bottleneck design. This involves downsampling the $h_{<ACT>}$ dimension, followed by multiple bottleneck blocks. Each bottleneck block performs an upsampling and then a downsampling operation, incorporating a SiLU activation function and dropout. This architecture is described as providing improved performance with fewer parameters compared to an isotropic architecture.
- **Advantages of the Training Framework:**
 - **Reduced Token Count:** By representing an entire action chunk (N time steps, D dimensions) with a single `<ACT>` token, the framework significantly reduces the number of generated tokens compared to traditional methods that might generate N*D tokens.
 - **Faster Inference:** This reduction translates to a single forward pass through the action head instead of multiple sequential decoder passes, leading to non-marginal inference acceleration.
 - **Lower Training Cost:** Fewer output tokens alleviate the need for padding numerous empty action embeddings during training, reducing input/output training tokens and speeding up decoding.
 - **Action-Tokenizer-Free Design:** The direct mapping from the hidden state of `<ACT>` to continuous actions via an MLP action head eliminates the need for a complex action tokenizer, enabling efficient parallel computation.
- **Training Objectives:** The training process combines two loss components:
 - **Action Loss ($L_{action}$):** An L1 loss calculated between the predicted actions ($\hat{a}$) and the ground-truth actions ($a$) across all action dimensions.
 - **Token Loss ($L_{token}$):** A cross-entropy loss applied to the prediction of the `<ACT>` token and all instruction tokens.
 - These are combined into a total weighted loss ($L_{total} = \lambda_{token}L_{token} + \lambda_{action}L_{action}$). Empirical tuning set $\lambda_{action} = 0.99$ and $\lambda_{token} = 0.01$ for optimal performance.

#### 3.2. Ensemble Voting for Inference Optimization

To address the insufficient utilization of generated actions, VOTE introduces a voting-based adaptive ensemble strategy during inference.

- **Problem Statement:** During inference, a VLA model predicts a sequence of actions. Standard practice often discards historical predictions for the current time step, relying solely on the most recent inference. This can lead to unstable trajectories.
- **Ensemble Voting Mechanism:**
 1. At time step $t$, the current observation $o_t$ yields a new action prediction $(a_t|o_t)$.
 2. A "committee" $H$ is formed, comprising the current prediction $(a_t|o_t)$ and $K$ historical predictions for time $t$ that were made based on previous observations ($o_{t-K}, \dots, o_{t-1}$).
 3. The cosine similarity is computed between each action in $H$ and the *current/newest* prediction $(a_t|o_t)$.
 4. Based on a predefined threshold $\tau$ (empirically set to 0.5), $H$ is divided into two subsets: $M$ containing actions highly similar to $(a_t|o_t)$, and $N$ containing actions with lower similarity.
 5. The final ensembled action $\tilde{a}_t$ for time step $t$ is calculated by averaging all actions within the majority subset (i.e., if $|M| > |N|$, average actions in $M$; otherwise, average actions in $N$).
- **Advantages of Ensemble Voting:**
 - **Improved Robustness:** By considering multiple predictions and leveraging a majority vote, the method selects actions more likely to be correct, enhancing trajectory stability.
 - **Mode Filtering:** It effectively filters out inconsistent or unreasonable predictions that might arise from different action modes, which can be an issue with simple averaging.
 - **Prioritization of Current Information:** The current prediction acts as the reference point for similarity calculations, ensuring that the most recent observation's influence is considered.
 - **Error Mitigation:** If the current prediction is erroneous, and a majority of historical predictions differ significantly from it, the method can disregard the incorrect current prediction and ensemble the more consistent historical predictions.
 - **Empirical Performance:** This strategy has demonstrated superior performance compared to straightforward averaging or static-weighted aggregation methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VOTE was conducted across various benchmarks, focusing on task success rates, inference efficiency, and the impact of architectural and strategic choices.

#### 4.1. Performance Evaluation

- **SimplerEnv WidowX Robot:** VOTE achieved an average success rate of 58.3% across four manipulation tasks (Put Spoon, Put Carrot, Stack Block, Put Eggplant). This performance surpassed state-of-the-art methods such as CogACT (51.3% average) and SpatialVLA (42.7% average). Specifically, VOTE recorded a 50.0% success rate on "Stack Block" and 95.8% on "Put Eggplant."
- **LIBERO Benchmark:** On the LIBERO benchmark, VOTE demonstrated superior performance across all four task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long). With a chunk size of 8, VOTE achieved an average success rate of 98.0%, outperforming OpenVLA-OFT (95.3%), π$^0$ (94.2%), and SpatialVLA (78.1%). The model achieved 98.8% for Spatial, 99.8% for Object, 97.6% for Goal, and 95.6% for Long tasks.
- **SimplerEnv Google Robot:** In the Visual Matching setting, VOTE achieved an average success rate of 74.9%, performing competitively against CogACT (82.7%) and SpatialVLA (73.8% fine-tuned). In the Variant Aggregation setting, VOTE's average success rate was 60.2%, compared to CogACT's 66.2% and SpatialVLA's 67.5% (fine-tuned).

#### 4.2. Inference Efficiency

The inference efficiency of VOTE was evaluated on both high-performance GPUs (NVIDIA A6000) and edge computing platforms (NVIDIA Jetson Orin).

- **NVIDIA A6000 GPU:**
 - VOTE achieved a latency of 78 ms (for both chunk sizes 8 and 16).
 - It demonstrated throughputs of 102.6 Hz (chunk 8) and 205.2 Hz (chunk 16).
 - This translates to a speedup of 24.4x (chunk 8) and 48.8x (chunk 16) compared to OpenVLA.
 - In contrast, OpenVLA had a latency of 240 ms, and SpatialVLA had 400 ms.
 - VRAM usage was comparable to OpenVLA (14.40 GB for VOTE vs. 14.35 GB for OpenVLA).
- **NVIDIA Jetson Orin (Edge Platform):**
 - VOTE maintained a latency of 346 ms (for both chunk sizes 8 and 16).
 - Throughput reached 23.1 Hz (chunk 8) and 46.2 Hz (chunk 16).
 - This represents a speedup of 19.3x (chunk 8) and 38.6x (chunk 16) over OpenVLA (latency 836 ms, throughput 1.2 Hz).
 - Notably, CogACT failed to execute on the Orin platform due to Out-of-Memory (OOM) errors, while OpenVLA-OFT incurred 33.8% more memory cost. VOTE exhibited negligible memory overhead (0.7%).

#### 4.3. Training Efficiency

- **LIBERO Benchmark:** VOTE demonstrated improved training efficiency compared to SpatialVLA. On average, VOTE required only 15.6% of the training effort (measured in epochs) to achieve higher success rates across LIBERO tasks. For example, on the LIBERO-Long task, VOTE achieved a 95.6% success rate with 25% of SpatialVLA's training effort, a 40.1% improvement over SpatialVLA.
- **Comparison with OpenVLA-OFT:** When training effort is defined by the total number of samples trained (training steps multiplied by global batch size), VOTE required less than 20% of the training effort for object, spatial, and goal tasks, and 54% for long-horizon tasks, compared to OpenVLA-OFT.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Ensemble Voting Strategy:** A comparison of action ensemble strategies on SimplerEnv (Google Robot VM/VA, WidowX Robot) showed that the proposed "Vote" ensemble strategy achieved an average success rate of 64.5%. This was higher than "None" (47.1%), "Temporal" (53.7%), "Adaptive" (60.4%), and simple "Average" (61.6%) strategies.
- **Action Head Architecture:** The bottleneck architecture, with 37 million parameters, achieved an average success rate of 64.5% on the SimplerEnv tasks (Google Robot VM/VA, WidowX Robot). This outperformed the isotropic architecture, which had more parameters (50 million) and a lower average success rate of 62.8%.
- **Hyperparameter $\tau$ Robustness:** The study indicated that VOTE's performance is robust to the choice of the cosine similarity threshold $\tau$ for the ensemble voting strategy. Optimal performance of 58.3% (WidowX Robot SR) was achieved when $\tau = 0.5$, and the method maintained superior performance across a range from 0.0 to 0.8, suggesting it does not require extensive hyperparameter tuning for this specific value.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The contributions of VOTE present several implications for the field of robotic manipulation using Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

**Enhanced Deployability:** The primary impact lies in the significant improvement in inference efficiency. VOTE's ability to achieve up to 48.8x speedup on an A6000 GPU and 38.6x speedup on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin edge platform, while maintaining competitive memory footprints, addresses a critical bottleneck for real-world robotic deployment. Real-time responsiveness is essential for many robotic tasks, and the demonstrated throughput (e.g., 46 Hz on Orin) indicates that VOTE is well-suited for practical integration into edge-computing-constrained robotic systems. This increased efficiency could facilitate wider adoption of VLA models in scenarios requiring low-latency control.

**Improved Performance and Robustness:** The consistent achievement of higher success rates across diverse and challenging benchmarks, such as LIBERO and SimplerEnv, demonstrates that VOTE's methodological innovations contribute to more effective and reliable action generation. The ensemble voting strategy, in particular, enhances the stability of action trajectories by intelligently aggregating predictions and mitigating the impact of individual erroneous outputs. This robust performance is critical for tasks in dynamic and uncertain real-world environments.

**Reduced Resource Consumption:** By drastically reducing the number of tokens generated and employing an efficient bottleneck action head, VOTE lowers both training and inference costs. The reduced training effort makes fine-tuning VLA models for new tasks and embodiments more accessible and economically viable, accelerating research and development cycles. This efficiency also broadens the accessibility of advanced VLA models to researchers and practitioners with limited computational resources.

**Simplified Model Development:** The introduction of a special `<ACT>` token and an action-tokenizer-free training methodology simplifies the overall VLA pipeline. This approach eliminates the need for complex action tokenizers or diffusion-based techniques, streamlining the process of developing and fine-tuning new VLA models. This simplification could lower the barrier to entry for VLA model development and promote innovation in architectural design.

**Generalizability of Methodology:** The "plug-and-play" nature of the ensemble voting strategy suggests that it could be integrated into various VLA architectures, offering a generalizable approach to improving action selection. Similarly, the design principles of the training framework for token reduction could inspire further research into more efficient action representation and generation within other multimodal models.

In summary, VOTE advances the field by providing a more efficient, robust, and accessible framework for VLA models, addressing key limitations that have hindered their practical deployment and widespread adoption.
