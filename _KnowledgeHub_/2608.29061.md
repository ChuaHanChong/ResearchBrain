# PathBridger: Subgoal Bridges for Offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> PathBridger is a hierarchical offline goal-conditioned RL method that makes the path between subgoal and action explicit. It learns transitive goal-reaching values, proposes value-tilted K-step endpoints, ranks them by a transitive score, expands the chosen endpoint into an endpoint-pinned state-space bridge, and decodes the bridge prefix into an action chunk with inverse dynamics. Its flow variant averages 82.7 percent across eight OGBench tasks.

## Problem

- Sparse goal-reaching rewards must propagate across long horizons offline, so value differences between locally distinct decisions shrink below approximation and sampling error.
- In existing hierarchies the selected subgoal only says where to go; the intervening state path stays implicit inside an endpoint-conditioned low-level policy.
- Offline training forbids extra interaction, so execution errors from bad values, policy extrapolation, or thin local coverage can never be repaired.

## Method

- **Transitive value learning** trains a bounded state-goal value with self-reachability and short-horizon anchors plus an ==expectile-weighted composition loss== over sampled intermediate states.
- **Value-tilted endpoint proposal** fits a Gaussian or ==rectified-flow== displacement model reweighted by advantage, then ranks sampled endpoints by the product transitive score.
- An **endpoint-pinned bridge** adds a ==masked residual network== to a linear interpolation, and ==inverse dynamics== decodes the first five transitions into an executed action chunk.

## Results

- The flow variant averages **82.7 percent** success on eight OGBench tasks, beating the strongest prior baseline by **10.4 points**, with the largest gains on multi-object Cube.
- Removing the bridge and reverting to an endpoint-conditioned actor drops the Gaussian variant from **60.7** to **46.0** average, isolating the execution mechanism's contribution.
- Oracle Puzzle waypoints supplied as selected subgoals give **98.7 percent** on both boards versus **40.0** and **52.0** when learned proposal and selection remain.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Specifying how to traverse toward a subgoal, not just which subgoal, is a distinct and separately valuable design axis in hierarchical control.
> - Anchoring generated paths structurally at both endpoints, and predicting local displacements instead of absolute states, keeps short-horizon trajectory models numerically well conditioned.
> - Strong execution can mask a weak planner: continuous endpoint proposers still fail on discrete combinatorial dynamics, where structure-aware subgoal models are needed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work operates within the field of offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL), which focuses on learning policies to achieve diverse goals solely from pre-recorded trajectory data, without additional interaction with the environment. This setting introduces two primary challenges: the propagation of sparse goal-reaching signals over long temporal horizons and the inability to correct execution errors or collect more data in an offline context.

Existing research in this area has explored several directions to address these challenges. One line of work aims to improve long-range value estimation, with examples including methods based on implicit Q-learning (e.g., GCIVL, GCIQL), contrastive learning (CRL), quasimetric learning (QRL), and transitive value learning (TRL). Another set of approaches introduces temporal abstraction to reduce the effective decision horizon. This includes option-based objectives, action-chunking methods (e.g., Q-chunking, DQC, MAC), and hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL). In hierarchical GCRL, a high-level policy selects intermediate subgoals, which a low-level policy then attempts to reach. Methods like HIQL and OTA fall into this category.

PathBridger identifies a specific limitation within several hierarchical GCRL methods. These methods often pass a selected intermediate state (subgoal) directly to an "endpoint-conditioned" low-level policy. This design leaves the *intervening state-space path* implicit, meaning the low-level policy is responsible for inferring and executing the trajectory to the subgoal without explicit guidance on the path itself. The authors suggest that this implicit path can be insufficient for robust execution and that the learned subgoal selection process can also be a bottleneck. PathBridger positions itself by explicitly addressing this gap, aiming to connect subgoal selection directly to short-horizon action execution through the construction and decoding of explicit state-space paths or "bridges."

The paper also relates to work in trajectory generation and inverse dynamics, where models are used to generate state or state-action sequences for planning and control (e.g., Trajectory Transformer, Diffuser, TAP, hierarchical diffusion planning). PathBridger leverages an inverse-dynamics model to translate its generated state-space bridges into executable actions, integrating concepts from model-based control into a hierarchical offline GCRL framework.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The core objective of PathBridger is to develop a hierarchical offline GCRL method that explicitly links subgoal selection with action execution, thereby improving performance on long-horizon, goal-conditioned tasks when learning from fixed datasets. This objective is driven by a detailed understanding of the challenges inherent in offline GCRL and a specific identified limitation in current hierarchical approaches.

The primary motivations for this work stem from two closely related challenges in offline GCRL:

1. **Propagation of Sparse Goal-Reaching Signals over Long Horizons**: In many complex control problems, rewards are sparse, indicating success only upon reaching a goal. For distant goals, the signal from these sparse rewards must propagate back through many steps, a process prone to difficulties. Value differences induced by local decisions can become negligibly small relative to approximation and sampling errors, making accurate value ordering and reliable policy improvement signals difficult to obtain. Furthermore, repeated temporal-difference backups can accumulate estimation bias across long horizons, diminishing the accuracy of learned value functions.

2. **Reliable Policy Execution in an Offline Setting**: The constraint of learning solely from fixed data means that errors cannot be rectified through further environment interaction. If values are inaccurate, the policy extrapolates beyond the data distribution, or local data coverage is insufficient, these issues cannot be repaired by online data collection or policy updates. The learned actions must be robust and effective under the distribution of the available offline data.

Within the context of hierarchical GCRL, the authors identify a specific interface challenge that PathBridger aims to address. While hierarchical methods decompose long-horizon tasks into a high-level policy selecting intermediate subgoals and a low-level policy executing actions toward them, current approaches often pass only the *endpoint* of the subgoal to the low-level policy. This leaves the *intervening state-space path* implicit. The motivation is that relying on an endpoint-conditioned low-level policy to infer the path can be insufficient, particularly in complex or constrained environments, leading to unreliable execution even if the subgoal itself is well-chosen. Additionally, the authors suggest that learned subgoal selection can be a separate bottleneck, even when the low-level execution is competent.

Therefore, PathBridger's specific objectives are:
- To propose a hierarchical framework that explicitly constructs a state-space bridge from the current state to a selected intermediate subgoal.
- To decode this explicit state-space bridge into a short, executable action chunk using an inverse dynamics model.
- To integrate this bridge-based execution with a transitive value-based system for proposing and selecting optimal intermediate endpoints.
- Ultimately, to demonstrate that this explicit path-level interface and execution mechanism can lead to improved performance and robustness in long-horizon offline GCRL tasks, particularly where the "how-to-get-there" is as important as the "where-to-go."

### 3. Methodology and approach

PathBridger is presented as a hierarchical offline GCRL framework designed to explicitly connect subgoal selection with action execution. Its operation involves a receding-horizon approach, where at each replanning step, it processes the current state ($s_t$) and the final goal ($g$) to generate and execute a short action chunk. The core components include local displacement parameterization, transitive value learning, and a bridge policy that encompasses endpoint proposal, transitive selection, endpoint-pinned bridge construction, and inverse-dynamics decoding.

**Local Displacement Parameterization and Horizons**:
To improve numerical conditioning and focus on local variations, PathBridger represents future states and state paths in a displacement frame relative to the current state. A displacement $\Delta_i = s_{t+i} - s_t$ is used for training and prediction, and then mapped back to absolute states $\hat{s}_{t+i} = s_t + \hat{\Delta}_i$. The **bridge horizon K** defines the length of the predicted endpoint and state-space bridge, while the **execution horizon $h_a \le K$** determines the prefix of the bridge decoded into actions and executed before replanning.

**Transitive Value Learning**:
PathBridger learns a bounded transitive value function, $V_\eta(s, g)$, which estimates the discounted hitting-time value of reaching goal $g$ from state $s$. This is motivated by the compositional property of goal-reaching values ($V^*(s, g) \ge V^*(s, z)V^*(z, g)$), where $V^*$ is the optimal value. The training objective for $V_\eta$ consists of three components:
1. **Self-reachability loss ($L_{self}$)**: Encourages $V_\eta(s, s)$ to be close to 1, signifying immediate reachability.
2. **Base loss ($L_{base}$)**: Anchors short, in-trajectory state-goal pairs. For segments $(s_i, s_j)$ with length $j-i \le H_b$, $V_\eta(s_i, s_j)$ is trained to approximate $\gamma^{j-i}$.
3. **Transitive loss ($L_{tr}$)**: For longer in-trajectory segments $(s_i, s_j)$, an intermediate state $s_k$ is sampled ($i < k < j$). The target $y_{tr}(i, k, j)$ is computed as the product of the values for the constituent segments $(s_i, s_k)$ and $(s_k, s_j)$, using observed $\gamma$ for short segments and the exponential moving average target network ($\bar{V}_{\bar{\eta}}$) for longer ones. An expectile-weighted binary cross-entropy loss is applied.
These losses are combined with optional distance-based re-weighting to emphasize shorter state-goal pairs.
At inference, candidate endpoints are ranked using a **transitive score $S_{tr}(s, z, g) = V_\eta(s, z)V_\eta(z, g)$**, which combines reachability from the current state to the candidate endpoint ($V_\eta(s, z)$) with continuation value from the candidate to the final goal ($V_\eta(z, g)$).

**Bridge Policy**:
The bridge policy maps the current state and goal to an executable action chunk via a four-step process:

1. **Value-weighted endpoint proposal**: Given $(s_t, g)$, a model $q_\chi(\Delta_K | s, g)$ proposes candidate K-step endpoint displacements. This model is trained to approximate a value-tilted empirical distribution of K-step displacements from the dataset. The tilting is based on an advantage-style centered continuation-value score, $\delta_{\bar{\eta}}(s, g, \Delta) = \bar{V}_{\bar{\eta}}(s + \Delta, g) - \bar{V}_{\bar{\eta}}(s, g)$, which favors endpoints leading to higher future values.
 - **PathBridger-Gaussian (PBG)**: Uses a diagonal Gaussian distribution for proposal.
 - **PathBridger-Flow (PBF)**: Employs a conditional rectified flow model to capture potentially multimodal endpoint distributions.

2. **Transitive selection**: From $N$ sampled candidate endpoints $z_n = s_t + \Delta^{(n)}_K$, the policy selects the optimal endpoint $z^*$ that maximizes the transitive score $S_{tr}(s_t, z_n, g)$.

3. **Endpoint-pinned bridge construction**: For the selected endpoint $z^*$, an explicit state-space bridge $(\hat{s}_t..., \hat{s}_{t+K})$ is constructed. This bridge is represented in displacement form $(\hat{\Delta}_0..., \hat{\Delta}_K)$. A learned residual model $R_\theta$ deforms a linear reference bridge, ensuring that the start ($\hat{\Delta}_0 = 0$) and end ($\hat{\Delta}_K = z^* - s_t$) points are pinned to the current state and selected endpoint, respectively. The bridge generator is trained to reconstruct real dataset bridge prefixes.

4. **Inverse-dynamics decoding**: The first $h_a+1$ states of the constructed bridge ($\hat{s}_t..., \hat{s}_{t+h_a}$) define an $h_a$-transition prefix. An inverse-dynamics model $I_\omega$ decodes each consecutive state pair $(\hat{s}_{t+i}, \hat{s}_{t+i+1})$ into an action $\hat{a}_{t+i}$. The inverse-dynamics model is trained on real adjacent transitions from the dataset.

After decoding, the resulting $h_a$-step action chunk is executed in the environment. PathBridger then observes the new state and replans, following a receding-horizon control strategy. This explicit construction and decoding of state-space paths distinguishes PathBridger from methods that rely on implicit execution to subgoals.

### 4. Main findings and results

PathBridger's performance was evaluated on eight state-based OGBench tasks, encompassing navigation (AntMaze), multi-object manipulation (Cube), combinatorial control (Puzzle), and scene rearrangement (Scene). The method was compared against several baseline algorithms, including GCIVL, CRL, HIQL, OTA, TRL, DQC, and HTVL (a controlled bridge-removal baseline).

**Benchmark Performance**:
- **Aggregate Performance**: PathBridger-Flow (PBF) achieved the highest overall average success rate across all evaluated OGBench tasks (82.7%), surpassing the next strongest prior method by 10.4 percentage points. PathBridger-Gaussian (PBG) also demonstrated strong performance with an average of 60.7%, outperforming TRL, HIQL, and OTA.
- **Task-Specific Gains**: PathBridger exhibited its most substantial performance improvements on the multi-object Cube manipulation tasks (Cube-single, Cube-double, Cube-triple). PBF achieved the best result on all three Cube tasks, with its performance margin over other methods increasing significantly on the more complex double- and triple-cube variants.
- **Competitive Performance**: On the AntMaze navigation tasks, PathBridger matched the performance of the leading baselines on `antmaze-medium-navigate-v0` and remained competitive on `antmaze-large-navigate-v0`.
- **Areas of Relative Underperformance**: On the Puzzle and Scene tasks, while PBF substantially outperformed hierarchical baselines like HIQL and OTA, its performance remained below DQC. TRL also achieved near-perfect performance on `puzzle-3x3-play-v0`.

**Training Efficiency**:
The estimated training time for 1 million gradient steps on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU showed that PBG (1.20 hours) and PBF (1.23 hours) were faster than HIQL, OTA, TRL, and DQC, although slower than GCIVL and CRL.

**Ablation and Diagnostic Analyses**:
To investigate the sources of PathBridger's performance and its limitations, several controlled experiments were conducted:

1. **Bridge-Removal Ablation (HTVL vs. PBG)**: HTVL, a baseline configured to use PBG's transitive value learner, Gaussian endpoint proposer, and selection procedure, but replaces bridge-based execution with HIQL's endpoint-conditioned low-level actor, showed an average success rate of 46.0%. This was an improvement over HIQL (33.8%), but significantly lower than PBG (60.7%). The 14.7-point performance gap between HTVL and PBG supports the conclusion that the explicit bridge construction and inverse-dynamics decoding contribute to PathBridger's performance beyond just improved subgoal selection.

2. **Oracle-Waypoint Diagnostic on Puzzle Tasks**: On the `Puzzle` tasks, precomputed valid sequences of board configurations (oracle waypoints) were used.
 - When these waypoints were supplied directly as *selected subgoals* (bypassing PathBridger's learned proposal and selection), PBG achieved nearly perfect success rates (98.7%) on both `puzzle-3x3` and `puzzle-4x4`.
 - However, when the same oracle waypoints were supplied as the *current task goal* (meaning PathBridger's learned subgoal proposal and selection mechanisms were still active), PBG's performance dropped substantially to 40.0% and 52.0% respectively.
 - This discrepancy indicates that while PathBridger's bridge-based execution can reliably achieve suitable intermediate states, its learned subgoal proposal and selection mechanisms remain a bottleneck for tasks with discrete, combinatorial dynamics like Puzzle. This diagnostic also further reinforced the benefit of bridge-based execution, as PBG substantially outperformed HIQL when both were provided with the same directly supplied subgoals.

3. **Dataset-Size Sensitivity**: An evaluation across different dataset sizes (100k, 300k, 500k, and 1M transitions) demonstrated that PBF maintained the highest aggregate success rate at every tested data budget. This suggests that PBF's relative performance advantage is robust across varying amounts of offline data.

In summary, the results indicate that PathBridger, particularly its rectified-flow variant (PBF), establishes a strong performance benchmark in state-based offline GCRL. The explicit state-space bridge construction and inverse-dynamics decoding are identified as key contributors to its effectiveness, especially in complex manipulation tasks. However, the learned high-level subgoal proposal and selection process presents an area for further development, particularly in domains with discrete and combinatorial state spaces.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of PathBridger represents a significant contribution to the field of offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning, primarily by addressing a previously underexplored interface in hierarchical methods. The core significance lies in its explicit connection between high-level subgoal selection and low-level action execution through the innovative use of state-space bridges and inverse dynamics.

The potential impacts of this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Advancing Hierarchical Offline GCRL**: PathBridger demonstrates that explicitly modeling the path to a subgoal, rather than implicitly relying on an endpoint-conditioned low-level policy, can lead to substantial performance improvements, especially in complex, long-horizon tasks like multi-object manipulation. This provides a new architectural pattern for designing more robust and effective hierarchical agents in offline settings.
2. **Enhanced Reliability and Interpretability**: By constructing an explicit state-space bridge, PathBridger offers a more interpretable and potentially more reliable mechanism for goal achievement. The intermediate "plan" in state space is directly generated and then translated into actions, which could facilitate debugging and understanding of agent behavior compared to purely end-to-end or implicit hierarchical policies.
3. **Robustness to Data Limitations**: The dataset-size sensitivity analysis indicates that PathBridger's performance advantages are maintained even with smaller offline datasets. This suggests that the method is data-efficient, which is a critical characteristic for practical applications where large, diverse datasets might be scarce.
4. **Insights for Future Research**: The detailed ablation and diagnostic analyses yield valuable insights. The bridge-removal ablation clearly demonstrates the benefit of PathBridger's explicit execution mechanism. More importantly, the oracle-waypoint diagnostic on Puzzle tasks precisely identifies learned subgoal proposal and selection as a key remaining bottleneck for the method, particularly in domains with discrete and combinatorial state dynamics. This finding directs future research efforts toward developing more sophisticated, possibly structure-aware or hybrid continuous-discrete, subgoal modeling techniques to further improve hierarchical planning. This could involve exploring models capable of handling discrete actions or state components within a continuous framework, potentially unlocking PathBridger's full potential across an even broader range of challenging environments.

In conclusion, PathBridger offers a robust and effective framework for offline GCRL, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with fixed datasets in complex environments. Its novel approach of explicit bridge construction and decoding not only achieves strong empirical results but also provides clearer insights into the interplay between planning and execution in hierarchical learning, thus charting a path for future advancements in the field.
