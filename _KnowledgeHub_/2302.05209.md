# A Survey on Causal Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey paper provides a comprehensive review and novel taxonomy for Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL), a field integrating causality with reinforcement learning. It formally defines CRL, categorizes existing methods based on the availability of prior causal information, and details how these approaches enhance sample efficiency, interpretability, and generalization in sequential decision-making tasks.

## Problem

- Conventional Reinforcement Learning (RL) suffers from high data inefficiency, requiring vast amounts of interaction data which is often costly or unfeasible to acquire.
- Many state-of-the-art RL algorithms lack interpretability, operating as "black boxes" that hinder understanding of decision-making processes and limit deployment in high-stakes applications.
- The rapidly growing field of Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL) lacked a comprehensive, systematically structured overview and taxonomy to guide researchers.

## Method

- The paper introduces a novel, two-category taxonomy for CRL methods, distinguishing between approaches that assume prior causal information and those where causal information must be learned.
- It provides a systematic review of current CRL methods within each category, formalizing their application across various RL models such as ==Markov Decision Processes==, ==Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes==, ==Multi-Arm Bandits==, Dynamic Treatment Regimes, and Imitation Learning.
- The survey consolidates foundational concepts from both causality and reinforcement learning to establish a common understanding for interdisciplinary research in CRL.

## Results

- The survey successfully classifies existing CRL approaches into two main categories, providing a structured framework for the field: "CRL with Prior Causal Information" and "CRL with Unknown Causal Information."
- It systematically details how causal insights improve sample efficiency, interpretability, and generalization across various RL models like MDPs, POMDPs, and Bandits.
- The paper compiles practical resources including evaluation metrics, open-source codes, and identifies key applications and future research directions for the CRL community.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Causality can address fundamental limitations of traditional RL by enabling more efficient data utilization through counterfactual reasoning and reducing data requirements.
> - Integrating causal models can enhance the interpretability and transparency of RL agents by explicitly characterizing the world model and explaining cause-effect relationships.
> - Learning true causal mechanisms allows Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL) agents to develop policies that are more robust to distributional shifts and capable of generalizing to unseen environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a cornerstone of artificial intelligence, enabling agents to master complex sequential decision-making tasks, achieving "human-level control" in domains ranging from sophisticated games like Go and StarCraft II to robotics control and recommender systems. This success, however, is not without significant limitations. The paper highlights two critical challenges faced by conventional RL: **data inefficiency** and a **lack of interpretability**. Current RL methods are "hungry for interaction data," which is often costly, time-consuming, or ethically unfeasible to acquire in real-world applications (e.g., healthcare, where only limited observational data might be available). Furthermore, many state-of-the-art RL algorithms, especially those leveraging deep neural networks, operate as "black boxes," making it difficult to understand the internal relationships between states, actions, and rewards, thus impeding their adoption in high-stakes industrial and clinical settings.

The integration of **causality** into RL, giving rise to **Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL)**, is presented as a promising paradigm to address these inherent challenges. Causality, a field concerned with uncovering and inferring cause-effect relationships (through "causal discovery" and "causal inference"), offers powerful tools. By enabling agents to perform "interventions" and "reason counterfactually" (concepts from Pearl's "ladder of causation"), causality can significantly reduce the need for extensive trial-and-error data. Moreover, causal models can "characterize the world model," providing a more transparent and interpretable understanding of how agents interact with their environment, moving beyond simple correlational patterns.

While the fields of causality and reinforcement learning have developed largely independently for decades, recent years have seen a "flourishing" of works attempting to reconcile and integrate them. Notable pioneering efforts include Bareinboim's unified framework for "causal reinforcement learning" and Lu's emphasis on its applicability in domains like healthcare. This survey positions itself as a timely and necessary response to this burgeoning research area, aiming to "collate these Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL) works, offer a review of CRL methods, and investigate the potential functionality from causality toward RL."

The authors distinguish their contribution from existing related surveys, noting that previous reviews were either too narrow in scope (e.g., Grimbly et al. on causal multi-agent RL, Bannon et al. on off-policy evaluation in batch RL) or lacked a comprehensive and integrated taxonomic framework (e.g., Kaddour et al.'s survey on causal machine learning, which included a CRL chapter but categorized by RL problem, potentially missing broader connections). This paper aims to provide a more holistic and systematically structured overview, offering a novel categorization that fundamentally shapes its contribution to the broader research landscape. It serves as a foundational reference point for a field that is still in its infancy but holds immense promise for overcoming the critical limitations of conventional RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this comprehensive survey stems directly from the current limitations of traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) and the emerging potential of integrating it with causality.

**Primary Motivations:**

1. **Addressing RL's Data Inefficiency:** A core challenge for RL algorithms is their insatiable demand for large volumes of interaction data. In many real-world domains, such as healthcare, medicine, or industrial control, collecting extensive data through trial-and-error is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, or even unethical. The authors highlight that causality, through its ability to model interventions and reason counterfactually, can "relax the requirements of a large amount of training data."
2. **Enhancing RL's Interpretability:** Current deep RL methods often act as "black boxes," making it difficult to understand the underlying mechanisms of decision-making or the relationships between states, actions, and rewards. This lack of transparency hinders their deployment in critical applications where trust and accountability are paramount. Causality, by enabling the explicit characterization of "the world model," can potentially "giv[e] interpretability to how the agents interact with the environment," making policies more transparent and amenable to analysis.
3. **Synthesizing a Rapidly Evolving Field:** The recent years have witnessed a "flourishing" of research works at the intersection of causality and RL. This rapid growth creates a need for a structured and comprehensive overview to help researchers navigate the landscape, identify key advancements, and understand the diverse ways causality is being leveraged in RL. The authors explicitly state the "great necessity and significance to collate these Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL) works."

**Key Objectives of the Survey:**

Based on these motivations, the paper sets out several ambitious objectives:

1. **Formal Definition of CRL:** To formally define "Causal Reinforcement Learning" as a distinct field, providing a clear conceptual foundation for future research.
2. **Novel Categorization:** To introduce a new, comprehensive taxonomy for existing CRL approaches. This is a central contribution, dividing methods into two primary categories: those that assume **prior causal information** (given in advance) and those where **causal information is unknown** and must be learned. This novel classification provides a structured lens for understanding the diverse methodologies.
3. **Comprehensive Methodological Review:** To offer a systematic review of current CRL methods within each of the proposed categories, detailing their formalizations across various RL models (Markov Decision Process, Partially Observed Markov Decision Process, Multi-Arm Bandits, Dynamic Treatment Regime, and Imitation Learning).
4. **Practical Insights:** To summarize and discuss practical aspects of CRL, including:
 - **Evaluation Metrics:** Standard and causal-specific metrics used to assess CRL algorithms.
 - **Open Sources:** Providing a valuable resource by listing available codebases, tutorials, and relevant competitions.
 - **Emerging Applications:** Highlighting how CRL is being applied or could be applied across various real-world domains (robotics, games, healthcare, autonomous driving, recommendation systems).
5. **Future Directions:** To identify and discuss promising avenues for future research and development in CRL, guiding the community toward impactful contributions.

In essence, the paper's overarching goal is to provide a definitive and structured resource that not only consolidates the current state of Causal Reinforcement Learning but also clearly articulates its potential to overcome core limitations of traditional RL, thereby fostering further innovation and practical application in the field of artificial intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopt a systematic and comprehensive survey methodology to dissect and organize the rapidly evolving field of Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL). Their approach is structured hierarchically, building from foundational concepts to specific algorithms, applications, and future outlooks.

**1. Foundational Review (Section II: Preliminary):**
The survey begins by establishing a common ground of understanding in both constituent fields:
- **Causality:** It provides a thorough overview of key concepts, including Structural Causal Models (SCMs), Rubin Causal Model, various types of Treatment Effects (ATE, ATT, CATE, ITE), confounders, instrumental variables (IVs), and graphical criteria like Back-Door and Front-Door. Crucially, it introduces Pearl's "Ladder of Causation" (Association, Intervention, Counterfactuals) which underpins much of CRL's philosophy. The section also outlines fundamental assumptions for causal discovery (Markov, Faithfulness, Sufficiency) and causal inference (SUTVA, Ignorability, Positivity), followed by a review of classic causal discovery (constraint-based like PC, FCI; score-based like GES; functional causal models like LiNGAM, ANM) and causal inference techniques (matching, reweighting, meta-learning, sensitivity analysis, IV regression, PCL). This detailed preliminary ensures readers from either RL or causality can grasp the interdisciplinary concepts.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** It defines standard RL models such as Markov Decision Processes (MDP), Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes (POMDP), Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB), Contextual Bandits (CB), Imitation Learning (IL), and Dynamic Treatment Regimes (DTR). The authors then classify classical RL algorithms into model-free (policy-based, value-based, actor-critic) and model-based methods, discussing their core principles and typical algorithms (e.g., PPO, DQN, SAC, MCTS).

**2. Core Taxonomy of Causal Reinforcement Learning (Section III: Causal Reinforcement Learning):**
This section presents the paper's central methodological contribution: a novel, two-category taxonomy for CRL approaches.
- **Formal Definition of CRL:** The authors formally define CRL as a tuple `(M, G)`, where `M` is an RL model setting (e.g., MDP) and `G` represents causal-based information (e.g., causal structure, latent confounders). This formalization provides a unifying conceptual framework.
- **Category 1: CRL with Prior Causal Information (Section III.A):** This category includes methods where causal information (like causal structure, or presence/location of latent factors) is explicitly *given or assumed* to be known beforehand, often from expert knowledge. The review within this category focuses on how this prior information is leveraged to improve sample efficiency, interpretability, or generalization in policy learning. The discussion is further structured by the specific RL model settings:
 - MDP: Discussions on IV-based debiasing, SCMs for data augmentation, narrowing search spaces, and off-policy evaluation.
 - POMDP: Focus on counterfactual reasoning, proximal causal inference, and multi-agent credit assignment.
 - Bandits: Causal bandit problems with unobserved confounders, optimal intervention strategies, and off-policy evaluation.
 - DTR: Learning optimal treatment regimes with causal bounds and instrumental variables.
 - Imitation Learning: Addressing unobserved confounders, covariate shifts, and temporal correlations.
- **Category 2: CRL with Unknown Causal Information (Section III.B):** This category covers methods where the causal information is *not known* a priori and must be actively learned from the data. These methods typically involve a two-phase process: first, learning the causal information (e.g., causal structure, representations, or latent confounders), and then using this learned information for policy optimization. The review here also subdivides by RL model settings:
 - MDP: Learning causal world-models, causal variables, goal-conditioned tasks, hierarchical structures, and invariant state abstractions.
 - POMDP: Discovering latent causal transition models, deconfounding RL, and learning action-sufficient representations.
 - Bandits: Causal bandit algorithms that discover the graph structure, and transfer learning in multi-environment settings.
 - Imitation Learning: Addressing "causal misidentification" and "copycat" problems by learning correct causal models and invariant representations.
Throughout these sections, the authors systematically present specific algorithms, their motivations, key techniques, and often include illustrative causal graphical models to explain the relationships between variables.

**3. Supporting Analysis and Future Outlook:**
- **Evaluation Metrics (Section IV):** The paper compiles common metrics from both RL (cumulative reward/regret, success rate) and causality (transition loss, causal graph accuracy like precision, recall, F1).
- **Applications (Section V):** A dedicated section reviews the practical deployment of CRL methods, predominantly in simulators but also noting real-world data uses, across key domains like robotics, games, healthcare, autonomous driving, and recommendation systems. This demonstrates the practical relevance and potential impact.
- **Open Sources (Section VI):** Lists publicly available code repositories, tutorials, and relevant competitions, providing invaluable resources for practitioners and researchers.
- **Future Directions (Section VII):** The authors conclude by outlining forward-looking research avenues, categorized into knowledge-based, memory-based, cognition-based, and application-based CRL. This provides a roadmap for future work in the field.

In summary, the methodological approach is rigorous and exhaustive, characterized by a clear hierarchical structure, a novel categorization of the field, detailed algorithmic discussions, and practical resource provision. The extensive use of causal graphical models visually reinforces the theoretical concepts and problem formulations discussed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a comprehensive survey, the "main findings and results" of this paper are not experimental outcomes, but rather the synthesized understanding, novel categorization, and systematic review of the burgeoning field of Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL).

**1. Formal Definition and Conceptualization of CRL:**
The paper formally defines CRL as an algorithmic suite aiming to "embed causal knowledge into RL for more efficient and effective model learning, policy evaluation, or policy optimization." This definition anchors the field, emphasizing its purpose of leveraging causal insights to enhance RL. It frames CRL as `(M, G)`, where `M` is an RL model and `G` represents the crucial causal information.

**2. Novel Two-Category Taxonomy of CRL Methods:**
The most significant organizational finding is the successful classification of existing CRL approaches into two distinct, comprehensive categories, which provides a structured lens for understanding the field's diversity:
 - **CRL with Prior Causal Information:** This category encompasses methods where the causal structure, latent factors, or confounder locations are explicitly known or assumed beforehand.
 - **Core Principle:** Leverage pre-existing causal knowledge to address RL challenges.
 - **Key Benefits Identified:** Significant improvements in **sample efficiency** (e.g., through counterfactual data augmentation, narrowing search spaces), enhanced **interpretability** (by explaining decisions through causal models), and improved **generalization** capabilities.
 - **Specific Techniques Highlighted:** Instrumental variable (IV) methods for debiasing in MDPs and DTRs; counterfactual reasoning in POMDPs for off-policy evaluation and policy transfer; exploitation of causal graph structure in bandits for optimal intervention and regret reduction; and causal graph criteria for imitation learning feasibility and dealing with confounders.
 - **CRL with Unknown Causal Information:** This category includes more challenging methods where the causal information is not given and must be learned directly from data.
 - **Core Principle:** Integrate causal discovery or causal representation learning as a precursor or iterative component to policy learning.
 - **Key Benefits Identified:** Development of more **robust** and **generalizable** policies by distinguishing true causal relationships from spurious correlations; learning of **interpretable causal models** and state abstractions; and facilitating **hierarchical reinforcement learning** through discovered causal structures.
 - **Specific Techniques Highlighted:** Learning causal world-models for offline RL; identifying causal variables via mediation analysis; causal induction from visual observations; causality-driven hierarchical RL; learning invariant state abstractions and representations for generalization; and addressing "causal misidentification" and "copycat" phenomena in imitation learning by discovering the correct causal models.

**3. Comprehensive Review across RL Models:**
Within each of the two categories, the survey systematically reviews methods across various foundational RL model settings:
 - **Markov Decision Processes (MDPs):** Numerous works focus on off-policy evaluation, confounding bias correction, and improving data efficiency using SCMs.
 - **Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs):** Explores counterfactuals, proximal causal inference, and multi-agent credit assignment in partially observable settings.
 - **Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB) & Contextual Bandits (CB):** Addresses unobserved confounders, optimal intervention selection, and transfer learning in bandit settings.
 - **Dynamic Treatment Regimes (DTRs):** Reviews methods for designing optimal adaptive policies, particularly relevant in personalized healthcare, often with confounding factors.
 - **Imitation Learning (IL):** Investigates how causal principles can mitigate issues like causal confusion, covariate shift, and temporally correlated noise in learning from expert demonstrations.

**4. Practical and Future-Oriented Insights:**
The survey also provides valuable practical insights by:
 - Listing common **evaluation metrics** used in CRL, drawing from both RL (rewards, regret) and causality (structure accuracy).
 - Collating extensive **open-source codes** and **tutorials**, which is a significant practical contribution for researchers.
 - Detailing a wide array of **real-world applications** (mostly in simulators, but with real-data examples) across robotics, games, healthcare, autonomous driving, and recommendation systems, underscoring the field's practical relevance.
 - Proposing four strategic **future directions** (knowledge-based, memory-based, cognition-based, and application-based CRL), guiding the research community toward addressing unresolved challenges and maximizing CRL's impact.

In essence, the paper provides a foundational "state-of-the-art" analysis for CRL, not just summarizing individual works but creating a cohesive framework that reveals the underlying principles, diverse methodologies, and significant potential of integrating causality into reinforcement learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey paper on Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL) holds substantial significance for the fields of artificial intelligence, machine learning, and various applied domains. Its potential impact is multifaceted, ranging from theoretical advancements to practical deployment.

**1. Advancing the Core Capabilities of Reinforcement Learning:**
The most profound impact of CRL lies in its ability to address the fundamental limitations of traditional RL.
- **Overcoming Data Inefficiency:** By leveraging causal reasoning, CRL methods can deduce how changes in actions causally affect states and rewards, allowing for more intelligent and efficient data collection. Counterfactual reasoning enables agents to learn from "what-if" scenarios without requiring direct interaction, dramatically reducing the need for costly or unsafe real-world trials. This makes RL applicable to data-scarce domains like clinical trials or rare event scenarios in robotics.
- **Enhancing Interpretability and Trust:** Causality provides a framework for understanding cause-effect relationships, moving RL beyond "black-box" decision-making. By explicitly modeling these relationships, CRL can offer human-understandable explanations for an agent's actions, fostering trust and enabling better debugging and validation in high-stakes applications such as autonomous driving, healthcare, or financial trading.
- **Improving Generalization and Robustness:** Learning true causal mechanisms, rather than mere correlations, allows CRL agents to develop policies that are inherently more robust to distributional shifts and capable of generalizing to unseen environments or tasks. This is crucial for deploying AI systems in dynamic, unpredictable real-world settings, where the training environment may differ from the deployment environment.

**2. Providing a Foundational Framework for a Nascent Field:**
The paper's formal definition of CRL and, crucially, its novel two-category taxonomy (based on whether causal information is prior or unknown) are foundational contributions. They bring much-needed structure and clarity to a rapidly expanding and often disparate research area. This organization helps researchers:
- **Navigate the Landscape:** Provides a clear map of existing approaches, their strengths, and their underlying assumptions.
- **Identify Research Gaps:** By systematically categorizing methods, the survey implicitly highlights areas that are under-explored or require more robust solutions (e.g., integrating causal discovery into complex online RL settings).
- **Standardize Terminology and Concepts:** The comprehensive preliminary section, defining core concepts from both causality and RL, serves as a valuable reference, promoting consistency in future research.

**3. Catalyzing Future Research and Development:**
The explicit articulation of future directions is a strong call to action for the research community:
- **Knowledge-based CRL:** Encourages deeper research into handling latent confounders with weaker assumptions, exploring the non-stationarity of causal structures, and ensuring learned models align with real-world dynamics. This pushes CRL towards greater realism and applicability.
- **Memory-based CRL:** Points towards the importance of designing sophisticated memory mechanisms for lifelong learning and efficient experience replay, crucial for agents operating continuously in evolving environments.
- **Cognition-based CRL:** Advocates for integrating more sophisticated cognitive processes like advanced intervention strategies and counterfactual analysis, leveraging these for more insightful and adaptable decision-making. The emphasis on characterizing causal invariances directly supports the goal of broad generalization.
- **Application-based CRL:** Critically highlights the need to bridge the "simulator-reality gap" and emphasizes the paramount importance of collecting large, diverse, real-world datasets for CRL. This practical emphasis could drive significant breakthroughs in the deployment of CRL solutions in industry and society.

**4. Bridging Disciplinary Gaps:**
By synthesizing knowledge from both theoretical causality and practical reinforcement learning, the survey acts as a critical bridge between these two powerful fields. It encourages interdisciplinary collaboration, fostering the development of hybrid methodologies that might not emerge from within a single discipline. The inclusion of open-source codes and competition information further lowers the barrier for entry and encourages wider experimentation and adoption of CRL techniques.

In conclusion, "A Survey on Causal Reinforcement Learning" is more than just a review; it is a foundational document that defines, organizes, and charts the future course for a highly promising field. Its comprehensive nature, novel taxonomy, and forward-looking perspectives position it as an indispensable resource for researchers and practitioners aiming to develop more intelligent, interpretable, data-efficient, and robust AI systems capable of tackling complex, real-world sequential decision-making challenges.
