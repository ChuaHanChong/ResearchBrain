# Robot Learning from Human Videos: A Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey systematically reviews Robot Learning from Human Videos (LfHV), categorizing skill transfer into task-oriented, observation-oriented, and action-oriented mechanisms. It analyzes how these pathways address the robot data bottleneck by leveraging abundant human video sources and highlights a growing preference for egocentric video data for precise action grounding.

## Problem

- Traditional robot learning (imitation learning, reinforcement learning) faces a critical bottleneck due to the high cost, time, and limited diversity of collecting robot-specific data, hindering scalable and generalizable robotic systems.
- Existing surveys on video-based robot learning lack a unified taxonomy centered on information flow from human videos to robot execution, systematic cross-comparison of transfer pathways, and organized analysis of human video data sources.
- The inherent differences in embodiment, observation, and action spaces between humans and robots create a complex gap that needs to be effectively bridged for skill transfer.

## Method

- Proposes a hierarchical ==taxonomy for human-robot skill transfer==, classifying methods into ==task-oriented==, ==observation-oriented==, and ==action-oriented== categories.
- Conducts cross-family analysis of LfHV approaches, examining viewpoint (egocentric vs. exocentric), reliance on robot-specific data, and learning paradigms (IL, RL).
- Provides a systematic summary of open-source human video datasets and human-object interaction analysis techniques.

## Results

- Identified three primary skill transfer mechanisms with intermediate bridges: task structures, visual embeddings, affordances, latent actions.
- Viewpoint shift from exocentric to egocentric: observation-oriented **49% egocentric**, action-oriented **52% egocentric**.
- **44%** of action-oriented methods enable deployment from human videos alone via executable interfaces; robot data still frequently required for fine-grained control.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot learning from human videos offers a scalable alternative to traditional robot data collection by leveraging the abundance and diversity of human activity videos.
> - Action-oriented transfer, utilizing explicit affordances or learned latent actions, provides the most direct pathway to robot execution and shows the highest potential for real-world robot deployment with minimal robot-specific data.
> - There is a clear trend towards egocentric human videos, especially after 2024, as they offer more direct and precise views of human-object interactions, which is crucial for grounded robot perception and action.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI and robotics has experienced significant advancements, largely propelled by developments in robot learning technologies such as imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL). However, a primary impediment to the widespread deployment of versatile and generalized robotic systems is the inherent difficulty and cost associated with acquiring large-scale, diverse robot-specific data. Traditional imitation learning methods are labor-intensive, requiring kinesthetic or teleoperated demonstrations, which restrict data diversity and acquisition efficiency. Reinforcement learning, while offering adaptability, relies on carefully engineered reward functions and extensive trial-and-error, leading to high training costs, sample inefficiency, and safety concerns in real-world systems.

Inspired by the data-driven success of large language models and vision-language models, the robotics community has increasingly explored alternative data sources that are easier to collect and scale. Human activity videos have emerged as a promising resource due to their abundance, rich task semantics, and diverse interaction patterns with objects. Advances in computer vision have further enabled more accurate and efficient analysis of these videos. This research trajectory aims to enable robots to passively acquire skills from readily available human demonstrations, fostering scalable learning for generalist robotic systems.

While several existing surveys provide insights into video-based robot learning, this paper identifies several gaps in the current literature. Specifically, it notes a lack of a unified taxonomy centered on the information flow from human videos to robot execution, a systematic cross-comparison of transfer pathways regarding viewpoint, reliance on robot data, and learning paradigms, and a fragmented organization of human video data sources concerning prevalence and the emerging role of video generation. This survey aims to fill these gaps by offering a comprehensive and structured review that incorporates recent advancements in the rapidly evolving field of robot learning from human videos (LfHV).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this survey stems from the "critical bottleneck" of scaling robot data, which currently hinders further progress in embodied AI and robotics. The authors highlight that conventional robot data collection, whether through teleoperation or real-robot interaction, is expensive, time-consuming, and yields limited diversity, thereby restricting the generalization capabilities of robot policies.

The primary objective of this survey is to provide a comprehensive and up-to-date review of human-video-based learning techniques in robotics. This review focuses specifically on both human-robot skill transfer mechanisms and the foundational data resources that enable them. The authors aim to establish a clearer foundation for future research in embodied AI and robotics by systematically categorizing and analyzing the current state of the art.

The main contributions and objectives can be summarized as follows:
1. **Hierarchical Bridging Mechanisms of Skill Transfer:** To propose a hierarchical taxonomy that categorizes the pathways linking human videos to robot execution. This taxonomy elucidates how skill transfer can be achieved at the task, observation, and action levels, identifying key video-derived intermediates (e.g., task structures, visual embeddings, affordances) that act as bridges. It also provides practical guidelines for selecting appropriate LfHV routes.
2. **Cross-Family Analysis Across Data Configurations and Learning Paradigms:** To conduct a comparative analysis of how different transfer families interact with critical design choices, including viewpoint selection (egocentric vs. exocentric), dependence on real-world robot data, and prevalent learning paradigms (imitation learning, reinforcement learning). This objective aims to reveal characteristic design couplings and methodological trade-offs.
3. **Systematic Summary of Human-Object Interaction (HOI) Analysis:** To compile a systematic overview of commonly used methods for parsing human-object interactions in videos. This includes reviewing tools for hand and object detection, tracking, reconstruction, and pose estimation in 2D, 3D, and 4D spaces, providing researchers with insights into practical and popular techniques.
4. **Evolution and Preferences of Human Video Data:** To present a large-scale statistical analysis of human video sources within the LfHV context. This involves summarizing the development trends of human video datasets and relevant generative techniques, and analyzing data preferences exhibited by different categories of LfHV methods.
5. **Future Directions Across Models, Data, and Benchmarks:** To identify and delineate promising avenues for future research based on observed trends in bridging mechanisms and data foundations. This includes highlighting opportunities in new modeling paradigms, richer data modalities, more standardized benchmarks, and more collaborative ecosystems.

By addressing these objectives, the survey intends to offer a structured understanding of the LfHV landscape, facilitating informed decisions for researchers developing scalable robot learning solutions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey conceptualizes the problem of robot learning from human videos (LfHV) as bridging the gap between human videos and robot manipulation through multiple levels of transfer. It starts by revisiting the foundational robot learning paradigms of imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) and discusses how human videos can interface with these frameworks for policy initialization or reward learning. A unified formulation is introduced to characterize LfHV as minimizing cross-embodiment discrepancies across observation, action, and objective gaps.

The core of the methodology involves a hierarchical taxonomy of human-robot skill transfer, divided into three main categories based on the level at which information flows from human videos to robot execution:

1. **Task-Oriented Transfer:** This category focuses on extracting high-level task knowledge from human videos.
 - **Task Structures as a Bridge:** Explicitly decomposes tasks into temporal instructions (e.g., fine-grained or coarse-grained phases). Methods are grouped into traditional discriminative approaches (e.g., probabilistic action grammars, CNN-RNN for verb-noun commands) and modern VLM-enhanced approaches (e.g., GPT-4V, chain-of-thought techniques for plan descriptions, VLM-based programming by demonstration).
 - **Task Intents as a Bridge:** Infers global task objectives or task-phase transition signals without constructing full instruction sequences. Sub-categories include global intent extraction (e.g., domain-adaptive meta-learning, conditioning policies on task embeddings) and phase signals generation (e.g., implicit intermediate phases for dense perceptual rewards, phase predictors).

2. **Observation-Oriented Transfer:** This category aims to transform raw human videos into visual representations directly compatible with the robot’s perception.
 - **Transformed Videos as a Bridge:** Directly translates human videos into embodiment-agnostic or robot-like visual formats. Methods include embodiment suppression (e.g., removing agents via video inpainting, agent-environment factorization) and embodiment transformation (e.g., translating human videos to robot videos via GANs, diffusion models with inpainting and robot overlay, wrist-aligned rendering).
 - **Visual Embeddings as a Bridge:** Compresses human videos into visual embeddings for perception model pretraining or reward construction. Sub-categories include visual pretraining (e.g., Temporal Contrastive Learning (TCL), Masked Pretraining (MP), Human Video Prediction (HVP), Joint Domain Adaptation (JDA)) and visual guidance (e.g., defining RL rewards based on visual embedding distances or binary classifiers for goal matching).

3. **Action-Oriented Transfer:** This category directly extracts action-relevant information for robot action planning.
 - **Affordances as a Bridge:** Exposes explicit geometric action cues. This section extensively reviews Human-Object Interaction (HOI) analysis techniques (2D detection/tracking, 3D hand/object reconstruction/pose estimation, 4D contact evolution). Based on HOI analysis, affordances are utilized in four ways: for robot policy backbone training (pretraining or co-training), reward construction in robot policy learning, as robot policy conditions (predicted or prompt-extracted), and as the robot policy itself (direct retargeting, warping, optimization).
 - **Latent Actions as a Bridge:** Learns compact and transferable action priors directly from unlabeled human videos using a latent action model (LAM) with an inverse dynamics model (IDM) and a forward dynamics model (FDM). Methods are divided into latent actions from visual reconstruction (e.g., VQ-VAE-based objectives, vision-language-latent-action frameworks) and latent actions from visual-action co-reconstruction (e.g., incorporating proprioceptive forward dynamics, optical flow consistency, explicit disentanglement of action-relevant/irrelevant components).

Beyond the methodological taxonomy, the survey includes a cross-family analysis covering:
- **Data Configurations:** Examines viewpoint preferences (egocentric, exocentric, mixed) and dependence on real-world robot data (human videos + robot demonstrations, human videos + robot interaction, human videos only).
- **Learning Paradigms:** Discusses the prevalence of imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and other approaches (e.g., direct retargeting, programmatic execution) within each transfer family.

Finally, the survey investigates "Data Foundations," systematically summarizing open-source human video datasets (including attributes like frames, hours, participants, views, annotations, and sources) and reviewing emerging human video generation techniques. The "Discussion" section outlines key challenges and future research directions, covering physically grounded world models, physics-aware functional affordance extraction, continual learning, multi-agent interaction, multimodal signals, utilization of low-quality videos, standardized benchmarking, and egocentric data ecosystems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey presents a comprehensive overview of LfHV, categorized into task-oriented, observation-oriented, and action-oriented transfer, and analyzes these categories across data configurations and learning paradigms.

#### 4.1. Human-Robot Skill Transfer Mechanisms

- **Task-Oriented Transfer:**
 - **Findings:** This pathway focuses on transferring high-level task knowledge. It has evolved from traditional discriminative approaches to more scalable VLM-enhanced methods for temporal task decomposition (e.g., using GPT-4V for instruction generation). Task intents, such as global objectives or phase signals, offer more flexible guidance without requiring explicit instruction sequences.
 - **Trade-offs/Limitations:** Provides embodiment-agnostic transfer but often requires additional grounding modules for robot-specific actions. Explicit task structures offer interpretability, while implicit task intents offer flexibility. A key bottleneck is precisely grounding task guidance into executable robot behaviors.

- **Observation-Oriented Transfer:**
 - **Findings:** Addresses the visual perceptual gap. Methods include "transformed videos" (e.g., agent suppression via inpainting, rendering robot arms into human videos using diffusion models) and "visual embeddings" (e.g., pretraining vision encoders via temporal contrastive learning (TCL), masked pretraining (MP), human video prediction (HVP), or joint domain adaptation (JDA), and using embeddings for visual guidance/rewards).
 - **Trade-offs/Limitations:** Transformed videos offer direct visual alignment but suffer from generation artifacts. Visual embeddings are more compact and robust to appearance gaps but may implicitly lose action-relevant dynamics. Progress shows a shift from appearance-level alignment to deeper modeling of interaction dynamics and cross-embodiment consistency, but preserving action-relevant dynamics remains challenging.

- **Action-Oriented Transfer:**
 - **Findings:** Provides the most direct bridge to robot actions.
 - **Affordances as a Bridge:** Leverages explicit geometric cues from human-object interaction (HOI) analysis (2D, 3D, 4D hand/object positions, poses, geometric representations, functionality). Affordances are used for robot policy backbone training (pretraining or co-training, often hand-centric), reward construction (multimodal hand-object coupling for successful interaction evaluation), policy conditioning (predicting future affordances as goals), and direct policy construction (retargeting cues into executable actions). The paper notes a strong correlation between end-effector type and affordance granularity, with dexterous hands requiring high-dimensional hand representations.
 - **Latent Actions as a Bridge:** Learns compact, transferable action priors from unlabeled human videos through latent action models (LAMs). This involves visual reconstruction (e.g., VQ-VAE for discrete latent actions) or visual-action co-reconstruction (e.g., constraining latent actions with proprioception, optical flow, or explicit disentanglement of action-relevant components).
 - **Trade-offs/Limitations:** Affordances offer interpretability and strong grounding but depend on reliable HOI analysis and robust retargeting, challenged by occlusion or morphology mismatch. Latent actions offer scalability and leverage large unlabeled datasets but sacrifice geometric explicitness, making controllability and physical grounding harder to verify. Both require grounding in robot-compatible action spaces.

#### 4.2. Bridging Mechanisms Across Data Configurations and Learning Paradigms

- **Viewpoint Preferences:**
 - **Task-oriented:** Predominantly uses exocentric videos (65%) for global scene understanding, with a smaller portion using egocentric (13%) or mixed views (22%).
 - **Observation-oriented:** Shows a preference for egocentric videos (49% vs. 40% exocentric), aligning with robot perception.
 - **Action-oriented:** Exhibits the strongest preference for egocentric videos (52% vs. 41% exocentric) due to their direct view of hand-object interactions and precise timing cues.
 - **Temporal Trend:** A significant shift from overwhelmingly exocentric videos before 2022 to a rapid expansion and dominance of egocentric sources, especially after 2024, driven by new datasets and HOI analysis techniques. Mixed views are less prevalent in the long term.

- **Dependence on Robot Data:**
 - **Task-oriented:** Has the weakest dependence on real-world robot data (48% use "Human Videos Only"), often stopping at semantic task specification.
 - **Observation-oriented & Action-oriented:** Show stronger dependence on robot data (demonstrations or interactions) for grounding, but action-oriented transfer has the largest number of methods enabling real-world robot deployment using *only* human video data (44%), by producing executable intermediate interfaces.
 - **Overall:** Human videos serve as a scalable layer in the data pyramid but rarely completely replace real robot data for generalist agents, necessitating robot-specific data for fine-grained control and closed-loop correction.

- **Learning Paradigms:**
 - **Task-oriented:** Primarily IL-style (conditioning policies on high-level prompts) or non-IL/RL (program generation).
 - **Observation-oriented:** Flexible, supporting IL (transformed/learned visual features as policy inputs) and RL (visual features for reward definition) or exploration policies.
 - **Action-oriented:** Spans the widest range; largely IL-based (for backbone pretraining/co-training of VLAs), but also includes RL (for complex interaction dynamics) and a significant number of direct execution methods (zero-shot retargeting, trajectory optimization).

#### 4.3. Data Foundations

- **Open-Source Datasets:** The survey reviews 50 datasets, noting trends such as consistently large video duration (hundreds to thousands of hours), a decline in purely in-the-wild datasets in favor of quality-controlled curated ones, a shift toward mixed dataset compositions for comprehensive training, increasing emphasis on fine-grained hand pose/joint annotations, and richer task-related language descriptions. Ego4D and EPIC-KITCHENS are the most frequently used.
- **Human Video Generation:** Emerging as a complement, moving from simulation-based synthesis to language-conditioned video generation coupled with pose retargeting or flow extraction, enabling expansion beyond physically recorded scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey contributes to the scientific understanding of robot learning from human videos by systematically dissecting its diverse methodologies and underlying data challenges. By providing a hierarchical taxonomy and a detailed cross-family analysis, the paper offers a structured framework for comprehending the current research landscape and identifying key design trade-offs. This organized perspective is significant for researchers in several ways: it clarifies how different approaches bridge the human-robot embodiment gap, informs decisions regarding appropriate data configurations and learning paradigms, and highlights the strengths and limitations of various transfer mechanisms.

The potential impact of this work extends to guiding future research and accelerating the development of scalable and generalizable robotic systems. The identified challenges and proposed future directions serve as a roadmap for the field:

- **Physically Grounded World Models:** Moving beyond purely visual imitation to models that anticipate physical interaction states and long-horizon consequences is crucial for robust, open-world manipulation and failure recovery.
- **Physics-Aware Functional Affordance Extraction:** Emphasizing object functionality and physical constraints beyond mere geometric cues will enhance robustness for tools, articulated objects, and contact-rich tasks.
- **Continual Learning with Human Video Data:** Developing frameworks that can continuously absorb new human video data will enable robots to adapt to novel tasks and environments more efficiently.
- **Multi-Agent Interaction:** Expanding LfHV to model collaboration and coordinated behaviors between multiple agents is essential for tasks requiring joint effort, extending beyond single-robot imitation.
- **Multimodal Signals Accompanying Human Videos:** Incorporating audio, gaze, and tactile information can provide richer interaction evidence, improving understanding in situations where visual cues alone are insufficient (e.g., occlusions, material properties).
- **Utilization of Low-Quality Human Videos:** Developing methods robust to noise, blur, and incomplete demonstrations in internet-scale data will maximize the scalability advantage of human videos.
- **Standardized Benchmarking:** Establishing common evaluation protocols across diverse robots, tasks, and human video sources will enable more meaningful comparisons and foster reproducible research.
- **Egocentric Data Ecosystems:** Building collaborative infrastructures for standardized collection, richer annotation, and shared evaluation of egocentric data is vital for sustained progress in this area.

Ultimately, this survey underscores that the advancement of LfHV requires an integrated approach where progress in modeling, data acquisition, evaluation, and ecosystem development are tightly coupled. Such synergistic efforts are projected to play a pivotal role in overcoming data bottlenecks and establishing a foundation for more intelligent and adaptable embodied AI systems.
