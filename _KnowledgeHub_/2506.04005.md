# Vocabulary-free few-shot learning for Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research defines and addresses vocabulary-free few-shot learning for Vision-Language Models, where target class names are absent. It introduces Similarity Mapping (SiM), an efficient and interpretable method that classifies new instances by learning a linear mapping from generic prompt similarity scores to target classes, achieving performance competitive with zero-shot CLIP and outperforming existing label mapping techniques.

## Problem

- Existing few-shot Vision-Language Model (VLM) adaptation methods heavily rely on predefined, explicit class names, limiting their applicability in real-world scenarios.
- Class names can be ambiguous, require complex descriptions, or may not exist for novel concepts, making traditional prompt-based adaptation difficult or impossible.
- A fundamental gap exists in adapting powerful VLMs to new tasks using only visual examples, without the need for corresponding textual labels.

## Method

- The authors introduce ==Similarity Mapping (SiM)==, which uses a fixed set of generic textual or visual prompts (e.g., ImageNet class names or embeddings) instead of target class-specific names.
- SiM learns a ==linear mapping matrix== by minimizing a regularized least-squares objective, translating similarity scores between few-shot examples and generic prompts into target class probabilities.
- For new images, their similarity scores with the generic prompts are computed and then transformed by the learned matrix to predict the target class, enabling ==vocabulary-free classification==.

## Results

- SiM consistently outperformed existing vocabulary-free baselines, such as One-to-One mapping and Bayesian Label Mapping, across **10** evaluated datasets and two CLIP backbones.
- With **16 shots** per class using the ViT-L/14 backbone, SiM achieved an average accuracy of **83.9%**, surpassing zero-shot CLIP on **8 out of 10** datasets and significantly narrowing the performance gap with label-dependent few-shot VLM methods.
- The learning process for the mapping matrix is highly efficient, typically completing in under **one second**, demonstrating the method's practicality for rapid adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A simple linear mapping from generic VLM similarity scores can effectively classify novel, unnamed visual categories in a few-shot setting, establishing a robust baseline for vocabulary-free learning.
> - The choice of generic prompt source (e.g., ImageNet text, ImageNet images, Wordnet) impacts performance but the method remains effective and scales with different modalities and sizes of prompt sets.
> - The learned weights of the mapping matrix provide interpretability, allowing for a semantic understanding of target classes by linking them to the most relevant generic concepts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical and often overlooked limitation in the rapidly evolving field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and few-shot learning. VLMs, exemplified by models like CLIP, have revolutionized cross-modal understanding by aligning images and text in a shared latent space, enabling impressive zero-shot generalization capabilities. Building on this, few-shot learning techniques for VLMs aim to adapt these powerful models to new tasks with minimal labeled data.

**Current Landscape of VLM Few-Shot Learning:**
Existing few-shot adaptation methods for VLMs primarily fall into several categories:
- **Prompt Tuning:** Methods like CoOp and MaPLe optimize learnable "soft prompts" (continuous tokens) that are appended to or replace hand-crafted textual prompts. These approaches critically rely on the *availability of explicit class names* to construct the initial prompts (e.g., "a photo of a {class\_name}").
- **Adapter-based Methods:** Techniques such as Tip-Adapter and TaskRes introduce small, task-specific modules (adapters) or modify subsets of parameters to enhance efficiency and adaptation without extensive fine-tuning of the entire VLM. These, too, typically assume known class names for guiding the adaptation.
- **Low-Rank Fine-tuning:** Approaches like CLIP-LoRA apply low-rank adaptation to VLM encoders, offering parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Again, this usually operates within a framework where target class identities are explicitly known.

**The Gap Addressed by This Work:**
The fundamental constraint across almost all existing VLM few-shot methods is their inherent reliance on *predefined class names*. The authors highlight that this assumption is often impractical in real-world scenarios due to:
1. **Vague or Ambiguous Class Definitions:** Some concepts are hard to describe with a single, precise name.
2. **Complex Descriptions:** Classes might require lengthy and nuanced textual descriptions that go beyond a simple name.
3. **Emergence of New Concepts:** Novel categories may appear that were not part of the VLM's pre-training vocabulary or are simply unknown when the classification task is formulated.

This paper introduces "vocabulary-free few-shot learning for VLMs" as a novel problem setting, directly challenging this dependency. In this paradigm, only visual instances of the target classes are available; their corresponding names are explicitly *unavailable*. This significantly expands the practical applicability of VLMs to scenarios where semantic labels are difficult or impossible to obtain, moving beyond the traditional label-driven classification.

**Relation to Related Fields:**
- **Vocabulary-free Classification:** The paper acknowledges existing work in "vocabulary-free image classification" (e.g., Conti et al., 2023, 2024), which assigns names to images from unconstrained sets of semantic concepts, or retrieval-augmented models. This work differentiates itself by focusing on *learning a classifier from groups of images (few-shot visual examples)* rather than improving single-image classification or generating names.
- **Label Mapping:** The proposed Similarity Mapping (SiM) shares conceptual similarities with label mapping techniques common in "visual reprogramming" (e.g., Elsayed et al., Tsai et al., Chen et al., Cai et al.). These methods aim to align the output space of a pre-trained model with the label space of a new, distinct downstream task. SiM adapts this concept by mapping similarities derived from generic prompts to target classes without requiring explicit target class names.

In essence, this work introduces a crucial new problem definition and a foundational baseline method, pushing the boundaries of VLM adaptation towards more autonomous and label-agnostic learning, thereby making VLMs more robust and versatile for real-world deployments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to address a significant limitation in the current paradigm of few-shot learning for Vision-Language Models (VLMs): the prerequisite of knowing and using predefined class names. The authors argue that this dependency severely restricts the practical applicability of VLMs in various real-world scenarios.

**Core Motivation:**
The primary motivation stems from the practical challenges encountered when explicit, well-defined class names are unavailable or difficult to formulate. These challenges include:
- **Ambiguity and Vagueness:** Many real-world concepts are not easily encapsulated by a single, precise class name, making it hard to craft effective textual prompts.
- **Complexity of Descriptions:** Some classes might require elaborate descriptions that cannot be adequately conveyed through a simple name in a prompt.
- **Emergence of Novel Concepts:** New categories or evolving concepts may arise that were not present in the VLM's pre-training data and for which no established names exist.
- **Operational Constraints:** In certain applications, obtaining or specifying exact class names might be costly, time-consuming, or even undesirable (e.g., privacy concerns).

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Introduce Vocabulary-Free Few-Shot Learning:** The foremost objective is to formally define and establish a new problem setting where visual instances of target classes are available, but their corresponding names are explicitly absent. This paradigm shifts the focus from label-driven to instance-driven adaptation for VLMs.
2. **Develop an Effective Baseline (SiM):** To tackle this novel problem, the authors aim to propose a simple yet effective method, dubbed Similarity Mapping (SiM), that can classify target instances solely based on their similarity scores with a set of *generic* prompts. This approach must eliminate the need for carefully hand-crafted, task-specific prompts that rely on class names.
3. **Demonstrate Simplicity and Efficiency:** A key objective for SiM is to operate with high computational efficiency, ideally learning the necessary mapping quickly (e.g., in less than a second), and functioning as a black-box model that primarily uses similarity scores.
4. **Provide Interpretability:** Beyond classification performance, the authors seek to demonstrate that their method can offer insights into the semantic understanding of target classes by linking them to the generic concepts used as prompts. This interpretability can enhance user trust and model analysis.
5. **Catalyze Future Research:** Ultimately, the authors intend for SiM and the vocabulary-free few-shot learning framework to serve as a foundational baseline, encouraging further research and development in this important, underexplored area of VLM adaptation.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to make VLMs more robust, versatile, and applicable in practical scenarios where traditional methods fall short due to their reliance on predefined class semantics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the introduction of **Similarity Mapping (SiM)**, a simple yet effective baseline for the proposed vocabulary-free few-shot learning setting. SiM fundamentally deviates from traditional VLM adaptation by eliminating the reliance on explicit target class names.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Standard VLM Classification

The paper first re-establishes the standard VLM classification framework, as exemplified by CLIP. In this setup, both images ($x_i$) and textual descriptions (e.g., "a photo of a {class$_c$}") are embedded into a common latent space. A visual encoder produces normalized image embeddings ($f_i$), and a textual encoder yields normalized text embeddings ($t_c$). Classification is achieved by computing similarity scores ($l_{i,c} = f_i^T t_c$) and selecting the class with the highest score. The critical point is that this approach becomes infeasible when target class names are unavailable, as the text embeddings ($t_c$) cannot be generated.

#### 3.2. Our Approach: Similarity Mapping (SiM)

**A. Generic Prompts:**
To overcome the lack of target class names, SiM introduces the concept of **generic prompts**. Instead of class-specific prompts, a predefined set of $K$ arbitrary and generic embeddings ($t_k \in S^{d-1}$) is used. These generic prompts can be derived from various sources, such as:
- **Generic Textual Prompts:** E.g., class names from a large external dataset like ImageNet (e.g., "a photo of a {class\_k}"), or words from a lexical database like Wordnet (e.g., "a photo of a {word\_k}").
- **Generic Visual Prompts:** E.g., specific images drawn from a large dataset, where their visual embeddings are used as $t_k$.

For any given image $x_i$, similarity scores ($l_{i,k} = f_i^T t_k$) are computed against these $K$ generic prompts. For a few-shot set of $N$ images, these scores form a matrix $L \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$.

**B. Learning from the Few-Shot Set:**
The central idea of SiM is to learn a linear mapping from these generic similarity scores to the target class assignments. Given $N$ few-shot images (belonging to $C$ target classes), their ground-truth labels are represented as one-hot encoded vectors, forming a matrix $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C}$.
SiM then learns a matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times C}$ that maps the similarity matrix $L$ to the label matrix $Y$, such that $Y \approx LW$. This mapping is achieved by minimizing a least-squares objective with Tikhonov regularization:
$$ W = \arg \min_W ||Y - LW||_F^2 + \lambda||W||_F^2 $$
where $||.||_F$ is the Frobenius norm, and $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter for regularization. The solution for $W$ is given by:
$$ W = (L^T L + \lambda I_K)^{-1} Y $$
This formulation allows for efficient computation, with the learning of $W$ typically taking less than one second.

**C. Classification of Test Images:**
Once $W$ is learned, for any new test image $x_i$, its similarity scores $l_{i,k}$ with the generic prompts are computed. These scores are then multiplied by the learned matrix $W$ to obtain target class scores $s_{i,c}$:
$$ s_{i,c} = \sum_{k=1}^K w_{k,c} l_{i,k} $$
The predicted class for $x_i$ is then $\hat{c} = \arg \max_c s_{i,c}$.

**D. Interpretability:**
A valuable aspect of SiM is its interpretability. By examining the magnitudes of the weights $w_{k,c}$ for a specific target class $c$, one can identify which generic prompts ($t_k$) are most strongly associated with that class. This can provide a high-level semantic understanding of the target class.

**E. Interpretation as Unsupervised Clustering:**
The authors note that the objective function for learning $W$ can be viewed as an unsupervised clustering problem, akin to K-means, operating on feature vectors derived from the similarities with generic prompts.

#### 3.3. Experimental Validation

- **Datasets:** Evaluation is conducted on 11 standard image classification datasets (e.g., ImageNet, SUN397, Aircraft, EuroSAT, Food101, Caltech101, UCF101), covering diverse domains and granularities.
- **Few-Shot Settings:** Performance is assessed with 4, 8, and 16 shots (labeled examples) per target class.
- **VLMs:** Two CLIP backbones are used: ViT-B/16 and the more powerful ViT-L/14.
- **Generic Prompt Sources:**
 - **ImageNet Class Names (Textual):** $K=1000$ prompts of the form "a photo of a {class\_k}" using ImageNet's original classes.
 - **ImageNet Images (Visual):** $K=1000$ prompts where each $t_k$ is the visual embedding of a randomly drawn image from an ImageNet class.
 - **Wordnet Vocabulary (Textual):** A larger vocabulary of $K=16452$ words from Wordnet, selected for relevance to general categories (e.g., "building", "animal").
- **Comparative Methods:**
 - **Vocabulary-free baselines:** One-to-One mapping (using Frequency Label Mapping), Bayesian Label Mapping (BLM), and a vision-only Centroids approach.
 - **Upper-bound references (require class names):** Popular few-shot VLM methods like CoOp, MaPLe, Tip-Adapter, TaskRes, and CLIP-LoRA. These are not vocabulary-free but serve to benchmark the performance gap.

This comprehensive methodology allows for rigorous evaluation of SiM's performance, efficiency, and interpretability in the novel vocabulary-free few-shot learning setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of SiM provides several key insights into vocabulary-free few-shot learning for Vision-Language Models.

1. **SiM Outperforms Existing Label Mapping Techniques and Vision-Only Baselines:**
 - The paper demonstrates that SiM consistently and significantly surpasses state-of-the-art label mapping strategies like One-to-One mapping (based on FLM) and Bayesian Label Mapping (BLM) across all 10 evaluated datasets for both CLIP ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 backbones.
 - One-to-One mapping generally performed worse than BLM, indicating the benefit of a many-to-many mapping.
 - Surprisingly, the simple vision-only Centroids baseline, which replaces textual prompts with mean visual features and does not use generic prompts, largely outperformed BLM. This highlights the limitations of existing label mapping in the VLM few-shot context and underscores SiM's stronger performance.
 - The performance gap between SiM and BLM was smallest on datasets like Pets and Caltech101, whose concepts align well with ImageNet, and largest on fine-grained datasets (Aircraft, Cars, Flowers) where generic ImageNet concepts might be insufficient.

2. **SiM is Competitive with Zero-Shot CLIP Classification:**
 - SiM demonstrates strong performance relative to zero-shot CLIP classification (which, notably, *requires* class names).
 - With just 4 shots per class, SiM surpasses zero-shot CLIP on 6 out of 10 datasets. This increases to 8 out of 10 datasets with 16 shots per class.
 - Performance varies by dataset; SiM significantly outperforms zero-shot CLIP on Flowers, DTD, and EuroSAT, even with few shots. However, for datasets like Food101 and Pets, SiM sometimes remains below zero-shot performance, even in the 16-shot setting. This suggests potential for improvement through incorporating additional prior knowledge, perhaps from captioning models.

3. **Performance Gap with Traditional Few-Shot VLM Methods (Requiring Class Names):**
 - As expected, a performance gap persists between SiM and traditional few-shot VLM methods (CoOp, MaPLe, Tip-Adapter, TaskRes, CLIP-LoRA) that benefit from direct access to predefined class names. These methods serve as upper bounds.
 - However, this gap significantly narrows when using a more powerful backbone (CLIP ViT-L/14) and with an increased number of shots. For example, for the ViT-L/14 backbone with 16 shots, SiM's average accuracy is 83.9%, while the best traditional method (CLIP-LoRA) achieves 87.7%. This suggests that the vocabulary-free setting, while challenging, can approach the performance of label-dependent methods under certain conditions.

4. **Versatility of Generic Prompt Sources:**
 - **ImageNet Textual Prompts (SiM):** This was the primary and generally most effective source for generic prompts.
 - **ImageNet Image Prompts (SiM (images)):** Using visual embeddings of ImageNet images as prompts resulted in slightly lower performance compared to textual prompts. However, this gap diminished with more shots and with the stronger ViT-L/14 backbone, where image-based and text-based prompts yielded comparable results on most datasets in the 16-shot setting. This indicates the flexibility of the approach to handle diverse prompt modalities.
 - **Wordnet Textual Prompts (SiM (Wordnet)):** Employing a much larger vocabulary of $K=16452$ words from Wordnet also yielded competitive results. While performance slightly degraded in the 4-shot setting (possibly due to increased noise from a larger $K$), it was on par with ImageNet class names in the 8- and 16-shot settings. The method scaled gracefully with this larger prompt set (e.g., 0.8s for 16-shot ImageNet learning).

5. **Interpretability of Learned Weights:**
 - The learned linear mapping ($W$) offers a degree of interpretability. By analyzing the weights $w_{k,c}$, the model can reveal which generic concepts (prompts) are most aligned with a given target class.
 - Examples include linking "gerenuk" to "impala" and "gazelle" (ImageNet text prompts), "rope climbing" to "abseil" (Wordnet text prompts), and "shop bakery" to "bagels," "dough," and "pretzels" (ImageNet image prompts).
 - **Limitations in Interpretability:** The interpretability is not always perfect, especially for ambiguous or fine-grained classes. For example, "Highway or road" was linked to "traffic light" or "viaduct" (related concepts but not visually present in the shots), and "2012 Chevrolet Corvette Convertible" was matched with unrelated concepts. Importantly, this lack of perfect interpretability did not necessarily hinder classification accuracy.

In summary, SiM establishes a strong, efficient, and interpretable baseline for vocabulary-free few-shot learning, significantly advancing VLM applicability in challenging, label-scarce environments. While a gap remains with label-dependent methods, the results suggest considerable potential for future developments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces "vocabulary-free few-shot learning for Vision-Language Models" as a new and crucial problem setting, offering significant theoretical and practical implications.

**1. Expanding VLM Applicability to Real-World Scenarios:**
The most profound significance of this work is its ability to bypass the critical reliance on predefined class names, a pervasive constraint in current VLM adaptation methods. By doing so, it makes powerful VLMs applicable to numerous real-world situations where:
- **Labels are Ambiguous or Dynamic:** Concepts in evolving domains (e.g., scientific discoveries, niche product categories) might lack established names or have fuzzy boundaries.
- **Data Annotation is Limited or Costly:** Manually assigning precise names to new classes is expensive and time-consuming.
- **Novelty Detection is Key:** The ability to learn new categories from visual examples alone, without prior textual definitions, is crucial for discovering emerging trends or anomalies.
- **Privacy Concerns:** In sensitive applications, explicit naming of categories might be undesirable or regulated.

**2. A Robust, Efficient, and Interpretable Baseline:**
SiM provides a simple yet highly effective baseline that serves as a strong starting point for this new problem. Its key advantages include:
- **Computational Efficiency:** Learning the mapping matrix $W$ is remarkably fast (typically less than a second), making it suitable for rapid adaptation in dynamic environments.
- **Black-Box Operation:** SiM relies solely on similarity scores, not direct access to embeddings, which could simplify integration with various VLM architectures or even proprietary models.
- **Interpretability:** The ability to link target classes to generic concepts provides valuable insights into the model's decision-making process, fostering trust and enabling better analysis by domain experts. This is particularly important for debugging and understanding "why" a model classifies something in a certain way.

**3. Catalyst for Future Research:**
The paper explicitly outlines several promising avenues, establishing a roadmap for future work in this nascent field:
- **Advanced Few-Shot Adaptation:** Adapting sophisticated techniques from existing few-shot VLM research (e.g., prompt tuning, adapters, low-rank adaptation) to the vocabulary-free setting could significantly bridge the performance gap with label-dependent methods.
- **Optimizing Generic Prompts:** Research into curating, expanding, or dynamically selecting more optimal sets of generic textual or visual prompts could drastically improve performance and interpretability. This could involve richer databases, multi-modal generic concepts, or techniques for identifying "semantically dense" generic concepts.
- **Automated Class Naming:** The ultimate challenge is to assign meaningful, human-comprehensible names to the learned vocabulary-free classes. This would complete the loop of discovery and understanding, moving towards truly autonomous concept learning.

**4. Shifting the Paradigm of VLM Adaptation:**
This work represents a paradigm shift from "label-centric" to "instance-centric" VLM adaptation. Instead of relying on predefined human language to structure classification, it leverages the inherent alignment capabilities of VLMs to group and discriminate based on visual similarity to broad, generic concepts. This approach brings VLMs closer to how humans might intuitively categorize new objects by resemblance rather than by strict dictionary definitions.

In conclusion, "Vocabulary-free few-shot learning for Vision-Language Models" is a foundational paper that not only defines a critical new problem but also offers a powerful, efficient, and interpretable solution. Its significance lies in unlocking the full potential of VLMs for broader, more flexible, and more autonomous application in diverse and challenging real-world scenarios, thereby pushing the boundaries of artificial intelligence in visual understanding.
