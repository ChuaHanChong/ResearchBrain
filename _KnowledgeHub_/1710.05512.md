# The Feeling of Success: Does Touch Sensing Help Predict Grasp Outcomes?

> [!summary] Summary
> An end-to-end visuo-tactile model predicts whether a parallel-jaw grasp will hold before liftoff. Two GelSight fingertip sensors and a front RGB camera each feed a ResNet-50 branch, using temporal image differences for touch, fused late into a fully-connected classifier. Trained on 9,269 autonomously collected grasps over 106 objects, the multimodal model reaches 77.8% prediction accuracy and 94% real grasp success versus 80% for vision alone.

## Problem

- Analytic grasp metrics depend on object, gripper, and environment models, so misspecification and unmodeled contact effects break the link to real outcomes.
- Prior learned grasping predicts outcomes from vision or depth only, leaving no direct evidence about contact force, pressure, or object compliance.
- Earlier tactile work fuses touch through hand-designed features or model-based pipelines, so the actual contribution of rich touch was never measured under controlled comparison.

## Method

- Multimodal ==late-fusion network==: per-modality ==ResNet-50== branches (ImageNet-initialized, weight-shared across the two fingers) whose pooled features concatenate into a two-layer fully-connected ==grasp-success classifier==.
- Tactile branch consumes the ==temporal difference== of GelSight images between rest and gripper closure, so gel deformation is encoded directly; the RGB branch takes both pre-grasp and at-grasp frames.
- ==Propose-and-reject grasping==: randomly sample end-effector pose, angle, and gripping force, close the fingers, and lift only when predicted success exceeds 0.9; 9,269 trials on 106 objects ==auto-labeled== by a GelSight contact classifier.

## Results

- Tactile plus vision reached **77.8%** test accuracy on held-out objects, beating tactile-only **75.6%**, depth **73.2%**, vision-only **68.8%**, and chance **61.8%**.
- Real-robot grasping on **12** unseen objects gave **94%** success for visuo-tactile versus **80%** vision-only and **43%** for the tuned depth-based data-collection baseline.
- Both GelSights (**75.6%**) beat either alone (**75.3%**, **73.8%**); hand-crafted indentation features with a linear SVM still hit **72.7%**, above vision; gripper pose added nothing (**68.8%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Touch and vision are complementary rather than redundant: fusion beats either modality alone, and touch alone already outperforms vision for outcome prediction.
> - Formatting tactile signals as images, and as temporal differences, lets ImageNet-pretrained convolutional backbones transfer directly, avoiding sensor characterization or calibration.
> - Gains come cheaply on failure modes vision cannot see, such as one finger not contacting, but a reject-only policy can need many attempts before accepting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic grasping has long sought to enable robots to reliably manipulate objects in diverse and unstructured environments. Traditionally, research in robotic grasping has bifurcated into two main approaches:

- **Analytic Grasping Models:** These methods rely on detailed geometric models of objects, grippers, and environments, often employing manually defined grasp metrics (e.g., force closure, form closure). While providing theoretical insights into physical interactions, these models are highly susceptible to real-world complexities such as model misspecification, unmodeled effects, and measurement errors (e.g., imperfect object pose estimation, material properties).
- **Data-Driven Approaches (Learning to Grasp):** More recently, the advent of deep learning and large-scale data collection has led to a surge in data-driven methods. These approaches, often using vision (RGB or depth images), directly learn grasp locations or outcome predictions from extensive datasets collected through human supervision, simulation, or autonomous robot trials. Pioneers in this area include works by Lenz et al., Pinto and Gupta, and Levine et al. – the latter being co-authored by Sergey Levine, indicating his significant contribution to this very subfield.

Within this landscape, the role of **tactile sensing** has been recognized as crucial for robust manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring fine motor skills, detecting slip, or handling deformable/fragile objects. Humans extensively utilize multimodal perception, including touch, for grasping. However, the adoption of tactile sensors in robotics has been slow due to:
1. **Hardware Limitations:** Issues with sensitivity, cost, durability, and integration.
2. **Modeling Challenges:** Tactile readings are difficult to model analytically, and even small errors can significantly degrade performance when integrated into traditional control schemes.

This paper addresses a significant gap at the intersection of these fields. While end-to-end learning has been successfully applied to vision-based grasping, its application to **tactile sensing, especially in a multimodal context with rich tactile data, has been largely unexplored.** Previous multimodal integration efforts were primarily model-based and often used simpler tactile features. The GelSight sensor, with its high-resolution image-based output, presents a unique opportunity to apply deep learning techniques (originally developed for visual data) directly to tactile information.

Therefore, this work positions itself as a pioneering effort to bring the power of end-to-end deep learning to multimodal (vision + touch) robotic grasping, directly tackling the modeling difficulties of tactile data and providing a quantitative evaluation of the benefits of touch sensing in a learned framework. It moves beyond theoretical discussions or feature-engineered approaches to demonstrate practical improvements in real-world robot performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to directly answer the question: **"Does touch sensing help predict grasp outcomes?"** More specifically, the authors aim to investigate whether incorporating high-resolution tactile readings substantially improves grasp outcome prediction accuracy and actual grasping performance when combined with visual information within a multimodal sensing framework.

The motivations driving this inquiry are multi-faceted:

- **Mimicking Human Capabilities:** Humans rely heavily on both vision (for initial localization and overall scene understanding) and touch (for fine-grained contact information, force, and compliance once contact is established). Replicating this multimodal synergy is critical for developing more dexterous and robust robotic manipulators.
- **Addressing Limitations of Unimodal Sensing:**
 - **Vision-only:** While powerful for initial localization, vision can struggle to accurately assess contact forces, material properties (e.g., compliance, slipperiness), or resolve occlusions once a grasp is initiated. Predicting grasp success solely from vision is "quite challenging."
 - **Tactile-only:** Touch sensing provides precise contact information but lacks the global context provided by vision.
- **Overcoming Traditional Modeling Difficulties:** Conventional, model-based techniques for integrating tactile information are "exceptionally difficult" due to the complexity of tactile readings, their sensitivity to measurement errors, and calibration challenges. This difficulty has historically hindered the widespread adoption of tactile sensors.
- **Leveraging Advances in End-to-End Learning:** Inspired by the success of deep neural networks in vision-based grasping, the authors hypothesize that end-to-end learning can bypass the need for explicit analytical models of tactile interactions, allowing the network to directly learn robust representations from raw sensor data. This approach simplifies the system design and potentially improves robustness.
- **Quantitative Evaluation:** The paper aims to provide a controlled, experimental evaluation of the relative importance of each modality (vision vs. touch) and their combination, thereby offering empirical evidence for the value of tactile sensing in learned grasping systems. To their knowledge, this is the "first to present an end-to-end learned system for robotic grasping that combines rich visual and tactile sensing, and provides a controlled evaluation of the benefits of touch sensing for grasp performance."

In essence, the motivation stems from the desire to develop more capable and reliable robotic grasping systems by exploring a human-inspired multimodal sensing paradigm, facilitated by modern deep learning techniques and advanced tactile sensor technology.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a data-driven, end-to-end learning approach to predict grasp outcomes from multimodal visual and tactile inputs.

**A. Sensory Setup:**
- **Robot:** A 7-DoF Sawyer arm is used for manipulation.
- **Gripper:** A Weiss WSG-50 parallel jaw gripper is equipped.
- **Tactile Sensors:** Two GelSight high-resolution tactile sensors are mounted on each finger of the gripper. These optical sensors capture images (1280x960 resolution) of the deformation of a soft elastomer surface upon contact, providing information about contact geometry, texture, and force. A key advantage is that their output is a standard 2D image, compatible with CNN architectures.
- **Visual Sensor:** A Microsoft Kinect 2 sensor is mounted in front of the robot, providing RGB (color) and depth images. Only the RGB images are used for the main visuo-tactile model.

**B. Data Collection:**
- **Scale:** Over 9,000 grasping trials were autonomously collected from 106 unique objects, which varied significantly in shape, dimension, weight, color, and material.
- **Automation:** The process was automated to allow for large-scale collection. The robot randomized grasp parameters (x-y-z coordinates, gripper angle, gripping force) around the object's approximate location (estimated via Kinect depth data).
- **Trial Chronology & Labeling:** Each trial involved:
 1. Moving to a chosen grasp position/orientation.
 2. Closing the gripper with a randomized force.
 3. Attempting to lift the object and holding it for two seconds.
 4. If the object was still held, it was placed back; otherwise, the trial was deemed a failure.
 - **Snapshots:** Three key snapshots were recorded:
 - T_a: Initial state (arm in rest).
 - T_b: Gripper fingers closed on the object (pre-lift).
 - T_c: Two seconds after lift-off (used for labeling success/failure).
 - **Automatic Labeling:** A separate deep neural network classifier trained on raw GelSight images at T_c was used for automated contact detection and labeling. Manual review was performed for ambiguous or mislabeled cases.

**C. Model Architecture (Deep Neural Network):**
- **Modality-Specific Feature Extraction:** The model processes images from different modalities using separate convolutional neural networks (CNNs).
 - **Vision:** RGB images from the frontal camera at T_a (before grasp) and T_b (at grasp) are fed into a ResNet-50 architecture. The spatially-pooled features from the penultimate layer are extracted.
 - **Tactile:** GelSight images from the left and right fingers, specifically their temporal difference (I_Tb - I_Ta) to capture deformation, are fed into a shared ResNet-50 architecture.
- **Multi-modal Fusion:** The extracted features from all CNNs (visual and tactile) are concatenated.
- **Grasp Outcome Prediction:** The concatenated features are then passed through a two-layer fully-connected network to predict the probability of a successful grasp (y = f(x)).
- **Training Details:**
 - **Initialization:** CNN weights were initialized from a model pre-trained on ImageNet.
 - **Optimization:** Trained for 20 epochs using the Adam optimizer.
 - **Data Augmentation:** Standard augmentation (cropping, resizing, horizontal flipping) was applied to RGB images. Despite non-stationarity, similar augmentations were applied to GelSight images to prevent overfitting.

**D. Grasp Selection Procedure (for Real-World Evaluation):**
- The trained model acts as a "rejection mechanism" for proposed grasps.
- A "proposal mechanism" randomly selects gripper parameters (position, orientation, force) in the vicinity of the object.
- The gripper moves to the proposed configuration and closes.
- The model predicts the success rate based on the current visual and tactile readings.
- If the predicted success rate is above a pre-defined threshold (0.9), the grasp is accepted, and the object is lifted to observe the true outcome.
- If below the threshold, a new random set of parameters is selected, and the process repeats. This "propose-and-reject" scheme was chosen for fair comparison of prediction models, acknowledging it's not the most efficient grasp selection method.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented two main sets of experimental results: one evaluating model prediction accuracy on unseen test data, and another evaluating real-world grasping performance on novel objects.

**A. Comparison on Outcome Prediction Accuracy (Table 1):**
- **Dataset Split:** The collected 9269 trials were divided into training and test sets, ensuring no object appeared in both sets to evaluate generalization. Results were averaged over three random splits.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Tactile Advantage:** The tactile-only model (GelSight L & R) achieved a 75.6% accuracy, significantly outperforming the vision-only model (68.8%). This clearly indicates the higher predictive power of touch sensing for grasp outcome *after* contact is made.
 - **Multimodal Superiority:** The `Tactile + Vision` model achieved the highest accuracy at **77.8%**, demonstrating that combining modalities provides a substantial improvement over either single modality alone. This suggests complementary information provided by vision and touch.
 - **Depth vs. RGB:** The depth-based visual model (73.2%) performed better than the RGB vision-only model (68.8%), highlighting the importance of geometric information in visual grasping. However, `Tactile (Both)` still surpassed `Depth`.
 - **Gripper Pose:** Adding gripper pose as an input to the visual network (`Vision + Gripper pose`, 68.8%) did not improve performance over `Vision only`, suggesting that the visual CNN implicitly captures sufficient pose information or that it's less critical than contact information.
 - **Hand-crafted Features:** The `Indentation` model, using simple hand-crafted features from GelSight images, achieved a respectable 72.7%. While strong for a simple model, the end-to-end learned `Tactile (Both)` still outperformed it, suggesting benefits of deep learning even with smaller datasets.
 - **Single vs. Dual Tactile Sensors:** Using both GelSight sensors (`Tactile (Both)`, 75.6%) slightly outperformed single-sensor models (`GelSight L`, 75.3%; `GelSight R`, 73.8%), indicating that having contact information from both fingers is beneficial. The variation between single sensors also highlights the impact of physical sensor conditions (e.g., gel wear).
 - **Baseline:** Chance performance was 61.8%, indicating that even single-modality models offer significant improvement over random guessing.

**B. Evaluation of Grasping Performance (Table 2):**
- **Real-World Test:** To assess practical utility, models were evaluated on a robot performing grasps on 12 entirely new objects never seen during training or testing of the classification models.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Data Collection Baseline:** The original depth-sensor-based automated data collection method (randomizing pose and force around a cylinder fit to the object). This method was heavily tuned for data collection but not optimized for success. It achieved an average success rate of **43%**.
 - **Vision Only Model:** The end-to-end learned model using only visual input. It achieved an average success rate of **80%**.
 - **Tactile + Vision Model:** The end-to-end learned multi-modal model. It achieved the highest average success rate at **94%**.
- **Significant Improvement:** The multi-modal tactile+vision model showed a **14% improvement** over the vision-only model and more than doubled the success rate of the data collection baseline.
- **Object-Specific Performance:**
 - The multi-modal model achieved 100% success on 8 of the 12 objects.
 - The "computer mouse" (object 1) was particularly challenging: the baseline failed completely (0%), vision-only managed 10%, while tactile+vision achieved 60% success. This demonstrates the critical role of touch for complex or geometrically challenging objects.
 - The paper notes that the purely visual model sometimes attempted grasps where one finger was not making contact, a problem "easily recognized with tactile sensing" and not observed with the visuo-tactile model.

These results conclusively demonstrate that incorporating tactile sensing, processed via an end-to-end deep learning framework, substantially improves both the ability to predict grasp outcomes and the actual success rate of robotic grasping in real-world scenarios, particularly for novel and challenging objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the field of robotics, particularly in manipulation and human-robot interaction.

**A. Significance of Findings:**
- **Empirical Validation of Tactile Importance:** The study provides robust empirical evidence, arguably for the first time in an end-to-end learned system with rich data, that tactile sensing is not merely a supplementary modality but a crucial component for achieving high grasp success rates. It definitively answers the central question of the paper: yes, touch sensing substantially helps.
- **Demonstration of End-to-End Learning for Multimodal Robotics:** The work successfully applies deep neural networks to integrate raw visual and tactile data for a complex robotic task, sidestepping the formidable challenges of traditional model-based integration and feature engineering for tactile sensors. This highlights the power of data-driven approaches for complex sensor fusion.
- **Simplicity and Robustness:** By learning representations directly from data, the method requires "minimal engineering of the actual grasping system" and no "characterization of the tactile sensors" or "model of the robot or object." This simplicity contributes to robustness against real-world uncertainties and model inaccuracies.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The high success rate (94%) on *previously unseen objects* underscores the model's ability to generalize beyond its training data, a critical aspect for practical robotic deployment in dynamic environments.
- **Improved Grasp Quality Assessment:** The ability of the visuo-tactile model to detect "empty grasps" (where a finger isn't making contact) suggests a more nuanced understanding of the grasp state, which is vital for robust manipulation.

**B. Potential Impact:**
- **More Capable and Reliable Robots:** The demonstrated increase in grasp success rate translates directly to more reliable and autonomous robotic systems. This is critical for applications in manufacturing, logistics, domestic assistance, and disaster response, where robust object manipulation is key.
- **Accelerated Adoption of Tactile Sensors:** By showing the clear benefits and a viable end-to-end learning pathway, this work could spur wider adoption and further development of advanced tactile sensing technologies in robotics.
- **Inspiration for Multimodal Learning:** The success of this multimodal approach can inspire similar research in other robotic tasks that could benefit from integrating diverse sensory inputs (e.g., force-torque, proprioception, haptics beyond touch).
- **Foundation for Future Research:**
 - **Proactive Grasp Planning:** While the current method uses a reactive "propose-and-reject" mechanism, the insights gained can inform the development of **proactive visuo-tactile grasp planning algorithms** that predict optimal grasp locations *before* contact, significantly speeding up the grasping process.
 - **Learning from Interaction:** The paper acknowledges that the current approach doesn't fully leverage the "interactive nature of tactile information for grasping" (e.g., real-time slip detection or post-liftoff events). Future work could integrate the learned models into closed-loop control for adaptive grasping.
 - **Material Recognition and Manipulation:** The high-resolution tactile data from GelSight could be further exploited for learning material properties, which is crucial for handling fragile, deformable, or slippery objects.

**C. Limitations and Future Work (as identified by authors):**
- **Grasp Proposal Mechanism:** The random search for grasp locations, though sufficient for the experimental hypothesis, is inefficient for real-world applications. Future work should focus on integrating optimization procedures for grasp selection.
- **Reactive Nature:** The current model predicts success *after* contact, serving as a rejection mechanism. A truly ideal solution would predict success *before* attempting the grasp, leading to more efficient planning.
- **Post-Liftoff Events:** The model does not explicitly consider events like slipping that occur after the object is lifted, which is a critical aspect of stable manipulation. Future work should incorporate dynamic information and potentially real-time feedback.

In conclusion, this paper provides a compelling argument for the indispensable role of tactile sensing in modern robotic grasping, demonstrating how it can be effectively integrated with vision using end-to-end deep learning. It lays a crucial groundwork for developing the next generation of highly dexterous and robust robotic manipulators.
