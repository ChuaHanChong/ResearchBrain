# Decoding Task Progress from VLA Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper probes pi_0.5's residual stream and finds task progress is linearly decodable from internal activations, already present before robot-specific fine-tuning. The authors define weak decodability, strong decodability, and steerability, showing the progress feature is decodable but not steerable. They repurpose it as a label-free OOD detector that matches or beats supervised baselines on cross-task and cross-perturbation generalization.

## Problem

- VLAs are deployed as general-purpose manipulation policies, but no tools exist to understand what they represent internally or monitor them at runtime.
- Task-specific fine-tuning needed for deployment degrades the generalization pretrained VLM backbones provide, causing loss of language sensitivity and brittleness to perturbations.
- Existing OOD detectors have complementary weaknesses: density-based methods transfer poorly across tasks, while supervised detectors like SAFE require labeled OOD data.

## Method

- Extract ==residual-stream== embeddings from every layer of **pi_0.5** for each observation, mean-pooled over tokens, paired with a free progress label tau = 1 - t/T.
- Test strong decodability via a language counterfactual that swaps the target noun; train a ==contrastive probe== with a hinge loss to recover language sensitivity.
- Build a runtime ==OOD detector== comparing predicted progress against an expected-completion-time baseline, evaluated on VLABench across 10 manipulation tasks with four image perturbations.

## Results

- Task progress is weakly decodable with low L1 loss in early layers, with a large gap versus shuffled-label control probes even at high MLP capacity.
- A naive probe hits **R2 = 0.95** in-distribution but ignores language counterfactuals; the contrastive probe trades this to **R2 = 0.33** while correctly tracking swapped prompts.
- As an OOD detector, the method is the only one whose AUROC does not degrade on held-out tasks (**0.796** to **0.833**), beating supervised SAFE detectors on unseen perturbations (**0.960** per-episode).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLAs encode rich, linearly readable quantities like task progress, and this structure emerges from large-scale robotic pre-training rather than task-specific fine-tuning.
> - Observability and controllability are separable: task progress is decodable yet fails to steer the policy, exposing a gap between what a VLA represents and what it acts on.
> - A label-free linear probe can match or beat supervised OOD detectors requiring failure labels, especially for generalizing to unseen tasks and perturbation types.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a developing area in artificial intelligence, aiming to achieve generalizable robot manipulation through the integration of pretrained vision-language model (VLM) backbones with action decoding heads. These models are designed to leverage rich semantic and visual priors, facilitating open-vocabulary task specification across varied environments and robotic platforms. However, the deployment of VLAs frequently encounters challenges. Fine-tuning for new robots or tasks has been observed to degrade the generalization capabilities that originally motivated the use of VLM backbones. This degradation manifests as reduced language sensitivity, brittleness to perceptual perturbations, and a propensity for near-zero success rates when training-scene assumptions are altered. Despite these documented failure modes, there is a recognized lack of tools for understanding the internal states of VLAs, monitoring their runtime behavior, or predicting when and why they might fail.

The field of interpretability, particularly for large models such as vision and language models, has generated various tools for understanding internal representations. Given that VLAs often build upon these same VLM backbones, interpretability methods are a natural fit for analyzing VLA internals. Prior work has begun to explore the readability of VLA representations, typically focusing on what the policy "knows about the world," such as semantic directions, motion primitives, physical states, or high-level logical states. This existing research has largely characterized external environmental or object properties known to the VLA.

This paper addresses a distinct gap: characterizing the VLA's internal representation of its *task progress*. Task progress, defined as the normalized time remaining in a trajectory, is considered a foundational quantity in sequential decision-making. It serves as an intrinsic goal-conditioned value signal that monotonically tracks the policy's proximity to task completion. While a body of research in robot learning has focused on training *external* vision-language models to estimate task progress—using these estimators as reward signals, planning heuristics, or success detectors—this work hypothesizes that VLAs, which process the same input modalities and produce actions implicitly dependent on task progress, may already encode this information within their own internal representations.

Furthermore, a significant safety concern for VLAs in deployment is their susceptibility to out-of-distribution (OOD) states, which can lead to silent failures. Current OOD detection methods for deep models typically fall into two categories: density and distance-based approaches (e.g., Mahalanobis distance, energy-based detection) that model in-distribution feature distributions and flag low-density points, or supervised approaches (e.g., SAFE) that train detectors on labeled in-distribution and OOD data. Both families have limitations for VLAs, such as memorizing training distributions or requiring OOD labels and poor transfer across perturbations. This work positions an internally-derived task progress signal as a potentially superior, label-free, and calibrated OOD detection mechanism.

In summary, this research advances the interpretability of VLAs by focusing on an internal, task-centric signal (task progress), distinguishes itself from prior work that focused on world knowledge, and proposes a novel application of this internal signal for robust, label-free OOD detection, thereby addressing critical deployment challenges.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether the quantity of task progress—defined as the normalized fraction of a trajectory remaining until completion—is linearly encoded within the internal representations of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The motivation stems from the observed challenges in deploying VLAs, where failures are common but tools for runtime monitoring and understanding internal states are lacking. The authors hypothesize that task progress, being an implicitly essential factor for sequential decision-making, might already be represented within the VLA's internal activations, negating the need for separate external progress estimators.

More specifically, the paper aims to achieve the following detailed objectives:

1. **Probe for Task Progress Decodability:** To determine if task progress is reliably decodable from intermediate embeddings (specifically, the residual stream) of a VLA model, using linear probing techniques. This involves assessing:
 - **Weak decodability:** Whether a linear signal correlated with task progress can be extracted from in-distribution activations across different layers of the VLA, and at various stages of its training (e.g., in the pretrained VLM backbone before robot-specific training).
 - **Strong decodability:** Whether the extracted linear probe generalizes to counterfactually transformed inputs, particularly language perturbations, indicating that the probe's output responds appropriately to changes in input factors upon which task progress depends. This aims to rule out spurious correlations.

2. **Evaluate Steerability of Task Progress:** To investigate whether the task progress signal, if decodable, can be causally manipulated to influence the VLA's behavior. This objective seeks to understand the relationship between feature observability and controllability by directly intervening on activations and assessing if such interventions lead to predictable changes in the policy's actions that match a desired counterfactual.

3. **Develop a Runtime Monitoring Tool:** To leverage the decodable task progress signal as a lightweight, interpretable mechanism for runtime monitoring of deployed VLAs. Specifically, the motivation is to:
 - **Implement a Label-Free OOD Detector:** To develop a method that identifies out-of-distribution (OOD) states by detecting stalled task progress, without requiring explicit OOD labels during training. The goal is for this detector to be competitive with state-of-the-art supervised OOD detection methods and to generalize robustly across unseen tasks and perturbations.
 - **Diagnose Language-Grounding Degradation:** To use the progress probe as a diagnostic tool for identifying instances where the VLA policy loses its sensitivity to language instructions, a known issue after fine-tuning.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to provide fundamental insights into the internal workings of VLAs, contributing to a more interpretable and robust deployment of these models in real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a structured approach involving VLA model analysis, novel definitions for feature properties, linear probing techniques, and an application to out-of-distribution (OOD) detection.

#### 3.1. Vision-Language Action Models and Embedding Extraction

- **Target VLA Model:** The primary model investigated is $\pi_{0.5}$, a VLA known for open-world generalization, built upon the PaliGemma vision-language model backbone.
- **Input Observation:** An input observation $x = (I, q, \ell)$ consists of an image $I$, proprioceptive data $q$, and a natural language prompt $\ell$.
- **Intermediate Embeddings:** The VLA processes this input through multiple layers of transformer blocks. At each layer $i$ (from $0$ to $L$), an intermediate embedding $z_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is produced. These embeddings follow a residual function $z_{i+1} = z_i + f_i(z_i)$.
- **Embedding Extraction:** For a dataset of $N$ expert trajectories $D = \{(x_t, a_t)\}_{i=0}^N$, where $t$ ranges from $0$ to $T$ for each trajectory, the intermediate embeddings $(z_{0,t}, z_{1,t}, \ldots, z_{L,t})$ are cached for each observation $x_t$. The embeddings are mean-pooled across tokens. These cached embeddings, paired with their corresponding task progress labels $\zeta_t$, form the dataset $D_{probe}$ used for training linear probes.

#### 3.2. Definition of Task Progress Feature ($\tau$)

- **Definition:** Task progress $\tau(x_t)$ is defined as the normalized time remaining in a trajectory $\xi = (x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_T)$. Specifically, $\tau(x_t) = 1 - \frac{\phi_\xi(x_t)}{T}$, where $\phi_\xi(x_t)$ is the index of observation $x_t$ within trajectory $\xi$. This yields a value in $$, where $1$ denotes the start and $0$ denotes completion.
- **Properties for Monitoring:** This definition is chosen because progress monotonically decreases along expert trajectories (predictable in-distribution behavior), is easily labeled from offline data, is suitable for flagging OOD states (when progress stalls), and is expected to generalize across tasks.

#### 3.3. Linear Probes for Feature Decodability and Steerability

The research formalizes three distinct notions of feature representation strength using a linear probe $\varphi(z_i) = w^Tz_i + b$ and an appropriate loss function $L$:

- **Weak Decodability:** Assessed by evaluating the probe's risk $R(\varphi; D_{val})$ on a validation dataset $D_{val}$ drawn from the same distribution as the training data. A low risk indicates that a linear signal correlated with the feature $\zeta$ (task progress $\tau$ in this work) can be extracted from in-distribution activations. To validate this, the performance of probes trained on true labels is compared against probes trained on shuffled labels across varying capacities (MLP sweeps).
- **Strong Decodability:** Addresses the limitation of weak decodability regarding spurious correlations. It is tested by introducing a counterfactual transformation $T: X \to X$ that perturbs inputs affecting $\zeta$. The probe's risk $R(\varphi; D_T)$ is then evaluated on $D_T = \{(z_i(T(x_k)), \zeta(T(x_k)))\}$. Low risk on $D_T$ (without retraining $\varphi$) indicates the probe responds correctly to counterfactual input changes, signifying strong decodability. For task progress, a language-perturbation counterfactual ($T_{lang}$), swapping the target noun in the instruction, is used.
 - **Contrastive Training:** To enhance strong decodability, especially with language sensitivity, a contrastive objective is employed. This involves augmenting $D_{probe}$ with $T_{lang}$ activations, labeled with a proxy progress (e.g., $\tau=1.0$ for swapped tasks), and training with a within-pair hinge loss that encourages separation between original and swapped prompt activations, combined with a BCE anchor on absolute progress labels.
- **Steerability:** Determines if the model *uses* the decodable signal for decision-making. This involves direct intervention on activations. A steered activation is defined as $\tilde{z}_i(x) = z_i(x) + \frac{\zeta(T(x)) - \zeta(x)}{\|w\|^2}w$, which shifts the activation along the probe's weight vector $w$ to approximate a counterfactual value. Steerability risk $R_S(\varphi; D, T) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K d(\tilde{\pi}_k, \pi^*_k)$ measures how well the steered action distribution $\tilde{\pi}_k$ approximates the true counterfactual action distribution $\pi^*_k$. For task progress, $T_{boost}$ is defined to inject forward progress, and the resulting actions are compared to future ground truth actions.

#### 3.4. Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection via the Progress Feature

- **Detector Mechanism:** The detector takes a progress probe $\varphi$ and a threshold $\delta$. The expected task completion time $E[T|\ell]$ is estimated from in-distribution rollouts. At runtime, for each replan, predicted progress $\tau_{pred} = \varphi(z_{i,t})$ is decoded. A residual $V_\tau(t) = \tau_{pred} - (1 - \frac{t}{E[T|\ell]})$ is computed, measuring the deviation from expected progress.
- **OOD Flagging:** The rollout is flagged as OOD the first time $V_\tau(t) > \delta$, indicating stalled or uncharacteristic progress.
- **OOD Data Generation:** OOD states are generated by applying four types of image augmentations to in-distribution states: Gaussian noise, color shift, blur, and occlusion. Perturbations are introduced at various points (25%, 50%, 75%) within trajectories.
- **Evaluation:** Robustness is evaluated by testing generalization to unseen tasks and unseen OOD conditions (holding out noise modes). Performance is compared against baselines like Mahalanobis distance, Variational Autoencoders (VAE), and supervised SAFE-MLP/SAFE-LSTM, using AUROC metric at both per-replan and per-episode granularities.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **VLABench Dataset:** Experiments are conducted on VLABench, which comprises 10 diverse manipulation tasks with 500 trajectories per task, providing semantic variation.
- **Models for Comparison:** Besides fine-tuned $\pi_{0.5}$, comparisons are made with PaliGemma (VLM backbone only), base $\pi_{0.5}$ (after large-scale robot pre-training but before VLABench fine-tuning), and VLABench fine-tuned $\pi_0$ (a distinct VLA architecture). This allows isolating the stage of training that establishes the progress feature.
- **Probe Training:** Linear probes are trained using an $L_1$ loss over 300 epochs, batch size 64, and learning rate $1e^{-3}$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the decodability, steerability, and utility of task progress within VLA representations.

#### 4.1. Emergence of the Progress Feature (Weak Decodability)

- **Layer-wise Decodability:** Task progress was found to be weakly decodable from the early vision-backbone layers and the initial layers of the Gemma transformer in fine-tuned $\pi_{0.5}$. The $L_1$ loss indicated that a linear probe could reliably recover task progress from these activations (Figure 2, left).
- **Meaningful Signal:** To ensure the signal was not spurious, probes with varying capacities (MLPs) were trained on both true and shuffled labels. A substantial performance gap was observed between probes trained on true labels and those trained on shuffled labels, even for high-capacity probes (Figure 2, right). This suggests that the observed weak decodability is a meaningful property of the layer's representation, rather than simply memorization of random targets.
- **Origin of the Feature:** Comparing probe performance across PaliGemma (VLM backbone only), base $\pi_{0.5}$ (after robot pre-training), and fine-tuned $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ revealed that the progress feature is observable in all four models. However, the $\pi_{0.x}$ variants significantly outperformed PaliGemma and performed comparably to each other (Figure 3). This indicates that the progress feature is primarily established during large-scale robotic pre-training, rather than during task-specific fine-tuning. Appendix B.4 further confirmed that task progress is decodable from VLA embeddings but not raw observations, reinforcing that it is a learned representation.

#### 4.2. Reaction to Language Perturbations (Strong Decodability)

- **Naive Probe Limitations:** A naively trained layer-0 probe, while successfully decoding task progress (R²=0.95), showed nearly identical outputs under original and language-perturbed (target noun swap, $T_{lang}$) instructions (Figure 4, left). This suggested the naive probe relied primarily on visual input, lacking language sensitivity, and thus task progress was not strongly decodable using this method.
- **Counterfactual Training for Strong Decodability:** By augmenting the training dataset with $T_{lang}$ activations (labeled with a proxy of $\tau=1.0$ for swapped tasks) and employing a contrastive objective at layer 10, strong decodability was achieved. The augmented probe successfully tracked progress under the original instruction while pinning near $\tau=1.0$ under $T_{lang}$ (Figure 4, right). This indicated that the probe now responded to language perturbations, recovering the desired language sensitivity, albeit with a trade-off in in-distribution R² (dropping to 0.33). Appendix B.3 showed that language sensitivity and in-distribution R² generally emerge and plateau together across mid-range Gemma layers, with layer 10 being a suitable probing point.

#### 4.3. Steerability of the Progress Feature

- **Lack of Propagation:** Intervening on activations by injecting a positive task progress boost ($\tau_{boost}=0.2$) at layer 0 did not result in a propagated change in predicted progress in subsequent layers (Figure 5, left). The predicted progress returned to normal levels beyond layer 0.
- **No Causal Effect on Actions:** Comparing the actions produced by the policy with injected activations ($A_{inj}$) to the ground truth future actions ($A_{GT}$) and the non-injected policy's actions ($A_{policy}$) showed no significant steering. The distance between $A_{inj}$ and $A_{GT}$ remained large and non-zero, comparable to the distance for unsteered actions, while $A_{policy}$ was close to $A_{GT}$ (Figure 5, right).
- **Conclusion:** The task progress feature $\tau$ was found to be observable (decodable) but not linearly steerable via the tested injection method at layer 0.

#### 4.4. Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection

- **Qualitative OOD Signal:** When the robot entered an OOD state (induced by Gaussian noise, color shift, blur, or occlusion), the predicted task progress signal qualitatively stagnated, failing to advance (Figure 6). This deviation provided a clear signal for OOD detection.
- **Cross-Task Generalization:** In evaluating generalization to unseen tasks (3 seen vs. 3 unseen), the progress-based OOD detector ($V_\tau$) demonstrated robustness. Its per-replan AUROC did not degrade across the split (0.796 $\to$ 0.833) (Table 1), consistent with its ID-only training. While SAFE-MLP achieved a higher unseen score (0.935) with OOD supervision, $V_\tau$ was competitive, especially at the per-episode level (0.851 unseen AUROC), outperforming other baselines. Further analysis in Appendix B.1 showed that progress probe generalization (R²) to unseen tasks improved with a higher number of training tasks, saturating around 8 tasks (Figure 7). Per-task breakdowns in Appendix B.2 (Table 5, Table 6) confirmed $V_\tau$'s strong per-episode performance and competitive per-replan performance.
- **Cross-OOD Generalization (Unseen Perturbations):** When tested on OOD types unseen during training (holding out two of four noise modes), $V_\tau$ achieved the best unseen AUROC scores in both per-replan (0.871) and per-episode (0.960) aggregations (Table 2). This performance surpassed both supervised SAFE detectors, despite $V_\tau$ never observing any OOD examples during its training. The advantage of $V_\tau$ was particularly pronounced at the episode level. Appendix B.2 (Table 7, Table 8) provided per-mode breakdowns, showing $V_\tau$'s consistent high performance across unseen modes, especially when aggregating replan scores to the episode level.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several significant contributions and implications for the development and deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models:

1. **Confirmation of Internal Semantic Representations:** The study confirms that VLAs, specifically $\pi_{0.5}$ and its variants, develop rich, linearly readable internal representations of semantic quantities such as task progress. This finding extends the understanding of VLA internals beyond representations of world knowledge to include higher-level, task-conditional states directly tied to behavior.
2. **Lightweight and Interpretable Monitoring:** The linear decodability of task progress offers a lightweight and interpretable pathway for monitoring deployed visuomotor policies. Unlike complex, external diagnostic systems, a simple linear probe can extract a meaningful signal, providing insight into the VLA's internal state regarding task completion.
3. **Effective, Label-Free Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** The progress probe proves to be a competitive and robust OOD detector. Its ability to detect stalled task progress without requiring any OOD supervision makes it a practical solution for real-world deployment where labeled OOD data is scarce or non-existent. The demonstrated strong cross-task and cross-perturbation generalization significantly enhances the reliability of VLAs in novel or adversarial conditions, addressing a critical safety concern.
4. **Diagnostic for Language-Grounding Degradation:** The strong decodability results, particularly with counterfactual training, highlight the probe's utility as a diagnostic tool. It can effectively identify instances where VLA policies may lose language sensitivity post-fine-tuning, offering a mechanism to evaluate and potentially mitigate such performance degradations.
5. **Distinction between Observability and Controllability:** The finding that task progress is observable (decodable) but not linearly steerable via simple interventions exposes a crucial gap between what a VLA *represents* and what it *acts upon*. This distinction is fundamental for mechanistic interpretability, suggesting that the presence of a feature in a model's activations does not automatically imply direct causal control over its behavior. This insight can guide future research into more sophisticated intervention methods or model architectures that allow for effective steering.
6. **Guidance for Future Interpretability Research:** The work suggests that probing for specific, scalar, task-conditional features can complement existing, heavier interpretability toolkits. This approach could be extended to validate progress decodability across different VLA architectures, evaluate the OOD detector on real-robot deployments, and explore the existence and utility of other rich task-conditional signals.

In conclusion, this research not only enhances the interpretability of VLAs by revealing internal representations of task progress but also translates this understanding into a practical and robust tool for monitoring and improving the reliability of these models in dynamic environments.
