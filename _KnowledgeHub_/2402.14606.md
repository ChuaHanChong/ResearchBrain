# Towards Diverse Behaviors: A Benchmark for Imitation Learning with Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Karlsruhe Institute of Technology introduces the D3IL benchmark, a new suite of simulation environments and datasets featuring diverse human demonstrations, along with novel metrics to quantitatively assess a model's ability to learn and replicate multi-modal behaviors. The study evaluates state-of-the-art imitation learning algorithms, finding that transformer-based architectures, particularly when combined with diffusion models, demonstrate improved performance and robustness to data scarcity in learning diverse behaviors.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning (IL) benchmarks often rely on synthetic data, lack sufficient behavioral diversity, or do not emphasize closed-loop sensory feedback, hindering the development of algorithms that can handle the multi-modal nature of human demonstrations.
- Many datasets are collected on real robots, making it difficult to replicate experimental setups and limiting the benchmarking of new IL algorithms.
- A lack of robust, quantitative metrics for assessing an IL model's capacity to effectively capture and replicate diverse behaviors, with many studies relying on qualitative analysis.

## Method

- Introduced the D3IL benchmark, a collection of five challenging Mujoco simulation tasks (Avoiding, Aligning, Pushing, Sorting-X, Stacking-X) designed with multiple human demonstrators to ensure inherent behavioral diversity and the need for closed-loop sensory feedback.
- Proposed two novel, tractable metrics: ==Behavior Entropy== and Conditional Behavior Entropy, to quantitatively measure a policy's ability to learn and reproduce diverse behaviors from a given set of initial states.
- Conducted a comprehensive benchmarking of state-of-the-art imitation learning algorithms, including MLP, Transformer, VAE, IBC, BeT, and Diffusion models, using both state and image observations across the D3IL tasks.

## Results

- Diffusion-based methods, especially those with transformer backbones, were effective in learning diverse behavior while maintaining high task performance, with DDPM-GPT showing a **21%** average improvement in success rate over DDPM-MLP on simpler tasks.
- Transformer-based methods like DDPM-GPT and BESO demonstrated greater robustness to data scarcity, maintaining higher success rates and diversity even with only **10%** of the demonstration data, with BESO achieving a **43%** success rate on the **10%** dataset.
- Velocity control consistently led to significant improvements in both success rate and behavior entropy compared to position control across tested tasks, offering practical guidance for algorithm implementation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transformer-based architectures consistently outperform MLP-based counterparts in terms of success rate and diversity on complex tasks, benefiting from historical inputs and sequential action prediction.
> - Diffusion models (e.g., DDPM, BESO) effectively capture multi-modal behavior distributions and exhibit greater robustness to reduced dataset sizes compared to other methods, suggesting improved generalization and data efficiency.
> - Learning diverse behaviors in tasks with numerous objects (e.g., Sorting-6, Stacking-3) remains challenging for all evaluated methods, highlighting a need for more robust state representations and scaling strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL) leveraging human expert data has been utilized to impart a range of skills to robots and autonomous agents. This approach often involves human experts demonstrating solutions to complex tasks, for instance, through tele-operation interfaces. While IL algorithms have demonstrated success in training robots for various tasks, a significant challenge arises from the inherent diversity in human behavior. This diversity, influenced by individual preferences, varying expertise, noise, and different problem-solving strategies, often results in multi-modal data distributions that current IL algorithms find challenging to process effectively.

Recently, there has been an increased focus on developing methods capable of capturing diverse behaviors. These efforts aim to improve generalization capabilities, enhance skill transfer, and provide advantages in competitive scenarios. However, existing approaches and benchmarks frequently encounter several limitations:
- They are often evaluated on synthetically generated datasets, or datasets with limited behavioral diversity or minimal requirements for closed-loop sensory feedback.
- Many datasets are collected directly on real robot platforms, which restricts benchmarking new algorithms due to the difficulty of replicating intricate real-world setups.
- A notable gap exists in the quantitative assessment of a model's ability to replicate diverse behaviors, with many studies relying predominantly on qualitative analysis.

The authors highlight specific deficiencies in current benchmarks:
- **D4RL and Robomimic:** Primarily designed for offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) benchmarks and do not emphasize behavioral diversity.
- **T-Shape Pushing and Block-Push:** While demonstrating some diversity (e.g., T-Shape has two solutions, Block-Push is diverse), Block-Push uses a scripted policy, which reduces variance and lacks human-like fallback strategies. T-Shape pushing is described as a relatively simple task.
- **Relay Kitchen:** Offers higher task diversity but its behaviors reportedly do not necessitate closed-loop feedback due to limited task variability.
- **RLBench and ManiSkill2:** These large-scale benchmarks for robot learning generate demonstrations through motion planning, consequently lacking the natural diversity characteristic of human behavior.
- **Meta-World:** Focuses on multi-task reinforcement learning but lacks human demonstrations, and most tasks typically have only one solution.

This work positions itself to address these limitations by introducing a new set of benchmark environments and associated datasets specifically designed to evaluate and quantify the learning of diverse, multi-modal human behaviors in simulation, while also providing quantitative metrics for diversity assessment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a comprehensive evaluation framework for imitation learning algorithms. This framework is specifically designed to assess a model’s capacity to learn from and replicate multi-modal data distributions inherent in diverse human demonstrations.

The detailed objectives and motivations include:

1. **Addressing the Challenge of Multi-modal Human Data:** The fundamental motivation stems from the inherent diversity in human behavior, which results in multi-modal data distributions. This poses a "formidable challenge" for existing imitation learning algorithms, as they often struggle to capture and reproduce the full spectrum of demonstrated solutions for a given task.
2. **Developing Novel Benchmark Environments and Datasets (D3IL):** The paper aims to introduce simulation benchmark environments and corresponding Datasets with Diverse human Demonstrations for Imitation Learning (D3IL). These are explicitly designed to evaluate an algorithm's ability to learn multi-modal behaviors. The environments are conceptualized to incorporate multiple sub-tasks, involve the manipulation of multiple objects to enhance behavioral diversity, and necessitate policies that rely on closed-loop sensory feedback. The authors state that other available datasets lack at least one of these challenging properties.
3. **Establishing Tractable Metrics for Diversity Quantification:** A key objective is to address the "open problem" of quantifying a model's capacity to effectively capture and replicate diverse behaviors. The research proposes novel, tractable metrics that can provide quantitative insights into a model's versatility and adaptability, thereby offering a practical means to assess the robustness of imitation learning algorithms.
4. **Benchmarking State-of-the-Art Methods:** The work aims to conduct a thorough evaluation of prominent imitation learning algorithms on the proposed D3IL task suite. This evaluation serves as a benchmark to assess their capabilities in learning diverse behaviors.
5. **Informing Future Algorithm Design:** By analyzing the performance, hyper-parameter sensitivities, and representational choices (e.g., state observations vs. image observations) of current methods on realistic, human-generated datasets within challenging environments, the study seeks to generate insights. These insights are intended to inform and guide the design and advancement of future imitation learning algorithms.
6. **Evaluating Data Efficiency:** The research also aims to analyze how well different imitation learning methods perform when trained with smaller subsets of the demonstration data, addressing the practical challenge of data collection cost.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the development of the D3IL benchmark, the introduction of quantitative diversity metrics, and a comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art imitation learning algorithms.

#### 3.1. D3IL Benchmark Environments and Data Collection

The D3IL benchmark consists of five distinct tasks (T1-T5) simulated within the Mujoco physics engine, utilizing a 7DoF Franka Emika Panda robot. The tasks are designed based on four key principles:
- **Diverse Behavior:** Each task is designed to have multiple valid approaches to successful completion, and these distinct behaviors are explicitly specified for quantification.
- **Multiple Human Demonstrators:** Demonstrations are collected from multiple human operators to capture natural variability in human behavior, including variations in quality and style.
- **Variable Trajectory Lengths:** Tasks feature varying demonstration durations, reflecting real-world scenarios and requiring policies to handle non-uniform data.
- **Task Variations and Closed-Loop Feedback:** To increase complexity and necessitate sensory feedback, tasks incorporate variations in initial conditions (e.g., object positions) requiring the agent to adapt its behavior.

The tasks are:
- **Avoiding (T1):** The robot navigates to a finish line without colliding with six obstacles, without object manipulation. There are 24 distinct avoidance strategies, with 96 demonstrations.
- **Aligning (T2):** The robot pushes a hollow box to a target position/orientation. Solutions include pushing from inside or outside, resulting in 2 behaviors. This task involves complex object manipulation, with 1,000 demonstrations.
- **Pushing (T3):** The robot pushes two blocks to designated target zones. The four possible pushing sequences define the 4 behaviors. This task also involves complex object manipulation and exhibits high variability in trajectory length, with 2,000 demonstrations.
- **Sorting-X (T4):** The robot sorts red and blue blocks to color-matching target boxes. 'X' denotes the number of blocks (tested with X=2, 4, 6). The number of behaviors is determined by the sorting order (e.g., 20 behaviors for X=6). This task is characterized by many objects, high diversity, complex manipulations, and variable trajectory lengths, with 1,600 demonstrations for X=6.
- **Stacking-X (T5):** The robot stacks blocks of different colors in a target zone. The stacking order defines the behaviors (6 for three blocks). This task demands dexterous manipulation (e.g., stacking a blue box upright) and is considered the most challenging, with approximately 1,000 demonstrations.

Demonstrations for T1-T4 were recorded using an Xbox gamepad controlling an inverse kinematics (IK) controller, while T5 used an augmented reality setup for end-effector control. Data collection aimed for an approximately equal number of demonstrations for each defined behavior descriptor.

#### 3.2. Diversity Quantification Metrics

To quantify a policy's ability to learn diverse behaviors, the authors introduce two metrics based on task-specific discrete "behavior descriptors" (β ∈ B):
- **Behavior Entropy (H[π(β)]):** Defined as H[π(β)] = − Σ_β∈B π(β) log_|B| π(β). The logarithm base |B| normalizes the entropy to a range of. An entropy of 0 indicates a policy consistently executing the same behavior, while 1 indicates a policy executing all behaviors with equal probability, matching the design of the collected data.
- **Conditional Behavior Entropy (E_{s0~p(s0)}[H[π(β|s0)]]):** This metric addresses the limitation that high behavior entropy could result from different deterministic behaviors for different initial states. It measures the expected entropy of behavior conditioned on the initial state (s0), approximating the expectation using a Monte Carlo estimate. A high conditional behavior entropy indicates that for the same initial state, multiple behaviors can be achieved.

#### 3.3. Benchmarking State-of-the-Art Algorithms

A range of state-of-the-art imitation learning algorithms were benchmarked, categorized by:
- **History Usage:** MLP backbones (no history) versus Transformer/GPT backbones (using a history of k=5 observations).
- **Prediction Type:** Single action prediction versus future action sequence prediction (action chunking), typically using Transformer Encoder-Decoder structures for the latter.
- **Diversity Modeling:** Deterministic models (BC-MLP, BC-GPT), models using clustering (BeT-MLP, BeT), VAEs (VAE-State, VAE-ACT), implicit models (IBC), and diffusion models (DDPM-MLP, DDPM-GPT, DDPM-ACT, BESO). Variants combining these aspects were also evaluated.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Evaluation Protocol:** Task performance was assessed by success rate, behavior entropy, and conditional behavior entropy. Multiple simulations were run for each policy from randomly sampled initial states (S0).
- **State/Observation Representation:** Experiments used either handcrafted state features or image observations from two camera views (in-hand and front view, 96x96 resolution). A ResNet-18 architecture was used as an image encoder for image-based methods.
- **Model Selection:** Datasets were split into 90% training and 10% validation. Models with the lowest validation loss were selected. Training involved 500 epochs for state representation and 200 for image observations to ensure convergence. Hyperparameters were tuned using Bayesian optimization, and results were averaged over six random seeds.
- **Ablation Studies:** Further analyses were conducted on the impact of history length, prediction horizon, dataset size, hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., number of diffusion steps, action clusters, KL loss scaling), and the choice between position and velocity control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation across D3IL tasks and algorithms yielded several findings regarding the capacity to learn diverse behaviors, the role of architectures, and data efficiency.

#### 4.1. Benchmark Using State Representation (Table 3)

- **Multi-Modal Behavior Learning:**
 - Deterministic models (BC-MLP, BC-GPT) generated only a single solution.
 - VAE-based models (VAE-State, VAE-ACT) could generate different behaviors but often exhibited low behavior entropy, attributed to mode collapse.
 - IBC, BeT, and diffusion-based models demonstrated the ability to learn multi-modal distributions. IBC showed high diversity in several tasks (Avoiding, Pushing, Sorting-2). BeT captured diverse solutions but sometimes with a performance reduction compared to deterministic baselines.
 - Diffusion-based methods, particularly those with transformer backbones, were effective in learning diverse behavior while maintaining high task performance across most tasks. An ablation study indicated that increasing the number of diffusion steps enhanced diversity (entropy) while preserving task performance.
- **Impact of Historical Inputs and Prediction Horizons:**
 - Transformer-based methods (e.g., BC-GPT vs. BC-MLP, DDPM-GPT vs. DDPM-MLP) consistently outperformed their MLP-based counterparts in terms of success rate. DDPM-GPT showed a 21% average improvement in success rate across Avoiding, Aligning, and Pushing compared to DDPM-MLP.
 - Transformer models incorporating historical inputs or prediction horizons demonstrated improved performance on more complex tasks like Sorting-4 and Stacking.
 - However, combining historical information with extended prediction horizons (DDPM-ACT) did not consistently provide substantial benefits over models like DDPM-GPT that did not predict future actions, and in some cases, excessively long histories or prediction horizons could reduce performance and entropy.
- **Scaling to More Complex Tasks:**
 - While state-of-the-art methods performed well on simpler tasks (Avoiding, Aligning, Pushing), a significant performance gap emerged for Sorting and Stacking tasks.
 - Scaling with an increasing number of objects in Sorting tasks (e.g., Sorting-6) proved challenging for all methods, with none achieving satisfactory performance. This was attributed to the increasing complexity of the observation space and task diversity, suggesting a need for more robust state representations.

#### 4.2. Benchmark Using Image Observations (Table 3)

- **State-Based vs. Image-Based Policies:**
 - State representations were effective for tasks requiring precise control (e.g., Aligning) but did not scale well with the number of objects.
 - Image representations showed a notable capacity to handle scenarios with multiple objects (e.g., Sorting-X). However, image-based policies performed worse than state-based policies on tasks demanding precise control like Aligning, indicating challenges in extracting fine-grained details and spatial relationships from images.
- **Sequential Information Across Visual Tasks:**
 - For state-based evaluation, historical inputs and prediction horizons generally improved success rate and entropy. This trend was less consistent for image-based results, where DDPM-GPT showed less improvement or slightly worse performance than DDPM-MLP on some sorting tasks.
 - Conversely, in the most challenging visual task, Stacking, transformer-based models consistently outperformed MLP models. DDPM-ACT showed significant improvements (11% to 40%) in stacking 1, 2, or 3 boxes compared to DDPM-MLP.

#### 4.3. Learning with Less Data (Figure 2, Table 6)

- MLP-based methods (e.g., VAE-State, BeT-MLP) exhibited a substantial performance degradation when trained with smaller datasets, with significant drops in success rate and entropy, becoming nearly non-functional on 10% of the data.
- Transformer-based methods (e.g., DDPM-GPT, BeT) showed greater robustness to reduced data availability, maintaining higher success rates and diversity even with 10% of the original dataset. BESO, in particular, achieved a 43% success rate on the 10% dataset. These findings suggest that transformer architectures generalize effectively with less training data, and diffusion-based methods may contribute to regularizing transformers, making them less data-hungry.

#### 4.4. Additional Findings (Appendix B)

- **Inference Time:** Methods employing (discretized) Langevin dynamics, such as IBC and diffusion-based models, generally exhibited longer inference times. Methods using action chunking (ACT) had lower per-step inference times due to predicting multiple actions simultaneously.
- **Control Type:** Velocity control consistently led to significant improvements in both success rate and entropy compared to position control across tested tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of imitation learning by addressing several challenges in the evaluation and development of algorithms capable of learning diverse human behaviors.

**Addressing Critical Gaps:** The introduction of D3IL directly addresses the need for a standardized and comprehensive benchmark to evaluate how effectively models learn multi-modal behaviors from human demonstrations. This is crucial given that existing benchmarks often lack the necessary properties to capture the richness and variability inherent in human expertise.

**Novel Benchmark and Dataset (D3IL):** D3IL offers a new suite of challenging simulation environments and corresponding datasets specifically designed to reflect key aspects of complex human tasks. These include multiple sub-tasks, manipulation of multiple objects, and the requirement for closed-loop sensory feedback. This design ensures that the benchmark environments are more realistic and demanding, setting a new standard for evaluating imitation learning algorithms.

**Quantitative Diversity Metrics:** The proposed tractable metrics, Behavior Entropy and Conditional Behavior Entropy, provide a quantitative means to assess a model’s capacity to acquire and reproduce diverse behaviors. This moves beyond qualitative assessments, enabling more rigorous and objective comparisons of algorithms, and offering valuable insights into their versatility and adaptability.

**Comprehensive Benchmarking of State-of-the-Art Methods:** The thorough evaluation of a wide array of current imitation learning algorithms on D3IL provides a valuable reference point. This analysis highlights the strengths and weaknesses of different architectural choices (MLP vs. Transformers) and diversity modeling strategies (VAE, clustering, implicit, diffusion models) in the context of multi-modal human data.

**Guiding Future Research and Development:** The findings offer specific insights that can inform the design of future imitation learning algorithms:
- **Architectural Efficacy:** The consistent superior performance of transformer-based architectures, particularly when integrated with diffusion models, suggests their effectiveness in capturing diverse behaviors while maintaining high success rates.
- **Scalability Challenges:** The identified performance gaps in highly complex tasks with many objects (e.g., Sorting-6, Stacking-3) underscore the need for further research into learning compact and resilient state representations that can scale effectively.
- **Data Efficiency:** The observation that transformer-based methods, especially diffusion models like BESO, exhibit greater tolerance for smaller datasets, is significant for real-world applications where collecting extensive human demonstrations can be resource-intensive. This indicates potential directions for developing more data-efficient algorithms.
- **Control Mechanisms:** The demonstrated benefits of velocity control over position control for improving both success rate and entropy provide practical guidance for algorithm implementation.

In summary, this work provides both the tools (D3IL benchmark and metrics) and the initial insights from a rigorous evaluation, thereby facilitating standardized comparisons and accelerating progress in developing more capable and versatile imitation learning algorithms for robotic systems.
