# Humanoid Hanoi: Investigating Shared Whole-Body Control for Skill-Based Box Rearrangement

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Oregon State University developed a control architecture for humanoid robots that enables robust, long-horizon box rearrangement by routing independently learned skills through a shared, task-agnostic whole-body controller. This framework, combined with a data aggregation procedure for WBC coverage expansion, achieved a 49% complete-task success rate in a challenging simulation benchmark under dynamics randomization and a 40% success rate on a physical Digit V3 robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face challenges in robustly composing independently learned loco-manipulation skills for long-horizon tasks, leading to accumulated errors and brittle execution.
- Current modular architectures often rely on skill-specific low-level controllers or complex transition mechanisms, limiting scalability and the flexible reuse of skills.
- Shared whole-body controllers struggle to maintain robustness when new skills introduce state and command distributions not covered in their initial training data.

## Method

![[2602.13850-method.png]]
*Overview of the hierarchical control system. High-level skills produce directives for a shared, task-agnostic Whole-Body Controller (WBC), which generates joint setpoints for a high-frequency PD controller. The WBC is improved through an offline data aggregation process.*

- A ==modular control architecture== where independently trained high-level loco-manipulation skills (e.g., GoTo, Pickup, Place) execute through a single, shared, ==task-agnostic Whole-Body Controller (WBC)==.
- A ==data aggregation== procedure that expands the WBC's training distribution by collecting and incorporating command sequences from new skill rollouts, then retraining the WBC with its original task-agnostic objective.
- A novel **Humanoid Hanoi** benchmark that involves stacking three boxes with specific rules, designed to evaluate ==long-horizon skill composition== and expose cumulative errors in humanoid box rearrangement.

## Results

- The proposed "Extended" method achieved a **49%** complete-task success rate in the Humanoid Hanoi simulation under dynamics randomization, significantly outperforming baseline adaptation methods.
- Individual skill evaluations showed the Extended method improved success rates and stability for Pickup and Place skills, particularly for challenging low box heights, demonstrating better robustness than skill-specific adaptations.
- Hardware experiments on a Digit V3 robot completed **40%** of Humanoid Hanoi tasks, highlighting the practical viability of the approach while identifying perception, navigation, and precise physical interaction as primary limitations for real-world deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, shared, task-agnostic whole-body controller can serve as a robust low-level interface for diverse loco-manipulation skills, simplifying composition and avoiding skill-specific controller adaptations.
> - Expanding the shared WBC's training data with command distributions generated by new skills during closed-loop execution significantly enhances its robustness and generalizability without altering its fundamental tracking objective.
> - Long-horizon, multi-skill benchmarks like Humanoid Hanoi are essential for uncovering systemic failure modes that arise from accumulated errors and interactions between skills, which are often missed in short-horizon evaluations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has made progress in learning complex loco-manipulation behaviors. Much of this progress has focused on training task-specific policies or pipelines using methods such as teleoperation, motion capture demonstrations, or reinforcement learning. These systems often excel at specific, predefined tasks, like picking up an object and walking, but typically lack the flexibility to arbitrarily resequence or recombine implicit sub-behaviors (skills) for solving diverse, long-horizon tasks.

Compositional architectures aim to address this limitation by learning reusable skills that can be flexibly sequenced. However, previous learning-based humanoid research has shown limited demonstrations of robust, flexible sequencing of independently learned loco-manipulation skills over extended durations, often involving tens of skill invocations. Some work, such as, has explored learning and composing separate whole-body policies for pickup, locomotion, and put-down. However, such designs can necessitate additional transition mechanisms (e.g., specialized transition training or runtime interpolation) to ensure stability when switching between distinct skill-specific low-level controllers.

This paper positions itself within this context by investigating a control architecture that aims to improve robustness and long-horizon task success for humanoid box rearrangement. It specifically addresses the challenge of brittle skill composition and the lack of exploitation of shared whole-body structure. By proposing a framework where independently trained skills execute through a *shared, task-agnostic whole-body controller (WBC)*, the work seeks to provide a unified low-level control interface, simplifying skill composition and enabling reuse without altering the underlying closed-loop control structure at skill boundaries. The paper also identifies a key challenge in this shared-WBC paradigm: maintaining robustness when new skills induce state and command distributions that differ from the WBC's pretraining data. To address this, it explores a data aggregation procedure for WBC coverage expansion, differentiating its approach from skill-specific modifications of low-level controllers common in prior work (e.g., residual policies or per-skill fine-tuning). The introduction of the "Humanoid Hanoi" benchmark further contributes to the landscape by providing a standardized, long-horizon evaluation environment for humanoid rearrangement tasks, designed to stress repeated skill reuse and expose accumulated execution errors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a control architecture for humanoid box rearrangement that facilitates robust, long-horizon execution by sequencing reusable skills through a shared, task-agnostic whole-body controller (WBC). The motivation stems from several identified challenges in current humanoid control approaches:

- **Long-Horizon Execution Robustness:** Long-horizon tasks like box rearrangement require reliable composition of locomotion and manipulation skills over extended periods. In practice, small errors accumulate, leading to failure modes that are often not apparent in isolated skill evaluations or short-horizon demonstrations. The research aims to improve the resilience of humanoid robots to these cumulative errors.
- **Brittleness of Skill Composition:** While modular, skill-based architectures are appealing for long-horizon tasks, composing independently trained skills can be brittle. This brittleness arises because skill composition can induce state and command distributions that deviate from those encountered during isolated skill training. Additionally, switching between skill-specific low-level controllers or objectives can alter closed-loop dynamics at skill boundaries, potentially causing transient instability. The authors are motivated to mitigate these issues.
- **Lack of Shared Whole-Body Structure Exploitation:** Training skills in isolation often leads to redundant effort and reduced scalability, as each skill might develop its own low-level control mechanisms without leveraging common whole-body capabilities. A unified control interface is sought to promote skill reuse and simplify the addition of new skills.
- **Shared-WBC Robustness Challenges:** While a shared, task-agnostic WBC offers advantages in skill composition, it presents its own challenge: maintaining robustness as the skill library grows and composed execution induces novel state and command distributions. The pretrained WBC may not have sufficient coverage for these new operational regimes, leading to degraded closed-loop performance, especially under disturbances or sim-to-real transfer. The research is motivated to address this coverage limitation without resorting to skill-dependent low-level control modifications.
- **Need for Comprehensive Benchmarking:** Existing work often lacks task-oriented rearrangement benchmarks with explicit long-horizon success and precision metrics, or a focus on skill reuse across varied instance distributions. The authors are motivated to introduce a benchmark that specifically stresses these aspects, exposing off-nominal robot and box configurations induced by accumulated execution error.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
1. **Investigate a shared-WBC skill composition architecture:** Develop and evaluate a modular framework where all independently trained skills execute through a single, always-on, task-agnostic WBC.
2. **Develop a method for shared-WBC coverage expansion:** Propose and test a data aggregation procedure to augment WBC training with rollouts from closed-loop skill execution under domain randomization, aiming to improve robustness against skill-induced distribution shifts.
3. **Introduce and evaluate a long-horizon benchmark:** Establish "Humanoid Hanoi" as a challenging benchmark for humanoid box rearrangement, using it to quantify the benefits of the proposed shared-WBC approach and conduct a failure mode analysis in both simulation and hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a modular, skill-based control architecture that routes independently trained high-level skills through a shared, task-agnostic whole-body controller (WBC). This system is evaluated on a novel long-horizon box rearrangement benchmark called Humanoid Hanoi.

**System Architecture:**
The proposed architecture consists of three main layers:
1. **High-Level Skills:** A library of reusable loco-manipulation skills (GoTo, GoTo-with-Box, Pickup, Place) is implemented as independent policies. These policies map robot observations and skill-specific conditioning inputs (e.g., box pose, target poses) to task-level commands, not direct robot actuation.
2. **Shared Whole-Body Controller (WBC):** All skill commands are fed into a single, always-on, task-agnostic WBC. This WBC, based on the pre-trained Masked Humanoid Controller (MHC), takes the robot's proprioceptive state and a masked motion directive from the active skill. It then outputs joint-level PD setpoints for all actuated joints. The MHC utilizes a masking mechanism to allow different skills to constrain different body parts while the WBC dynamically completes unspecified degrees of freedom.
3. **Low-Level PD Controller:** A fixed-gain PD controller on the robot tracks the joint-level PD setpoints produced by the WBC at a high frequency (2 kHz).

**Skill Learning:**
Each high-level skill is a two-layer LSTM policy operating at 50 Hz. They are trained using reinforcement learning (RL) with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in a MuJoCo simulation environment for the Digit V3 humanoid robot. Robustness is enhanced through dynamics randomization (Table I), which includes variations in body mass, joint damping, CoM position, friction coefficients, and communication delay. Box properties (mass, size, displacement, yaw rotation, friction) are also randomized. Rewards are generally weighted sums of bounded exponential terms.

- **Pickup Skill:** Trained to grasp boxes of varying pose, dimensions, and mass, and return to a stable standing position while holding the box. It uses a phased curriculum (approach, contact, lift, stand) with distance-based trajectory tracking, grasp, stability, and smoothness rewards.
- **Place Skill:** Trained to set a held box at an upright, yaw-only target pose while maintaining whole-body stability. Reference motions are generated by reversing successful pickup rollouts. It uses privileged critic inputs and rewards for box target pose, box rotation, hand contact, elbow position, and base stability.
- **GoTo Skills:** Two variants: GoTo (unloaded) and GoTo-with-Box (carrying a box). Both drive the robot to SE(2) targets using a constellation reward. GoTo-with-Box additionally observes box dimensions and pose relative to the base and includes upper-body targets for stabilization. Rewards encourage stable foot pose, smooth actions, low effort, and box-specific terms for transport.

**Shared WBC Coverage Expansion (Extended Method):**
A core aspect of the methodology is addressing the robustness challenge of the shared WBC. The authors propose a data aggregation procedure to expand the WBC's training distribution:
1. **Initial WBC:** The pretrained MHC (Base) is trained on a reference trajectory set `R0` (AMASS dataset and trajectory-optimization-derived motions).
2. **Rollout Collection:** When new high-level skills (`πi`) are trained, the hierarchical system executes these skills with the current shared WBC. Successful closed-loop directive sequences (targets and masks issued to the WBC) are recorded, forming a skill-specific reference set `Si`.
3. **Data Aggregation:** The new skill references are incrementally added to the overall reference set: `Ri = Ri-1 ∪ Si`.
4. **WBC Retraining:** The shared WBC is then retrained on the expanded distribution `D[Ri]` using its original task-agnostic tracking objective, along with domain randomization and disturbance injection. This process aims to robustify the WBC in the parts of trajectory space that the new skills rely on without altering its architecture or requiring skill-specific objectives.

**Humanoid Hanoi Benchmark:**
A novel benchmark inspired by the Tower of Hanoi puzzle is introduced to evaluate long-horizon box rearrangement.
- **Task:** Move and stack three boxes according to Tower of Hanoi rules (one box at a time, fixed box ordering by size/index, higher-ordered boxes not on lower-ordered ones). It requires 7 sequential moves, each involving GoTo, Pickup, GoTo-with-Box, and Place skills.
- **Randomization:** Workspace radius ([1.5, 2.5] m), tower locations (on circumference), box sizes (small, medium, large categories), box mass ([0.5, 3.0] kg), and sliding friction coefficient ([0.5, 0.7]) are randomized per episode. Standard dynamics randomization is applied to the robot.
- **Evaluation:** 100 randomized episodes are run. Metrics include complete-task success rate and final box pose error relative to the ideal target tower for successful trials.
- **Failure Analysis:** A qualitative analysis categorizes dominant error modes.

**Approaches Compared:**
The research compares four WBC adaptation approaches:
1. **Base (Frozen MHC):** Uses the pretrained MHC without any skill-specific adaptation.
2. **Finetune:** Adaptively fine-tunes the WBC separately for each skill using the skill's RL reward (not the MHC tracking objective). This creates skill-specific WBCs.
3. **Residual:** Freezes the pretrained MHC and learns a skill-specific residual policy, optimized with the corresponding skill's RL reward.
4. **Extended (Ours):** The proposed method, maintaining a single shared WBC and expanding its coverage via data aggregation from skill rollouts, then retraining with the original task-agnostic MHC objective.

**Hardware Setup:**
Experiments are performed on a Digit V3 humanoid robot. Perception for box pose estimation and robot localization relies on AprilTags detected by onboard Intel RealSense D445 RGB-D cameras. An Intel NUC performs onboard computation, communicating with the robot via UDP. IMU odometry is used when AprilTags are not visible and is reset after each box pickup to mitigate drift.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents simulation and hardware results evaluating the shared-WBC approach, its coverage expansion mechanism, and the Humanoid Hanoi benchmark.

**Individual Skill Evaluation (Simulation):**
Table II presents the performance of Pickup and Place skills across various box heights, randomized box parameters, and under dynamics randomization.
- All three adaptation methods (Finetune, Residual, Extended) generally outperformed the **Base (Frozen MHC)** WBC in terms of success rate and stability metrics (CoM error, foot force distribution).
- The **Extended (Ours)** method achieved the best or second-best performance across most metrics, demonstrating the largest gains in stability. For low box heights (< 0.2 m), where balance margins are tight, Extended showed a notable improvement in success rate (e.g., Pickup: 95.03% vs. Base 85.00%, Finetune 89.43%, Residual 89.43%; Place: 91.50% vs. Base 85.06%, Finetune 90.40%, Residual 86.93%) and significantly reduced CoM error.
- Extended achieved these gains by optimizing the task-agnostic MHC objective, contrasting with Finetune and Residual, which optimize skill-specific task rewards.
- Table III indicates that Extended preserved or improved upper-body tracking performance on trajectories from the baseline training dataset compared to Base WBC.

**Humanoid Hanoi Evaluation (Simulation):**
Fig. 4 shows cumulative task success rates for 100 random Humanoid Hanoi instances.
- **Without Dynamics Randomization (No DR):**
 - **Extended** achieved approximately 70% complete-task success, outperforming all baselines. While its early-stage survival was slightly lower than Residual and Base, it degraded more slowly over the long horizon, yielding the highest overall completion rate.
 - **Finetune** showed sharp drops in survival under long-horizon composition, resulting in worse complete-task success than Base, suggesting limited generalization when skills are chained.
- **With Dynamics Randomization (DR):**
 - **Extended** maintained a substantial margin over other methods throughout the horizon, achieving 49% complete-task success compared to Finetune (21%), Residual (29%), and Base (22%). This indicates improved robustness to environmental variability and skill-induced distribution shift.
- **Placement Accuracy (Table IV):** For successful Humanoid Hanoi trials, all approaches achieved similar final box position and yaw errors, with no statistically significant differences. The authors note that Extended often succeeded in stabilizing and stacking even when the pose deviated from the nominal target, meaning its reported errors correspond to a broader and potentially more challenging subset of successful executions.

**Humanoid Hanoi Analysis of Failure Modes (Simulation):**
Fig. 6 illustrates failure counts across skills.
- **Dominant Failure:** Most failures occurred during the **Place** skill, attributed to tight balance margins, stack stability constraints, and precise end-effector control requirements.
- **Placement-Induced Failures:** Common issues included unintended box pitch rotation leading to unstable support and stack collapse, and robot contact with the stack during post-placement recovery steps.
- **Accumulated Upstream Errors:** Failures often originated from preceding skills:
 - Off-centered grasps during Pickup introduced residual box pose errors.
 - Residual localization errors after GoTo-with-Box reduced placement margin, as the robot transitions to Place within a 5 cm tolerance.
- **Object-Unaware Locomotion:** GoTo skills were not object-aware, leading to collisions with the stack during backward walking or in-place rotation after a Place action.

**Hardware Experiments (Digit V3 Robot):**
Table V reports results on three fixed Humanoid Hanoi workspace configurations (C1-C3) with 5 trials each.
- The overall complete-task success rate was 40% across 15 trials.
- The average number of completed box moves per trial (including failures) was 4.2. Successful trials took approximately 5 minutes.
- **Dominant Failure Categories:**
 - **Perception/State Estimation (2 trials):** Primarily AprilTag perception failures during pickup.
 - **Navigation/Alignment at Skill Handoffs (2 trials):** Yaw misalignment causing issues during skill transitions.
 - **Physical Interaction during Manipulation/Placement (3 trials):** Box flipping during Place due to unintended contact, and a box being kicked during placement/stand-up.
 - Other failures included large lateral localization error causing unsuccessful grasp (1) and an IMU fault degrading walking and leading to tag loss (1).
- Hardware robustness was observed to be limited by tag robustness, base-pose accuracy entering Place, and the lack of clearance-aware body motion during placement and recovery.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for long-horizon humanoid box rearrangement that emphasizes a shared, task-agnostic whole-body controller (WBC) for skill composition. This approach has several implications and potential impacts:

- **Scalability and Skill Reuse:** By demonstrating that multiple, independently learned high-level skills (locomotion, pickup, place) can execute through a single, consistent low-level WBC, the work supports a more scalable approach to developing complex humanoid behaviors. This architecture simplifies skill composition and promotes reuse, as adding new skills primarily involves defining new high-level policies rather than designing or adapting multiple low-level control systems.
- **Robustness in Long-Horizon Tasks:** The introduction of a data aggregation procedure for expanding WBC training coverage addresses a critical challenge in shared control systems—maintaining robustness as new skills induce novel state and command distributions. The observed improvements in simulation, particularly under dynamics randomization and at challenging configurations (e.g., low box heights), suggest a method for enhancing the reliability of humanoid systems operating over extended durations, where accumulated errors are prominent. This is significant for real-world deployment where conditions are variable.
- **Simplified Skill Integration:** The shared-WBC paradigm avoids the complexities associated with switching between skill-specific low-level controllers, such as managing transient instabilities at skill boundaries or developing specialized transition policies. This simplification can reduce development effort and improve the predictability of composite behaviors.
- **Contribution to Benchmarking:** The Humanoid Hanoi benchmark provides a standardized and challenging environment for evaluating long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks for humanoids. Its design, which stresses repeated skill reuse and exposes accumulated errors, is valuable for comparing different control architectures and driving progress in the field. The public release of the benchmark will facilitate further research.
- **Identification of Future Research Directions:** The detailed failure analysis, both in simulation and on hardware, offers clear directions for future work. Identified limitations, such as object-unaware locomotion, issues with placement stability, and reliance on external perception markers, highlight areas where advancements in perception, motion planning, and control are needed to further improve humanoid autonomy and precision. For instance, incorporating onboard perception and developing clearance-aware body motions could significantly enhance robustness.
- **Practical Applicability:** While significant headroom for improvement exists, the demonstration of fully autonomous box rearrangement on a physical Digit V3 humanoid robot, successfully completing complex multi-step sequences over several minutes, indicates the practical viability of the shared-WBC framework for real-world manipulation tasks. This suggests potential applications in logistics, manufacturing, or disaster response scenarios where humanoids could perform complex manipulation sequences.
