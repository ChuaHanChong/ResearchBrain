# Learning to Act under Visual Interruptions with Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> MAIL-Bench is a paired, closed-loop benchmark on 18 RoboCasa365 tasks that cuts camera roles mid-episode, timed relative to each policy's own healthy completion. Its companion method, MINT, fine-tunes VLAs with masked camera inputs so they keep acting when views go missing. At inference it fills in missing views with optical flow or an action-conditioned world model, and reliability gates withdraw those predictions once they drift. MINT raises GR00T N1.5's MAIL score by up to 0.26 and works on a real AgiBot G2.

## Problem

- VLA benchmarks and robustness suites assume every camera stream stays available, testing only appearance, pose or image-quality changes, never the complete loss of a view.
- Existing missing-camera evaluations drop views from the episode start or score open loop, so they miss persistent mid-episode loss during closed-loop execution.
- Masked or dropout training lets policies act without a view but cannot recover its information, while world-model view prediction builds up errors as the scene changes.

## Method

- **MAIL-Bench** interrupts the wrist, third-person or all visual roles at 30/45/60% of each policy's healthy completion time, scoring retained success conditional on healthy success.
- Availability-masked fine-tuning marks unavailable cameras and drops their visual tokens from attention, training under a curriculum of persistent and intermittent missing patterns without changing the architecture.
- Reliability-gated view prediction uses ==Farnebäck optical-flow== extrapolation for wrist or all-vision loss, and a ==LoRA==-tuned ==PersistWorld== model for third-person loss, irreversibly withdrawn when checks such as wrist consistency fail.

## Results

- On GR00T N1.5, MINT raises the MAIL score by **+0.229**, **+0.260** and **+0.168** across pick-and-place, small-appliance and fixture task categories; π0.5 gains are smaller, **+0.154**, **+0.119** and **+0.023**.
- Healthy success also rises, e.g. GR00T small-appliance tasks go from **0.290** to **0.450**, so masked training costs nothing on normal execution.
- On a real AgiBot G2, successes out of **40** rise from **17** to **29** without the third-person view, **7** to **20** without the wrist and **2** to **10** without vision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Which view is lost matters more than how many: losing one wrist camera can hurt more than losing both third-person cameras.
> - Robustness and reconstruction complement each other: masked training controls the drop after early loss, while prediction helps most when a surviving view can check the generated one.
> - Generated observations need a fallback: monitoring a predicted view against a still-observed view, then withdrawing it, guards against world-model drift in closed loop.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

When a robot is partway through a manipulation task, one of its cameras can stop sending images. Picture a robot that must put a cube in a drawer and close it while its head camera stops showing the scene. The control policy still has to keep acting without that view. Camera loss removes information, so the problem is more than an image that is harder to interpret. MINT handles this in two steps. It prepares the policy to act with missing camera inputs. It then temporarily supplies a predicted view when one can be generated and passes reliability checks. In the authors' demo, the head view is interrupted after 6.2 seconds. The robot continues with the wrist camera still available, puts the cube in the drawer, and closes it.

Three lanes: the real head-camera view, hidden from the policy after 6.2 seconds; the head-view stream served to the policy, with real frames before interruption and accepted world-model predictions afterward (blue), and no frame where none is accepted; and the wrist-camera view, which remains available.

### 2. A missing view is not a bad image

The wrist camera looks close to the gripper and object, and third-person cameras give a broader workspace view. The MAIL-Bench benchmark removes a camera stream during a closed-loop rollout, rather than perturbing an image while continuing to deliver it. The loss is persistent and can begin mid-task, and the remaining cameras continue streaming.

The benchmark uses 18 RoboCasa365 tasks with 50 fixed scenes per task, 900 total. A scene is eligible for interruption evaluation only if that policy first completes it with all cameras available. The scene is then replayed from the same initial state and policy seed. A camera role is interrupted at 30%, 45%, or 60% of that policy's own healthy completion time and stays missing. No black or frozen image is substituted.

Each interruption score is the fraction of the same policy's healthy successes that remain successful under that interruption. The task score weights the healthy result and nine interruption conditions equally, and MAIL-Bench averages across all 18 tasks. The score therefore mixes competence with retention of already demonstrated capability.

### 3. Train the fallback, then gate the prediction

First, the policy is fine-tuned on healthy and missing-camera examples. A missing view is marked unavailable and its visual tokens are excluded, so the policy learns to act from the views and proprioception that remain.

Second, a prediction route is selected:

- For missing wrist input or loss of all vision, Farnebäck optical flow extrapolates a short horizon from the last two frames.
- For missing third-person views, an action-conditioned world model takes the surviving wrist view and executed actions as inputs.

Route-specific diagnostics check each prediction. Predictions are accepted only while the checks pass. Once a route is rejected, its prediction is withdrawn for the rest of that episode, and the policy falls back to the missing-input behavior it learned in training. This gating does not guarantee that predictions are correct.

MINT's proposed mechanism. A world-model route handles third-person loss, and an optical-flow route starting from the last two wrist frames handles wrist or all-vision loss. Each route passes through a reliability gate. The schematic shows the design, not evidence that it works.

### 4. What the comparisons establish

On the overall MAIL-Bench score, GR00T N1.5 rises from 0.246 without MINT to 0.462 with MINT. The paired difference is +0.216, with a 95% interval of [+0.190, +0.240]. The authors describe MINT as outperforming the base models in "almost all" interruption conditions. For π₀.₅, the W30 difference is −0.010 (interval [−0.068, +0.044]), so that comparison does not establish an improvement.

Two GR00T N1.5 ablations separate the stages:

- **Clean FT + prediction** omits missing-view training but keeps prediction. MINT scores 0.462 versus 0.351 (+0.111, [+0.087, +0.133]). This supports the value of missing-view training under matched prediction.
- **Missing-view FT** disables generated observations. MINT scores 0.462 versus 0.438 (+0.024, [+0.008, +0.044]). This supports an added contribution from prediction with the policy weights held fixed.

GR00T N1.5 ablation for wrist, third-person, and all-vision loss. Scores are averaged over the three task categories, and H is the healthy-input score. The lines have no plotted uncertainty intervals.

On a physical AgiBot G2, the authors fine-tuned an availability-aware GR00T N1.5 policy on 203 successful teleoperated episodes across four tabletop tasks. They report that MINT beats the base model in the missing-input settings across these tasks, with no healthy-setting degradation.

### 5. Recovery has a cost

MINT does not make lost visual information available indefinitely. It learns to proceed without a view and withdraws a prediction if its checks fail. Prediction also adds cost: healthy per-step wall-clock time is unchanged, but the world-model route adds 9.2 GiB of GPU memory and about 100 TFLOPs per generation during third-person loss. The simulator evidence covers 18 RoboCasa365 tasks with persistent camera losses.
