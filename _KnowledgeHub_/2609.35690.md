# Agent Priors-guided Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> APPL (Agent Priors-guided Policy Learning) uses each skill policy's structural prior both as a training inductive bias and as a runtime interface. A construction agent segments demonstrations into skills, then proposes, trains and verifies several prior-specific Diffusion Policies per skill. A runtime agent reads their documented priors to select and compose them, reaching 89.6% MetaWorld OOD success from two demonstrations and solving 8 of 16 unseen ManiSkill compositions.

## Problem

- Task-level composers in task-and-motion planning and LLM-agent systems see skills only through names, instructions, or symbolic operators, which omit where each learned policy actually succeeds.
- Structural priors for few-demonstration policies, like object-centric or relative-frame representations, are hand-chosen per task and used only in training, never by the system composing them.
- Closest precedents fix structure in advance: SymSkill to relative-frame dynamical-system skills, LGA and KALM to one prior per task, while diffusion policies extrapolate poorly out of distribution.

## Method

- ==Construction agent== segments complete demonstrations into reusable skills with deliberately overlapping ==handoff windows==, broadening each successor's training support around states its predecessor actually reaches.
- Per-skill prior proposal: the agent proposes several ==structural priors==, implements each as representation, ==action frame==, or ==auxiliary loss==, trains one ==Diffusion Policy== each, verifies them, then adds a ==feedback-driven prior==.
- ==Runtime interface== of prior description, handoff conditions, training support, and verification evidence lets a frozen ==runtime agent== in **APPL** choose policies, arguments, durations, and stop conditions to compose skills each episode.

## Results

- MetaWorld six-task OOD success with two demonstrations: **89.6%** for APPL versus **37.9%** for a fixed relational prior and **29.0%** for vanilla Diffusion Policy; margins over relational prior **37-52 points**.
- ManiSkill long-horizon tasks: **50.0%** motion-OOD success versus **10.0%** for full-task Diffusion Policy and SinglePrior, **92.5%** task-level OOD, and **8/16** unseen compositions versus **6/16** for an agent-called VLA.
- Hiding interface information from the runtime agent drops motion-OOD from **50.0%** to **20.0%**, task-level from **92.5%** to **65.0%**, and compositions from **8/16** to **2/16**; a fixed-order rule solves **1/16**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training-time inductive biases are reusable knowledge: exposing them to downstream planners as applicability hypotheses improves skill selection beyond what skill names or success scores convey.
> - Keeping several policy implementations per skill with different priors, then selecting per situation, beats committing to one hand-chosen abstraction when demonstrations are few.
> - Verification only on demonstrated entry states can misrank policies under shift, as peg assembly fell from 8/8 to 1/8; verification should match deployment states and handoff rules.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a central problem in robot learning from demonstrations: enabling robots to perform tasks that differ from their training examples while using only a small number of demonstrations. The paper focuses on two related forms of generalization.

First, **skill generalization** concerns whether an individual learned behavior, such as grasping, opening a drawer, or placing an object, remains effective when objects are moved, when the robot starts from a different configuration, or when the preceding behavior leaves the robot in an unfamiliar state. Second, **compositional generalization** concerns whether a robot can recombine existing skills in new sequences or execute only part of a demonstrated task.

Existing research typically treats these problems separately. Diffusion policies and other imitation-learning methods can learn low-level behaviors from demonstrations, but their behavior outside the demonstrated state distribution is often difficult to predict. Task and motion planning systems provide high-level composition through symbolic preconditions and effects, but these abstractions are frequently hand-designed or learned independently from the representations used by the low-level policy. Vision-language-action models provide broader task-level interfaces, but their descriptions often specify only what a policy does, rather than the physical conditions under which it is expected to work.

The paper is situated at the intersection of several research directions: few-shot imitation learning, diffusion-based robot policies, skill discovery and chaining, task-and-motion planning, symbolic abstraction, and language-model-based robot agents. Its main distinction is that it treats the **structural prior used to train a policy as part of the policy’s runtime interface**. A structural prior may state, for example, that a manipulation should depend on the object-relative pose rather than on absolute world coordinates. The same assumption is used to shape the policy during training and to inform the high-level agent about where the policy may generalize.

This differs from methods that use a single fixed representation, such as a predetermined relational frame, and from methods that retain only a skill name or task description. The paper also differs from agent-based policy-design systems that use a language model to choose a training representation but discard that design after training. APPL retains the design and exposes it during execution.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper’s main objective is to improve robot performance when demonstrations are few and deployment conditions differ from training in both spatial configuration and task structure.

The authors identify an information gap between the low-level and high-level components of a robot system. During training, the low-level policy is shaped by assumptions about which features of the scene matter. For example, a policy may learn to move relative to the manipulated object, the destination, or the robot’s current tool-center point. However, after training, a high-level composer may see only a name such as “place object” or a generic instruction. It therefore lacks information about the situations in which a particular policy implementation is likely to succeed.

The paper proposes that this lost information should be preserved. The structural prior serves two roles:

1. **Coverage role:** The prior influences the policy representation, action parameterization, architecture, or auxiliary objectives. It is intended to enlarge the policy’s success region beyond the demonstrated states in a particular way.
2. **Selection role:** The prior is described in the policy interface and informs the runtime agent about expected invariances, limitations, training support, handoff conditions, and verification results.

The authors also motivate maintaining multiple policies for the same skill. There may be no single representation that generalizes well across all deployment states. An object-relative policy may be useful when an object is displaced, whereas a TCP-relative correction policy may be more effective when the robot enters the skill from a different handoff configuration. Retaining alternatives allows the runtime agent to select among them.

A related objective is to improve skill transitions. Skills often fail not because their individual motions are inherently inadequate, but because a successor policy receives a state outside the entry distribution represented in its demonstrations. APPL addresses this by overlapping adjacent skill segments during training. The overlap includes portions of the predecessor’s end and the successor’s beginning, thereby exposing the successor to states close to actual handoffs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. System Structure

APPL consists of an offline **construction agent** and an online **runtime agent**. The construction agent receives complete, initially unsegmented demonstrations, task goals, and information about the observation and action interfaces. It produces a frozen library of skill-specific policies and accompanying interfaces.

The construction process has four main stages:

1. Demonstrations are segmented into reusable skills.
2. Several structural priors are proposed for each skill.
3. A separate diffusion policy is implemented, trained, and verified for each prior.
4. Each policy is documented with an interface that the runtime agent can inspect.

The resulting library can contain multiple policies for the same skill. These policies have the same broad functional purpose but differ in their structural assumptions and expected generalization behavior.

#### 3.2. Skill Segmentation and Handoff Overlap

The construction agent identifies distinct physical responsibilities in each demonstration, such as opening a drawer, transferring an object, or inserting a peg. Adjacent skill segments intentionally overlap around their transitions. This means that a successor skill is trained not only on nominal starts but also on states reached shortly before or during the handoff from its predecessor.

The overlap does not add new demonstrations. Instead, it reuses actions and observations already present in the complete trajectories. The intended effect is to broaden the successor policy’s effective initiation support and reduce errors caused by timing differences at skill boundaries.

#### 3.3. Prior Proposal and Policy Training

For each skill, the agent proposes multiple complementary priors. Examples include object-relative frames, destination-relative frames, and corrections relative to the current TCP pose. Priors can alter the policy’s input representation, action coordinates, learned encoders, or auxiliary losses.

Each candidate is implemented as a conditional Diffusion Policy using a shared diffusion-policy backbone. Demonstrated Cartesian or native actions are transformed into the coordinate system specified by the prior, and predicted actions are transformed back into commands for execution. In the ManiSkill experiments, a shared inverse-kinematics module converts predicted end-effector targets into joint commands.

The policy objective combines behavior-cloning loss with any prior-specific auxiliary loss. The prior is therefore not merely descriptive: it determines how the policy is trained and what behavior it is encouraged to represent outside the training examples.

In Experiment 1, the construction agent initially proposes three candidates for each task and demonstration count. After development results are available, it creates a fourth candidate informed by observed failures. Candidate selection is based on development out-of-distribution success, not on the hidden test set.

#### 3.4. Verification and Policy Documentation

Before policies are frozen, each candidate is tested from the demonstrated entry state of each corresponding skill segment. It is executed for 1.5 times the segment duration and evaluated against the demonstrated exit condition. The construction agent uses these results to produce usage notes and, in the second experiment, to design an additional policy.

Each policy interface contains four main information categories:

- A **prior description**, explaining the structural assumption, expected invariances, and limitations.
- A **handoff description**, specifying entry and exit conditions and the states represented near neighboring skill boundaries.
- A **support description**, summarizing demonstrated ranges and training-start conditions.
- **Verification evidence**, recording observed validation performance and usage guidance.

The interface also specifies typed arguments, such as the object to manipulate or its destination.

#### 3.5. Runtime Composition

At deployment, the runtime agent receives the task goal, current state and goal predicates, recent history, an episode notebook, and the policy catalogue. It selects a policy, supplies its typed arguments, chooses a duration, and defines numeric stop conditions. The executor runs the selected policy with fresh observations and returns control when a stop condition fires or when the overall goal is satisfied.

The runtime agent can continue with the current policy, switch to another implementation of the same skill, invoke a different skill, or terminate the task. It cannot modify policy weights or write new control code. This design isolates the effects of policy selection and composition from online policy adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Skill Generalization in MetaWorld

Experiment 1 used six MetaWorld manipulation tasks and evaluated policies trained with 2, 5, 10, or 20 demonstrations. Test states were either within demonstrated spatial ranges, recombinations of demonstrated factors, or extrapolations outside those ranges.

The main baselines were a vanilla Diffusion Policy and a fixed relational-prior policy. APPL’s first proposed prior already improved average out-of-distribution performance. Selecting the best of three candidates improved it further, and adding a fourth candidate based on development feedback produced the highest results.

At two demonstrations, APPL’s final selected policy reached 89.58% OOD success, compared with 28.96% for vanilla Diffusion Policy and 37.92% for the fixed relational baseline. Across all demonstration counts, APPL’s final OOD results were 89.58%, 93.54%, 93.33%, and 93.13% for 2, 5, 10, and 20 demonstrations, respectively. The mean OOD success across the four data regimes was 92.40%, compared with 37.86% for vanilla Diffusion Policy and 47.29% for the fixed relational prior.

IID success was similar among methods at larger demonstration counts, indicating that the main improvement was in distribution shift rather than ordinary fitting. The paper gives examples in which feedback led the construction agent to introduce action residuals relative to a handle or to reduce reliance on correlations between object and goal positions. However, revisions were not uniformly beneficial, and some candidate changes slightly reduced performance.

#### 4.2. Long-Horizon Composition in ManiSkill

Experiment 2 evaluated five long-horizon tasks with twelve demonstrations per task. APPL was compared with a full-task Diffusion Policy, a single full-task policy using one language-model-designed prior, a vision-language-action baseline, and several APPL ablations.

Under motion-level OOD, APPL succeeded on 50.0% of the 40 shifted-object cases. The full-task Diffusion Policy and SinglePrior each achieved 10.0%, while Agent+VLA achieved 32.5%. Under task-level OOD, which involved resumed or truncated versions of demonstrated sequences, APPL reached 92.5%, compared with 70.0% for Agent+VLA. APPL solved 8 of 16 previously unseen compositions.

Removing all interface information while retaining the same learned policy library reduced performance to 20.0% on motion OOD, 65.0% on task-level OOD, and 2 of 16 compositions. Hiding only prior, handoff, and support information while retaining verification scores produced 45.0%, 72.5%, and 3 of 16, respectively. Replacing the runtime language agent with a fixed rule reduced results to 22.5%, 52.5%, and 1 of 16.

These comparisons suggest that the learned library alone was insufficient. The information used to describe and select policies contributed substantially, particularly for new task states and compositions. Verification also helped policy selection, although it sometimes misranked policies under shifted conditions because validation occurred only at demonstrated entry states and used a fixed final-time criterion.

#### 4.3. Failure Patterns

The reported failures included dropped objects, drawer states that were not recoverable by available policies, and skill invocations from entry conditions absent from the demonstrations. The library contained no explicit recovery skills, and success was evaluated by first attainment of geometric goals. The authors note that some reported successes could be transient because sustained stability was not always tested.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

APPL’s principal contribution is conceptual and architectural: it proposes that training-time assumptions about policy generalization should remain available to the high-level system that later composes policies. This provides a bridge between representation learning and task-level planning without requiring a single universal policy or a fully hand-designed symbolic abstraction.

The results indicate that language-model agents can be used not only to call existing robot skills but also to design multiple skill implementations, document their expected applicability, and select among them during execution. The approach could support more modular robot systems in which alternative policies are retained for different spatial arrangements, contact conditions, or handoff states.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation. Both experiments use simulation, and the priors do not guarantee competence outside the demonstrated support. The experiments use a single training configuration and agent-design process, so variation across random seeds and language-model outputs is not fully characterized. Some comparisons combine multiple changes, such as segmentation, policy alternatives, overlap, verification, and runtime selection. In addition, evaluation relied on executor-assisted stopping and did not consistently test sustained goal satisfaction. Future work would need stronger verification methods, targeted data collection for missing entry states and recovery behaviors, real-robot validation, and possibly integration with motion planners or symbolic predicates.

Overall, the paper presents APPL as a method for preserving and operationalizing the structural knowledge used to train learned skills. Its findings support the view that skill generalization and composition should be designed jointly rather than treated as independent stages.
