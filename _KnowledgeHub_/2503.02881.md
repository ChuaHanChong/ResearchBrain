# Reactive Diffusion Policy: Slow-Fast Visual-Tactile Policy Learning for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP), a novel slow-fast visual-tactile learning approach, paired with TactAR, a low-cost augmented reality teleoperation system, to enable precise and reactive robot manipulation in contact-rich environments. RDP achieves over 35% improvement in performance across tasks like peeling and wiping compared to existing baselines, demonstrating human-like reactive behaviors and immediate adaptation to disturbances.

## Problem

- Existing visual imitation learning policies execute action chunks in an open-loop manner, limiting real-time reaction to environmental changes or precise contact dynamics in complex tasks.
- Current teleoperation systems often lack real-time, fine-grained tactile/force feedback, hindering the collection of high-quality human demonstrations for contact-rich manipulation.
- Robots struggle to perform dexterous, contact-rich tasks (e.g., peeling, wiping) with human-like precision and adaptability due to insufficient reactive capabilities.

## Method

- Introduces **TactAR**, a low-cost, ==AR-based teleoperation system== for collecting ==visual-tactile demonstration data== with real-time, intuitive ==3D deformation field feedback==.
- Proposes **Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP)**, a ==slow-fast hierarchical imitation learning== algorithm that uses a ==Latent Diffusion Policy== for high-level planning and an ==Asymmetric Tokenizer== for high-frequency, closed-loop reactive control based on tactile/force input.
- Utilizes a ==low-dimensional PCA representation== of tactile data and employs ==relative trajectory prediction== combined with ==latency matching== to enhance policy generalizability and robustness.

## Results

- **Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP)** outperforms Diffusion Policy baselines by over **35%** in overall scores across peeling, wiping, and bimanual lifting tasks, achieving success rates of **0.90–0.95** in peeling and **0.77–0.87** in wiping.
- RDP demonstrates immediate reaction to external disturbances, vastly outperforming baselines (e.g., **0.8** vs. **0.15** in perturbation scenarios), by dynamically adjusting trajectories at sub-millimeter scales.
- The TactAR teleoperation system improves data quality, leading to significantly better normalized peeling length (**0.91** vs. **0.72**) and a higher ratio of stable contact force (**0.87** vs. **0.58**) in collected demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating tactile signals directly into action prediction via a high-frequency "fast policy" is crucial for real-time reactivity in contact-rich tasks, unlike merely adding them to observations.
> - A slow-fast hierarchical policy architecture effectively balances long-horizon action planning with immediate, fine-grained reactive control using tactile feedback.
> - High-quality, stable tactile/force demonstration data, facilitated by intuitive teleoperation systems, is essential for learning robust and human-like reactive robot behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of robotic manipulation, imitation learning, and multi-modal sensing (visual and tactile/force). The broader goal in robotics is to enable robots to perform complex tasks in unstructured environments, especially those requiring physical interaction and fine motor control, akin to human dexterity.

**Current State and Limitations:**
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** Recent advancements in visual imitation learning (e.g., Diffusion Policy, ACT) have shown promise for learning complex behaviors from human demonstrations. These methods often employ "action chunking" to model long-horizon, non-Markovian behaviors and improve temporal consistency. However, a significant limitation is their *open-loop* nature during the execution of these action chunks. This makes them unable to react instantly to unexpected environmental changes or precise contact dynamics, making them unsuitable for high-precision, contact-rich tasks (e.g., peeling, wiping). They typically succeed in lower-precision tasks like pick-and-place.
- **Tactile/Force Sensing Integration:** Researchers recognize the critical role of touch in human manipulation. Efforts have been made to integrate tactile input into robot policies. However, most existing approaches use tactile information primarily at the *observation level* (e.g., for visual occlusion or contact state determination), rather than for *closed-loop, high-frequency action control*. Furthermore, collecting high-quality human demonstration data with fine-grained tactile feedback remains a challenge due to limitations in existing teleoperation systems.
- **Teleoperation Systems:** Traditional teleoperation systems often rely solely on visual feedback, which is insufficient for tasks requiring precise force control. While some haptic teleoperation systems exist, they are often expensive, rely on isomorphic hardware, or provide only coarse feedback (e.g., vibration in VR headsets).

**Contribution to the Landscape:**
This paper directly addresses these critical gaps. It introduces:
1. **TactAR:** A low-cost, versatile teleoperation system that enables the collection of high-quality human demonstration data with *real-time, fine-grained tactile/force feedback* via Augmented Reality (AR). This system lowers the barrier for researchers to acquire the necessary data for contact-rich tasks, which is a significant bottleneck in the field.
2. **Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP):** A novel learning algorithm that unifies the strengths of existing IL methods (complex trajectory modeling via action chunking and diffusion models) with the crucial ability for *high-frequency, closed-loop reactive control* based on tactile/force feedback. This "slow-fast" hierarchical approach allows robots to plan high-level actions while simultaneously making rapid, fine-tuned adjustments during execution, mimicking human motor control strategies.

By combining an innovative data collection method with a sophisticated learning algorithm, this work pushes the frontier of robot manipulation towards achieving human-like dexterity and reactivity in real-world, contact-rich scenarios. It represents a significant step beyond purely visual, open-loop control, paving the way for more robust and capable robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the observation that humans effortlessly perform complex contact-rich tasks (e.g., peeling vegetables, wiping surfaces) by seamlessly integrating vision and touch. This human capability involves a dual control mechanism: a feedforward/predictive component for overall action planning and a closed-loop fine-tuning component based on real-time sensory feedback, especially tactile signals. Replicating this capability in robots remains a grand challenge.

The paper identifies two major bottlenecks in current robot learning for such tasks:
1. **Lack of Real-time Reactivity in Policy Execution:** While state-of-the-art visual imitation learning methods (like Diffusion Policy or ACT) can model complex action trajectories using "action chunking," they execute these chunks in an open-loop manner. This means they cannot respond instantly to unforeseen environmental changes or dynamically adjust contact forces during the execution of a pre-planned action segment, severely limiting their applicability in scenarios requiring high precision or robust contact handling.
2. **Inadequate Tactile/Force Feedback in Data Collection:** Most existing teleoperation systems used for collecting human demonstrations either lack tactile/force feedback entirely or provide insufficient, coarse, or expensive feedback. This deficiency prevents the collection of high-quality, fine-grained data necessary for training policies that can truly leverage touch for precise manipulation and reactive control.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of this research are:
- **To develop a novel, low-cost teleoperation system (TactAR)** capable of providing real-time, fine-grained tactile and force feedback through Augmented Reality (AR). This system aims to overcome the limitations of existing teleoperation setups, making high-quality data collection for contact-rich tasks more accessible and effective.
- **To propose an innovative slow-fast visual-tactile imitation learning algorithm (Reactive Diffusion Policy - RDP)**. This algorithm is designed to emulate the human control strategy, enabling robots to:
 - Model complex, non-Markovian action trajectories effectively (the "slow" component).
 - Achieve high-frequency, closed-loop control based on tactile/force feedback for instant reaction to environmental changes and precise force management (the "fast" component).
- **To empirically demonstrate the superior performance and versatility of RDP** across various challenging contact-rich tasks, showcasing its ability to handle precision, adaptive force control, fast response to disturbances, and bimanual coordination, while being applicable across different types of tactile/force sensors.

In essence, the paper aims to bridge the gap between high-level robot planning and low-level reactive execution by leveraging multi-modal sensory input, ultimately enabling robots to perform complex dexterous tasks with human-like proficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology proposed in this paper tackles the challenges of contact-rich manipulation through a two-pronged approach: a novel teleoperation system for data collection and a hierarchical learning algorithm for policy training.

#### 3.1. Teleoperation System: TactAR

TactAR is designed as a low-cost, versatile Augmented Reality (AR) based teleoperation system for collecting high-quality visual-tactile demonstration data.

- **Real-time Tactile/Force Feedback via AR:**
 - Utilizes a consumer-level VR headset (Meta Quest 3) for AR visualization.
 - Tactile and force information is represented as a **3D deformation field**. For optical tactile sensors (like GelSight Mini, MCTac), this field is derived from tracking marker deformations on the gel surface using OpenCV to calculate 2D optical flow, then extended to 3D by inferring z-offset (or directly visualizing force components for force sensors).
 - This 3D deformation field is rendered in AR and visually "attached" to the virtual robot end-effector, providing intuitive, real-time feedback (including tactile image, normal force, shear force, torsional torques). Adaptive color changes based on magnitude enhance visibility.
- **Cross-Sensor and Cross-Embodiment Deployment:**
 - The 3D deformation field serves as a **unified representation**, making TactAR compatible with diverse tactile/force sensors (e.g., GelSight Mini, MCTac, built-in robot joint torque sensors like Flexiv Rizon 4).
 - Supports deployment on different robot arms (single-arm or bimanual setups) and grippers, as it only requires robot TCP pose and the 3D deformation field.
- **Low-Cost:** Emphasizes affordability by using readily available hardware like Meta Quest 3 (~$500) and optical tactile sensors (commercial GelSight Mini ~$549, custom MCTac ~$50). This contrasts with expensive isomorphic haptic devices or force/torque sensors (e.g., ATI mini45 ~$3000).
- **System Architecture:** ROS2-based, ensuring synchronized data streams from sensors, robot controllers, and cameras.

#### 3.2. Learning Algorithm: Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP)

RDP is a "slow-fast" hierarchical imitation learning algorithm inspired by human control strategies, enabling both complex trajectory modeling and rapid reactive adjustments.

- **Tactile/Force Representation:**
 - For optical tactile sensors, a **low-dimensional representation** is generated using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the marker deformation field. This makes the model robust to tracking errors, noise, and changes in texture/lighting (e.g., gel replacements). The PCA components are shown to be highly explicable, representing different force/torque directions.
 - For force sensors, the 6-D wrench (force + torque) is directly concatenated into the observation vector.
- **Slow-Fast Policy Learning Architecture:**
 - **Fast Policy (Asymmetric Tokenizer - AT):**
 - **Purpose:** Closed-loop, high-frequency fine-tuning based on tactile/force feedback. Acts as a "learnable impedance controller."
 - **Structure:** Consists of a 1D-CNN encoder and a GRU decoder.
 - **Mechanism:** The encoder compresses the original action chunk into a latent space representation. Crucially, the decoder reconstructs the action using *both* this latent action chunk *and* instantaneous high-frequency tactile/force representation. This "asymmetric" design ensures the latent action chunk captures high-level strategies, while the decoder handles precise, reactive adjustments using tactile feedback.
 - **Training:** Trained with an L1 reconstruction loss and a small Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty to smooth the latent space.
 - **Inference Speed:** Designed for very fast inference (<1ms), supporting high-frequency inputs.
 - **Slow Policy (Latent Diffusion Policy - LDP):**
 - **Purpose:** High-level action planning and modeling complex, non-Markovian behaviors. Acts as a "neural planner."
 - **Structure:** A Diffusion Policy adapted to operate on the *latent action chunks* generated by the AT encoder.
 - **Mechanism:** It learns to iteratively denoise noisy latent action chunks into clean ones, conditioned on low-frequency observations (visual inputs, tactility, proprioception).
 - **Advantages:** Reduces computational costs due to downsampled latent representation. The asymmetric design of AT simplifies the learning task for LDP by offloading reactive behaviors.
 - **Training:** Trained using the standard DDPM objective.
- **Key Implementation Suggestions:**
 - **Relative Trajectory:** Actions are represented as relative end-effector trajectories with respect to a base frame (the last observation frame in an action chunk). This promotes generalizable reactive strategies and compresses the latent space.
 - **Latency Matching:** Policy inference and action execution latency are accounted for by discarding initial predicted action steps, ensuring smooth transitions and preventing out-of-distribution tactile signals from causing abnormal actions.

This comprehensive methodology, spanning advanced data collection to a sophisticated hierarchical learning architecture, aims to enable robots to perform highly dexterous and reactive contact-rich manipulations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive empirical validation for both the TactAR system and the Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP) algorithm, demonstrating significant improvements over state-of-the-art baselines in challenging contact-rich tasks.

#### 4.1. Impact of Tactile Signals and RDP Capabilities (Q1, Q2, Q4)

- **Simple Tactile Integration is Insufficient (Q1):** Simply adding raw tactile images or low-dimensional tactile embeddings to the *observation* space of a vanilla Diffusion Policy (DP) does *not* significantly improve overall performance in complex contact-rich tasks. While it might mitigate overly aggressive force application (reducing sensor damage risk), it leads to different failure modes like getting stuck due to an inability to perform fine adjustments in an open-loop manner. This highlights that tactile signals need to be actively incorporated into the *action prediction* for true reactive behavior.
- **RDP's Superior Performance (Q2):** RDP significantly outperforms all Diffusion Policy baselines (pure visual, visual+tactile image, visual+tactile embedding) across three challenging tasks: Peeling, Wiping, and Bimanual Lifting. RDP achieves a substantial performance improvement of over 35% in overall scores.
 - **Peeling:** RDP scores (0.90-0.95) are much higher than DP baselines (0.39-0.44), indicating superior precision and robustness.
 - **Wiping:** RDP (0.77-0.87) again surpasses DP (0.50-0.57), demonstrating better adaptive force control with rotation.
 - **Bimanual Lifting:** RDP (0.48-0.70) dramatically improves over DP (0.00-0.08), showcasing strong precise force control and bimanual coordination, especially critical for delicate objects like paper cups.
- **Human-like Reactive Behavior (Q2, Q4):** Visualization and case studies reveal that RDP learns reactive behaviors analogous to human fine-tuning. For instance:
 - In Peeling, RDP makes upward adjustments to reduce sudden contact force and downward adjustments to follow the cucumber surface closely, even at sub-millimeter scales.
 - In Bimanual Lifting, RDP predicts outward corrective actions immediately upon contact with the paper cup, preventing squeezing.
- **Immediate Reaction to Disturbances (Q4):** RDP shows dramatically improved response speed to external perturbations compared to DP baselines. For "Perturbation after Contact" scenarios, RDP (e.g., 0.8 for GelSight in Peeling) vastly outperforms DP (0.15). This is because RDP's fast policy can immediately modify the predicted trajectory in a closed-loop manner, preventing failures like broken peels or incomplete wiping caused by open-loop chunk execution.
- **Multi-modality Retention (Q2):** In Bimanual Lifting, RDP with force input exhibits multi-modal behavior, suggesting that the slow policy (LDP) retains the powerful generative capabilities of diffusion models.

#### 4.2. Versatility and Design Choices (Q3, Q5)

- **Cross-Sensor Applicability (Q3):** RDP performs effectively with different optical tactile sensors (GelSight Mini, MCTac) and even with noisy built-in joint torque sensors. The performance with GelSight Mini and MCTac is very close in Peeling (0.9 vs 0.88), and RDP successfully uses both simultaneously in the Bimanual Lifting task. Surprisingly, using simple force input from robot joint torque sensors often yields the best results across tasks, potentially due to lower latency and dimensionality. This confirms the effectiveness of the unified tactile representation (PCA embedding) and the generalizability of RDP.
- **Importance of Slow-Fast Hierarchy (Q5):** The ablation studies confirm that the slow-fast hierarchical design is critical.
 - **Small Chunk Size vs. Reactivity:** Simply reducing the action chunk size in DP to increase reactive frequency has severe side effects. It makes the policy highly sensitive to non-Markovian behaviors (e.g., pauses) in demonstrations, leading to policies getting stuck and significantly lower success rates (e.g., grasp rate dropping from 100% to 20%).
 - **Temporal Ensembling:** While temporal ensembling can provide semi-closed-loop control, its performance is highly sensitive to the smoothing coefficient. It struggles to balance temporal consistency with reactivity, leading to either small chunk size-like issues (low grasp rate) or over-smoothed, non-reactive behavior.
- **Importance of Relative Trajectory and Latency Matching (Q5):**
 - **Relative Trajectory:** Using relative end-effector trajectories for action representation significantly improves performance compared to absolute action prediction. This is attributed to easier learning for the fast policy, leading to more generalizable reactive strategies and latent space compression.
 - **Latency Matching:** Discarding initial predicted action steps based on latency calculation is crucial for smooth action transitions between chunks and mitigating out-of-distribution behaviors, further enhancing policy robustness.

#### 4.3. Impact of TactAR Data Quality (Q6, Q7)

- **TactAR Improves Data Quality (Q6):**
 - **User Study:** A user study with 10 participants showed that over 70% found the AR tactile/force feedback "very helpful" for data collection, regardless of their role (teleoperator or object holder).
 - **Quantitative Analysis:** Data collected with TactAR resulted in significantly better normalized peeling length (0.91 vs. 0.72 for traditional VR teleoperation) and a higher ratio of stable contact force (0.87 vs. 0.58). TactAR also dramatically reduced the rolling standard deviation of force, indicating much more stable contact forces during demonstrations.
- **High-Quality Data is Crucial for RDP (Q7):** When RDP was trained with low-quality data collected via traditional VR teleoperation (without tactile/force feedback), its performance dropped by over 30% across all perturbation scenarios. This underscores that the fast policy's ability to learn robust reactive behaviors is highly dependent on the quality and stability of the tactile/force signals in the training data, validating TactAR's contribution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring intricate physical interaction and real-time adaptability. Its contributions extend across both hardware/data collection and learning algorithms, offering a comprehensive solution to long-standing challenges in the field.

#### 5.1. Significance of TactAR

- **Democratization of Data Collection:** By offering a low-cost and versatile teleoperation system, TactAR significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners to collect high-quality, contact-rich robot manipulation data. This addresses a critical bottleneck, as expert human demonstrations with precise tactile feedback are currently scarce and difficult to obtain.
- **Improved Data Quality:** The real-time AR-based tactile/force feedback provides human teleoperators with crucial proprioceptive information, leading to more stable contact forces and higher-quality demonstrations. This directly translates to better training data for robot learning algorithms, enabling them to discover more robust and generalizable reactive behaviors.

#### 5.2. Significance of Reactive Diffusion Policy (RDP)

- **Bridging the "Slow-Fast" Gap:** RDP effectively solves the trade-off between modeling complex, long-horizon behaviors and achieving instant, high-frequency reactivity. By adopting a hierarchical "slow-fast" architecture inspired by human control, RDP enables robots to perform both high-level planning and precise, closed-loop adjustments based on real-time tactile feedback. This is a crucial step towards human-like dexterity.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The ability of RDP to react immediately to external disturbances and precisely control contact forces marks a substantial improvement over existing open-loop imitation learning methods. This makes robots more robust in uncertain environments and capable of handling delicate objects or variable contact conditions, which are prevalent in real-world manipulation tasks.
- **Generalizability Across Sensors:** The demonstrated applicability of RDP across different types of tactile/force sensors (optical GelSight, MCTac, and joint torque sensors) highlights its robustness and generalizability. This reduces reliance on specific, often proprietary, hardware, making the approach broadly applicable.
- **Foundation for Future Dexterous Manipulation:** RDP moves robot control beyond simple pick-and-place towards highly precise and adaptive contact-rich tasks like peeling, wiping, and delicate bimanual lifting. This opens up new possibilities for automation in fields requiring fine motor skills, such as manufacturing, service robotics, and even surgical assistance.

#### 5.3. Potential Impact

The combined impact of TactAR and RDP is twofold: it provides the tools for better data acquisition and the algorithms for more capable learning. This work is an important step towards developing robots that can seamlessly interact with the physical world, exhibiting intelligent, adaptive behaviors that were previously limited to human operators. This could accelerate research in dexterous manipulation, human-robot interaction, and the deployment of autonomous systems in complex, unstructured environments.

#### 5.4. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards exciting future research directions:
- Improving TactAR's intuitiveness and further reducing latency.
- Extending TactAR and RDP to multi-fingered dexterous hands.
- Incorporating high-frequency *visual* inputs into the fast policy network for broader task applicability.
- Integrating RDP with Vision-Language Action (VLA) models to bring reactive, tactile-enabled closed-loop control to generalist robot policies, potentially leading to more versatile and intelligent robot agents.

In conclusion, "Reactive Diffusion Policy" represents a significant leap forward in visual-tactile imitation learning. By providing both a practical data collection system and a sophisticated hierarchical control algorithm, it pushes the boundaries of what robots can achieve in contact-rich manipulation, bringing them closer to human levels of dexterity and adaptability.
