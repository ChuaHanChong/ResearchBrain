# CurES: From Gradient Analysis to Efficient Curriculum Learning for Reasoning LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> CURES is a curriculum learning method for fine-tuning Large Language Models (LLMs) on reasoning tasks within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) framework. This approach, informed by a gradient-centric theoretical analysis, dynamically allocates computational resources, leading to up to 5.5 times faster convergence and superior accuracy across mathematical reasoning benchmarks compared to established baselines.

## Problem

- Current RLVR methods for LLMs often use uniform sampling, inefficiently allocating computational resources to prompts that are either too trivial or excessively challenging.
- Existing curriculum learning strategies for LLMs are typically static and lack the adaptability to align with the model's evolving capabilities during training.
- Previous dynamic computation reallocation techniques often address only isolated aspects of training acceleration or introduce additional computational overhead for explicit parameter estimation.

## Method

- CURES provides a theoretical analysis of gradient optimization, deriving optimal ==prompt sampling== distributions that prioritize moderately difficult examples.
- It implements an optimal rollout allocation strategy to minimize gradient variance, assigning more computational budget to prompts that yield higher learning signal.
- The method uses a lightweight Bayesian posterior estimation framework to dynamically track and update prompt difficulty (question-answering accuracy) and gradient variance with minimal overhead.

## Results

- CURES consistently achieved higher Pass@1 scores on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with an average of **44.94%** for **1.5B** models and **52.41%** for **7B** models, outperforming GVM and other baselines.
- The method significantly accelerated convergence, reaching peak performance up to 5.5 times faster for **1.5B** models and 4.3 times faster for **7B** models compared to standard RL baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the difficulty-aware prompt sampling and the adaptive rollout allocation mechanisms are critical, each contributing to the observed performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The maximum optimization potential for a given prompt is bounded by a term that is maximized when the prompt's difficulty (model's success probability) is around 0.5, indicating that moderately difficult prompts are most informative.
> - Optimal allocation of rollouts to minimize gradient estimator variance also prioritizes prompts with moderate difficulty, ensuring stable and efficient updates.
> - Bayesian inference effectively estimates and adapts prompt difficulty without significant computational cost, enabling a dynamic curriculum that evolves with the model's learning state.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of enhancing the training efficiency of Large Language Models (LLMs) for complex reasoning tasks, particularly through curriculum learning and within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) paradigm. RLVR, characterized by deterministically verifiable reward signals, has become a prominent method for training LLMs on tasks like mathematical reasoning and code generation.

However, the authors identify several limitations in existing approaches that their work aims to address. Current RLVR methods frequently rely on uniform sampling, which treats all training instances identically. This approach fails to account for the inherent variations in prompt difficulty and the diverse training utility each prompt offers. Consequently, computational resources are often allocated inefficiently, being expended on prompts that are either too trivial (yielding diminishing returns) or excessively challenging (leading to negligible model progress).

The paper distinguishes its contribution from other curriculum learning strategies. While some works propose progressive training curricula that divide the training process into predefined stages of increasing difficulty, these are often considered too coarse and lack the adaptability to align with the evolving capabilities of reasoning models during training. Other approaches involve online data filtering, where samples are generated and then pruned. The authors argue that this method primarily prunes *after* initial generation, thus failing to significantly conserve computational resources in the rollout generation phase and leading to suboptimal sample efficiency. Recent research exploring dynamic computation reallocation across prompts with minimal overhead also exists. However, the authors note that these techniques often address only isolated aspects of training acceleration or introduce additional computational overhead for explicit parameter estimation, such as prompt difficulty.

This work contributes by offering a systematic and theoretical investigation from the perspective of reinforcement learning gradient optimization. It aims to develop a comprehensive solution that integrates both prompt sampling and rollout quantity allocation, guided by theoretical analysis and informed by a lightweight, adaptive difficulty estimation mechanism. This positions CurES as an advancement that seeks to overcome the computational waste and adaptability issues prevalent in prior curriculum learning and RLVR training methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to significantly enhance the training efficiency of Large Language Models (LLMs) when applied to reasoning tasks, specifically within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) framework. The authors aim to achieve this by addressing the inefficiencies and limitations observed in current training paradigms.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges associated with existing approaches:
1. **Inefficient Resource Allocation:** Traditional RLVR methods often employ uniform sampling, assigning equal weight to all training prompts. This overlooks the diverse difficulty levels and varying training utility that different prompts possess. As a result, computational resources are often wasted on prompts that are either too easy for the model (providing minimal learning signal) or too difficult (leading to negligible progress). This inefficient allocation hinders overall training speed and cost-effectiveness.
2. **Lack of Adaptability in Curriculum Design:** Prior curriculum learning strategies, particularly those involving progressive training stages, are often static and manually designed. These fixed curricula struggle to adapt dynamically to the LLM's evolving capabilities as training progresses, potentially leading to suboptimal learning trajectories.
3. **Suboptimal Sample Efficiency:** Methods that rely on online data filtering, where samples are first generated and then pruned based on criteria, do not fundamentally conserve computational resources during the initial, costly rollout generation phase. This results in an inefficient use of the overall sampling budget.
4. **Isolated and Overhead-Prone Solutions:** Existing dynamic computation reallocation techniques tend to focus on individual facets of training acceleration. Furthermore, some of these methods necessitate explicit estimation of dataset-related parameters (e.g., prompt difficulty), which can introduce additional computational overhead, diminishing their efficiency gains.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth several specific objectives:
- **Theoretical Foundation:** To conduct a systematic and theoretical investigation into the factors influencing the training efficiency of LLMs from a reinforcement learning gradient optimization perspective. This involves elucidating how the sampling distribution of prompts and the allocation of rollout quantities affect gradient descent and overall update stability.
- **Unified Method Development:** To propose a practical training method, named CurES (Curriculum Learning for Efficient Reasoning LLMs), that integrates the theoretical insights to accelerate convergence and minimize computational overhead.
- **Adaptive Difficulty Assessment:** To leverage Bayesian posterior estimation for dynamically refining the model's question-answering accuracy (which quantifies prompt difficulty). This approach aims to provide robust and adaptive resource allocation while incurring minimal computational cost, by continuously updating estimates based on new samples.
- **Empirical Validation:** To experimentally demonstrate that CurES outperforms existing state-of-the-art baselines across a range of mathematical reasoning benchmarks and various model scales, in terms of both reasoning accuracy and convergence speed, thereby showcasing its superior sample efficiency.

Through these objectives, the research aims to provide a more principled and efficient approach to training reasoning LLMs, overcoming the limitations of static curricula and inefficient resource utilization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of CurES is structured around a theoretical analysis of gradient optimization, which then informs a practical algorithm integrating dynamic prompt sampling and rollout allocation, supported by Bayesian posterior estimation. The framework operates within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) paradigm.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Analysis: Prompt Difficulty and Optimization Potential

The first component involves a theoretical investigation into how prompt difficulty influences the efficiency of gradient-based optimization.
1. **Definition of Prompt Difficulty:** The authors define prompt difficulty as the model's question-answering accuracy, denoted *p_θ(x) = E_y∼π_θ[r(x, y)]*, where *r(x, y)* is a binary reward (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect).
2. **Loss Function and Trust Region:** For a given prompt *x*, the optimization problem minimizes *L(x; θ)* (the negative expected advantage) subject to a KL divergence constraint *D_KL(π_θ_old(·|x)∥π_θ(·|x)) ≤ δ*. This is a standard trust-region optimization problem.
3. **Gradient Update Derivation:** Using the Lagrange multiplier method, a first-order Taylor expansion of the loss function, and a second-order Taylor expansion of the KL divergence term around *θ_old*, the authors derive the optimal parameter update direction *d*. This derivation involves the Fisher Information Matrix *F(x; θ)*.
4. **Optimization Potential Bound:** A key result is the derivation of an upper bound on the magnitude of the loss function update for a single prompt: *|L(x; θ_old + d) - L(x; θ_old)| ≤ √(2δp_θ_old(x)(1 - p_θ_old(x)))*. This inequality, derived using the Cramér-Rao inequality, reveals that the maximum potential for loss reduction is directly tied to prompt difficulty. Specifically, the expression *p_θ_old(x)(1 - p_θ_old(x))* is maximized when *p_θ_old(x)* is 0.5 (moderately difficult prompts), and is zero for *p_θ_old(x)* = 0 or 1 (trivial or impossible prompts).
5. **Optimal Sampling Distribution:** To maximize the *expected* optimization potential across the entire prompt distribution *ρ*, while also encouraging exploration, the authors formulate an objective to maximize *E_x∼ρ[√(2δp_θ_old(x)(1 - p_θ_old(x))) + αH(ρ)]* under the constraint that *Σρ(x_i) = 1*. Solving this constrained optimization problem yields the optimal sampling distribution *ρ*(x)*:
 *ρ*(x) = exp(√(p_θ_old(x)(1 - p_θ_old(x)))/τ) / Σ_x' exp(√(p_θ_old(x')(1 - p_θ_old(x')))/τ)*. This distribution prioritizes sampling prompts with moderate difficulty.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Analysis: Optimal Rollout Allocation

The second part of the theoretical analysis focuses on allocating computational budget for rollouts.
1. **Objective:** Given a fixed total rollout budget *N* for a batch of *m* sampled prompts, the goal is to allocate *n_i* rollouts to each prompt *x_i* to minimize the discrepancy between the practical gradient update and the theoretical efficiency limit.
2. **Variance Minimization:** The authors reformulate the problem as minimizing the total variance of the estimated gradient, *Tr(V(ˆg))*, subject to the total rollout budget constraint *Σn_i = N*. Minimizing the trace of the variance is a common strategy to reduce the overall uncertainty of the gradient estimator.
3. **Optimal Rollout Allocation Derivation:** By expanding the variance of the stochastic gradient estimator and applying the Lagrange multiplier method, they derive the optimal allocation of rollouts *n_i*:
 *n_i = σ_i / (Σ_j σ_j) * N*, where *σ_i = √(Tr(V_y∼π_θ_old(h(y, x_i; θ_old))))*.
4. **Variance Term Computation:** The term *σ_i* represents the standard deviation of the gradient contribution for prompt *x_i*. Its calculation involves the model's accuracy *p_θ_old(x_i)* and the squared norm of policy gradients for both correct and incorrect rollouts. This allows the variance to be estimated using the same difficulty estimates and gradient information already collected during training. Similar to prompt sampling, this allocation strategy implies that prompts with moderate difficulty (where *p_θ(x)* is around 0.5) will receive more rollouts to reduce variance and stabilize gradient updates.

#### 3.3. Algorithmic Implementation (CurES)

Based on these theoretical insights, the authors propose CurES, a practical algorithm for efficient curriculum learning.
1. **Bayesian Posterior Estimation for Difficulty:** To dynamically estimate *p_θ_old(x)* and adapt to model evolution without significant overhead, CurES employs a Bayesian inference framework. The number of correct answers for a prompt *x_i* is modeled as a binomial distribution. The success probability *p_θ_old(x_i)* is assumed to follow a Beta distribution, which is the conjugate prior for the binomial likelihood. Initial Beta parameters (α₀, β₀) are set using a small batch of initial rollouts. As new rollouts are observed, these parameters (α, β, representing cumulative correct/incorrect counts) are updated, providing a refined posterior estimate of *p_θ_old(x_i)*. The mean of the Beta distribution is used as the estimated difficulty.
2. **Iterative Training with Distribution Reset:** To mitigate the issue of distribution shift as the model learns, the entire dataset is divided into *T* non-overlapping subsets. Training is performed iteratively over these subsets. At the beginning of each iteration, the prompt difficulty and gradient variance estimations are reset, allowing the model to adapt its strategy to its current capabilities.
3. **Two-Phase Iteration:** Each training iteration consists of two phases:
 - **Parameter Estimation Stage:** For each prompt *x_i* in the current subset, a small number of *N'* rollouts are sampled. These are used to obtain preliminary Bayesian estimates of *p_θ(x_i)* and compute the gradient variance term *σ_i*. This initial estimation informs the sampling probabilities *ρ*(x)* and rollout quantities *n_i* for the subsequent training stage.
 - **Large-scale Training Stage:** This stage runs for *M* steps. In each step, a batch of *m* prompts is sampled according to the calculated *ρ*(x)*. For each sampled prompt *x_i*, *n_i* rollouts are collected. The Bayesian posterior for *p_θ(x_i)* is continuously updated with these new rollouts. Finally, the policy *π_θ* is updated using RL training (e.g., GRPO or RPP), and *ρ*(x)* is re-calculated for the next step.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Training Framework:** VERL (Sheng et al., 2025).
- **Base Models:** Qwen2.5-Math (1.5B and 7B parameters), Llama3.2-3B-Instruct, Phi-4-mini-Instruct.
- **Training Dataset:** Numina-Math (Li et al., 2024a), partitioned into 15 subsets.
- **Training Configuration:** 15 training iterations, 4 initial rollouts per prompt (*N'*) for parameter estimation, total sample budget of 8 × 1024, 10 steps per iteration. Learning rate: 1 × 10^-6.
- **Advantage Estimators:** GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and REINFORCE++ (RPP) (Hu et al., 2025).
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:** MATH500, AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023, GSM8K, Gaokao-EN 2023, Mineva, and OlympiadBench.
- **Baselines:** GRPO, RPP, Speed-RL (Zhang et al., 2025), GVM (Yao et al., 2025), DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), and MoPPS (Qu et al., 2025a).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of CurES aimed to answer three research questions concerning its performance, sampling behavior, and efficiency.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance (RQ1)

- **Learning Curves:** Across Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and 7B models, CurES consistently demonstrated higher progressive and final accuracy compared to GVM (Figure 2), indicating more efficient utilization of samples throughout the training process.
- **Quantitative Benchmarking (Table 1):** CurES consistently outperformed GVM and other baselines (Speed-RL, GRPO, RPP) across eight mathematical reasoning benchmarks for both 1.5B and 7B model scales.
 - For the 1.5B model, CurES-GRPO achieved an average Pass@1 score of 44.94%, surpassing GVM-GRPO (42.82%), Speed-RL-GRPO (41.34%), and vanilla GRPO (41.64%). Similar trends were observed with RPP as the advantage estimator.
 - For the 7B model, CurES-GRPO achieved an average Pass@1 score of 52.41%, exceeding GVM-GRPO (50.64%), Speed-RL-GRPO (48.84%), and vanilla GRPO (47.59%).
- **Additional Baselines (Table 3):** Compared to DAPO and MoPPS on the 1.5B model, CurES-GRPO achieved an average Pass@1 of 44.94%, demonstrating a lead over DAPO (42.80%) and MoPPS (43.98%).
- **Cross-Architecture Generalization (Table 4):** CurES-GRPO consistently outperformed vanilla GRPO when applied to Llama3.2-3B-Instruct and Phi-4-mini-Instruct models, confirming its robustness across diverse LLM architectures.

#### 4.2. Sampling Behavior (RQ2)

- **Evolution of Estimated Accuracy (Figure 3):** The distribution of estimated prompt accuracy evolved significantly over training iterations. Initially, it was broadly distributed, but as training progressed, it shifted towards higher accuracy values and became more concentrated. This indicates that models were effectively learning and mastering the samples. This dynamic shift in difficulty underscores the importance of adaptive sampling, as uniform sampling would become inefficient in the presence of such bimodal difficulty distributions.
- **Dynamic Rollout Quantity Allocation (Figure 4, 8):** CurES demonstrated adaptive allocation of rollout quantities, concentrating more computational effort on prompts of moderate difficulty. The distribution of rollouts approximated a "bell-shaped" curve, peaking at intermediate accuracy levels. As training advanced, this distribution became progressively sharper and narrower, indicating that CurES dynamically increased rollouts for the *remaining* moderately difficult prompts to maximize performance gains per step.
- **Average Gradient Norms (Figure 7):** CurES variants generally exhibited higher average gradient norms compared to their respective baselines, particularly for the 1.5B model. This suggests that CurES effectively selects more informative prompts, leading to stronger and more consistent learning signals that accelerate parameter updates.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Analysis (RQ3)

- **Impact of Sampling Configurations (Figure 5, 9):** Experiments exploring different combinations of initial parameter estimation sample size (N') and training-phase sample budgets (n) showed that while performance improved, increasing N' or n did not yield proportional benefits relative to the increased computational cost. This indicated that CurES is highly sample-efficient, achieving effective training with relatively small sampling budgets.
- **Convergence Speed (Figure 6, 10):** CurES significantly accelerated convergence compared to baselines.
 - For the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model, CurES-GRPO reached the same peak performance as GRPO 5.5 times faster. CurES-RPP achieved its peak performance 1.75 times faster than RPP.
 - For the Qwen2.5-Math-7B model, CurES-GRPO achieved peak performance 4.3 times faster than GRPO, and CurES-RPP was 3.7 times faster than RPP.
- **Reduced Training Time (Figure 11):** CurES achieved significant reductions in actual training time. For the 1.5B model, it reached the baseline's peak accuracy approximately 4.51 times faster (4.5 hours vs. 20.1 hours). For the 7B model, it delivered a 3.86 times speedup (10.8 hours vs. 41.8 hours).
- **Ablation Study (Table 2):** An ablation study on the 1.5B model demonstrated that both the difficulty-aware prompt sampling strategy and the adaptive rollout allocation mechanism contribute significantly to the overall performance gains. Removing either component led to a notable decrease in average accuracy, confirming their complementary roles.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CurES presents a notable advancement in the training of reasoning Large Language Models (LLMs) within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) framework. Its significance and potential impact span theoretical understanding, practical efficiency, and the broader development of intelligent systems.

**Theoretical Contributions:**
- CurES provides a gradient-centric theoretical foundation for curriculum learning in RLVR. By rigorously analyzing the relationship between gradient efficiency, prompt sampling distribution, and rollout allocation, the paper offers a deeper, principled understanding of how to optimize training processes. This theoretical grounding moves beyond heuristic-based curriculum design, providing clear guidance on which prompts are most informative and how to allocate computational resources for stable and rapid learning.

**Enhanced Training Efficiency and Resource Optimization:**
- The primary practical impact of CurES is its demonstrated ability to significantly enhance training efficiency. By prioritizing moderately difficult prompts and dynamically allocating rollout budgets, CurES leads to faster convergence and reduced computational requirements (both in terms of steps and overall training time). This reduction in computational cost is particularly critical for training increasingly large and complex LLMs, where resource consumption is a major bottleneck. The observed speedups (up to 5.5x faster convergence) represent substantial savings in GPU hours and energy.

**Adaptive and Robust Learning:**
- The integration of a Bayesian framework for dynamically assessing prompt difficulty allows CurES to adapt its learning strategy to the evolving capabilities of the LLM. This adaptive mechanism addresses a key limitation of static curriculum approaches, ensuring that the model is consistently presented with optimally challenging samples. The robustness of CurES is further demonstrated by its consistent performance across different model scales (1.5B, 7B, Llama3.2-3B, Phi-4-mini) and a diverse suite of challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

**Practical Implementability and Low Overhead:**
- The Bayesian posterior estimation approach enables lightweight and adaptive tracking of prompt difficulty. This minimizes the additional computational overhead that often accompanies dynamic curriculum methods, making CurES a practically viable solution for researchers and practitioners. Its algorithmic design, detailed in Algorithm 1, provides a clear roadmap for implementation.

**Broader Implications for AI Development:**
- By improving the efficiency and effectiveness of fine-tuning LLMs on reasoning tasks, CurES contributes to the development of more capable and resource-efficient AI systems. This could accelerate progress in various domains that require verifiable reasoning, such as scientific problem-solving, code generation, logical deduction, and complex decision-making. The methods developed could also inspire future research into more sophisticated adaptive learning strategies, dynamic resource allocation, and gradient-based curriculum design across different areas of reinforcement learning and deep learning.

In summary, CurES offers a principled, efficient, and adaptive approach to curriculum learning, providing both a deeper theoretical understanding and a practical, high-impact solution for training advanced reasoning LLMs.
