# SemiVL: Semi-Supervised Semantic Segmentation with Vision-Language Guidance

> [!summary] Summary
> SemiVL introduces a framework for semi-supervised semantic segmentation that leverages Vision-Language Models to improve class discriminability, achieving mIoU gains across various datasets and requiring eight times fewer labels on Pascal VOC for similar performance.

## Problem

- Existing semi-supervised semantic segmentation (SSSS) methods struggle with distinguishing visually similar classes due to limited labeled data.
- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) possess rich semantic knowledge but lack fine-grained spatial localization capabilities for dense pixel-level tasks like segmentation.
- The high cost and effort of pixel-wise annotation for semantic segmentation datasets limit the scalability of fully supervised approaches.

## Method

- **SemiVL** integrates Vision-Language Models (VLMs) via ==VLM pre-training initialization== and a ==parameter-efficient spatial fine-tuning== strategy for the vision backbone.
- A novel ==language-guided decoder==, featuring decoupled spatial and semantic reasoning, is designed to align vision and language features effectively.
- The framework employs ==dense CLIP guidance== and ==class definition guidance== to stabilize training, mitigate pseudo-label drift, and incorporate dataset-specific semantic nuances, especially in low-label regimes.

## Results

- SemiVL achieved mIoU improvements of **+6.1** on Pascal VOC (92 labels), **+13.5** on COCO (232 labels), and **+9.7** on ADE20K (158 labels) compared to UniMatch (ViT backbone).
- On Pascal VOC, SemiVL with only **92** labeled images matched the performance of UniMatch (ViT) trained with approximately **732** labels, demonstrating an **8-fold** reduction in annotation requirements.
- Ablation studies confirmed that VLM pre-training and spatial fine-tuning were the most impactful components, with dense CLIP and class definition guidance providing additional benefits, particularly in low-label settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLM pre-training provides a powerful semantic prior that, when properly adapted, significantly enhances class discriminability in semi-supervised semantic segmentation.
> - A parameter-efficient spatial fine-tuning approach for VLM backbones, focusing on attention layers, effectively adapts image-level VLM features for dense prediction while preserving semantic knowledge.
> - Decoupling spatial and semantic reasoning within the decoder, along with explicit linguistic guidance, allows for efficient learning of fine-grained semantic boundaries with limited labeled data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Semantic segmentation models are fundamental for various applications, including autonomous driving, augmented reality, robotics, medical imaging, and remote sensing. However, training these models traditionally requires extensive pixel-wise human annotations, which is a resource-intensive process. Semi-supervised semantic segmentation (SSSS) aims to mitigate this by leveraging a limited number of labeled images alongside a larger corpus of unlabeled data.

Current state-of-the-art SSSS methods, such as UniMatch, have demonstrated effectiveness in learning accurate segmentation boundaries. Nevertheless, they often encounter difficulties in distinguishing between classes that exhibit similar visual characteristics. This limitation arises from the scarcity of labeled examples, which hinders the learning of precise semantic decision boundaries. For instance, such methods might correctly segment an object but misclassify a cow as a dog or a boat as a chair due to visual ambiguity and insufficient semantic context from labels.

Concurrently, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP have advanced significantly by learning diverse semantic knowledge from vast image-caption datasets. These models are adept at recognizing and differentiating a wide array of semantic concepts based on natural language. However, a key challenge for VLMs in dense prediction tasks like semantic segmentation is their inherent training on image-level rather than pixel-level data. This results in noisy segmentation outputs and a lack of fine-grained spatial localization despite their robust semantic understanding.

This research positions itself at the intersection of these two fields, aiming to integrate the strengths of VLMs into semi-supervised semantic segmentation. The work seeks to combine the good spatial localization capabilities of existing SSSS methods with the rich semantic discriminability offered by VLM pre-training. The paper states that SemiVL is the first approach to utilize vision-language guidance specifically for semi-supervised semantic segmentation to address the limitations posed by limited dense labels. This distinguishes it from prior VLM-based segmentation methods, which either operate in a zero-shot setting without dense labels (leading to limited performance) or rely on large-scale, costly annotated segmentation datasets. SemiVL’s contribution lies in enabling high-quality semantic segmentation in a semi-supervised context using only a small number of labels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a semi-supervised semantic segmentation framework, named SemiVL, that effectively integrates the rich semantic knowledge derived from Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to enhance class discriminability. This integration aims to improve segmentation accuracy, particularly when relying on a small number of labeled images, while maintaining precise segmentation boundaries.

The detailed motivations and specific problems that SemiVL seeks to address are:

1. **Overcoming Semantic Ambiguity in Existing SSSS:** Current semi-supervised semantic segmentation methods, while capable of generating accurate segmentation masks, frequently misclassify objects that share visual similarities. This issue stems from the limited labeled data available, which is insufficient for models to learn fine-grained semantic distinctions and precise decision boundaries for all classes. For example, previous methods might confuse different types of furniture (e.g., chair vs. sofa) or animals due to visual overlap. SemiVL aims to leverage VLMs' broader semantic understanding to mitigate these misclassifications.

2. **Improving Localization of VLM Semantic Knowledge:** Vision-Language Models, trained on vast image-caption pairs, possess extensive semantic knowledge. However, their training typically focuses on image-level understanding, leading to features that are not inherently optimized for dense, pixel-level localization. Consequently, direct application of VLM features to semantic segmentation often results in noisy and spatially imprecise predictions. A core motivation is to adapt this rich but globally-oriented semantic knowledge for accurate local segmentation.

3. **Reducing Annotation Effort:** The creation of pixel-wise annotations for semantic segmentation datasets is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. Semi-supervised learning attempts to alleviate this burden by utilizing unlabeled data. SemiVL is motivated by the goal of making semi-supervised learning even more label-efficient, enabling the achievement of high segmentation performance with a significantly smaller fraction of labeled images, thereby reducing the overall annotation cost.

4. **Mitigating Pseudo-Label Drift:** In self-training-based semi-supervised methods, models generate pseudo-labels for unlabeled images. If these pseudo-labels contain errors, the model can inadvertently reinforce these inaccuracies, leading to a phenomenon known as self-confirmation bias or pseudo-label drift. A motivation for SemiVL is to introduce mechanisms that anchor the training process using the robust, pre-trained semantic priors from VLMs, thereby stabilizing learning on unlabeled data.

5. **Incorporating Dataset-Specific Semantic Nuances:** Annotation guidelines for datasets often provide precise definitions or exclusions for classes (e.g., whether a bench is considered a "chair" or "background"). With limited labeled data, models may not learn these specific decision boundaries. SemiVL is motivated to allow direct linguistic guidance, such as class definitions, to inform the model about these dataset-specific semantic nuances efficiently, without requiring additional image annotations for corner cases.

In summary, SemiVL aims to fuse the best aspects of dense prediction and semantic understanding to achieve a balance of segmentation quality and fine-grained semantic discriminability in a highly label-efficient manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SemiVL framework builds upon a consistency regularization approach for semi-supervised learning and introduces five distinct components that integrate vision-language guidance.

#### 3.1. Consistency Training

The foundational training approach for SemiVL is consistency training. This method leverages both labeled data with a supervised loss (e.g., pixel-wise cross-entropy, $L_s$) and unlabeled data with a consistency loss ($L_u$). For unlabeled images, the model generates pseudo-labels ($p_u$) from an unperturbed input and perturbed predictions ($p_p$) from strongly augmented inputs or feature-perturbed inputs. The consistency loss, $C(p_p, p_u)$, computes the cross-entropy between these, focusing only on pseudo-labels exceeding a confidence threshold ($\tau$). SemiVL adopts the state-of-the-art UniMatch method, which enforces consistency over two strong data augmentations and feature perturbation. The total loss is $L = \frac{1}{2}(L_s + L_u)$.

#### 3.2. Vision-Language Pre-Training Initialization

To provide a rich semantic prior, the semantic segmentation encoder is initialized with a vision encoder from a pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically CLIP's vision encoder. This replaces traditional ImageNet pre-training. VLM pre-training, conducted on web-scale image-caption pairs, offers greater data diversity and captures richer semantic representations not limited to a fixed set of classes, which is crucial for distinguishing visually similar objects.

#### 3.3. Spatial Fine-Tuning

VLM pre-training is optimized for image-level understanding, not dense pixel-level localization. To adapt the backbone for semantic segmentation while preserving its semantic knowledge and preventing overfitting due to limited labeled data, SemiVL employs a parameter-efficient spatial fine-tuning strategy for the ViT backbone. Only the attention layers, which govern interactions between different patches and are responsible for spatial reasoning, are fine-tuned. The MLP layers, which operate locally on each patch, are frozen to retain their pre-trained semantic reasoning capabilities. Additionally, position embeddings are fine-tuned to accommodate changes in input size and the shift from global to dense reasoning.

#### 3.4. Language-Guided Decoder

SemiVL integrates vision-language alignment directly into its decoder architecture. This is achieved by first generating dense vision-language similarity maps. These maps are computed as patch-wise cosine similarities between the vision embeddings from the encoder and text embeddings of class prompts generated by a text encoder. The decoder then processes these similarity maps through:
- **Spatial Reasoning Module:** This module operates independently on each class's similarity map, sharing learned spatial reasoning across classes. It uses a 7x7 convolution for local structures, followed by a residual ASPP (Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling) for modeling long-range context.
- **Semantic Reasoning Module:** This component models inter-class relationships. It processes each pixel in the similarity volumes independently (sharing parameters across spatial locations) using two Transformer blocks. The class name text embeddings serve as semantic anchors, supplementing the features.
- **Upsampling Blocks:** Two upsampling blocks, employing transpose convolutions and skip connections from earlier encoder layers, are used to recover high-resolution features, addressing the typically lower resolution of vision transformer features. These blocks operate independently per class for label-efficient learning. A final convolution maps features to class logits.

#### 3.5. Dense CLIP Guidance

To prevent training drift and self-confirmation bias on unlabeled images, SemiVL regularizes the consistency training with predictions from a *frozen* auxiliary CLIP model. This frozen VLM extracts dense vision-language similarity maps for unlabeled images, which are then softmaxed to generate pseudo-labels ($p_{DC}$). Only high-confidence $p_{DC}$ (exceeding a threshold $\zeta$) are utilized to avoid noisy guidance. An additional loss term, $\lambda_{DC} \cdot H(p_p, p_{DC})$, is incorporated into the consistency loss, guiding the model towards the robust predictions of the frozen CLIP. The weighting factor $\lambda_{DC}$ is linearly decayed throughout training, reflecting that VLM guidance is more critical in the initial stages of learning.

#### 3.6. Class Definition Guidance

For specific datasets, especially with limited labels, the model might struggle with ambiguous class boundaries (e.g., differentiating between a chair and a bench based on dataset-specific rules). SemiVL addresses this by providing explicit language guidance in the form of class definitions, often derived from dataset annotation guidelines. Instead of raw text, a set of "concepts" (e.g., "armchair" for "chair") is extracted for each class. The frozen CLIP infers vision-text similarity over these concepts. These concept-level similarities are then aggregated back to class-level pseudo-labels (e.g., $p_{DC_{ija}} = \max_{b \in b_a} p_{concept_{ijb}}$), using the concept with the highest score to determine the class prediction. This refined guidance is integrated into the dense CLIP guidance mechanism. This approach is more efficient than manually annotating edge-case images.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SemiVL achieved consistent and substantial improvements over previous state-of-the-art semi-supervised semantic segmentation methods across multiple benchmarks. The key findings and results are detailed below:

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Improvements

SemiVL demonstrated significant performance gains on Pascal VOC, COCO, ADE20K, and Cityscapes datasets, particularly in scenarios with fewer labeled images. The improvements were measured in mean Intersection over Union (mIoU).

- **Pascal VOC (21 classes):** SemiVL improved mIoU over UniMatch (ViT backbone) by +6.1 (with 92 labeled images) to +3.3 (with 1464 labeled images). Notably, SemiVL with 92 labels achieved performance comparable to UniMatch(ViT) with approximately 732 labels, demonstrating an 8-fold reduction in label requirement for similar performance. Class-wise analysis revealed significant gains in challenging categories such as "chair," "dining table," and "sofa," which are often confused with visually similar objects or the background.
- **COCO (81 classes):** SemiVL showed major mIoU gains, up to +13.5 (for 232 labeled images) over UniMatch(ViT), indicating its effectiveness in datasets with a higher number of classes.
- **ADE20K (150 classes):** SemiVL achieved gains of up to +9.7 mIoU (for 158 labeled images) over UniMatch(ViT), further highlighting its robustness in highly challenging and diverse scene understanding benchmarks.
- **Cityscapes (19 classes):** SemiVL outperformed UniMatch+ViT by up to +2.4 mIoU (for 100 labeled images). The improvements were relatively smaller compared to other datasets, which the authors hypothesize is due to the presence of many small, fine-grained objects (e.g., distant poles, traffic signs) that are less frequently represented or described in generic VLM pre-training captions.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

A comprehensive ablation study on Pascal VOC confirmed the individual contribution of each SemiVL component:
- **Vision-Language Pre-Training (VL Pretr.):** This was identified as the most impactful component, contributing significantly to semantic understanding.
- **Spatial Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Showed substantial improvements, demonstrating its role in adapting image-level VLM features for dense segmentation.
- **Language-Guided Decoder (Lang.Dec.):** Further enhanced performance by effectively integrating vision-language alignment.
- **Dense CLIP Guidance (CLIP Guid.):** Primarily beneficial in low-label settings (e.g., 92 labels), stabilizing training and preventing drift. Its impact diminished with more labels, where sufficient ground truth data likely provided adequate regularization.
- **Class Definition Guidance (Cls.Def.):** Also provided noticeable gains with limited labels, demonstrating its utility in refining class boundaries based on linguistic cues. Similar to CLIP Guidance, its effect was less pronounced with a larger labeled set.
All components exhibited stronger improvements when fewer labels were available, affirming their effectiveness for label-efficient learning.

#### 4.3. Fine-Tuning Strategies Comparison

SemiVL's proposed Spatial Fine-Tuning strategy was compared against other adaptation methods for the VLM backbone on Pascal VOC:
- Spatial Fine-Tuning (29.1 M updated parameters) achieved 81.7 mIoU (92 labels) and 86.7 mIoU (1464 labels).
- This performance surpassed Deep Prompt Tuning (0.7 M parameters, 79.5/84.3 mIoU) and LoRA (1.4 M parameters, 80.5/85.8 mIoU), as well as Full Fine-Tuning (86.8 M parameters, 80.6/86.0 mIoU) in the higher label setting. This suggests that the full-rank fine-tuning of attention layers, while preserving MLP layers, offers an effective balance for adapting VLMs to dense prediction tasks.

#### 4.4. Decoder Ablation Study

The design of the language-guided decoder was evaluated:
- Decoupled spatial and semantic reasoning significantly outperformed a joint reasoning baseline (82.7 mIoU vs. 64.9 mIoU for 92 labels). This indicates that parameter sharing across classes for spatial reasoning and across locations for semantic reasoning is crucial for label-efficient learning and preventing overfitting.
- Each sub-module of the decoder (upsampling, spatial reasoning, semantic reasoning) was shown to be critical for the overall performance.

#### 4.5. Class Definitions Study

An investigation into different sources of class definitions on Pascal VOC revealed:
- Class definitions extracted from dataset annotation guidelines, aggregated using the maximum score over concepts, yielded the highest performance (84.0 mIoU for 92 labels).
- This approach outperformed using raw text guidelines, dictionary definitions, or concepts generated by large language models like GPT-3.5. This finding underscores the importance of dataset-specific context and carefully curated linguistic information for fine-tuning semantic boundaries.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SemiVL framework represents a notable advancement in the field of semi-supervised semantic segmentation, primarily by effectively integrating the semantic understanding capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with dense pixel-level prediction. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Elevated Performance in Label-Scarce Environments:** SemiVL establishes a new benchmark for semi-supervised semantic segmentation, particularly demonstrating substantial performance improvements when only a small fraction of labeled data is available. This directly addresses one of the most significant practical hurdles in semantic segmentation: the immense cost and labor of pixel-wise annotation. The ability to achieve comparable performance with considerably fewer (e.g., one-quarter) annotated images has strong implications for making advanced segmentation models more accessible and deployable in various resource-constrained settings.

- **Effective Fusion of Vision and Language Modalities:** The research provides a robust methodology for bridging the gap between global, image-level semantic understanding inherent in VLMs and the local, pixel-level precision required for semantic segmentation. This work demonstrates a successful strategy for transferring and adapting rich pre-trained semantic priors from foundation models to fine-grained visual tasks.

- **Enhanced Semantic Discriminability:** A common weakness of existing semi-supervised methods is their tendency to confuse visually similar classes due to limited supervision. SemiVL, by leveraging VLM guidance, demonstrably improves the model's ability to differentiate such ambiguous classes. This leads to more accurate and reliable semantic interpretations, which is critical for safety-sensitive applications like autonomous driving.

- **Novel Architectural and Training Paradigms:**
 - **Spatial Fine-Tuning:** This parameter-efficient strategy for adapting VLM backbones is a significant contribution, allowing for task-specific refinement without catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained knowledge or overfitting to limited labels.
 - **Language-Guided Decoder:** The proposed decoder architecture, with its decoupled spatial and semantic reasoning, represents an efficient design that maximizes the utility of scarce labeled data by promoting parameter sharing and structured information processing.
 - **Dense CLIP and Class Definition Guidance:** These training mechanisms introduce effective regularization, anchoring the self-training process to a reliable external semantic prior and allowing for direct, efficient incorporation of human-curated linguistic knowledge (e.g., annotation guidelines) to resolve dataset-specific class ambiguities.

- **Broadened Applicability:** The consistent superior performance of SemiVL across diverse datasets (Pascal VOC, COCO, ADE20K, Cityscapes) suggests its wide applicability across various domains. Industries such as autonomous navigation, medical image analysis, robotics, and remote sensing, which heavily rely on accurate dense semantic understanding, could benefit from the enhanced label efficiency and performance offered by SemiVL.

- **Stimulus for Future Research:** This work opens new avenues for exploring how large-scale, pre-trained vision-language models can be effectively fine-tuned, adapted, and guided for a multitude of dense prediction tasks under limited supervision. It also highlights the untapped potential of integrating structured linguistic metadata (like annotation instructions) into model training pipelines.
