# CollisionSplatting: Collision-Aware Motion Planning in 3DGS Scenes with Image-Conditioned Objectives and Adjustable Conservatism

> [!summary] Summary
> CollisionSplatting is a GPU-accelerated collision metric that works directly on standard 3D Gaussian Splatting scenes. It linearizes an ellipsoid scaling-function distance around each Gaussian, turns the splat's covariance into uncertainty over that distance, and subtracts a tunable multiple of it to set how conservative the planner is. Plugged into batched RRT and MPPI planners with DINOv2 image-goal costs, it matches baseline collision classification at up to 1.6M checks/s using 0.16 GB VRAM.

## Problem

- Splat-Nav and SAFER-SPLAT replace Gaussians with deterministic ellipsoid primitives and use per-query binary checks or numerical optimization, giving only a few hundred collision checks per second.
- ATLASNav's probabilistic metric assumes isotropic Gaussians, spherical robots and homogeneous density, while SPLANNING's guarantees hold only for a custom normalized 3DGS reconstruction, not standard scenes.
- Batched probabilistic metrics allocate large intermediate tensors, needing 14.5 to 55 GB VRAM at batch 4,096. That blocks sampling-based planners like MPPI, which need massive parallel collision checking.

## Method

- **Covariance-propagated distance**: linearizes the ==ellipsoid scaling-function distance== at each Gaussian mean and propagates the splat covariance into a 1D variance, supporting anisotropic splats and ellipsoidal multi-link robots.
- **Tunable conservatism factor** ==n_sigma== subtracts n_sigma standard deviations from the distance; zero recovers a plain point-cloud distance. Per-splat values are aggregated by ==recursive softmin== and ==spatial hashing== in one ==Warp kernel==.
- **Joint visual-geometric** ==MPPI== and batched GPU ==RRT==: the collision cost is combined with a terminal ==DINOv2== embedding distance between the goal image and 3DGS-rasterized rollout images, for image-goal reaching and navigation.

## Results

- Matches Splat-Nav, SAFER-SPLAT and SPLANNING precision-recall on the Stonehenge scene with about 200k splats, and beats ATLASNav, whose isotropic assumption hurts on anisotropic scenes.
- Reaches **215,579** checks/s at **0.16 GB** VRAM for batch 4,096, versus SPLANNING's **157,539** checks/s at **14.5 GB**. At batch 32,768 it reaches **1.64M checks/s** at **0.166 GB**.
- Raising n_sigma from 0 to 2 lifts refined minimum clearance from **0.030** to **0.046**. On a 7-DoF arm, the collision cost turns **100%** collisions into **0.017 m** positive clearance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A 3DGS scene plus its rasterizer acts as a cheap action-conditioned world model, letting planners score visual goals without large learned video world models.
> - Linearized uncertainty propagation fails safely: large, poorly reconstructed Gaussians inflate the variance, making planning more conservative exactly where the map is least trustworthy.
> - For sampling-based MPC, the bottleneck was memory, not compute. Streaming per-element kernels without intermediate tensor allocations decide whether a collision metric scales, not GPU batching alone.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot moving through a visually reconstructed 3D world has to avoid obstacles and still use what the scene looks like. Planners often need a separate collision model for a visually rich 3D scene, and converting the scene to a simpler geometric representation can discard information. CollisionSplatting checks collisions directly against the scene's own building blocks, its Gaussian splats, and adds a knob that adjusts how cautiously the planner treats them. The approach also keeps the ability to render candidate views for image-based objectives. In the authors' demo, a **simulated** ground robot plans with a receding horizon through a generated library. At each step it scores 512 candidate rollouts directly against raw splats, without a mesh or retraining.

The authors describe a simulated ground robot navigating a generated World Labs Marble library. Each receding-horizon step scores 512 rollouts with CollisionSplatting on raw splats, with no mesh or retraining.

### 2. From splat to clearance

3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) represents a scene as Gaussian splats. Each splat has a center and a spatial extent, described by a covariance. CollisionSplatting represents the robot as one or more ellipsoidal links.

For each splat, the method first measures how far the splat's center lies from a robot ellipsoid using a scaling-function distance. A value below 1 means the center is inside the ellipsoid, and a value above 1 means it is outside. The method then propagates the splat covariance through that distance by a first-order linearization and treats the result as uncertainty in the distance.

The metric lowers the center-based distance by $n_{\sigma}$ times the propagated standard deviation and clamps the result at zero. A higher $n_{\sigma}$ means more conservative checking. Distances to nearby splats are combined with a recursive soft minimum, and a spatial hash grid limits checks to nearby scene regions.

The knob lets planners trade reach for clearance, but the metric is **probability-inspired, not statistically calibrated**. Its covariance propagation is most accurate when the distance function is not too nonlinear nearby and splats are small relative to the robot–obstacle distance.

RRT paths for $n_{\sigma}=0,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 1.5$. Higher conservatism avoids tight passages.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

The paper measures precision–recall for collision classification over a grid of points in Stonehenge, a normalized-scale scene with about 200,000 splats. Baseline metric evaluations use a sphere of radius 0.015. On this test, the authors report CollisionSplatting's performance as on par with Splat-Nav, SAFER-SPLAT, and SPLANNING, and better than ATLASNav. These are comparisons of metrics, not of complete planners.

The SPLANNING metric was evaluated on the paper's standard, unnormalized scene because its custom normalized reconstruction pipeline was unavailable. The authors say the swept precision–recall threshold makes this minimally affect fairness.

The efficiency comparison used a single spherical robot on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Pro. The results at a batch size of 4,096 were:

- **CollisionSplatting:** 215,578.9 ± 7,660.2 GPU checks/s and 0.16 GB VRAM.
- **SPLANNING:** 157,538.5 ± 603.6 checks/s and 14.50 GB VRAM.

These figures measure collision-checking throughput and memory. They do not measure end-to-end planning time, and they do not provide a safety guarantee.

The [CollisionSplatting repository README](https://github.com/machines-in-motion/CollisionSplatting) reports a post-paper correction. The paper's evaluation code transposed splat rotation when computing propagated uncertainty for anisotropic splats, and the released version uses the standard convention. The README says results at $n_{\sigma}=0$ are identical. For $n_{\sigma}>0$, it says the Stonehenge precision–recall curve is the same or better at every recall level.

### 4. Contribution and boundary

CollisionSplatting checks collisions against the same Gaussian splats it can render, which links collision avoidance and visual goals within one planning loop. Its central contribution is a GPU-friendly, adjustable distance metric. The real-robot demonstrations show how the metric can be used in planning, not that it guarantees safety in arbitrary scenes.
