# BiRP: Learning Robot Generalized Bimanual Coordination using Relative Parameterization Method on Human Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Chinese University of Hong Kong developed BiRP, a method for robots to learn generalized bimanual coordination from human demonstrations using relative parameterization. The approach successfully extracts and reproduces complex spatio-temporal coordination, enabling humanoid robots to perform diverse leader-follower and synergistic tasks with adaptable motion generation.

## Problem

- Robots face challenges achieving complex bimanual manipulation due to difficulty describing and transferring spatio-temporal coordination from human movements, requiring an adaptable method to represent and reproduce the relative motions between two arms.
- Traditional Learning from Demonstration (LfD) methods for bimanual tasks often lack an explainable coordination paradigm and robust generalization to new task parameters.
- Existing robot programming is often rigid and not suitable for tasks with variable movements or dynamic environments.

## Method

- The BiRP method extends Task-Parameterized Gaussian Mixture Model (TP-GMM) and Linear Quadratic Tracking (LQT) by introducing a relative parameterization approach.
- It defines a dynamic relative frame for each arm, using the trajectory of the other arm as time-varying task parameters to explicitly model coordination.
- Coordination is embedded into both the probabilistic motion representation (by combining task-specific frames with the relative frame via Product of Experts) and the control process (by augmenting the LQT cost function with a coordination term).

## Results

- BiRP successfully extracted and constructed underlying coordination relationships from synthetic bimanual motions, demonstrating generalization to new task parameters like varying meeting points in 2D and 3D.
- Robot experiments on the CURI humanoid robot showed successful execution of palletizing (synergistic) and pouring (leader-follower) tasks, generalizing to task parameters different from human demonstrations.
- Motions generated with explicit coordination consistently outperformed those without, accurately maintaining desired relative arm relationships and demonstrating the necessity of specific coordination modeling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly parameterizing the relative motion between bimanual arms is crucial for effectively extracting and generalizing complex spatio-temporal coordination from human demonstrations.
> - Integrating a dynamic relative observation frame alongside static task-specific frames allows for a robust representation of both task context and inter-arm coordination relationships.
> - Embedding coordination within both the probabilistic motion generation and optimal control cost function enables adaptive and unified bimanual robot execution that maintains learned relationships under varying task conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics aims to enable humanoid robots to perform complex manipulation tasks, often involving multiple degrees of freedom. A significant challenge in this area is ensuring coordination between robotic arms, particularly in bimanual tasks that are common in human daily activities. Traditional robot programming methods are often too rigid and difficult to apply to tasks with complex, variable movements or dynamic environments.

Learning from Demonstration (LfD) has emerged as a prominent approach to address these challenges, allowing robots to acquire skills by observing human performance rather than explicit programming. While LfD has shown promise for single-armed robots, its application to bimanual systems presents increased complexity due to the need to capture and reproduce intricate spatio-temporal coordination between arms. Previous research has explored taxonomies of human bimanual manipulation, sequence transduction models for leader-follower tasks, and offline reinforcement learning for bimanual coordination. However, the development of an explainable and generalized paradigm for describing and learning bimanual coordination that can adapt to new task parameters remains an active area of research. This work positions itself within this context by proposing a method to extract and generalize bimanual coordination relationships from human demonstrations, aiming to provide an adaptable framework for robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to propose an explainable paradigm for learning generalized bimanual coordination from human demonstrations and applying it to robot motion generation. The authors identify that human bimanual manipulation performs complex tasks by leveraging spatio-temporal coordination between arms, a characteristic that is challenging to accurately describe and transfer to robotics.

The specific motivations and objectives driving this work are:

- **Addressing the open topic of bimanual coordination description:** The paper highlights that a comprehensive description of bimanual coordination is still lacking in robotics, making it difficult to establish an explainable coordination paradigm for robots. The work aims to contribute to this by classifying bimanual tasks into "leader-follower" and "synergistic" coordination and providing a method to represent them.
- **Enabling learning from human demonstration:** The authors seek to develop an LfD method specifically tailored for bimanual tasks. This is motivated by the recognized benefits of LfD, such as efficiency, adaptability, and the ability to learn complex movements that are difficult to program explicitly.
- **Achieving generalization to new task parameters:** A key limitation of many LfD approaches is their inability to generalize learned skills to new situations or task parameters (e.g., different object positions). This research aims to develop a method that allows robots to apply learned bimanual coordination to novel scenarios while maintaining the essential spatio-temporal relationships between arms.
- **Proposing a "relative parameterization method":** The core motivation for the proposed method (BiRP) is to explicitly capture the relative motion relationship between bimanual arms. This parameterization is intended to embed coordination characteristics directly into the motion representation and generation processes for each arm.
- **Facilitating different coordination manners:** The method aims to support both leader-follower coordination, where one arm's motion dictates the other's, and synergistic coordination, where both arms adapt simultaneously to new situations.
- **Developing an "easy-to-use" framework:** The authors aspire for their method to be practical and potentially serve as a data augmentation plugin for training larger robot manipulation models, indicating a motivation for accessibility and broader applicability.

In summary, the work is motivated by the need for robots to perform human-like complex bimanual tasks, which necessitates a robust, explainable, and generalizable method to learn and reproduce arm coordination from human demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed BiRP (Bimanual coordination using Relative Parameterization method), extends the Task-Parameterized Gaussian Mixture Model (TP-GMM) and Linear Quadratic Tracking (LQT) framework to incorporate bimanual coordination. The core idea is to parameterize the relative relationship between bimanual arms and embed this relationship into both the demonstration representation and the motion generation/control processes.

**A. Foundation: Task-Parameterized Gaussian Mixture Model (TP-GMM) and Linear Quadratic Tracking (LQT)**

The method builds upon established LfD techniques.
- **Demonstration Representation (TP-GMM):** TP-GMM probabilistically encodes human demonstrations by observing motions in multiple task-specific reference frames (e.g., start points, end points, object positions). For each frame `j`, demonstrations `ξ` are transformed into `ζ_j = A_j^{-1}(ξ - b_j)`. These transformed motions are then represented by a Gaussian Mixture Model `{π^(k), {μ^(k)_j, Σ^(k)_j}_(j=1)^P}_(k=1)^K`. For new task parameters `{\hat{b}_j, \hat{A}_j}_(j=1)^P`, a new task-specific GMM `N(\hat{ν}^{(k)}, \hat{Γ}^{(k)})` is generated using the Product of Experts (PoE) principle, combining the GMMs from different reference frames.
- **Motion Generation (LQT):** Once a task-specific GMM is obtained, optimal control methods like LQT are used for trajectory planning and robot control. LQT minimizes a cost function that considers tracking precision to the GMM mean `\hat{ν}` and control command effort `u`. The system dynamics are assumed to be linear (`x_(t+1) = A_s x_t + B_s u_t`), and an analytical solution for the optimal control commands `\hat{u}` can be derived.

**B. Representation with Relative Parameterization**

The BiRP method introduces the concept of "relative parameterization" to explicitly model bimanual coordination.
- **Dynamic Relative Frame:** For each arm `h`, a new observation frame is defined, taking the trajectory of the *other arm* as dynamic task parameters. This creates time-varying transformation matrices `A_(c,t)` and `b_(c,t)`. The relative motion `ζ_c = A_(c,t)^{-1}(ξ - b_(c,t))` is also represented by a GMM `{π^(k), μ^(k)_c, Σ^(k)_c}_(k=1)^K`.
- **Combined GMM Representation:** The task-specific GMM for each arm `h` is then obtained by combining the traditional task-parameterized frames with this new relative frame using PoE: `N(\hat{ν}^{(k)}, \hat{Γ}^{(k)}) \propto (\prod_(j=1)^P N(ν_j^(k), Γ_j^(k))) \cdot N(ν_c^(k), Γ_c^(k))`. This essentially "entangles" the representation of the bimanual arms, forcing them to consider each other's motions.
- **Leader-Follower Motion Generation:** If the motion of one arm (leader) is predefined or adjusted, its trajectory `ξ_l` provides the dynamic relative parameters `A_(c,t), b_(c,t)` for the other arm (follower). This allows generating a corresponding follower arm motion that incorporates the learned spatial-temporal coordination.
- **Synergistic Motion Generation:** When both bimanual motions are initially unknown, the method first generates independent motions for each arm using only the static task-specific frames. These preliminary motions are then iteratively used as the "relative frame" for the other arm, embedding the learned coordination into both trajectories simultaneously.

**C. Control with Relative Parameterization**

Coordination is also embedded at the control level by modifying the LQT cost function.
- **Composition Cost Function:** The individual LQT cost functions for each arm `h` (`C_h^(vanilla)`) are augmented with an additional coordination cost term `(ν_c - x_c)^T Q_c (ν_c - x_c)`, which penalizes deviations from the learned relative motion `ν_c`. The total cost is `C = \sum_h C_h^(vanilla) + (ν_c - x_c)^T Q_c (ν_c - x_c)`.
- **Unified Control Vector:** To solve this combined optimization, a unified control command vector `U` for the entire system is introduced. A binary coordination matrix `C` is used to relate individual arm control commands to the unified vector.
- **PoE for Control:** The modified cost function, when expressed in terms of the unified control vector `U`, can be analytically solved as a Product of Experts over Gaussian distributions, yielding an optimal unified control command `\hat{U}` and its covariance `\hat{Σ}_U`. This `\hat{U}` then provides the coordinated control commands and motions for each arm.

**D. Weighted Relative Parameterization**

A weighting coefficient `σ` can be introduced to adjust the influence of the coordination relationship in both the GMM representation and the LQT controller. This allows for tuning the importance of maintaining the learned coordination.

**E. Experimental Setup**

The method's effectiveness is demonstrated using both synthetic B´ezier curve motions (2D and 3D) and real human demonstration data collected via an OptiTrack system for tasks like palletizing (synergistic) and pouring (leader-follower). The demonstrations recorded end-effector trajectories with position and orientation. The method is deployed on a self-designed humanoid robot called CURI.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the BiRP method's ability to learn and generalize bimanual coordination.

**A. Coordination Learning Performance with Synthetic Motions**
- **Demonstration of Coordination Extraction:** For synthetic bimanual motions, where arms were designed to meet at a specific point, the method successfully extracted and constructed the underlying coordination relationship. This was achieved by using three observation frames: start points, end points, and a dynamic relative observation frame based on the other arm.
- **Generalization to New Task Parameters:** The core finding from synthetic data was the method's capability to generate coordinated motions in new situations with different task parameters (i.e., new meeting points).
- **Superiority of Coordinated Motion Generation:** A direct comparison was made between motions generated *with* the learned coordination (red and blue trajectories) and those generated *without* coordination (light red and light blue trajectories). The results indicated that merely treating bimanual arms as a combination of two single arms was insufficient for tasks requiring precise coordination. Motions generated without coordination failed to maintain the desired relative relationship, while the proposed method successfully ensured that the arms met at the specified new target point in both 2D and 3D examples. This suggests that explicit parameterization of coordination is crucial for achieving bimanual tasks.

**B. Real Robot Experiment Performance**
- **Human Demonstration Collection:** Real human demonstration data for palletizing (synergistic) and pouring (leader-follower) tasks was collected using OptiTrack, capturing both arm and object poses.
- **Generalization in Robot Execution:** The learned coordination model was deployed on the CURI humanoid robot. For the palletizing task (shown in Fig. 4), the robot successfully performed the task even when the task parameters, such as the initial and target poses of the boxes, were different from those in the human demonstrations. This demonstrated the generalization capability of the method in a real-world scenario.
- **Maintenance of Synergistic Coordination:** In the palletizing task, the robot maintained the synergistic coordination (where both arms cooperatively grasped, carried, and placed the box) despite varying task parameters. This highlights the method's ability to preserve the implicit coordination from demonstrations during generalized execution.
- **Leader-Follower Coordination:** The pouring example (shown in Fig. 2) served as a typical leader-follower task. Although detailed results are not explicitly described in the text for this task beyond its illustration, its inclusion implies successful application of the leader-follower generation aspect of the method.
- **Observational Frames for Complex Tasks:** For the palletizing task, five observation frames (start, end, center of transport box, center of destination box) were utilized, enabling the robot to execute a multi-phase task, including reaching for the box, carrying it, placing it, and releasing it.

In essence, the findings indicate that the BiRP method effectively extracts, represents, and reproduces bimanual coordination relationships from human demonstrations. It enables generalization to new task parameters for both leader-follower and synergistic tasks, outperforming approaches that do not explicitly model coordination, as evidenced by both synthetic data and real-robot experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The BiRP method offers several significant contributions to the field of robot learning and manipulation, with potential impacts across various applications.

**A. Advancing Bimanual Coordination Learning:**
The primary significance lies in providing an explainable and generalized paradigm for bimanual coordination. By formally defining two common types of coordination (leader-follower and synergistic) and proposing a method to parameterize their relative motion, the work addresses a recognized open problem in robotics. The explicit embedding of coordination characteristics into both the representation and control phases offers a structured approach to a complex problem.

**B. Enhanced Generalization from Demonstration:**
A key challenge in Learning from Demonstration (LfD) is the ability to generalize learned skills to novel situations. BiRP demonstrates robust generalization capabilities by adapting bimanual motions to new task parameters while preserving the critical spatio-temporal coordination. This is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured and dynamic environments where exact replication of demonstrations is often not possible.

**C. Practical Applicability for Humanoid Robots:**
The deployment of the method on a humanoid robot (CURI) for real-world tasks like palletizing showcases its practical feasibility. The ability to learn complex bimanual tasks from human demonstrations and execute them in varying environments can significantly reduce the programming effort for such robots.

**D. Potential as a Data Augmentation Plugin:**
The authors propose that BiRP has the potential to serve as a robust data augmentation method for training large robot manipulation models. This suggests that the method could generate diverse, coordinated bimanual motion data, which is often scarce, thereby enriching training datasets and improving the performance and robustness of more complex, data-hungry learning architectures. This could accelerate the development of more capable and intelligent robotic systems.

**E. Modular and Tunable Approach:**
The introduction of weighted relative parameterization provides a mechanism to adjust the influence of coordination during representation and control. This tunability allows researchers and engineers to fine-tune the robot's behavior, emphasizing or de-emphasizing coordination as required by specific task constraints, thus offering flexibility.

**F. Open-Sourced Contribution:**
The decision to open-source the corresponding code (Rofunc) contributes to the broader research community by facilitating reproducibility, further development, and integration of the proposed method into other robotics projects.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge limitations, including the method's current application only to Cartesian space trajectories, without considering joint space or whole-body coordination. This suggests future research could extend BiRP to more complex robot kinematics. Furthermore, processing high-frequency demonstration data with Gaussian mixture models can be time-consuming, indicating a need for potential improvements in computational efficiency, possibly through methods like those using Tensor structures. Addressing these limitations could further enhance the method's utility and broaden its impact.
