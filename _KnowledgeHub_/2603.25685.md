# Persistent Robot World Models: Stabilizing Multi-Step Rollouts via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A post-training reinforcement learning framework stabilizes action-conditioned video diffusion robot world models, enabling coherent multi-step rollouts over longer horizons (up to 11 seconds) by mitigating exposure bias. The method, developed at the Czech Institute of Informatics, Robotics and Cybernetics, achieves up to a 4.09 dB PSNR gain and 46.6% LPIPS reduction on external cameras on the DROID dataset and is preferred by humans in 80% of blind comparisons.

## Problem

- Action-conditioned video diffusion world models suffer from "exposure bias," where errors accumulate during long autoregressive rollouts.
- This error accumulation leads to rapid visual degradation and loss of scene coherence, limiting their use as reliable robot simulators.
- Current models are typically optimized for short-term, single-step prediction based on ground-truth history (teacher forcing), creating a mismatch with multi-step deployment.

## Method

- An online reinforcement learning (RL) post-training scheme is adapted, utilizing ==reward-contrasted denoising== for x0-prediction diffusion models to improve stability.
- A novel ==autoregressive training protocol== generates multiple candidate futures from a shared, evolving rollout state, using a ==variable-length prefix== to expose the model to various error regimes.
- Dense, multi-view visual fidelity rewards are designed by combining LPIPS, SSIM, and PSNR across three camera views, aggregated at the clip level and group-normalized.

## Results

- The post-trained model achieved up to a **4.09 dB** PSNR gain and a **46.6%** LPIPS reduction on external camera views compared to baselines on the DROID dataset, with improvements concentrated in object- and robot-centric regions.
- Human preference studies favored rollouts from the proposed model **80%** of the time over the baseline in blind side-by-side comparisons, indicating qualitatively superior perceived realism and temporal consistency.
- The world model demonstrated improved utility for policy evaluation, achieving a Pearson Correlation of **0.822** (vs. 0.796 baseline) and reduced MMRV (**0.006** vs. 0.053 baseline) in simulated task progression.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly training world models on their own autoregressive outputs, rather than just ground-truth histories, is crucial to address the closed-loop gap and prevent error compounding.
> - Contrastive RL objectives can be effectively adapted for x0-prediction diffusion models to optimize for long-horizon generation stability, with theoretical convergence guarantees.
> - Combining diverse perceptual metrics across multiple camera views provides a robust and comprehensive reward signal for improving visual fidelity in complex, dynamic robotic scenes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically focusing on the development and improvement of robot world models. World models, as learned dynamics models, are integral to model-based reinforcement learning (RL) by enabling planning through imagined rollouts. Recent advancements have seen action-conditioned video diffusion world models emerge as a promising alternative to traditional physics-based engines for simulating complex, human-centric tasks. These models, often built upon pre-trained video diffusion backbones and fine-tuned with action conditioning on large-scale robotics datasets like DROID, demonstrate the ability to generate high-fidelity visual clips consistent with robot actions.

However, a fundamental challenge known as "exposure bias" or the "closed-loop gap" limits the practical utility of these models. Current world models are typically optimized for short-term, single-step prediction based on ground-truth history frames (teacher forcing). When deployed autoregressively for long-horizon rollouts—where each predicted clip becomes the context for the next—errors accumulate rapidly, leading to visual degradation, loss of object structural identity, and decoherence of scene configurations. This discrepancy between training and inference regimes hinders their use as reliable virtual environments for benchmarking and improving Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies.

The broader research landscape includes several related areas:
- **Video Diffusion Models**: While effective for high-fidelity, open-loop video generation, they face challenges with temporal drift and degradation during autoregressive stitching of short segments. This paper addresses this challenge in an action-conditioned, multi-view robotics context.
- **Robotic World Models and Video-Based Planning**: Previous works have framed robot planning and evaluation as video generation or adapted pre-trained video diffusion backbones for controllable robot world models (e.g., Ctrl-World, WPE, AVID, UWM). However, these approaches generally do not directly address the training-inference mismatch encountered during self-conditioned rollouts.
- **Post-Training Diffusion Models**: Methods to align diffusion models with downstream objectives through RL or preference optimization exist, such as DiffusionNFT, which proposes a negative-aware fine-tuning objective for efficient online RL updates. DPPO applies RL fine-tuning to diffusion-based robot action policies. RLVR-World improves world model transition quality using RL but with token-based models. WorldCompass, a contemporary work, also applies contrastive RL post-training to camera-pose-conditioned world models. This paper builds upon these advancements by adapting contrastive RL to an x0-prediction parameterization common in some robot world models and introduces specific protocols for multi-view, dynamic manipulation scenes.
- **Exposure Bias and the Rollout Gap**: This problem is well-documented in sequence generation and is amplified in video generation, where small per-frame errors lead to significant degradation over long rollouts. This work directly tackles this issue.
- **Large-Scale Robot Datasets**: Datasets like DROID and Open X-Embodiment facilitate training data-intensive video world models and provide ground-truth trajectories for reward computation.

This paper positions itself as a contribution that bridges the "closed-loop gap" for action-conditioned robot world models by developing an RL post-training scheme that explicitly optimizes the model for stable, long-horizon autoregressive generation, thereby enhancing their utility as reliable robot simulators.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing action-conditioned robot world models when deployed for long-horizon autoregressive rollouts. Despite their ability to generate high-fidelity short clips, these models exhibit a phenomenon called "exposure bias," where errors accumulate and compound over time, leading to rapid visual degradation and loss of scene coherence. This degradation significantly reduces their practical utility for tasks such as simulating complex robotic manipulation, benchmarking VLA policies, or enabling planning through imagined futures. The inability to produce persistent, stable visual predictions over multiple seconds fundamentally undermines the potential of these models to serve as scalable and reliable virtual environments.

To address this challenge, the research outlines several key objectives:

1. **Develop an RL Post-Training Scheme for Robot World Models**: The objective is to introduce a post-training methodology that optimizes the world model directly on its own autoregressive rollouts, rather than relying solely on ground-truth histories during initial training. This aims to align the model's training distribution with its deployment conditions. This involves adapting a recent contrastive RL objective designed for diffusion models to the specific x0-prediction parameterization used by some robot world models, ensuring theoretical convergence guarantees carry over.

2. **Design a Training Protocol for Autoregressive World Models**: Given that autoregressive video generation lacks a fixed "prompt" from which multiple independent candidates can be drawn (unlike image generation), an objective is to devise a novel training protocol. This protocol should enable the generation and comparison of multiple candidate variable-length futures from the same evolving rollout state, thereby reinforcing higher-fidelity predictions over lower-fidelity ones. The protocol incorporates a variable-length shared prefix to expose the model to various error regimes.

3. **Create Efficient, Multi-View Visual Fidelity Rewards**: The research aims to design dense, low-variance training signals in the form of clip-level rewards. These rewards must combine complementary perceptual metrics (LPIPS, SSIM, PSNR) across all three camera views (two external, one wrist-mounted) to holistically assess visual quality. The rewards are aggregated at the clip level and normalized to reflect relative quality within candidate groups. Furthermore, the objective includes developing object-centric and robot-centric masked evaluations to confirm that improvements are genuinely due to better modeling of task-relevant dynamics rather than merely background preservation.

4. **Achieve State-of-the-Art Rollout Fidelity**: The ultimate objective is to demonstrate that the proposed approach significantly improves rollout fidelity on a relevant robotics dataset, establishing a new state-of-the-art across various visual quality metrics and human preference studies. This empirical validation serves to confirm the effectiveness of the methodological contributions.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to transform action-conditioned video diffusion models from producing merely high-fidelity short clips into robust and persistent simulators capable of generating coherent, long-horizon multi-step rollouts for complex robot manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology focuses on addressing the "closed-loop gap" in action-conditioned robot world models through an online reinforcement learning (RL) post-training framework. The approach can be broken down into three main components: problem characterization, the RL objective formulation, the autoregressive training protocol, and the design of multi-view visual rewards.

#### 3.1. The Closed-Loop Gap in Autoregressive World Models

The paper first characterizes the core problem: a structural mismatch between training and deployment. During training, models are "teacher-forced," meaning they predict future frames conditioned on clean, ground-truth history. However, during autoregressive deployment, the model must condition on its own previously generated (and potentially imperfect) outputs. This leads to an error compounding loop, where minor inaccuracies at one step propagate and amplify, causing rapid degradation of visual coherence and scene identity over longer rollouts. Standard training, by design, does not expose the model to its own imperfect outputs, thus failing to incentivize robustness against this compounding error.

#### 3.2. Online RL Post-Training via Reward-Contrasted Denoising

To resolve the training-inference mismatch, the authors adapt a contrastive denoising approach (DiffusionNFT) for online RL. This method avoids the computational expense of backpropagating through the full denoising process required by standard policy-gradient methods. The key adaptation involves formulating policy improvement as contrastive denoising for models using an x0-prediction parameterization (where the network directly estimates the clean data x0), as opposed to velocity-prediction flow-matching models. The paper formally demonstrates that the theoretical convergence guarantees from the original framework transfer directly to this x0-prediction setting without additional correction terms.

The adapted objective constructs implicit positive and negative clean data (x0) predictions based on the current model's output (ˆx0,θ) and a frozen exponential moving average (EMA) copy (ˆxold0) serving as a reference policy. For a candidate with a normalized reward weight *r* ∈ and a mixing coefficient *β*:
- **Positive Prediction (ˆx+0)**: (1 - *β*) ˆxold0 + *β* ˆx0,θ
- **Negative Prediction (ˆx-0)**: (1 + *β*) ˆxold0 - *β* ˆx0,θ

The reward-weighted denoising loss (L(θ)) is then minimized:
L(θ) = E[ *r* ||ˆx+0 - x0||² + (1 - *r*) ||ˆx-0 - x0||² ]

Intuitively, the loss encourages the model to shift its predictions towards the direction taken by high-reward samples (reinforcing drift from the reference for *r* ≈ 1) and away from the direction taken by low-reward samples (penalizing drift for *r* ≈ 0). This steers the model towards generating outputs that receive higher rewards.

#### 3.3. Adapting Group-Relative Training to Autoregressive Video

The RL formulation requires a shared conditioning input from which multiple candidate outputs can be drawn and compared. For autoregressive video, the accumulated history buffer immediately before any generation step serves as this shared context. The training protocol consists of four stages per training step:

1. **Generate a Shared Prefix (S1)**: Starting from a ground-truth observation, the model autoregressively generates P consecutive clips, feeding its own outputs back into the history buffer. The prefix length P is sampled uniformly from {0, 1..., 9}, exposing the training to varying degrees of accumulated errors (from nearly clean to severely degraded history states).
2. **Branch K Candidate Continuations (S2)**: From the frozen history buffer state created in S1, K=16 independent candidate short autoregressive sequences (F chunks) are generated. Each candidate follows its own distinct stochastic trajectory, enabling comparison from a common context.
3. **Score and Rank (S3)**: A visual reward R(k) is computed for each candidate clip by comparing its generated frames against held-out ground-truth frames across all three camera views. These rewards are then group-normalized using z-score normalization over the K candidates, converting absolute reward values into relative rankings (advantages) and then rescaling them to the range.
4. **Update the Model (S4)**: The group-normalized reward weights are used to scale the positive and negative denoising losses (Eq. 2), and the model's LoRA adapters and action encoder are updated via gradient descent. The main backbone weights remain frozen.

This variable prefix length strategy ensures that the model is trained to maintain quality across the full rollout depth and is robust to diverse history buffer corruption profiles.

#### 3.4. Multi-View Visual Rewards for Video Clips

An effective reward signal for autoregressive robot video generation must be dense, perceptually relevant, and consistent across multiple camera views.
- **Per-Clip, Per-View Metrics**: For each candidate clip, per-frame LPIPS, SSIM, and PSNR metrics are first averaged temporally over the L frames of the clip for each view (wrist, external1, external2).
- **Combined Scalar Reward**: These per-view, per-metric averages are then equally averaged across the three views and combined into a single scalar reward:
 R_t = -w_LPIPS * LPIPS_t + w_SSIM * SSIM_t + w_PSNR * PSNR_t
 LPIPS is negated as it is a lower-is-better metric. The weights (w) are chosen to bring the components to comparable numerical scales (w_LPIPS = w_SSIM = 1, w_PSNR = 1/32). This combination aims to capture distinct failure modes (perceptual similarity, structural fidelity, global signal-to-noise).
- **Group Normalization**: Rewards R(k) for the K candidates are z-score normalized to derive advantages A(k), which are then clipped to [-1, 1] and rescaled to to form the reward weights r(k) used in the loss function. This emphasizes relative quality differences within a group, making the gradient bounded and robust to absolute quality variations across different rollout positions.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

The research utilizes a pre-trained Ctrl-World model as its base. LoRA adapters (rank r=64, α=64) are applied to the UNet backbone, and the action encoder is fine-tuned, while other parameters are frozen. Training consists of 8,000 steps with a learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴ using the Muon optimizer, a batch size of 64, and a group size of K=16. Subsampling of group elements (taking the 10 most informative samples) is also performed. Training was conducted on 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for 3 days on the DROID dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The conducted research presents several findings regarding the improvement of autoregressive rollout fidelity in robot world models.

#### 4.1. State-of-the-Art Rollout Quality

The post-trained model demonstrates improved autoregressive rollout quality on the DROID dataset. Compared to the Ctrl-World baseline, the approach shows gains on external cameras, including a PSNR improvement of 1.40 dB and an LPIPS reduction of 14.0%. Against other baselines such as WPE and IRASim, these improvements are more pronounced, with PSNR gains of 4.09 dB and 3.06 dB, and LPIPS reductions of 46.6% and 40.2%, respectively. On the wrist camera, improvements include a 9.1% increase in SSIM and a 1.59 dB increase in PSNR. These results suggest enhanced fidelity in capturing fine-grained object manipulation.

In paired comparisons, the model outperformed the baseline in approximately 98% of validation samples. Analysis of performance over extended rollouts indicates that while degradation occurs for both models with increased horizon, the proposed method maintains higher fidelity and slower error accumulation.

#### 4.2. Object- and Robot-Centric Evaluation

To assess task-relevant fidelity, masked evaluation metrics were computed on object-only and robot-only pixels, segmented using RoboEngine. The findings indicate that the model's improvements are concentrated in these dynamic, task-critical regions. For object-masked pixels, external camera LPIPS decreased by 16.3%, and wrist-camera SSIM improved by 5.4%. For robot-centric metrics, PSNR increased by 1.63 dB for external views and 1.74 dB for wrist views. These results suggest that the training objective contributes to modeling intricate robot-object interactions rather than relying solely on background fidelity.

#### 4.3. Human Preference Study

A blind human preference study, which presented side-by-side video pairs from the model and baseline alongside a ground-truth reference, reported that human raters favored rollouts from the proposed model 80% of the time (174 wins vs. 43 for the baseline). The Elo ratings were 884.8 for the proposed model and 715.2 for the baseline. These results indicate that the quantitative gains translate to qualitatively superior perceived realism and temporal consistency.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of key design choices:
- **Learning Rate**: A learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴ was found to be effective, with higher rates leading to degradation.
- **Combined Reward**: Using a combination of LPIPS, SSIM, and PSNR as the reward signal performed better than any single metric, suggesting that it functions as a regularizer preventing collapse to single perceptual dimensions.
- **Prefix Sampling Strategy**: Uniform random sampling over the full prefix range (P ∼ Unif{0..., 9}) matched or exceeded fixed-window baselines and outperformed a structured curriculum schedule.
- **Group Size and Best-of-N Filtering**: Using a group size of 16 and a "best-of-5" filtering strategy balanced efficiency and signal consistency. "Best-of-2" filtering showed further metric gains, indicating a potential avenue for improvement.
- **Prediction Horizon**: A single-step prediction horizon (H=1) for post-training consistently outperformed a multi-step horizon (H=3), providing a tighter gradient signal.
- **View-Specific Weighting**: Assigning higher weight to the wrist view improved wrist camera performance marginally but decreased external camera performance, leading to the selection of equal weighting (w_wrist = 1).
- **KL Regularization**: Adding KL regularization improved five out of six metrics, particularly on the wrist camera, by preventing the fine-tuned policy from drifting excessively from the pretrained reference.
- **Old-Policy EMA Schedule**: A warming EMA schedule for the frozen reference policy, rising to 0.5, showed marginal benefits and helped prevent early training instability.
- **EMA on Learning Policy**: Applying EMA to the learning policy's weights did not yield significant improvements.

#### 4.5. World Model for Policy Evaluation

The proposed world model was also evaluated for its utility in policy evaluation. It showed improved Pearson Correlation (r = 0.822 vs. 0.796 for baseline) and reduced MMRV (0.006 vs. 0.053 for baseline) when comparing simulated task progression rates with real-world policy performance across three manipulation tasks (Put Banana in Box, Put Green Block in Bowl, Rotate Marker). These metrics suggest that the post-trained model offers a more reliable simulated environment for assessing policy performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a critical limitation in the application of action-conditioned robot world models by tackling the "exposure bias" inherent in autoregressive generation. By introducing a reinforcement learning post-training framework, the work demonstrates a method for stabilizing multi-step rollouts, allowing robot world models to maintain visual coherence and structural integrity over significantly longer horizons (up to approximately 11 seconds).

The significance of these contributions lies in several areas:

- **Enhanced Reliability of Robot Simulators**: The improved stability transforms video-generative world models into more reliable virtual environments. This is crucial for simulating complex, human-centric tasks that are difficult to model with traditional physics engines, thereby expanding the scope of tasks that can be virtually tested.
- **Improved Policy Evaluation and Benchmarking**: The ability to generate persistent and coherent rollouts makes these world models more effective tools for evaluating and benchmarking Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. The improved correlation with real-world task progression rates, as demonstrated in the policy evaluation experiments, suggests that policies can be more accurately assessed in simulation, potentially reducing the need for extensive real-world robot experimentation.
- **Acceleration of Robotic Policy Development**: By providing a more stable and accurate simulation environment, the framework has the potential to accelerate the development and iteration cycle of robust robotic agents, particularly in dynamic and manipulation-intensive environments.
- **Theoretical and Methodological Advancements**: The adaptation of a contrastive online reinforcement learning objective to x0-prediction backbones, along with the design of a variable-length branching training protocol and multi-view perceptual rewards, represents a methodological contribution to the field of diffusion models and reinforcement learning for robotics. The work demonstrates that explicitly training models on their own generated outputs under realistic deployment conditions can mitigate the inherent limitations of teacher-forced learning.
- **Qualitative and Quantitative Validation**: The consistent improvements across a range of quantitative metrics (LPIPS, SSIM, PSNR), coupled with the strong preference rates in human studies and better policy evaluation correlations, provide robust evidence for the practical effectiveness of the proposed approach.

Despite these advancements, the work acknowledges limitations, such as the increased computational overhead during post-training due to sampling multiple candidates. Future work is intended to explore additional consistency rewards, including physics-informed and geometry-aware metrics, to further enhance physical realism. The long-term impact involves investigating the direct integration of these persistent world models into policy optimization loops to further accelerate the development of robust robotic agents in human-centric environments.
