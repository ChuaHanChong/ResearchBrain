# Unmasking On-Policy Distillation: Where It Helps, Where It Hurts, and Why

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Apple introduced a training-free diagnostic framework to analyze on-policy distillation (OPD) in large language models at a token-level granularity. This framework quantifies how well a teacher's guidance aligns with successful reasoning outcomes, revealing that OPD is most beneficial on incorrect reasoning paths and that optimal distillation strategies vary significantly with student capacity and task complexity.

## Problem

- Practitioners lack principled guidance for configuring on-policy distillation (OPD) in LLMs, leading to costly trial-and-error training.
- Existing aggregate performance metrics for OPD obscure nuanced token-level dynamics, failing to distinguish between beneficial, neutral, or detrimental teacher signals.
- It is unclear which teacher models or context designs are most effective for various tasks and student capacities, hindering efficient resource allocation.

## Method

- Developed a ==training-free diagnostic framework== that estimates an "ideal" per-token gradient to maximize a student's success probability, even for long reasoning chains.
- Defined a ==gradient alignment score== as the ==cosine similarity== between distillation gradients and this ideal gradient, quantifying per-token teacher efficacy.
- Introduced scalable computation techniques, like ==targeted rollouts== and ==exponential depth windows==, to efficiently estimate success probabilities and share generation trees across teacher configurations.

## Results

- Incorrect reasoning paths exhibited higher gradient alignment (mean cosine of **0.058**) than correct paths (**0.001**) for Qwen3-1.7B on BoolQ.
- Smaller student models (Qwen3-0.6B) showed **2-3 times higher** alignment with self-distillation from correct demonstrations, while larger students (Qwen3-1.7B) performed best with external teachers like Qwen3-8B on certain tasks.
- Including wrong demonstrations was detrimental on simpler tasks (BoolQ/MMLU) but beneficial for hard mathematical reasoning problems (AIME), highlighting task-dependent efficacy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distillation guidance is significantly more reliable and aligned with success on incorrect reasoning paths, suggesting it is most beneficial when the student is struggling.
> - The effectiveness of teacher configurations, especially self-distillation vs. external teachers and the use of summarized or wrong demonstrations, strongly interacts with student capacity and task complexity.
> - There is no universal optimal distillation recipe; the best strategy for teacher selection and context design is highly conditional on the specific task and student model.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as a fundamental post-training technique for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Models such as Qwen3, MiMo, and GLM-5 integrate OPD into their training pipelines. The core concept involves providing dense, per-token supervision to a student model based on a teacher's distribution. This can manifest as teacher distillation, where a larger model serves as the supervisor (e.g., Hinton et al., 2015), or self-distillation, where the student model itself acts as the teacher, typically augmented with additional context not available during inference (e.g., a correct solution trace). OPD complements sparse binary reward reinforcement learning (RL) methods by providing a continuous gradient signal at every token generation step.

Despite its widespread adoption and demonstrated gains in various reasoning tasks, practitioners currently face a lack of principled guidance regarding the optimal configuration of on-policy distillation. Key unanswered questions persist:
- Which teacher model is most effective?
- What specific context should be used for self-distillation (e.g., full solution trace vs. concise summary)?
- Does the optimal choice of teacher or context vary depending on the specific token being generated or the complexity of the task?

Traditional approaches to addressing these questions involve extensive and costly training runs. These methods typically yield only aggregate performance metrics, which obscure the nuanced dynamics occurring at the level of individual tokens. This aggregation prevents a detailed understanding of *when* and *why* a teacher's signal is beneficial or detrimental, as it cannot differentiate between signals that are reasoning-critical, stylistically neutral, or actively harmful.

This research addresses this gap by introducing a novel, training-free diagnostic framework. This framework operates at a granular level—per token, per question, and per teacher configuration—to empirically evaluate the quality of teacher guidance. By providing a detailed, token-level analysis, this work aims to offer a more rigorous understanding of on-policy distillation's efficacy and inform more effective training strategies, moving beyond opaque aggregate performance metrics. It connects the teacher’s token-level signal directly to downstream outcomes, providing a mechanistic explanation for observed distillation phenomena.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the widespread adoption of on-policy distillation in reasoning model training and the concurrent lack of clear, principled guidelines for its effective implementation. While OPD offers a dense supervision signal, it remains unclear under which conditions this signal aids student learning and under which it impedes it. Practitioners currently rely on costly, trial-and-error training runs, whose aggregate results do not reveal the fine-grained impact of distillation at the level of individual tokens or reasoning steps. This leads to inefficient resource allocation and sub-optimal distillation strategies.

The researchers observed that, within a single reasoning trajectory, disagreements between a teacher's distribution and a student's prediction can be heterogeneous. These disagreements might represent:
1. **Beneficial guidance:** The teacher steers the student towards a correct reasoning path.
2. **Neutral guidance:** The teacher suggests stylistic variations (e.g., "four" vs. "4") that do not impact the correctness of the reasoning.
3. **Detrimental guidance:** The teacher pushes the student towards an incorrect or less optimal reasoning path.

Current distillation methods typically treat all these signals equally, potentially introducing noise from irrelevant preferences or actively harmful updates alongside genuinely beneficial ones. The core difficulty is that the teacher's probability distribution alone cannot reliably distinguish these cases.

Therefore, the key objective of this research was to develop a more rigorous methodology: a training-free diagnostic framework capable of assessing, at the finest feasible level of granularity (per token, per question, per teacher configuration), the extent to which a teacher’s guidance aligns with the behaviors that ultimately yield correct answers. This involved:
- Deriving an "ideal" per-token gradient that maximally increases the student's probability of success.
- Developing a scalable algorithm to efficiently estimate this ideal gradient, even for long chains of intermediate thoughts.
- Defining a "gradient alignment score" to quantify how well any given distillation gradient approximates this ideal signal, thereby providing a direct measure of the teacher's per-token efficacy.

By achieving these objectives, the research aimed to provide actionable insights for optimizing distillation practices, enabling practitioners to make informed decisions about teacher selection and context design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper introduces a training-free diagnostic framework to analyze on-policy distillation at a granular level. The approach proceeds in several stages:

#### 3.1. Decomposing Generation and Teacher Advantage

The generation process of a student model is conceptualized as a **generation tree**. Given *G* trajectories sampled from the student model (πθ) on a prompt, shared prefixes form nodes, and next-token choices form edges. At each node *u*, the framework estimates the **empirical success probability** (ˆP_k_succ): the probability of reaching a correct answer if token *k* is chosen next from node *u*.
With ˆP_k_succ, the **teacher advantage** is defined as the difference between the teacher's and student's expected success rates at node *u*: Adv(u) = Σ_k_ (P_k_te * ˆP_k_succ - P_k_θ * ˆP_k_succ). A positive advantage indicates the teacher's distribution aligns more with successful continuations.

#### 3.2. Derivation of the Ideal Reference Gradient

To evaluate teacher guidance, an **ideal local objective** at each node *u* is defined as maximizing the student’s expected success rate from that point: L_ideal(u) = Σ_k_ P_k_θ * P_k_succ.
The gradient of this objective with respect to the student’s logit *z_j* at node *u* is derived using the softmax Jacobian, resulting in: ∂L_ideal/∂z_j = P_j_θ (P_j_succ - P_succ_bar), where P_succ_bar is the student’s current expected success at node *u*. This gradient indicates the direction the student’s logits should move to maximally improve its chance of success.
The authors further demonstrate that **Dr. GRPO** (a variant of GRPO without per-trajectory length normalization) recovers this ideal gradient in expectation, thereby validating it as a principled, reward-based reference. In practice, the ideal gradient is computed directly from empirical ˆP_k_succ estimates, which offers higher accuracy than finite-sample GRPO estimators.

#### 3.3. Distillation Gradients

The paper then analyzes the gradients produced by various common distillation algorithms, showing that they all share a similar local structure.
- **GKD (Generalized Knowledge Distillation):** Minimizes KL(π_θ_ || π_te_). Its gradient is proportional to -P_j_θ (ℓ_j - ℓ_bar), where ℓ_j = log P_j_θ - log P_j_te. This gradient pushes logits towards tokens where the teacher assigns relatively higher probability.
- **Single-sample GKD estimator (Thinking Lab):** This importance-weighted estimator, requiring only the sampled token, is shown to recover the GKD gradient in expectation.
- **MiniLLM:** Uses a REINFORCE-style gradient with a trajectory-level reward-to-go, making the reward function trajectory-dependent.
Crucially, all these methods produce per-node gradients of the form P_j_θ (f_j - f_bar). This shared structure enables direct comparison of their directions via cosine similarity with the ideal gradient. The P_j_θ factor indicates that the gradient magnitude is gated by the student's current probability, limiting the ability to introduce entirely new continuations in a single step.

#### 3.4. The Gradient Alignment Score

The central metric is the **gradient alignment score**, defined as the cosine similarity between the ideal gradient (Equation 2.3) and a specific distillation gradient (e.g., GKD gradient) at node *u*.
- **Align(u) > 0:** Distillation gradient pushes towards success-leading tokens (helpful).
- **Align(u) ≈ 0:** Distillation gradient is orthogonal to the reward signal (neutral, stylistic).
- **Align(u) < 0:** Distillation gradient pushes towards failure-leading tokens (harmful).
This score quantifies the teacher’s per-token efficacy and differentiates between reasoning-critical and stylistic disagreements without requiring additional training.

#### 3.5. Scalable Computation

Estimating ˆP_k_succ reliably across long reasoning chains is computationally intensive due to sparsity. The framework employs:
- **Targeted Rollouts:** For undersampled (node *u*, token *k*), a prefix (*prompt* + *path to u* + *token k*) is constructed, and completions are sampled from the student. Each rollout enriches success probability estimates for *u* and its ancestors/descendants.
- **Exponential Depth Windows:** The generation sequence is partitioned into exponentially growing depth windows (e.g., 1-50, 51-150 tokens). A fixed budget of rollouts is allocated within each window, prioritizing tokens with high GKD gradient magnitude or large student-teacher probability difference. This strategy adapts to decreasing branching diversity at greater depths.
- **Budget and Scalability:** The total compute scales with a user-chosen budget (number of windows × per-window budget), not linearly with sequence length. Tokens with P_k_θ or P_k_te below 0.02 are typically excluded. Nodes require at least 20 (or 40 for AIME) visits per child for significant ˆP_k_succ estimation. Each question requires approximately 45K–200K targeted rollouts.
- **Teacher-Independent Tree Sharing:** The generation tree and ˆP_k_succ estimates are dependent only on the student model and its rollouts, not on the teacher. Thus, a single enriched tree is generated once, and shared across all teacher configurations. Each teacher then only requires a single forward pass to compute its gradient and alignment score, reducing total compute by approximately 7x.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

- **Student Models:** Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B.
- **Teacher Configurations (8 total):**
 - **Self-distillation:** Self-1C (1 correct solution), Self-Sum-1C (1 correct summarized by Qwen3-32B), Self-1C1W (1 correct + 1 wrong solution), Self-Sum-1C1W (both summarized), Self-1C (32B) (1 correct solution generated by Qwen3-32B).
 - **External teachers:** Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B.
- **Datasets:** BoolQ (reading comprehension, short reasoning), MMLU (multiple-choice knowledge, medium reasoning), and AIME 2025 (mathematical reasoning, long traces; for case studies).
- **Metrics:** Gradient alignment (mean cosine, success-rate-range weighted cosine), teacher advantage, and success rate statistics. These are aggregated per path (mean cosine), per question (correct vs. incorrect rollouts), and per teacher.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The application of this diagnostic framework across various student capacities, teacher configurations, and reasoning tasks yielded several findings:

#### 4.1. Distillation Guidance Is More Reliable on Incorrect Rollouts

A consistent observation across all settings is that **incorrect paths exhibit significantly higher gradient alignment than correct paths**. When the student model is already on a trajectory leading to a correct answer, the teacher's signal tends to be noisy and weakly aligned with the ideal gradient. Conversely, on rollouts that are currently failing, the teacher's guidance reliably pushes the student towards tokens that increase the probability of success. For example, for the Qwen3-1.7B student on BoolQ, the mean cosine alignment on incorrect paths was 0.058 compared to 0.001 on correct paths (∆ = -0.056, p < 10^-9). This pattern holds true for both mean cosine and weighted cosine metrics, suggesting that distillation is most beneficial when the student is struggling.

#### 4.2. Context Design and Student Capacity Interact Strongly

The effectiveness of different teacher configurations is not universal but depends on the student model's capacity and the target task.
- **Smaller Student (Qwen3-0.6B):** Self-distillation teachers using only correct demonstrations (Self-1C, Self-Sum-1C, Self-1C (32B)) consistently achieved 2-3 times higher alignment than external teachers on both BoolQ and MMLU. This suggests that smaller models may struggle to comprehend the complex, potentially "foreign" reasoning patterns or knowledge encoded in much larger external models, making the dense gradient signal less exploitable. In this scenario, being shown a correct solution in its *own* reasoning style provides a more understandable and actionable signal.
- **Larger Student (Qwen3-1.7B):** For the larger Qwen3-1.7B student on BoolQ, external teachers, particularly Qwen3-8B, achieved the highest alignment, outperforming all self-distillation variants. The reduced capacity gap to an 8B teacher likely makes the genuinely different knowledge and reasoning patterns from the larger model more comprehensible and beneficial.
- **Impact of Wrong Demonstrations:** Including wrong demonstrations in the context (Self-1C1W variants) generally **hurt performance** on BoolQ and MMLU, introducing noise rather than useful contrastive signals.
- **Effect of Summarization:** Summarizing demonstrations (Self-Sum-1C) nearly doubled alignment for the 1.7B student on BoolQ and MMLU but had mixed or even detrimental effects for the 0.6B student. This indicates that larger models can effectively extract key signals from concise summaries, while smaller models often require full, verbose reasoning traces to parse the information effectively. Similarly, a Qwen3-32B-generated solution (Self-1C (32B)) worked well for 0.6B on simpler tasks but poorly for 1.7B and on the more complex AIME tasks, possibly due to a reasoning style gap becoming too difficult for the student to follow.

#### 4.3. No Universal Recipe Exists

The optimal distillation strategy is highly conditional.
- Among self-distillation variants, contrastive examples (correct + wrong, i.e., Self-1C1W) were detrimental on simple reasoning tasks (BoolQ/MMLU) but proved beneficial on hard mathematical reasoning problems (AIME). This implies that for highly complex tasks, seeing common mistakes provides valuable explicit contrastive signal, whereas on simpler tasks it is merely distracting noise.
- External teachers outperformed self-distillation for 1.7B on BoolQ but not on MMLU. This indicates that the most effective teacher or context depends on the specific task characteristics and difficulty. These findings collectively advocate for per-task, per-token diagnostic analyses rather than fixed, universally applied distillation pipelines.

#### 4.4. Divergence Predicts Alignment, but Weakly

Within individual reasoning paths, a positive correlation was observed between teacher-student divergence (measured by KL divergence, JS divergence, or L2 distance between probability distributions) and gradient alignment. Conversely, distributional similarity correlated negatively with alignment. This suggests that the most useful distillation signals occur where the teacher and student genuinely disagree. However, the correlations were uniformly weak (absolute Spearman correlation coefficient |ρ| < 0.05). This implies that while divergence may be a necessary condition for a helpful signal, it is not a reliable predictor of alignment on its own, as high divergence can still lead to negatively aligned, harmful guidance.

#### 4.5. Case Studies: Mathematical Reasoning (AIME 2025)

Analysis of four AIME 2025 questions (with reasoning traces up to 30K tokens) further reinforced the primary finding: **incorrect paths exhibited higher alignment** across all questions. However, the optimal teacher choice diverged from BoolQ/MMLU results. Self-1C1W (including a wrong demonstration) was identified as the best teacher on the two harder AIME questions, contradicting its negative impact on shorter reasoning tasks. This supports the "comprehensibility" hypothesis, where on hard math, a wrong demonstration might provide valuable contrastive insight to avoid common errors. Additionally, summarized contexts lost to raw demonstrations on hard math for the 0.6B student, suggesting that compacting complex arguments can hinder a smaller model's ability to decipher them. These case studies underscore the necessity of adaptive distillation strategies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel and granular diagnostic framework that significantly advances the understanding of on-policy distillation. By quantifying, at the token level, whether a teacher's guidance aligns with successful outcomes, the work offers a mechanistic explanation for observed phenomena in distillation, moving beyond aggregate performance metrics.

The **significance** of this work is multi-faceted:
- **Enhanced Understanding of OPD:** It provides empirical evidence and a detailed analytical tool to explain *when* and *why* on-policy distillation helps or hurts, depending on factors like student capacity, context design, and the correctness of the student's current reasoning path. This addresses a critical gap in the field, where OPD's effectiveness has often been observed but not thoroughly understood at a mechanistic level.
- **Principled Guidance for Practitioners:** The findings offer concrete, principled guidance for designing more effective distillation strategies. Instead of relying on costly and opaque trial-and-error, practitioners can leverage insights such as prioritizing distillation on failing trajectories, tailoring context design to student capacity and task difficulty, and recognizing that no single universal distillation recipe exists.
- **Offline Diagnostic Tool:** The framework itself serves as a general-purpose, training-free offline testbed for evaluating any token-level training algorithm. This allows for rapid iteration and pre-screening of distillation configurations without incurring the high computational cost of full training runs.
- **Unifying Perspectives:** By demonstrating that reward-based and distillation objectives share a similar local gradient structure, the work unifies these distinct perspectives, enabling direct comparison and analysis across different training paradigms.

The **potential impact** of this research extends to several areas:
- **Optimization of LLM Training:** The insights can inform the development of more sophisticated and efficient distillation algorithms. For instance, future work could focus on rollout-weighted distillation that selectively emphasizes teacher signals on incorrect trajectories, where alignment is highest.
- **Adaptive Distillation Strategies:** The finding that optimal teacher configurations are task- and capacity-dependent motivates research into adaptive context selection and multi-teacher distillation, where complementary signals from multiple teachers could be combined for improved overall alignment.
- **Gradient Filtering:** The weak but consistent positive correlation between teacher-student divergence and alignment suggests that divergence-based filters could be incorporated into training to selectively apply distillation gradients only where a teacher's signal is likely to be beneficial, thus avoiding noisy or harmful updates.
- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated for reasoning tasks, the diagnostic framework could potentially be adapted to analyze other fine-tuning techniques for LLMs or sequential decision-making problems, providing a granular lens into their mechanisms.

In conclusion, this research provides a rigorous analytical framework that moves beyond black-box evaluations of on-policy distillation, offering clear insights into its dynamics and paving the way for more informed and effective training methodologies for reasoning models.
