# COMPASS: Cross-embodiment Mobility Policy via Residual RL and Skill Synthesis

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called COMPASS enables robust cross-embodiment robot mobility, utilizing expert demonstrations from only a single robot type. It integrates imitation learning, residual reinforcement learning, and policy distillation to achieve high success rates and efficient navigation across diverse robot morphologies in simulation and real-world environments with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Problem

- Developing robust mobility policies that generalize across diverse robot embodiments with varying hardware, kinematics, and sensing configurations is challenging.
- Classical mobility approaches require extensive manual retuning for each new robot, limiting scalability and rapid deployment.
- Learning-based approaches, like imitation learning, demand distinct, high-quality demonstration datasets for every new embodiment, which is expensive and time-consuming.

## Method

- **COMPASS** employs a three-stage workflow: first, ==Imitation Learning (IL)== pre-trains a base mobility policy using demonstrations from a single standard mobile robot.
- Second, Residual Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tunes this base policy for specific robot embodiments by learning small corrective adjustments, leveraging the IL prior for faster convergence.
- Third, Policy Distillation consolidates the embodiment-specific residual RL specialists into a single generalist policy by minimizing KL divergence between its outputs and the specialists' recorded action distributions.

## Results

- Residual RL specialists achieved 5X to 40X higher success rates and approximately 3X more efficient travel time compared to the Imitation Learning baseline (X-Mobility) across various embodiments and scenarios.
- The distilled generalist policy maintained performance comparable to, and in some cases slightly exceeding, that of individual specialist policies, demonstrating successful cross-embodiment generalization.
- COMPASS policies showed robust zero-shot transferability to real robot platforms (NVIDIA Carter and Unitree G1), achieving approximately **80%** success rates in cluttered real-world environments without additional fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging a strong, pre-trained imitation learning policy as a mobility prior significantly mitigates the sparsity issues common in reinforcement learning for complex tasks, leading to faster and more stable convergence for specialist policies.
> - Residual RL provides an effective mechanism to adapt a general base policy to embodiment-specific constraints by learning only the necessary corrective actions, avoiding the need to retrain the entire policy.
> - Policy distillation can effectively consolidate multiple embodiment-specific policies into a single generalist policy without substantial performance degradation, allowing it to condition decisions on robot morphology via an embodiment embedding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has witnessed advancements in deploying robots across various domains. A persistent challenge remains the development of robust mobility policies that can generalize across diverse robot embodiments. Robots exhibit morphological differences in hardware, kinematics, and sensing configurations, leading to significant variations in dynamics and perception. These differences complicate the creation of a universal and adaptable mobility policy.

Classical mobility approaches, such as traditional navigation stacks, are often effective for specific robot platforms (e.g., wheeled robots) but necessitate extensive manual retuning or redevelopment when applied to new embodiments. This per-robot engineering requirement limits scalability.

Learning-based approaches, particularly imitation learning (IL), have been explored as an alternative to scale mobility policies by leveraging data-driven training. However, IL typically requires a distinct set of high-quality demonstrations for each new robot embodiment. As morphological and sensor differences between robots increase, the demand for embodiment-specific data also rises, becoming prohibitively expensive or impractical for complex modalities like humanoids. Furthermore, IL can suffer from distribution shift issues, where policies may fail when encountering states not present in the training data. While methods exist to mitigate distribution shift within a single embodiment, extending them to greater morphological diversity amplifies data requirements and training complexity.

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a framework that aims to achieve scalable cross-embodiment mobility from demonstrations collected on only a single embodiment. It seeks to leverage shared mobility principles across robots while efficiently adapting to embodiment-specific constraints, thereby reducing the data collection burden and enhancing generalization capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework named COMPASS (Cross-embOdiment Mobility Policy via ResiduAl RL and Skill Synthesis) that enables scalable cross-embodiment mobility using expert demonstrations from only a single robot embodiment.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key observations and challenges in current robotics research:

- **Scalability Limitations of Classical Methods:** Classical mobility stacks, while effective for specific robots, require extensive and costly manual retuning or complete redesign for each new embodiment due to differences in hardware, kinematics, and sensor suites. This engineering overhead hinders the rapid deployment of robots across diverse platforms.
- **Data Bottleneck in Learning-Based Approaches:** Imitation learning (IL) offers a data-driven alternative, but its application to diverse embodiments faces a significant bottleneck. Each new robot typically requires its own set of high-quality demonstrations, which can be expensive, time-consuming, or even impractical to collect, especially for complex or physically demanding tasks (e.g., humanoids).
- **Distribution Shift in Imitation Learning:** IL policies can be brittle and fail when encountering states not present in the demonstration data (out-of-distribution states), a problem exacerbated by morphological diversity.
- **Shared Mobility Principles:** The authors observe that many fundamental aspects of mobility, such as environmental understanding, obstacle avoidance, and goal reaching, are inherently shared across different robot embodiments despite their physical differences. This insight suggests that it should be possible to build a cross-embodiment mobility policy by leveraging demonstrations from a single embodiment, rather than requiring separate datasets for each.

By addressing these issues, COMPASS aims to provide a data-efficient and robust solution for deploying mobility policies across a wide range of robot designs without extensive, embodiment-specific data collection or engineering. The goal is to amortize the effort of obtaining expert demonstrations, allowing for robust generalization and adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The COMPASS framework employs a three-stage workflow: (1) Imitation Learning for mobility priors, (2) Residual Reinforcement Learning (RL) to fine-tune specialist policies, and (3) Policy Distillation to combine these specialists into a generalist cross-embodiment policy.

#### 3.1. Problem Statement

The task is defined as point-to-point mobility across different robotic embodiments. At each time step `t`, the robot observes a state `x_t = (I_t, v_t, g_t, e)`, where `I_t` is camera input, `v_t` is measured velocity, `g_t` is route/goal information, and `e` is an embodiment embedding. The policy `π_θ` maps `x_t` to a velocity command `a_t = (v_t, ω_t)`, which is executed by a low-level joint controller. A reward function `R(·)` encourages efficient, collision-free progress to the goal. The objective is to maximize the expected discounted return, with the challenge being a single policy that leverages `e` to accommodate morphological constraints.

#### 3.2. Step 1: Imitation Learning for Mobility Priors

The first stage establishes a generic mobility baseline. This involves pre-training a base policy using imitation learning (IL) with expert demonstrations from a single standard mobile robot.
- **Latent State Modeling:** A world model is trained to capture environment dynamics. Raw observations `o_t = (I_t, v_t)` are used to learn a latent state `s_t`. The world model predicts `s_t = f_φ(s_{t-1}, a_{t-1})` and reconstructs `o_t = g_ψ(s_t)`. Training minimizes reconstruction and predictive losses over expert demonstrations `𝒟`.
- **Policy Learning in Latent Space:** A policy `π^IL_θ` is trained to predict `a_t` from a policy state `p_t`. `p_t` is fused from the latent state `s_t` and a route embedding `r_t = f_θ(g_t)`. Training minimizes the action discrepancy `ℓ(π^IL_θ(p_t), a^*_t)` between policy outputs and teacher actions. The world model aids generalization by providing a robust encoded representation.
- **X-Mobility:** The X-Mobility framework, which integrates an autoregressive world model with a velocity-prediction policy, is leveraged as the base policy. Its latent state encapsulates environment dynamics, and the policy head combines this with route information to generate velocity commands.

#### 3.3. Step 2: Residual RL to Fine-Tune Specialists

This stage refines the pre-trained IL policy for specific robot embodiments using residual reinforcement learning (RL).
- **Residual Policy Setup:** The base action `a^base_t = π^base_θ(p_t)` is augmented by a residual policy `π^res_φ` that outputs a correction `a^res_t`. The final action is `a_t = π^base_θ(p_t) + π^res_φ(p̂_t)`. The `π^res_φ` is designed to make small corrective adjustments.
- **Residual Policy State:** A residual policy state `p̂_t = 𝒢(p_t)` augments the base policy state `p_t` with additional task-relevant information (e.g., goal heading) not considered by the base policy, allowing modular extension without retraining the base policy.
- **Residual Policy Network Architecture:** The residual policy network reuses the base policy model architecture, initialized by copying base policy weights, but with a reinitialized output layer to learn only the residual correction. The critic network is a standard multi-layer perceptron (MLP).
- **Reward Design:** A reward function `R` promotes safe and efficient mobility, including components for progress (distance reduction), collision avoidance (penalties), and goal completion (large positive reward).
- **Training Loop:** A PPO-based RL optimizer trains `π^res_φ` while `π^base_θ` remains frozen. The agent executes combined actions in a simulation environment, observes next states and rewards, and updates `π^res_φ` via gradient methods. The environment resets on goal, collision, or timeout. This framework mitigates sparse sampling by building on a robust base policy, enabling faster convergence.

#### 3.4. Step 3: Policy Distillation to Combine Specialists

After training embodiment-specific residual RL specialists, they are consolidated into a single multi-embodiment policy through policy distillation.
- **Data Collection from Specialists:** After residual RL training, each specialist's input and output distributions (policy state `p`, one-hot embodiment identifier `e`, and mean/variance of Gaussian action distribution from PPO) are recorded. This logged dataset forms the basis for distillation.
- **Distillation Method:** A distilled policy `π^dist_θ` is trained to output `μ_θ(p, e)` by minimizing the KL divergence between its output distribution `𝒩(μ_θ(p, e^(i)), σ^2_θ)` and the recorded specialist distributions `𝒩(μ^(i)(p), σ^2)` for each embodiment `i`.
- **Embodiment Embedding:** A one-hot encoding vector `e` is used to represent each robot's morphology, allowing the distilled policy to condition decisions on the specific robot type.
- **Distilled Policy Network Architecture:** The distilled policy uses the same latent processing pipeline but conditions on the embodiment embedding `e` before generating the final action distribution, consisting of an MLP for mean prediction and a global variance parameter.

This three-step framework sequentially addresses general mobility priors, embodiment-specific refinements, and consolidated generalization into a single policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address four primary questions, alongside ablation studies and application demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Residual RL Performance for Embodiment Specialists

Residual RL effectively yielded high-performance embodiment specialists. Compared to the Imitation Learning (IL) baseline (X-Mobility), specialist policies significantly outperformed X-Mobility across all embodiments and scenarios (Table 1).
- **Success Rate (SR):** Specialists achieved 5X to 40X higher SR.
- **Weighted Travel Time (WTT):** Specialists demonstrated approximately 3X improvement in travel efficiency.
- **Generalization:** X-Mobility performed best on the Carter robot (its training platform) but showed significant performance degradation when deployed zero-shot on other embodiments (H1, Spot, G1), highlighting the necessity for embodiment-specific adaptations.
- **X-Mobility on Carter:** A performance drop for Carter in warehouse environments compared to the original X-Mobility paper was noted, attributed to simplified routes and rendering variations in Isaac Lab.

#### 4.2. Policy Distillation for a Robust Generalist

Policy distillation successfully consolidated specialist policies into a single generalist policy without a substantial performance drop.
- **Performance Comparison:** The generalist policy achieved performance comparable to, and in some cases, slightly exceeding that of the individual specialist policies (Table 1), particularly for the G1 robot, suggesting benefits from increased diversity during distillation.
- **Cross-embodiment Capability:** An example showcased G1 shortening its path by navigating under a shelf while H1, due to height limitations, took a detour (Figure 6), illustrating the generalist's ability to account for distinct morphological constraints.

#### 4.3. Comparison to Training from Scratch

Training a policy from scratch using the same RL setup but without leveraging the IL base actions (RL-from-scratch) struggled to converge even after 1,000 episodes (Figure 7). In contrast, the residual RL method demonstrated notably faster convergence, indicating that leveraging an IL baseline effectively mitigates sparsity issues common in RL for mobility tasks.

#### 4.4. Sim-to-Real Transferability

The COMPASS policy, trained in simulation, exhibited robust zero-shot transfer to real robot platforms (NVIDIA Jetson Orin-powered Carter and Unitree G1).
- **Real-world Performance:** Both robots successfully navigated cluttered environments without additional fine-tuning, achieving approximately 80% success rate across 20 trials with randomly placed obstacles.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Onboard inference with TensorRT required approximately 30 ms per step (Table 2), indicating computational efficiency suitable for real-time deployment.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Residual RL:**
 - **Curriculum on Goal Distance:** Introducing a curriculum did not outperform the baseline, suggesting that the strong IL base policy might make extensive curriculum less beneficial, potentially limiting exploration in challenging scenarios (Table 3).
 - **Critic State:** Providing an explicit depth image to the critic via a ResNet18 improved performance in office scenarios (where tall obstacles are present) but reduced performance in warehouse environments with low-lying obstacles, indicating context-dependent benefits (Table 3).
 - **Training Environment:** Restricting training to only warehouse environments (instead of a combined environment) slightly enhanced performance in warehouse scenes but reduced it in office and combined settings, highlighting the importance of diverse training environments for generalization (Table 3).
- **Policy Distillation:**
 - **Loss Function (KL vs. MSE):** Minimizing KL divergence (which preserves variance) yielded slightly better performance than using only MSE loss on mean predictions, particularly for the H1 robot (Table 4). This suggests that maintaining variance offers more flexibility for managing training noise.
 - **Dataset Quality (All vs. Good):** Constructing a dataset by excluding failure cases only marginally improved performance (Table 4). The authors hypothesize that removing failure cases might eliminate valuable corner cases that could aid generalist policy learning.

#### 4.6. Applications

- **Open Vocabulary Object Navigation:** COMPASS's flexible interface allows integration with object localization models (e.g., Locate3D) to enable open-vocabulary object navigation by generating target poses from language prompts (Figure 8).
- **Synthetic Datasets for VLA Fine Tuning:** The high-quality RL specialist policies can generate large-scale synthetic datasets. Post-training the Gr00t N1.5 model on a COMPASS distillation dataset (from G1 expert) resulted in robust navigation capabilities. This fine-tuned Gr00t model achieved success rates comparable to COMPASS in in-distribution warehouse environments and significantly outperformed COMPASS in an out-of-distribution hospital environment (Table 5). It also demonstrated seamless zero-shot Sim2Real transfer for loco-manipulation tasks (Figure 9).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The COMPASS framework represents a systematic approach to addressing the challenge of robust and scalable mobility across diverse robot embodiments, integrating imitation learning, residual reinforcement learning, and policy distillation. The significance of this work lies in its potential to advance robot autonomy and deployment by:

- **Reducing Data Requirements:** By leveraging expert demonstrations from only a single embodiment, COMPASS significantly reduces the data collection burden typically associated with scaling learning-based policies to new robot designs. This makes the development and deployment of mobility solutions more efficient and cost-effective, particularly for complex and data-intensive robot types like humanoids.
- **Enhancing Generalization and Adaptability:** The framework demonstrates robust generalization across a wide range of robot platforms (wheeled, quadrupeds, humanoids) and adaptability to various environment configurations. The generalist policy's ability to account for embodiment-specific morphological constraints (e.g., different height clearances) without explicit per-robot training is a notable advancement.
- **Improving Training Efficiency:** The use of an IL-based pre-trained policy as a foundation for residual RL significantly accelerates convergence compared to training RL policies from scratch, mitigating the sparse sampling challenges inherent in complex mobility tasks.
- **Enabling Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstration of robust zero-shot transferability to real-world robots indicates the practical applicability of policies trained within the Isaac Lab simulation environment, reducing the need for costly and time-consuming real-world fine-tuning.
- **Modular and Extensible Design:** The three-stage workflow allows for modular improvements at each step. The flexible architecture of COMPASS also facilitates integration with other robotic capabilities, such as open-vocabulary object navigation, by combining it with object localization models.
- **Facilitating Foundational Model Training:** The high-quality synthetic datasets generated by COMPASS specialist policies can be leveraged to fine-tune foundational Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for navigation tasks, contributing to the development of more generalist robot intelligence.

Potential future impacts suggested by the authors include exploring learned embodiment embedding strategies for broader or continuous embodiment spectrums, understanding the trade-offs in policy distillation (e.g., averaging effects), developing specialized designs and tuned hyperparameters for optimal performance across all embodiments, and integrating hierarchical mobility stacks for enhanced long-horizon route planning in complex environments. These directions could further broaden the applicability and performance of cross-embodiment mobility policies.
