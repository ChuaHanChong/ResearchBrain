# UniWAM Technical Report: Unified Mobile Manipulation via Mixed-Stream World-Action Modeling and Manipulation Anchor Pose Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> UniWAM is a mobile-manipulation world-action model. Navigation and manipulation run as separate streams, each with its own views, action encoder and head, on top of shared Wan2.2 video and action DiTs. Each training sample supervises only one stream. Training also uses MAP-Data, 1.5M synthetic episodes labeled with Manipulation Anchor Poses. On MAP-Bench, position error falls 30.1% and heading error 44.0%. Real-robot mobile-manipulation success reaches 80.0%.

## Problem

- Unified VLA and world-action policies either share one action token or split experts only inside the action module, though navigation and manipulation need different views, action spaces and control rates.
- Joint supervision of both behaviors in every sample wastes training on idle arm or base commands. It also blocks direct use of navigation-only or manipulation-only data, which are cheaper to collect.
- Prior work learns the manipulation-ready stopping pose only implicitly, from costly full trajectories. Base-placement methods output terminal candidates or affordance maps and never supervise the approach trajectory itself.

## Method

- ==Mixed-stream batch packing==: navigation and manipulation samples become separate batch rows with no cross-row attention. They share video and action DiT weights through ==mixture-of-transformers== attention, plus learnable per-stream tokens.
- **Manipulation Anchor Pose (MAP)**: a VLM-grounded front edge is ray-cast to the navigable boundary to set stop position and heading. ==Bezier trajectories== toward it, synthesized over HM3D, InteriorGS and in-house scenes, yield MAP-Data and MAP-Bench.
- Image-plane auxiliary targets: target boxes and projected MAP points for navigation, plus ==DINOv2== visibility-gated end-effector tracks for manipulation, are denoised jointly with the actions by ==flow matching==. Navigation predicts pose increments, and a causal mask handles the action prefix.

## Results

- On MAP-Bench, UniWAM-Scaled reaches **9.85 cm** and **2.30 degrees** overall, versus **14.1 cm** for GR00T N1.7 and **4.11 degrees** for pi0.5. Long-range position error is **14.2 cm**, versus **22.0 cm** for the best baseline.
- Across 24 real-robot tasks, mobile-manipulation success is **80.0%** versus **62.5%** for pi0.5, with non-overlapping Wilson intervals. Manipulation success is **68.9%** versus pi0.5's **71.4%**. Trained only on synthetic MAP-Data, the zero-shot model docks within **18.7 cm**.
- In controlled architecture tests, mixed-stream with independent sampling reaches **63.1%** success versus **46.9%** for unified action tokens. Velocity actions instead of pose increments raise long-range error from **21.0 to 30.9 cm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Splitting behaviors at the level of control streams, with separate inputs, encoders, heads and sampling, beats splitting only action experts. Shared weights still let heterogeneous single-behavior datasets reinforce each other.
> - Bimodal labels such as base velocities that sit mostly at zero or the limit are hard for continuous generative policies. Integrating them into pose increments makes targets smooth and improves long-horizon accuracy.
> - Explicit, cheaply synthesized geometric targets like the stopping pose scale better than implicit learning from full trajectories. The gains concentrate where errors compound, especially on long approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Before a robot's arm can work on an indoor object, the robot has to stop at a base position and heading from which the arm can actually reach and act. Getting near the object is not enough. The two halves of the job also need different things. Navigation benefits from a view of distant targets and free space, manipulation needs close views of the workspace, and the two use different actions.

A technical report from Fudan University's Physical Superintelligence Lab (Wei Xue, Keliang Liu, and colleagues) addresses both needs. Its model, UniWAM, keeps navigation and manipulation in one model while giving each its own inputs and action interfaces. It also teaches navigation an explicit place to stop, learned from automatically generated approach examples. The paper finds more accurate docking and higher success on complete mobile-manipulation tasks, while manipulation-only performance remains competitive rather than best.

A schematic, not a measured result. It shows mobile manipulation as precise navigation followed by reliable manipulation. The left side contrasts a coarse goal region with a pose learned implicitly from costly complete trajectories, and contrasts separate-policy handoff with joint action prediction. On the right, 3D-scene data produces explicit MAP supervision, which feeds separate navigation and manipulation rows, encoders, and heads over shared video/action DiTs.

### 2. A pose, not just a destination

The authors frame these as linked architecture and data problems. Joint training can tie the behaviors together even when one is idle. Collecting real-world trajectories with explicit docking-pose labels is costly.

Their proposed target is a Manipulation Anchor Pose (MAP): the base position and heading at which a robot should stop to manipulate a particular object. It is defined from scene geometry rather than from a particular manipulation policy.

The training data, called MAP-Data, consists of approach trajectories and image-plane labels generated automatically from 3D scenes. The pipeline has three steps:

1. Curate valid objects and scenes.
1. Identify the manipulation-facing surface, then compute a feasible MAP and observation poses.
1. Synthesize collision-checked approach trajectories.

Each frame records the RGB observation, the navigation action, the target bounding box, and the MAP position projected into the image. The dataset contains over 1.5 million episodes and 7,500 hours. With these labels, the navigation stream learns both where the object is in view and where the base should stop.

### 3. Two lanes, one model

Each behavior, or stream, has its own camera inputs, action encoder, and output head. Both streams use shared video and action transformer backbones.

During training, navigation and manipulation examples are drawn independently and packed into separate batch rows. The rows do not attend to one another, but they update the same shared weights. As a result, a navigation-only episode can train navigation without a paired manipulation command, and the reverse holds for manipulation-only episodes.

Each stream also predicts targets in the camera image alongside its actions. Navigation predicts target boxes and projected MAP points. Manipulation predicts end-effector tracks in the camera view alongside camera-frame arm actions.

The authors describe two earlier world-action approaches, [[2608.04657|MobileWAM]] and [[2607.00678|ABot-M0.5]], as separating the behaviors inside the action module, with shared visual inputs and both behaviors supervised in each sample. The paper does not directly compare against those implementations on the same benchmark.

### 4. Does the separation matter?

**Docking accuracy on MAP-Bench.** UniWAM-Scaled reports 9.85 cm overall position error and 2.30° overall heading error. The strongest external baselines report 14.1 cm (GR00T N1.7) and 4.11° (π₀.₅), respectively. The metrics are defined as follows:

- **Position error** is the mean distance between the reached and reference MAP positions.
- **Heading error** is the wrapped heading discrepancy.
- **"Overall"** averages three initial-distance ranges: short (<1.5 m), mid (1.5–3.5 m), and long (≥3.5 m).

All models were evaluated closed-loop on the same held-out starting poses and instructions, with 40,000 training steps on 16 H200 GPUs. Only UniWAM received bounding-box and MAP auxiliary targets. Benchmark targets are excluded from the supplementary MAP-Data, but other targets in benchmark scenes may be present.

**Controlled architecture test.** The test that best probes the mechanism holds initialization, data, budget, evaluation protocol, and image-plane auxiliary targets fixed. Under joint sampling, mixed streams have lower docking errors than streams concatenated in one sequence: 18.6 vs. 22.1 cm and 5.87° vs. 6.78°, at the same 60.0% success rate. Switching the mixed-stream model to independent sampling gives 17.1 cm, 5.30°, and 63.1% success. These are controlled variants within the authors' framework, not reimplementations of prior models.

Table 10 compares unified tokens, separate experts, sequence concatenation, and mixed-stream models with joint or independent sampling. Rows report position error (cm), heading error (degrees), and success rate (%) on the four Mobile Manipulation tasks. Lower errors and higher success are better. These are framework variants, not scores of prior papers.

**Real robots.** The evaluation covers 24 tasks across four embodiments. On Mobile Manipulation, UniWAM-Scaled reaches 80.0% success versus 62.5% for π₀.₅, with non-overlapping 95% intervals. On Manipulation-only tasks, its mean is 68.9%, below π₀.₅'s 71.4%, with overlapping intervals. UniWAM-Scaled also receives MAP-Data, 402 additional manipulation tasks, and more training compute. The real-robot result therefore does not isolate architecture from added data or compute.

### 5. Docking is learnable

UniWAM makes the manipulation-ready stopping pose an explicit target. It also lets navigation examples train that behavior separately while keeping a shared model for the later interaction. The measured advantage is on a particular MAP-navigation benchmark and, on real robots, on docking and complete mobile-manipulation tasks. It does not extend to manipulation alone.
