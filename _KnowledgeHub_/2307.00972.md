# MoVie: Visual Model-Based Policy Adaptation for View Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> MoVie, a method from researchers at Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute and affiliated universities, enables visual model-based reinforcement learning agents to adapt and generalize to unseen camera views during deployment. It consistently improved performance by 33% in DMControl, 86% in xArm, and 152% in Adroit tasks over non-adapted baselines.

## Problem

- Visual Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often fail to generalize effectively from fixed training camera views to novel or dynamically changing deployment views.
- Traditional generalization techniques, such as extensive data augmentation or domain randomization, are insufficient or computationally prohibitive for the complex and dynamic nature of 3D view changes.
- The inability to adapt to varying camera perspectives during deployment limits the practical applicability of visual RL in real-world robotics.
- Current robot learning approaches focusing on view-invariant representations are often constrained by the number of available camera views during training.

## Method

- **MoVie** introduces a ==test-time adaptation== mechanism that fine-tunes a ==Spatial Adaptive Encoder (SAE)== by incorporating ==Spatial Transformer Networks (STNs)== into its shallow layers.
- It leverages the pre-trained, frozen latent dynamics model of the base RL agent as a self-supervisory signal, guiding the SAE to produce latent representations consistent with the learned dynamics from novel views.
- The adaptation process occurs online during deployment, using a small replay buffer to collect recent transitions and update the SAE without modifying the original policy's training or requiring explicit rewards.

## Results

- MoVie achieved overall relative performance improvements of **33%** in DMControl, **86%** in xArm, and **152%** in Adroit tasks compared to non-adapted baselines across four view generalization settings.
- In robotic manipulation tasks, MoVie significantly increased success rates: from **28%** to **52%** in xArm and from **21%** to **53%** in Adroit, particularly demonstrating strong gains in novel and moving view scenarios.
- Ablation studies confirmed the efficacy of placing STN blocks in the initial encoder layers and highlighted that freezing the dynamics model provided better adaptation results than end-to-end fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Test-time adaptation of the visual encoder, guided by a stable, frozen dynamics model, is a crucial and effective strategy for enabling view generalization in visual model-based RL.
> - Integrating Spatial Transformer Networks into the shallow layers of the image encoder efficiently normalizes low-level spatial features affected by viewpoint changes, preserving the integrity of deeper semantic features.
> - A differential learning rate strategy for the Spatial Adaptive Encoder (faster for STN layers, slower for core encoder) optimizes the adaptation process, allowing rapid spatial alignment while maintaining feature stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Visual Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in various domains, including video games, robotic manipulation, and locomotion. However, a significant challenge for the practical deployment of visual RL agents is their ability to generalize from training environments to unseen scenarios, particularly concerning visual variations. This work specifically addresses the problem of **view generalization**, where an agent trained with a limited, fixed camera view must operate effectively when the camera's position, orientation, or field of view changes at deployment time.

The broader research landscape of visual generalization in RL can be categorized into several approaches. Many existing efforts have concentrated on generalizing to different appearances and backgrounds within a scene. These methods often employ techniques such as data augmentations (e.g., SODA) or domain randomization, which involve training with varied visual inputs to make the policy more robust. However, view generalization presents distinct challenges. When the deployment view is unknown and can vary in 3D space, standard data augmentations or exhaustively randomizing all possible views become less effective or computationally prohibitive. For instance, large-scale datasets like RoboNet, which feature diverse views for manipulation tasks, still yield limited success rates (10-20%) for unseen views, indicating the difficulty of this problem.

Previous work in robot learning has explored third-person imitation learning and view-invariant visual representations. These approaches are often constrained by the number of available camera views during training. In contrast, this research focuses on a more challenging scenario where agents are initially trained on a single fixed view and are expected to generalize to a wide range of dynamic and novel camera settings.

Another related area is test-time training, where neural networks are adapted during deployment using self-supervised learning objectives. Approaches like PAD, for example, incorporate an inverse dynamics model (IDM) objective into model-free policies for both training and test time to improve appearance generalization. This work distinguishes itself by focusing on visual model-based policies, requiring no modifications during the initial training phase, and specifically targeting view generalization through a self-supervised dynamics prediction objective.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable visual Model-based Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents, trained on limited and fixed camera views, to adapt and generalize their learned abilities to various unseen camera views during deployment. The motivation stems from the inherent difficulty of this "view generalization" problem, which poses a significant hurdle for real-world robotics applications.

The authors systematically categorize the view generalization problem into four distinct and challenging scenarios, which they argue closely resemble real-world situations:
1. **Novel View:** The camera changes to a new fixed position and orientation, different from the training view.
2. **Moving View:** The camera continuously moves along a predefined trajectory around the scene.
3. **Shaking View:** The camera experiences constant, random shaking due to noise applied to its position at each time step.
4. **Novel Field of View (FOV):** The camera's field of view is altered from its training configuration.

These formulations aim to provide a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating view generalization, facilitating advancements in research, and supporting the utilization of robot datasets and deployment on physical robotic platforms.

The core motivation behind the proposed approach, MoVie, is the hypothesis that successful adaptation to novel views during test time is crucial, rather than attempting to learn a perfectly view-invariant policy during initial training. The authors argue that existing visual generalization methods, often relying on data augmentations or domain randomization, are less effective or too costly for the complex and dynamic nature of view changes in 3D space. Consequently, the research aims to develop a straightforward yet effective mechanism for agents to adapt online to new visual inputs from varying camera perspectives without requiring explicit reward signals or modifications to the original training process. The goal is to improve the success rates of RL agents in real-world scenarios where camera views are highly variable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named MoVie (Visual Model-based policy adaptation for View generalization), is designed to enable visual RL agents to adapt to novel camera views during test time. It operates without modifying the training process of the base RL agent and does not require explicit reward signals during adaptation.

**Foundation:** MoVie builds upon model-based RL algorithms, specifically TD-MPC (Temporal Difference learning for Model Predictive Control) for DMControl and xArm environments, and MoDem (Accelerating visual Model-based reinforcement learning with Demonstrations) for Adroit environments. These algorithms learn a visual representation (an image encoder `h`) that maps high-dimensional observations `o` into a latent state `z`, and a latent dynamics model `d` that predicts the next latent state `z'` from the current latent state `z` and action `a`.

**Test-Time Adaptation Objective:**
During training, the latent dynamics prediction objective is defined as:
`L_dynamics = ||d(h(o_t), a_t) - h(o_{t+1})||^2`
where `h` is the image encoder and `d` is the latent dynamics model.

During test time, when observations `o` come from an unseen view `O'`, their corresponding latent space `Z'` also changes. The original encoder `h` is trained to map `O -> Z`, making it difficult for the policy (which maps `Z -> A`) to generalize to `Z' -> A`. MoVie addresses this by adapting the projection `h` to a new function `h'` that maps `O' -> Z`.

The core idea is to *freeze* the pre-trained latent dynamics model `d`, designating it as `d*`. This frozen dynamics model then serves as a self-supervisory signal. The image encoder `h` is modified into a **Spatial Adaptive Encoder (SAE)**, denoted `h_SAE`. The test-time adaptation objective then becomes:
`L_view = ||d*(h_SAE(o_t), a_t) - h_SAE(o_{t+1})||^2`
This objective guides the adaptation of `h_SAE` such that the new latent representations `h_SAE(o)` are consistent with the dynamics learned during training in the original latent space, even when observing novel views. The frozen dynamics model `d*` acts as an anchor, enabling the SAE to transform new visual inputs into a latent space compatible with the pre-trained policy.

**Spatial Adaptive Encoder (SAE):**
The SAE architecture integrates Spatial Transformer Networks (STNs) into the shallow layers of the original image encoder. STNs are designed to perform affine transformations on feature maps, effectively normalizing spatial variations. In MoVie, two STN blocks are inserted: one before the first convolutional layer and another before the second convolutional layer of the encoder. The motivation for placing STNs in shallow layers is that these layers process low-level visual features (e.g., RGB features), which are most directly affected by viewpoint changes. Transforming these early features is intended to align them with the patterns seen during training, thereby preserving the similarity of deeper, semantic features. Each STN block comprises a localization network (which predicts a 6-parameter affine transformation) and a grid sampler (which applies the transformation).

**Training Strategy for SAE:**
During test-time adaptation:
- A replay buffer of size 256 stores recent transitions `(o_t, a_t, o_{t+1})` collected from online interactions in the new view.
- After each interaction with the environment, the SAE is updated 32 times. Each update involves randomly sampling 32 tuples from the buffer.
- A differential learning rate strategy is employed: a learning rate of `1 × 10^-5` is used for the STN layers, and `1 × 10^-7` for the remaining parts of the encoder. This allows the STN components to adapt more quickly while the core encoder features adapt more gradually.

**View Generalization Settings:**
To systematically evaluate view generalization, the authors defined four scenarios:
1. **Novel View:** Camera position and orientation are fixed but different from training.
2. **Moving View:** Camera moves continuously along a trajectory.
3. **Shaking View:** Camera position has Gaussian noise added at each time step.
4. **Novel FOV:** Camera's field of view is altered.

These settings are applied across 18 tasks from three domains: 11 DMControl tasks, 4 xArm tasks, and 3 Adroit hand tasks, resulting in 18 × 4 configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across 18 tasks in three domains (DMControl, xArm, Adroit) and four view generalization settings, totaling 72 configurations (excluding ablations). Each experiment was run with 3 seeds and 20 episodes per seed. Cumulative rewards were reported for DMControl and success rates for robotic manipulation tasks (xArm, Adroit).

**Baselines:**
- **TD-MPC (or MoDem):** The original trained visual model-based policy without any test-time adaptation.
- **DM:** Test-time adaptation of the encoder using the dynamics model objective, but *without* STN blocks (i.e., MoVie without STN).
- **IDM+STN:** Test-time adaptation using an inverse dynamics model objective to predict actions, combined with STN blocks.

**Overall Performance:**
MoVie consistently demonstrated improved view generalization abilities across all evaluated domains and settings compared to baselines. The overall relative improvements over the non-adapted TD-MPC baseline were 33% in DMControl, 86% in xArm, and 152% in Adroit. These results suggest MoVie enhances performance in diverse visual conditions.

**Performance Across Domains and Settings:**
- **Robotic Manipulation Tasks (xArm and Adroit):** MoVie exhibited substantial improvements in these domains. For xArm, the overall success rate increased from 28% (TD-MPC) to 52% (MoVie), an 86% relative improvement. For Adroit, the overall success rate increased from 21% (TD-MPC) to 53% (MoVie), a 152% relative improvement.
- **DMControl Tasks:** MoVie also improved performance in DMControl, with an overall average reward increase from 492.71 (TD-MPC) to 656.43 (MoVie), representing a 33% relative improvement.
- **Specific Settings:**
 - **Novel View:** MoVie achieved a 57% relative improvement in DMControl, 188% in xArm, and 325% in Adroit over TD-MPC.
 - **Moving View:** MoVie showed an 11% relative improvement in DMControl, 110% in xArm, and 200% in Adroit.
 - **Novel FOV:** MoVie yielded a 46% relative improvement in DMControl, 121% in xArm, and 119% in Adroit.
- **Shaking View:** While MoVie generally improved performance (26% relative improvement in DMControl, 110% in Adroit), its performance in xArm tasks (45% success rate) was close to the TD-MPC baseline (42%). This indicates that the shaking view scenario presents a greater challenge for the method.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Integration of STN with Low-Level Features:** Experiments confirmed that placing STN blocks in the initial two convolutional layers of the encoder was most effective. Adding STNs to deeper layers did not provide significant additional benefits, supporting the hypothesis that transforming low-level spatial features is critical for view adaptation.
- **Different Novel Views:** Varying the difficulty of novel view settings (easy, medium, hard) demonstrated a consistent decrease in performance for all methods as difficulty increased. MoVie maintained its superior performance across these difficulty levels.
- **Efficacy of STN with IDM:** An ablation showed that integrating STN blocks also improved the performance of the IDM baseline, reinforcing the general effectiveness of the Spatial Adaptive Encoder for visual representation adaptation.
- **Finetune or Freeze the DM:** An ablation comparing freezing the dynamics model (MoVie) versus finetuning it end-to-end with the encoder showed that freezing the dynamics model produced better results. This supports the strategy of using the frozen dynamics model as a stable self-supervisory signal.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents MoVie as a method for adapting visual model-based policies to achieve view generalization, which has implications for the deployment of RL agents in dynamic and uncontrolled visual environments.

The systematic categorization of view generalization into four distinct settings (novel, moving, shaking, and novel FOV) provides a framework for evaluating and advancing research in this specific area of visual generalization. This categorization could help guide future development of robust RL agents.

The core contribution of MoVie, which involves finetuning a spatial adaptive image encoder using a frozen dynamics model as a self-supervised objective during test time, offers a practical approach to addressing view changes. A notable aspect is that MoVie requires no modifications during the initial training phase, suggesting that it can be applied to existing pre-trained model-based RL agents. The method also requires only small-scale online interactions for adaptation, which is relevant for scenarios where extensive online data collection or retraining is impractical.

The experimental results, showing consistent performance improvements across a wide range of tasks and particularly significant gains in robotic manipulation domains (xArm and Adroit), indicate the potential utility of MoVie. For instance, the relative improvements of 86% in xArm and 152% in Adroit suggest that MoVie could substantially enhance the reliability and applicability of visual RL in complex robotic systems where camera viewpoints are not static or perfectly controlled. The method's effectiveness in common scenarios like novel view, moving view, and novel FOV is particularly relevant for real-world robotic deployments.

While the study acknowledges the limitation of not conducting real robot experiments, the extensive evaluation on simulated robotic tasks and locomotion environments provides evidence for MoVie's capabilities. The findings suggest that the approach could enable the direct deployment of policies trained in simulation or offline datasets to real-world robots, potentially reducing the need for costly and time-consuming domain randomization or extensive data augmentation during the training phase. The potential to adapt existing trained policies with minimal online interaction implies improved success rates and broader utility of visual RL in practical applications.
