# Large Language Models are Pretty Good Zero-Shot Video Game Bug Detectors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Alberta investigated the zero-shot capabilities of large language models for video game bug detection and classification from textual gameplay descriptions. Their findings demonstrate that models like InstructGPT's davinci can identify buggy events with up to 70.66% accuracy and classify bug types with 44.01% accuracy, establishing a new method for automated quality assurance.

## Problem

- Fully automating video game testing is challenging due to the need for both game-specific contextual knowledge and common sense reasoning.
- Traditional AI agents often struggle with nuanced bug identification and understanding intended game mechanics, necessitating reliance on manual human testing.
- Existing automated methods (e.g., heuristic search, reinforcement learning, computer vision) lack the general reasoning capabilities required to detect novel bugs from diverse event sequences.

## Method

- The study formulated the video game bug detection problem as a ==zero-shot question-answering== task for large language models based on textual event sequences.
- A ==two-stage prompting== technique, incorporating ==trigger sentences==, was used to guide LLMs through step-by-step reasoning and precise answer extraction for both bug identification and classification.
- A new benchmark dataset, "**GameBugDescriptions**," was created, featuring 167 real-world buggy gameplay videos with detailed textual event descriptions and labeled bug types.

## Results

- The InstructGPT `davinci` model achieved the highest accuracy for zero-shot buggy event identification at **70.66%** using the "Let’s reason the events according to the reference game" trigger sentence.
- The `davinci` model also demonstrated an overall accuracy of **44.01%** for bug type classification, with high performance for "Player Animation" (**96.43%**) and "Teleportation" (**75.00%**) bug types.
- LLMs showed relative robustness to different textual descriptions of the same event sequences, with performance variations mostly not being statistically significant across varied prompts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large language models can leverage their pre-trained knowledge to perform common sense and game-specific reasoning for bug detection in a zero-shot setting.
> - Carefully designed multi-stage prompting strategies, particularly with specific trigger sentences, can significantly enhance LLM performance and reasoning clarity.
> - LLMs exhibit a dual capability to both identify buggy events and categorize their types, offering more comprehensive feedback for game development.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Video game testing is a critical component of software quality assurance, ensuring that games are functional, engaging, and free of defects. However, automating game testing presents significant challenges, primarily due to the need for both game-specific contextual knowledge and common sense reasoning about in-game events. Traditional AI-driven agents can be trained to play games and detect some issues, but they often lack the capacity for common sense reasoning and an understanding of intended game mechanics, leading to difficulties in identifying subtle or logically inconsistent bugs. This often necessitates reliance on manual human testers, a process that is resource-intensive and not fully scalable.

Prior work in automated game testing has explored various techniques. Some approaches focus on automatically playing games using heuristic search strategies, reinforcement learning, or evolutionary strategies. While these methods can explore different gameplay paths, they are typically optimized for specific reward functions and may not effectively identify bugs that arise from unexpected interactions or violations of game rules. Other research has leveraged computer vision for graphical bug detection or Natural Language Processing (NLP) for retrieving gameplay moments based on text queries or improving test case descriptions. However, these methods often require pre-existing knowledge of bug instances or lack the general reasoning capabilities needed to detect previously undiscovered bugs based on a sequence of events.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has introduced a new paradigm, demonstrating capabilities in tasks for which they were not explicitly trained, particularly in zero-shot and few-shot learning settings. These models, trained on vast textual datasets, are hypothesized to acquire extensive world knowledge, including information pertinent to video games. This study positions itself at the intersection of language modeling, video game research, and software engineering. It specifically aims to bridge the gap in automated game testing by exploring whether LLMs can provide the necessary common sense and game-specific reasoning to identify and classify video game bugs without explicit fine-tuning for this task. The paper represents an initial empirical evaluation of LLMs as zero-shot video game bug detectors, distinguishing itself from prior work by focusing on the interpretation of textual event sequences to detect novel bugs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties in fully automating video game testing. As highlighted, automated game testing tools face two main obstacles: the requirement for extensive game-specific knowledge to understand valid and invalid behaviors within a particular game's world, and the need for common sense reasoning to interpret event sequences and identify anomalies that constitute bugs. Current AI-driven agents often struggle with these nuanced aspects, frequently leading to situations where manual human testing remains indispensable. Human testers possess an intuitive understanding of expected gameplay and can identify illogical or unintended events that signify a bug. The goal of this work is to explore if advanced artificial intelligence, specifically large language models, can emulate this human capacity for reasoning about game events.

The key objectives of this study are as follows:

1. **To investigate the zero-shot capabilities of large language models for video game bug detection.** The central aim is to determine if LLMs, without any task-specific training or fine-tuning, can accurately identify buggy events within a sequence of textual descriptions of gameplay. This involves formulating the bug detection problem as a question-answering task, leveraging the LLM's inherent language understanding and reasoning abilities.
2. **To introduce a novel benchmark dataset for video game bug detection.** To facilitate the evaluation of LLMs in this context, the researchers aimed to create the "GameBugDescriptions" dataset. This dataset is designed to provide real-world buggy gameplay videos with corresponding textual event descriptions and bug type labels, serving as a resource for assessing LLM performance in this out-of-distribution (OOD) task.
3. **To extensively evaluate the performance of different large language model families.** The study aimed to test various models from prominent LLM families, specifically InstructGPT and OPT, across different model sizes, to understand their varying capacities for bug detection and the impact of architectural differences.
4. **To analyze the robustness of language models to variations in event descriptions.** Recognizing that textual descriptions of the same event sequence might differ, a further objective was to assess how consistently LLMs identify bugs when presented with alternative textual representations of the same underlying video.
5. **To explore the capability of LLMs for bug type classification.** Beyond merely identifying a buggy event, the research aimed to determine if LLMs could also accurately categorize the type of bug (e.g., collision, animation, logic error), which is valuable for bug prioritization and remediation efforts in software development.

By addressing these objectives, the researchers sought to demonstrate the potential of LLMs to play a significant role in automating complex aspects of video game quality assurance, thereby potentially reducing reliance on manual testing and enhancing the efficiency of game development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study involved formulating the video game bug detection problem as a question-answering task for large language models. The overall approach can be broken down into problem formulation, a multi-stage prompting technique, a secondary task for bug type classification, dataset creation, and experimental setup.

**Problem Formulation:**
For a given video game `G` and a sequence `S` of textual descriptions of events `{e1, e2, ..., en}`, the task is to identify which event `ei` is a bug. This is structured as a triplet `<Q, S, A>`, where `Q` is a question template, `S` provides the multiple-choice options (events a, b, c, d), and `A` is the correct answer (the buggy event). To provide context, the question template `Q` explicitly includes the game's name (e.g., "In the [G] video game, the following sequence of events happened: [S] Which event is a bug?").

**Multi-Stage Prompting:**
A two-stage prompting technique was utilized to encourage step-by-step reasoning in the LLMs, inspired by previous work.

1. **Stage 1 - Elicit Reasoning:** The input to the LLM is formatted as "Q: [X]. [Z]", where `[X]` is the concatenation of the question template `Q` (including the game name and event sequence `S`) and `[Z]` is a "trigger sentence". The trigger sentences serve two purposes: to guide the LLM towards a progressive reasoning process and to inject different perspectives (e.g., reasoning as a game designer or tester, or comparing with real-world physics). Seven different trigger sentences were tested, including "Let’s think step by step" and game-specific cues like "Let’s reason the events according to the reference game". The LLM then generates textual reasoning leading to an intermediate answer.
2. **Stage 2 - Answer Extraction:** The output from Stage 1 (the input and the generated reasoning) is then concatenated with an "answer extraction" prompt. For the multiple-choice format, the prompt "Among (a) through (d), the answer is" was used to instruct the LLM to extract the final choice based on its previous reasoning. This approach avoids external text processing for answer extraction.

**Bug Type Classification:**
As a secondary objective, the models were evaluated on their ability to classify bug types. For this task, a different question template was used: "In the [G] video game, the following sequence of events happened: [eb] What is the type of bug? [O]". Here, `[eb]` is the identified buggy event, and `[O]` provides multiple-choice options for the bug types. No trigger sentences were used for this task.

**Dataset: GameBugDescriptions:**
A custom dataset was created to evaluate the models.
- **Source:** Started with the `GamePhysics` dataset, sampling 200 videos from 8 popular video games.
- **Filtering:** Videos that did not clearly showcase a bug, contained severe graphical glitches, or involved game modifications were manually excluded. This resulted in 167 suitable videos.
- **Labeling:** Two authors independently created step-by-step textual descriptions for each video, up to four sentences long, without interpreting whether an event was a bug. This generated two distinct descriptions (Descr1 and Descr2) for each video, leading to 334 question-answer pairs. This dual description approach allowed for assessing model robustness to linguistic variation.
- **Bug Type Classification:** One author manually classified each video into a single bug type, which was then refined and finalized by another author. Nine distinct bug types were identified (e.g., Player Animation, Teleportation, Collision, Irregular force, Logic). The "Irregular force" type was the most frequent.
- **Quality Assessment:** The CLIP Score was calculated between each sentence description and video frames, with a median score of 0.30 reported, indicating a reasonable similarity threshold.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Models:** Six LLMs were tested: four from the InstructGPT family (ada, babbage, curie, davinci, ranging from 0.3B to 175B parameters) and two from the OPT family (66B and 175B parameters).
- **Parameters:** A temperature of 0 was used for all models to encourage deterministic outputs. The stopping sequence was set to "Q:" to prevent repetition. `max_tokens` was set to 256 for Stage 1 and 32 for Stage 2.
- **Evaluation:**
 - **Buggy Event Identification:** Accuracy was calculated for each set of descriptions (Descr1 and Descr2) and averaged across models and trigger sentences. Accuracy per game was also calculated for the top-performing model. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess the statistical significance of differences in accuracy between the two description sets, evaluating robustness.
 - **Bug Type Classification:** Accuracy was determined per bug type and across the entire dataset for the best-performing model from the first task.
- **Inference:** InstructGPT models were accessed via the OpenAI API, while OPT models were run on an NVIDIA DGX System with 8xA100 GPUs using their official implementation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the application of large language models for zero-shot video game bug detection and classification.

**Informal Assessment of Game Knowledge:**
An initial informal analysis was conducted to ascertain if LLMs possessed game-specific knowledge. Using a set of 34 multiple-choice and 10 free-format questions designed by the researchers, the `davinci` model from the InstructGPT family demonstrated a high level of game knowledge, correctly answering 91% of the questions. This indicated that LLMs could serve as a foundational component for game-related reasoning tasks.

**Task 1: Buggy Event Identification:**
The evaluation of LLMs for identifying buggy events in a zero-shot setting demonstrated promising results.

- **Overall Performance:** LLMs exhibited a capability to detect video game bugs based on textual descriptions. Performance varied significantly depending on the model size and the specific trigger sentence used in the prompting strategy.
- **Best Performing Model and Trigger:** The `davinci` model (InstructGPT family, 175B parameters) achieved the highest accuracy. With the trigger sentence "Let’s reason the events according to the reference game" and the first set of descriptions (Descr1), it reached an accuracy of 70.66%. The average accuracy for the `davinci` model across both description sets and all trigger sentences was 65.27%.
- **Model and Trigger Sentence Variation:** No consistent trends were observed where a single trigger sentence universally optimized performance across all models. For instance, while trigger sentence #1 performed best for `davinci`, for the OPT-66B model, "According to the rules of the game" yielded the highest average performance (40.12%). Generally, larger models (like `davinci` and `curie`) outperformed smaller ones, though the OPT-175B model surprisingly showed lower performance than OPT-66B, which was later attributed to issues with the answer extraction stage.
- **Robustness to Descriptions:** The `davinci` model demonstrated relative robustness to different textual descriptions of the same event sequence (Descr1 vs. Descr2). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test indicated that only trigger sentence #1 led to a statistically significant difference in accuracy between the two description sets. For most other trigger sentences, the model's performance was consistent across varied descriptions.

**Task 2: Bug Type Classification:**
The ability of the `davinci` model (selected as the best-performing from Task 1) to classify bug types was also assessed.

- **Overall Accuracy:** The `davinci` model achieved an overall accuracy of 44.01% in correctly classifying bug types across the dataset.
- **Performance by Bug Type:** Accuracy varied considerably across the nine defined bug types. "Player Animation" bugs were classified with very high accuracy (96.43%). Other types like "Teleportation" and "Graphics" also showed reasonable accuracy (75.00% and 70.00% respectively). However, "Irregular force" (24.38%) and "Logic" (16.67%) were the most challenging bug types for the model to classify. "Irregular force" was the most frequent bug type in the dataset and was often confused with other physics-related bugs (Collision, Graphics, Animation, Teleportation).
- **Performance by Game:** The `davinci` model performed best on the "Fallout 4" game (42.11% average accuracy for bug type classification) and worst on "Red Dead Redemption 2" (32.70%). The poor performance on "Red Dead Redemption 2" was correlated with the high proportion of "Irregular force" bugs in that game's subset of the dataset.

**Discussion of Failure Cases:**
An analysis of `davinci` model failures revealed recurring patterns:

- **Wrong Reasoning and Wrong Prediction:** In some instances, the model's intermediate reasoning steps were vague or incorrect, leading to an incorrect final prediction without proper justification (e.g., misinterpreting player death from a single bullet in Fallout 4).
- **Reasoning Does Not Match Prediction:** Occasionally, the model arrived at a correct final prediction, but its step-by-step reasoning did not adequately justify that specific choice, indicating a potential disconnect between intermediate thought processes and the ultimate answer extraction.
- **Wrong Assumptions:** The model sometimes made incorrect assumptions about in-game physics or logic, such as attributing an event to a non-existent earthquake in Far Cry 5.
- **Problem with Reference Game:** Despite explicit prompting, in a few cases, the model failed to recognize the provided game context, stating that the "reference game is not provided," which impacted its ability to reason within the correct game's rules.
- **OPT-175B Specific Issue:** A notable failure case for the OPT-175B model was its difficulty in the answer extraction stage. While its intermediate reasoning was often correct, it frequently failed to select the correct multiple-choice option in the final stage, suggesting an issue with prompt engineering for this specific model variant.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study demonstrates that large language models possess a discernible capability to detect video game bugs from textual descriptions in a zero-shot setting, representing a novel application of these models. This finding holds several significances and potential impacts:

**Advancing Automated Game Testing:** The work directly addresses the long-standing challenges in automating video game testing, particularly the need for common sense reasoning and game-specific knowledge. By showing that LLMs can interpret event sequences and identify anomalies that constitute bugs, the research opens a new avenue for incorporating advanced AI into quality assurance pipelines. This could lead to a reduction in the reliance on purely manual testing, allowing human testers to focus on more complex, subjective aspects of game quality rather than routine bug identification.

**Bridging Disciplines:** The study successfully bridges the fields of natural language processing, software engineering, and game development. It highlights the potential for cross-disciplinary research and the transferability of LLM capabilities to domain-specific problems, particularly those involving complex, contextual reasoning.

**Dataset Contribution:** The creation of the `GameBugDescriptions` dataset is a significant contribution. As the first dataset of its kind, featuring buggy gameplay videos with detailed, human-authored textual event descriptions and bug type labels, it provides a valuable benchmark for future research. This dataset can be utilized by researchers to further evaluate and improve LLM performance in bug detection, as well as to develop new methods for automated game testing. Its out-of-distribution nature also makes it suitable for benchmarking the generalization capabilities of LLMs.

**New Tools for Developers and Testers:** The demonstrated ability of LLMs to identify and even classify bug types could lead to the development of new assistive tools for game developers and quality assurance teams. Integrating LLMs into development workflows could enable earlier and more frequent bug detection during various stages of game production, potentially reducing development costs and improving game quality upon release.

**Foundational for Future Research:** The study's findings, particularly the analysis of failure cases, provide clear directions for future research. This includes:
- **Improving Prompt Engineering:** Investigating more effective prompting strategies, especially for specific LLM architectures like OPT-175B, to ensure accurate answer extraction from generated reasoning.
- **Automating Event Description:** Exploring robust computer vision or game engine integration techniques to automatically generate textual descriptions of gameplay events, thereby fully automating the input stream for LLM-based bug detection.
- **Optimizing Question Formats:** Further research into optimal question templates (`Q`) to maximize LLM accuracy and elicit more precise reasoning.
- **Addressing Inference Speed and Efficiency:** While LLMs are computationally intensive, advancements in quantization and model compression techniques could make them more practical for real-time application in game testing.

In conclusion, this research establishes that LLMs are capable zero-shot video game bug detectors, offering a promising new direction for enhancing the automation and efficiency of game quality assurance processes. The introduced dataset and empirical results lay a foundation for further exploration into the integration of sophisticated language models within the complex domain of game development.
