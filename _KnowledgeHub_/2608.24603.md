# Gripper-aware Vision Language Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> GVLA makes vision-language-action policies gripper-aware. It adds a three-level soft-prompt tokenizer (platform, gripper-type, instance) plus a dual mixture-of-adapters that routes action tokens by platform and gripper, fine-tuned on MiGA, a new 103,000-demonstration dataset spanning five gripper types in simulation and reality. On a pi-0.5 backbone it lifts average success from 58.38% to 66.00%.

## Problem

- Existing VLAs implicitly assume gripper invariance, yet grasping strategy is embodiment-dependent: a suction cup and a parallel jaw solve the same task in different ways.
- Large robot datasets such as Open X-Embodiment, DROID and Bridge V2 are dominated by parallel-jaw grippers, so morphology-dependent strategy divergence is never supervised.
- Prior gripper representations target grasp-pose transfer between similar hands; MLP, VQ-VAE and language-prompt encodings yield embeddings unrelated to gripper morphology or collapse together.

## Method

- **MiGA** dataset: 103,000 human demonstrations, 36 tasks, five gripper types across five robots in Isaac Lab and real arms, with sub-step decompositions, multi-view RGB-D, and roughly 5% failure demonstrations.
- ==Multi-gripper tokenizer==: learnable ==soft prompts== at three granularities, platform, gripper-type and instance, concatenated and prepended to observation tokens so parameter sharing and strategy specialization are factorized.
- ==Dual mixture-of-adapters==: cascaded platform and gripper routers ==top-k select== bottleneck adapters added as residuals at the most type-sensitive final layer, trained with ==flow-matching==, gripper-classification and load-balance losses.

## Results

- On simulated MiGA tasks GVLA reaches **66.00%** average success versus **58.38%** for pi-0.5, **41.88%** for OpenVLA-OFT and **11.50%** for AnyGrasp, which scores zero on flat and stacked scenes.
- Tokenizer comparison: prediction error **0.032** versus **0.051-0.053** for MLP, VQ-VAE and language prompts, counterfactual divergence **1.34**, and layer-probe accuracy rising from **59%** to **80%** by layer 12.
- Ablating the dual adapters drops unseen-gripper adaptation from **0.92** to **0.52**; a real UR5 with Robotiq 2f-85 improves stacked tasks **0.70** to **0.85** after **10** demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sharing a task objective does not imply a shared strategy space; conditioning a policy on end-effector morphology matters alongside conditioning on language.
> - Representation-level prompting alone is insufficient: gripper information must also modulate computation, and layer probing shows type sensitivity concentrates in the final action layers.
> - Platform and type tokens carry most transfer value while instance tokens add little; residual vision-gripper entanglement still causes misalignment failures under domain shift.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic grasping and manipulation has seen advancements through Vision Language Action Models (VLAs), which enable robots to interpret visual observations and natural language instructions for generating action sequences. However, a prevailing limitation in current VLA research is the implicit assumption of gripper invariance. Existing VLA models and their foundational datasets largely focus on parallel-jaw grippers, neglecting the diverse range of end-effectors and the embodiment-dependent nature of grasping strategies. Different gripper types, such as suction cups, multi-finger hands, and soft grippers, require distinct interaction strategies to achieve identical grasping objectives. For example, manipulating a flat object might involve sliding and side-grasping with a parallel-jaw gripper, while a suction gripper can approach directly from above. This divergence in strategy, dictated by the robot's embodiment, is often not explicitly encoded in existing datasets or considered in VLA architectures.

Prior work on gripper representation in robotics has explored various approaches, including geometric distance fields, diffusion-based synthesis, graph-based encodings, and contact-centric representations. However, these methods primarily concentrate on transferring grasp pose representations among morphologically similar grippers or adapting to variations within a single gripper type. They have not extensively addressed strategy-level differences that arise from fundamentally distinct gripper morphologies and their implications for the entire manipulation trajectory.

Similarly, large-scale robotic datasets, such as Open X-Embodiment, Bridge V2, and DROID, while instrumental for VLA training, predominantly feature data collected with parallel-jaw grippers. This homogeneity limits the ability of VLAs to learn and generalize morphology-dependent manipulation strategies. The development of methods like soft prompt learning and Mixture-of-Adapters (MoA) in vision-language models (VLMs) has shown potential for parameter-efficient adaptation and embodiment generalization. However, even these approaches in robotics have largely focused on robot-level diversity (e.g., X-VLA) rather than the specific influence of end-effector morphology on task execution strategies.

This research addresses these gaps by introducing a multi-gripper-aware dataset and a VLA framework designed to explicitly learn and leverage gripper-specific manipulation strategies. It aims to bridge the disconnect between general-purpose VLA capabilities and the nuanced requirements of diverse robotic embodiments, thereby pushing towards more robust and versatile robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observation that current Vision Language Action (VLA) models often operate under an implicit assumption of gripper invariance, despite the inherently embodiment-dependent nature of grasping and manipulation strategies. The authors pose the fundamental question: "Can VLAs learn embodiment-dependence and strategy-level divergence of multiple robotic grippers to tackle diverse, real-world tasks?"

To address this question, the research identifies two primary challenges and corresponding objectives:

1. **Addressing the Lack of Gripper-Diverse Data:**
 - **Motivation:** Existing large-scale robotic datasets predominantly feature parallel-jaw grippers, leading to a significant limitation in training VLAs to understand and execute tasks with varied end-effectors. Different gripper types necessitate distinct manipulation strategies (e.g., a suction cup lifting a flat object from above versus a parallel-jaw gripper pushing it to an edge and grasping it from the side). Current datasets do not explicitly encode this embodiment-dependent strategy divergence, hindering VLA's ability to learn gripper-aware policies.
 - **Objective:** To introduce MiGA (Multi-gripper-aware dataset), a large-scale dataset designed to explicitly capture the coupling between gripper morphology and grasping strategy. This dataset aims to provide diverse demonstrations spanning multiple gripper types and tasks, where identical objectives require fundamentally different solutions based on the gripper.

2. **Developing Gripper-Aware Learning Mechanisms for VLAs:**
 - **Motivation:** Existing gripper representations in robotics often focus on grasp pose transfer or geometric encoding, neglecting the broader strategy-level differences induced by gripper morphology throughout an entire manipulation sequence (from approach trajectories to final execution). Traditional VLA architectures, without explicit mechanisms for conditioning on gripper type, struggle to learn and differentiate these strategies, leading to suboptimal performance or generalization limitations when confronted with diverse grippers. Visual cues alone, or simple embeddings, have proven insufficient to capture the structured semantic information of different gripper types.
 - **Objective:** To propose GVLA (Gripper-aware Vision Language Action), a framework that incorporates explicit gripper conditioning into VLA models. This involves two key components:
 - A novel multi-gripper tokenization method to encode gripper information at multiple levels of granularity (platform, type, and instance), thereby inducing structured embedding information that balances parameter sharing and strategy differentiation.
 - A dual Mixture-of-Adapters (MoA) mechanism that modulates action generation through gripper-specific expert routing, enabling parameter-efficient fine-tuning while facilitating knowledge sharing across grippers and encouraging specialized pathways.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to enable VLAs to effectively reason about and execute tasks with a wider array of robotic embodiments, thereby enhancing their versatility and applicability in real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the MiGA dataset and the GVLA model to facilitate gripper-aware learning in Vision Language Action (VLA) models.

#### 3.1. The MiGA Dataset

MiGA is a multi-gripper-aware dataset designed to capture the interplay between gripper morphology and grasping strategy. It possesses three key properties:
- **Multi-gripper Coverage:** Demonstrations were collected using five common gripper types: parallel-jaw (Franka Panda, Robotiq 2f-85), three-finger (Robotiq 3-Finger), an in-house soft two-finger gripper, suction (Cobot Pump, UR10 Suction Cup), and a dexterous five-finger hand (Inspire Hand). These grippers differ in geometry, contact mechanics, force transmission, and degrees of freedom (DoF), necessitating distinct strategies for identical tasks.
- **Gripper-specific Task Design:** The dataset includes a broad set of tasks intentionally constructed to elicit morphology-dependent solutions. Tasks are categorized into four scenarios:
 - **Singulated tasks:** Vary object geometry, texture, and pose to highlight how contact feasibility depends on morphology.
 - **Stacked scenes:** Introduce occlusion and collision constraints requiring morphology-specific pre-grasp planning.
 - **Constrained-space tasks:** Impose spatial limitations, emphasizing trade-offs between gripper size, reachability, and alignment.
 - **Semantic grasping:** Requires reasoning about object function and task context for safe contact region selection.
- **Real-to-sim Parity:** Data was collected in both simulation and real-world environments. Simulation utilized NVIDIA Isaac Lab with Franka Panda and UR10 robots. Real-world demonstrations involved UFACTORY xArm7, Franka Panda, and UR5 robots. All setups captured multi-view RGB-D observations from wrist-mounted and third-view cameras, along with proprioceptive states.

MiGA comprises 103,000 demonstrations across 36 tasks and 5 gripper types. Each task features demonstrations from at least three distinct gripper types, ensuring multi-strategy supervision under shared objectives. Natural language descriptions are provided for each gripper-task pair. Approximately 5% of the dataset consists of failure demonstrations, which are included for analysis of embodiment limitations.

#### 3.2. Gripper-aware Vision Language Action Model (GVLA)

GVLA is a fine-tuning framework designed to integrate gripper information into existing VLA models, specifically built upon the π0.5 backbone due to its competitive accuracy.

- **4.2.1 Gripper Representation (Multi-gripper Tokenizer)**
 To condition observations with gripper configurations, GVLA employs a multi-granularity representation scheme using soft prompts. These are learnable embeddings, initialized randomly and optimized end-to-end. Gripper hardware configurations are mapped to a continuous prompt space. The embodiment information is decomposed into three levels of learnable embeddings:
 - **Platform token (P^(r))**: Captures platform-specific kinematic structure and configuration for robot platform `r`.
 - **Gripper-type token (P^(g))**: Encodes mechanism-specific manipulation priors shared across grippers of the same category `g`.
 - **Instance-level token (P^(u))**: Models fine-grained characteristics unique to individual gripper instances `u`.
 These tokens are concatenated (P^(h) = [P^(r); P^(g); P^(u)]) and prepended to the embedded visual observation (X) to form the conditioned input (X̃^(r,g,u)). This hierarchical factorization aims to encode gripper information at varying specificities, facilitating adaptation to new gripper instances while leveraging shared knowledge.

- **4.2.2 Gripper-Aware Mixture of Adapters (Dual MoA)**
 To modulate action generation based on gripper information, a dual Mixture-of-Adapters (MoA) mechanism is introduced. This mechanism decomposes adaptation into platform-specific and gripper-specific modulation.
 - Each MoA computes a gating function G(P) over pooled conditioning tokens (Softmax(TopK(MLP(mean(P))))).
 - Two parallel routers are instantiated: a platform-aware gate G^(p) driven by mean(P^(r)), and a gripper-aware gate G^(g) driven by mean([P^(g); P^(u)]). These return normalized weights and selected expert indices.
 - Each expert implements a bottleneck transformation (A = W_up(GeLU(W_down(x)))).
 - The final output combines both platform and gripper adaptations additively, applying top-k selected adapters as residual connections to the layer activation.
 **Placement of MoA:** A layer probing analysis was conducted to determine optimal MoA insertion. By measuring gripper type sensitivity (S_type) across transformer layers, it was observed that sensitivity increases sharply in the final layer. Consequently, MoA is inserted at the final layer to directly modulate action generation.

- **4.2.3 Fine-tuning Objective**
 GVLA is optimized using a combined loss function: L = L_action + λ_gripper * L_gripper + λ_LB * L_LB.
 - **Action Loss (L_action):** A conditional flow matching loss (following π0), where the network predicts a vector field that transports noisy actions back to clean actions.
 - **Gripper Prediction Loss (L_gripper):** An auxiliary classification loss that uses the pooled visual observation representation to predict the gripper type. This encourages gripper-discriminative features in shared vision-language embeddings.
 - **Load Balance Loss (L_LB):** A regularization term that penalizes imbalanced activation patterns across adapters within each platform and gripper type pool. This ensures uniform adapter utilization and prevents router collapse.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GVLA was conducted through extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world settings, using various metrics to assess overall performance, gripper-aware transfer capabilities, and component contributions.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

GVLA, fine-tuned on the MiGA dataset, was benchmarked against traditional two-stage grasping methods (AnyGrasp, GraspMAS) and VLA-based methods (GraspVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π0, π0.5).
- Traditional two-stage methods and GraspVLA showed limited capacity to generalize, collapsing on tasks like flat and stacked scenes, indicating their reliance on simple grasp configurations or a single downward grasping prior.
- Among VLA-based baselines, π0.5 achieved the highest average success rate (58.38%).
- GVLA, built upon the π0.5 backbone, achieved an average success rate of 66.00%, representing a 7.62% improvement over the π0.5 baseline. This suggests that gripper-aware conditioning enables the policy to capture gripper-specific manipulation strategies in diverse scenarios.

#### 4.2. Gripper-Aware Analysis

- **Specialized Pathways and Gripper Discrimination:** Linear Probe Accuracy (LPA) was used to measure how well gripper information is encoded across network layers.
 - Baselines with "Noise" or "Padding" tokens showed consistently low LPA, indicating that neither arbitrary tokens nor visual cues alone sufficiently provide embodiment information.
 - GVLA with multi-gripper tokenizer exhibited consistently elevated LPA (from 59% at layer L0 to 80% at L12), demonstrating that the gripper-discriminative signal is effectively encoded and propagated throughout the network.
- **Tokenizer Comparison:** Comparing different gripper tokenization approaches (MLP, VQ-VAE, Language Prompt, GVLA) showed that GVLA achieved the lowest Prediction Error (PE) (0.032), the highest Counterfactual Action Prediction Divergence (CAPD) (1.34), and the highest Gripper Contribution Score (GCS) (0.249). This indicates that GVLA learns effective gripper-specific behavior, improving both predictive accuracy and policy specialization aligned with each gripper's affordances.
- **Cross-object Generalization:** GVLA demonstrated robust zero-shot transfer capability, maintaining strong performance across unseen tasks involving new objects that shared functional attributes with the training set. For example, on cross-object generalization tasks, GVLA achieved success rates ranging from 0.74 to 0.90, significantly higher than baseline performance.
- **Gripper-Aware Adaptation:** Few-shot adaptation experiments indicated that GVLA exhibits faster adaptation on new tasks with the same gripper type, suggesting shared underlying knowledge representation. Training with mixed-gripper data further improved performance, attributed to reduced ambiguity between different gripper types.
- **Component Analysis:** An ablation study confirmed the contribution of each component:
 - Removing the gripper-type token (P^(g)) degraded both prediction quality (PE) and adaptation performance.
 - Removing the platform token (P^(p)) significantly reduced cross-embodiment transfer despite comparable PE, emphasizing the necessity of platform disentanglement.
 - Removing the instance token (P^(u)) resulted in a minor drop in adaptation, suggesting its role in fine-grained specialization.
 - Ablating the dual MoA severely reduced adaptation and restored PE to baseline levels, demonstrating that representation-level prompting alone is insufficient without computational-level modulation.
 - Removing either the gripper-specific (MoA^(g)) or platform-specific (MoA^(p)) MoA yielded similar degradation, indicating their complementary routing roles. The full GVLA achieved the highest PE and adaptation score.

#### 4.3. Real-world Robotic Validation

A targeted evaluation on a real UR5 arm with a Robotiq 2f-85 gripper, adapting to unseen tasks with few-shot demonstrations (10 demonstrations, 20k fine-tuning steps), showed that GVLA outperformed the π0.5 baseline across all task categories. For instance, on semantic tasks, GVLA achieved a success rate of 0.90, compared to the baseline's 0.70. This demonstrates that gripper-aware conditioning facilitates embodiment-specific knowledge transfer and enables efficient few-shot adaptation to novel tasks across unseen platform-gripper configurations in real-world settings.

#### 4.4. Failure Cases

Observed failure cases included:
- **Intra-type misalignment:** The model selected a type-consistent strategy but failed to precisely align the end-effector, indicating a lack of fine-grained geometric detail for contact planning.
- **Physical gripper limitation:** In certain tasks, the gripper's physical constraints prevented grasping despite feasible trajectories generated by the model.
- **Kinematic failure:** In complex tasks, the robot occasionally entered kinematically infeasible configurations without recovery, highlighting a disconnect between high-level action prediction and low-level kinematic feasibility.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, with implications for the development of more versatile and capable robotic systems.

Firstly, the introduction of **MiGA, a large-scale multi-gripper-aware dataset**, addresses a critical limitation in current robotic learning: the pervasive reliance on datasets predominantly featuring parallel-jaw grippers. MiGA's comprehensive coverage of five distinct gripper types across various tasks, explicitly capturing embodiment-dependent strategy divergence, provides a foundational resource for training and benchmarking gripper-aware policies. This dataset is designed to encourage the learning of entire manipulation trajectories and high-level planning, moving beyond mere grasp pose prediction. Its real-to-sim parity and inclusion of failure demonstrations offer valuable insights for robust algorithm development and analysis of embodiment limitations.

Secondly, the proposed **GVLA framework** represents an advancement in integrating explicit gripper information into Vision Language Action (VLA) models. By combining a novel multi-gripper tokenizer with a dual Mixture-of-Adapters (MoA) architecture, GVLA effectively conditions the policy on gripper-specific knowledge. The multi-granularity tokenizer, encoding platform, type, and instance-level features, enables the model to form structured representations that balance knowledge sharing and specialization. The dual MoA, through its cascaded routing mechanism, allows for parameter-efficient adaptation and modulates action generation based on the specific gripper, leading to tailored manipulation strategies.

The empirical results demonstrate that GVLA significantly **improves manipulation success rates** over gripper-agnostic baselines in simulation. Furthermore, it exhibits enhanced **zero-shot generalization** to new objects and tasks, and robust **few-shot adaptation** to unseen grippers and task configurations, including validation on real-world robots. These capabilities suggest that explicit gripper-awareness can lead to more robust and adaptable robotic policies.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:
- **Enhanced General-Purpose Robotics:** By enabling VLAs to account for diverse end-effector capabilities, this research contributes to the development of truly general-purpose robotic systems that can operate effectively with various tools and in varied environments.
- **Broader Adoption of Diverse Grippers:** The ability to train and deploy VLAs with different gripper types could encourage the wider adoption of specialized grippers in industrial and service robotics, where task requirements vary significantly.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** Robots capable of understanding and adapting to different gripper affordances based on natural language instructions could lead to more intuitive and flexible human-robot collaboration scenarios.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** MiGA serves as a benchmark for future research into cross-gripper learning, embodiment generalization, and the development of more sophisticated gripper representation and control mechanisms. The identified failure cases also highlight specific areas for future investigation, such as incorporating richer geometric or kinematic modeling for fine-grained contact planning and recovery from kinematically infeasible configurations.

In summary, this work addresses a crucial limitation in current robotic VLA research, demonstrating that explicitly learning and conditioning on gripper morphology is essential for achieving advanced, adaptable manipulation capabilities across diverse robotic embodiments.
