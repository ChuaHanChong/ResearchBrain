# SPOT: SE(3) Pose Trajectory Diffusion for Object-Centric Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> SPOT is an object-centric imitation learning framework that leverages SE(3) object pose trajectories and diffusion models to learn complex robotic manipulation skills. It decouples robot embodiment and automatically learns planning constraints from demonstrations, achieving 79.4% average success on RLBench and successfully transferring skills from just eight iPhone-shot human demonstrations to a robot for tasks like pouring water.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic imitation learning methods tightly couple sensory inputs with robot-specific actions, limiting generalization across different robot embodiments and hindering learning from action-less human demonstrations.
- Complex manipulation tasks often involve implicit planning constraints (e.g., maintaining object orientation) that typically require manual rule-crafting or pre-programmed solutions, hindering scalability.
- Acquiring large quantities of high-quality robot demonstration data is resource-intensive and often inaccessible to non-experts.

## Method

- Introduce an object-centric, embodiment-agnostic representation by tracking SE(3) pose trajectories of task-relevant objects relative to each other using zero-shot 6D pose estimation.
- Develop a diffusion model that learns to synthesize future object pose trajectories, implicitly encoding complex planning constraints observed in demonstrations.
- Implement a closed-loop control system using receding horizon control to translate the synthesized object pose trajectories into robot end-effector subgoals, enabling reactive execution.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **79.4%** across 13 RLBench tasks, performing comparably to a four-camera state-of-the-art method with only a single camera and outperforming other single-view baselines.
- Demonstrated successful transfer of skills to a physical robot from as few as eight action-less, iPhone-shot human video demonstrations for complex real-world tasks like pouring water.
- Showed superior performance over a point-tracking baseline in real-world scenarios, effectively adhering to implicit task constraints (e.g., maintaining object orientation) and exhibiting higher precision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The full SE(3) object pose trajectory, relative to a target object, acts as an effective intermediate representation for implicitly capturing complex task planning constraints from demonstrations.
> - Decoupling robot embodiment through object-centric pose trajectories enables learning from diverse, action-less human video demonstrations and facilitates cross-embodiment generalization.
> - Employing a diffusion model to synthesize future object pose trajectories provides a robust and generative approach for planning and executing intricate manipulation sequences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "SPOT" addresses critical challenges within the field of robotic imitation learning, visuomotor control, and object-centric manipulation. It builds upon and extends several lines of research:

**Evolution of Imitation Learning (IL):**
Imitation learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for teaching robots complex skills by demonstrating them. Early approaches often relied on **end-to-end visuomotor policies** that directly mapped raw RGB inputs to robot actions. While conceptually simple, these methods struggled with generalization to novel viewing angles, environmental perturbations, and out-of-distribution spatial configurations due to their lack of explicit 3D understanding.

A subsequent trend involved using **3D scene representations**, such as point clouds or 3D volumes (e.g., RVT, PerAct). These methods captured more structural information but often required extensive demonstration data and suffered from a tight coupling of observation and action pairs, limiting their deployment across different camera setups or learning from action-less data.

**Emergence of Object-Centric Methods:**
To address the data inefficiency and generalization limitations, **object-centric methods** gained prominence. These approaches extract task-relevant information (e.g., object detection, 6D object poses, 2D/3D flows) from visual observations, providing more compact and generalizable inputs for downstream policies. While these methods show impressive data efficiency and generalization, most typically focused on **last-inch manipulation** or required **manual rule-crafting** for intermediate planning constraints in complex, multi-stage tasks (e.g., keeping a container upright while pouring). This oversight left a significant gap in fully autonomous long-horizon task execution.

**Integration of Diffusion Models in Robotics:**
More recently, **diffusion models** have been adapted for robotic policy learning (e.g., Diffusion Policy, 3D Diffuser Actor, DNAct, 3D Diffusion Policy). These generative models are adept at learning complex, multimodal action distributions and synthesizing sequences of actions, offering enhanced robustness and expressive power compared to discriminative models. However, many current diffusion policies still operate on raw sensory inputs or directly on robot end-effector actions, inheriting the challenges of embodiment coupling and data requirements.

**Learning from Video and Cross-Embodiment Transfer:**
A parallel trend focuses on **learning from visual demonstrations** (videos) rather than explicit robot action data. This aims to democratize data collection by allowing humans to provide demonstrations, often using readily available tools like smartphones. The challenge here is bridging the **embodiment gap** (human vs. robot kinematics) and **action-less data** (videos often don't provide explicit robot joint commands). Some methods use point tracking or synthesize target object poses, but often still require robot-specific action data or struggle with complex intermediate constraints.

**SPOT's Contribution to the Landscape:**
SPOT strategically integrates these advancements to address the remaining gaps:
1. **Intermediate Object Pose Trajectory:** Unlike prior object-centric methods, SPOT explicitly models the *entire SE(3) object pose trajectory* relative to a target object. This enables learning planning constraints implicitly from demonstrations, eliminating the need for manual rules in tasks with critical intermediate actions (e.g., pouring water).
2. **Decoupling Embodiment:** By using object pose trajectories as an intermediate representation, SPOT decouples embodiment actions from sensory inputs. This is crucial for **cross-embodiment generalization** (human to robot) and learning from **action-less data** (human hand demonstrations recorded via iPhone).
3. **Diffusion Policy for Trajectories:** SPOT leverages the generative power of diffusion models, but instead of predicting raw actions, it synthesizes these high-level object pose trajectories. This provides a structured, robust way to generate future plans.
4. **Closed-loop Control:** Integrating the diffusion-synthesized trajectories with receding horizon control allows for reactive, closed-loop execution, enhancing robustness to dynamic uncertainties (e.g., object slips).

In essence, SPOT pushes the boundaries of imitation learning by combining the generalization benefits of object-centric representations, the expressive power of diffusion models, and the accessibility of learning from diverse video demonstrations, all while tackling the critical challenge of automatically capturing complex task constraints over long horizons.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations driving the development of SPOT are centered around enhancing the flexibility, autonomy, and practicality of robotic manipulation through imitation learning:

- **Objective 1: Decoupling Embodiment Actions from Sensory Inputs.**
 - **Motivation:** Traditional visuomotor policies often tightly couple observations (e.g., raw RGBD images from a robot's camera) with robot-specific actions (e.g., end-effector commands). This tight coupling severely limits generalization across different robot embodiments (e.g., different types of robotic arms or grippers) and makes it difficult to learn from diverse demonstration data, especially **action-less human demonstrations**. Human videos are abundant and easy to collect but lack explicit robot action data. The authors aim to create a representation that is generalizable across these embodiments and data types.

- **Objective 2: Automatically Capturing Task Planning Constraints from Demonstrations.**
 - **Motivation:** Many complex manipulation tasks involve crucial intermediate actions or states that must satisfy specific constraints (e.g., keeping a cup upright to prevent spillage while moving it, or precise insertion trajectories). Prior object-centric methods often focus on "last-inch" manipulation and rely on manually crafted rules or pre-programmed planners for these intermediate stages. This manual effort hinders scalability and reusability. The authors seek a representation that inherently encodes these constraints observed in demonstrations, allowing the policy to learn them automatically.

- **Objective 3: Guiding Robot Execution with SE(3) Object Pose Trajectories via a Diffusion Policy.**
 - **Motivation:** Once an abstract, embodiment-agnostic plan (like an object pose trajectory) is generated, a robust mechanism is needed to translate this into executable robot actions. Diffusion models have shown promise in generating high-quality, multimodal distributions for actions. By conditioning a diffusion policy on object trajectories, the goal is to leverage the strengths of diffusion models for stable and effective robot control, specifically to synthesize future *object pose trajectories* rather than raw robot actions.

- **Objective 4: Facilitating Learning from Diverse and Accessible Demonstration Data.**
 - **Motivation:** Collecting large quantities of high-quality robot demonstration data is laborious and often requires specialized hardware and expertise. The authors are motivated to "democratize" data collection, allowing users to easily record demonstrations using widely available devices like smartphones (e.g., an iPhone equipped with RGB and LiDAR sensors). This would significantly lower the barrier to entry for training robotic skills.

- **Objective 5: Achieving Closed-Loop Control with Enhanced Robustness.**
 - **Motivation:** Real-world robotic tasks are inherently uncertain, with potential dynamic perturbations like objects slipping in the gripper or target objects moving unexpectedly. Many methods operate in an open-loop fashion, which is brittle to such uncertainties. The authors aim for a closed-loop control system that can react and adapt to updated observations throughout the task execution, ensuring robustness in dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPOT introduces an object-centric imitation learning framework that leverages SE(3) object pose trajectories as a key intermediate representation. The methodology can be broken down into three main phases: Demonstration Video Parsing, Object Trajectory Diffusion (training), and Closed-loop Policy Execution.

**A. Demonstration Video Parsing**
The first step is to transform raw video demonstrations into a structured, object-centric format suitable for learning.
- **Object-Centric Representation:** SPOT chooses SE(3) (3D position and orientation) object pose trajectories as the representation, arguing that it is compact and enhances accuracy compared to dense point flow methods.
- **Task-Relevant Objects:** For prehensile manipulation, the task is defined by a graspable "source object" ($v_a$) and a "target object" ($v_b$). For example, a kettle as $v_a$ and a mug as $v_b$ for a pouring task.
- **Pose Trajectory Extraction:**
 - A **zero-shot 6D object pose estimation method (FoundationPose)** is used to extract the SE(3) pose trajectory for both $v_a$ and $v_b$ from demonstration RGBD videos.
 - **Canonicalization:** To generalize across different environments and absolute configurations, all source object poses are converted into the target object’s coordinate frame ($T_a^b$). This creates an embodiment-agnostic representation of the task's object-centric motions and implicitly encodes planning constraints (e.g., maintaining an object's upright orientation).
- **Keyframe Selection:** To address noise and redundancy in continuous human video demonstrations, a keyframe selection strategy (inspired by PerAct) is employed. Frames are selected if velocity is zero (direction change) or if translation/rotation exceeds a threshold from the previous keyframe.

**B. Object Trajectory Diffusion (Training)**
The core of the learning mechanism is a diffusion model that learns to synthesize future object pose trajectories.
- **Problem Formulation:** The model learns to infer a future object pose trajectory ($T_{t:t+h} \in SE(3)$) conditioned on the current object pose ($T_t \in SE(3)$) and historical poses.
- **Diffusion Model Adaptation:** SPOT adapts the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) framework, typically used for action prediction (e.g., Diffusion Policy). Instead of predicting raw robot actions ($A_t$), the model predicts **future object pose transformations** ($T_{t+1}$).
 - The reverse diffusion process (denoising) reconstructs the object pose transformation $T^{k-1}_t$ from a noisy version $T^k_t$ and the current object pose $T_t$, guided by a trained score function $\epsilon_\theta$.
 - The model is trained using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss on the predicted noise.
- **Pose Feature Encoder:** A lightweight three-layer MLP with LayerNorm and a projection head encodes the 6D object poses into compact 64-dimensional feature vectors.
- **End State Prediction:** A separate MLP network is trained as a binary classifier to predict task completion. It takes the current object pose as input and outputs a binary signal (1 for end state, 0 otherwise). This controls gripper actions (e.g., opening the gripper when the prediction exceeds 0.95).
- **Multi-task Language Conditioning:** For multi-task settings, the model can be conditioned on language descriptions. CLIP sentence embeddings are concatenated with the object pose features as input, enabling a single policy to handle various tasks.
- **Implementation Details:** DDIM is used as the noise scheduler with 100 timesteps during training and 10 during inference. Training occurs for 3000 epochs with a batch size of 128.

**C. Closed-Loop Policy Execution**
During deployment, the trained models are used to derive an action plan from observations in a closed-loop fashion.
- **Observation to Object Pose:**
 - **Object Pose Tracking:** FoundationPose is used for real-time 6D object pose estimation and tracking. It requires a 3D object model (obtained from iPhone scans using BundleSDF) and initial 2D detection (provided by YOLO-World).
 - Similar to training, estimated source object poses are transformed into the target object’s frame before being fed to the diffusion model.
- **Trajectory Synthesizing:**
 - At each time step, the diffusion model uses the current (and optionally historical) object poses to predict a future object pose trajectory for N steps.
 - **Receding Horizon Control (RHC):** To ensure robustness and reactivity to dynamic uncertainties, SPOT employs RHC. The model forecasts N steps, but only the first K steps are executed, after which the process repeats with updated observations. This enables closed-loop feedback.
- **Action Plan Generation:**
 - The synthesized object pose trajectory, representing desired object states, is converted into end-effector subgoals.
 - A fixed transformation ($T_{obj}^{EE}$) from the object pose to the end-effector pose is used. This transformation is derived from extrinsic camera calibration.
 - The robot's actions ($A_{t:t+h}$) are then computed by applying this transformation to the predicted object poses ($A_{t:t+h} = T_{obj}^{EE} \cdot T_{t:t+h}$).
 - The policy continues execution, following these end-effector subgoals, until the end-state prediction signals task completion, at which point the gripper state is adjusted.

This holistic approach allows SPOT to learn abstract, transferable skills from diverse demonstrations and execute them robustly on a physical robot, effectively bridging the perception-action gap through a learned object-centric trajectory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of SPOT was conducted in both simulation and real-world settings, addressing key research questions regarding the effectiveness of its representation, ability to learn from action-less data, compliance with task constraints, and data collection flexibility.

**A. Simulation Results (RLBench)**
- **Effectiveness of Pose Trajectory Representation:** SPOT was evaluated on 13 manipulation tasks in RLBench, a standard multi-task benchmark. It was compared against state-of-the-art 3D-based imitation learning methods: RVT2 (multi-view, 4 cameras) and 3D Diffuser Actor (single-view).
- **Overall Performance:** SPOT achieved an average success rate of 79.4%, which is comparable to RVT2 (76.4%) even though SPOT uses a single-camera setup while RVT2 uses four. SPOT significantly outperformed 3D Diffuser Actor (54.5%) in the single-view setting.
- **High-Precision Tasks:** SPOT particularly excelled in tasks requiring high precision, such as "Insert Peg" (78.7% vs. RVT2's 44.0%) and "Place Cups" (62.7% vs. RVT2's 33.3%). This suggests that the pose trajectory representation effectively captures the fine spatial relationships and intermediate transformations needed for accurate execution.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks:** SPOT also showed superior performance in long-horizon tasks like "Stack Blocks" (94.0% vs. RVT2's 81.3%) and "Stack Cups" (96.0% vs. RVT2's 60.0%). This validates the approach of decomposing complex tasks into a series of sub-policies learned from sequential object pose trajectories.
- **Limitations in Simulation:** The method struggled with specific object types:
 - Thin objects (e.g., "Drag Stick" at 80.0% vs. RVT2's 98.7%).
 - Small objects that get occluded by the gripper (e.g., "Screw Bulb" at 48.0% vs. RVT2's 86.7%).
 - Tasks involving a mix of objects with different symmetries (e.g., "Sort Shape" at 32.0% vs. RVT2's 49.3%). These issues are likely related to the robustness of 6D pose tracking or the model's ability to discriminate fine features under occlusion.

**B. Real-world Results (iPhone-shot Demonstrations)**
- **Learning from Action-less Data and Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** SPOT was tested on four real-world tasks involving complex constraints: "mug-on-coaster," "plant-in-vase," "pour-water," and "put-plate-in-oven." Crucially, for each task, only **eight demonstrations** were collected using an **iPhone** (RGBD video of human hands, without robot actions), in diverse everyday environments. The robot then executed the policy in a drastically different test environment with a Kinova Gen3 arm.
- **Compliance with Task Constraints:** This was a key focus of the real-world evaluation, categorized into placing failure, task constraint failure, and tracking failure.
 - **"mug-on-coaster":** Both SPOT and the "POINT-TRACKING" baseline performed well, as it's a general pick-and-place task without complex intermediate constraints.
 - **"plant-in-vase":** Requires high precision. POINT-TRACKING struggled with accuracy (noisy trajectories), leading to placing failures. Both methods experienced some tracking failures due to camera setup and arm occlusion, a shared limitation.
 - **"pour-water":** Requires keeping the kettle upright. SPOT achieved high success while complying with this constraint. POINT-TRACKING frequently failed due to violating the upright constraint, causing spillage.
 - **"put-plate-in-oven":** Requires keeping the plate upright while inserting it into a narrow space. SPOT consistently produced smooth, constraint-adhering trajectories. POINT-TRACKING often drastically changed orientation, causing food to fall off the plate (task constraint failure).
- **Democratization of Data Collection:** The success of SPOT with only 8 iPhone-shot demonstrations clearly demonstrates its ability to learn from minimal, easily collectible, action-less human video data, validating the objective of democratizing data collection.
- **Baseline Comparison (POINT-TRACKING):** The implemented POINT-TRACKING baseline, which uses optical flow to derive object trajectories, consistently struggled with precision and, critically, with adhering to implicit task constraints. This highlights the advantage of SPOT's direct SE(3) pose trajectory modeling in capturing detailed motion and constraint information.

In summary, SPOT demonstrates strong performance in both simulation and real-world tasks. Its object-centric pose trajectory representation effectively captures task-relevant 3D information and implicitly learns complex planning constraints. The method successfully transfers skills from action-less human demonstrations to a robot, even with minimal data, significantly outperforming baselines in tasks requiring precision and constraint adherence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SPOT represents a significant advancement in robotic imitation learning, addressing several critical challenges that have hindered the widespread adoption of learned policies in real-world scenarios. Its potential impact spans across research, development, and practical deployment of robotic systems:

- **Enhanced Generalization and Data Efficiency:** By leveraging an embodiment-agnostic, object-centric SE(3) pose trajectory representation, SPOT achieves impressive generalization capabilities. It can learn from action-less human demonstrations and apply these skills to a robot in diverse environments, overcoming the typical embodiment and domain gaps. The fact that only eight iPhone-shot demonstrations sufficed for complex real-world tasks is a testament to its data efficiency, drastically reducing the data collection burden compared to traditional methods.

- **Automatic Learning of Task Constraints:** One of the most impactful contributions of SPOT is its ability to implicitly learn complex planning constraints (e.g., maintaining object orientation during manipulation) directly from demonstrations, without the need for manual rule-crafting or intricate state machines. This capability is crucial for scaling robotic systems to handle a wider variety of tasks, particularly those requiring intermediate object states to satisfy specific conditions. This feature significantly simplifies policy development and makes it more robust to variations.

- **Democratization of Robotic Data Collection:** The ability to learn effectively from human demonstrations recorded with consumer-grade devices like an iPhone is a game-changer. This lowers the barrier to entry for collecting robotic manipulation data, making it accessible to a broader range of users, including non-robotics experts. It enables rapid prototyping of new skills and allows for on-the-fly demonstration collection, accelerating the development cycle for robotic applications.

- **Robust Closed-Loop Control:** The integration of the diffusion model with receding horizon control provides a robust closed-loop execution strategy. This allows the robot to react to dynamic uncertainties (e.g., object slips, unexpected environmental changes) by continuously updating its plan based on real-time observations, which is essential for reliable deployment in unpredictable real-world environments.

- **Foundation for Future Multimodal and Multi-task Learning:** By building upon diffusion models and allowing for language conditioning, SPOT lays a strong foundation for future research in multi-task and multimodal robotic learning. A single policy can potentially handle various tasks specified by natural language, further increasing the versatility and intelligence of robotic systems.

- **Bridging Research Gaps:** SPOT effectively bridges several existing research gaps: between abstract object-centric planning and concrete robot execution, between learning from human videos and deploying on robots, and between simple pick-and-place tasks and complex, constraint-heavy manipulations.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations, which also point to avenues for future research:
- **Rigidity Assumption:** SPOT relies on 6D pose tracking, which assumes rigid objects. This limits its applicability to non-rigid, deformable, or highly articulated objects.
- **Perception Dependency:** Performance is inherently tied to the robustness of 6D object pose tracking, which can struggle with small/thin objects, significant occlusion, or require reconstructed 3D object meshes (though tools like BundleSDF make this more accessible).
- **Task Scope:** Currently, SPOT focuses on prehensile manipulation involving SE(3) poses of grasped objects. It does not handle non-prehensile tasks or tasks requiring precise control over robot speed, force, or intricate contact dynamics.

Despite these limitations, SPOT represents a significant step forward in making robotic imitation learning more flexible, autonomous, and accessible. Its core ideas have the potential to influence the design of future robotic manipulation systems, particularly in domains where data collection is challenging and task constraints are complex.
