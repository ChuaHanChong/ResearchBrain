# Grasping with Chopsticks: Combating Covariate Shift in Model-free Imitation Learning for Fine Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research develops model-free imitation learning methods to combat covariate shift in robotic fine manipulation tasks using chopsticks. The proposed techniques, including object-centric transformations, synthetic noise injection for corrective labels, and an ensemble of parametric and non-parametric policies, achieved an 80% success rate in grasping various small objects, aligning closely with human expert performance of 82.7%.

## Problem

- Robotic fine manipulation with simple tools like chopsticks faces significant control complexity, and accurate physical models are often difficult to obtain.
- Model-free imitation learning approaches are susceptible to covariate shift, where the robot deviates from demonstrated trajectories and accumulates errors, leading to task failure.
- Existing methods to mitigate covariate shift typically require an interactive expert, which is computationally expensive and impractical for complex, high-precision fine manipulation tasks.

## Method

- An ==object-centric coordinate frame (ObjC)== was used to represent robot end-effector poses relative to the object, increasing data density in critical manipulation regions and promoting generalization.
- Synthetic corrective labels were generated by injecting small ==Gaussian noise== into a subset of demonstration states and reusing the original expert actions for these perturbed states, without requiring an interactive expert.
- A hybrid agent (**Ensemble**) was developed, combining a ==Behavior Cloning (BC)== agent (with ObjC and noise) and a ==k-Nearest Neighbor (k-NN)== agent (with ObjC), using a decision tree to switch between them based on deviation from training data.

## Results

- The Ensemble model achieved an **80%** success rate in picking up various small objects with chopsticks, comparable to the human expert's **82.7%** and the hardware replay rate of **86.7%**.
- The object-centric transformation (BC+ObjC) improved the success rate from **37.3%** to **44.0%**, and the subsequent addition of noise injection (BC+ObjC+Noise) further increased it to **72.0%**.
- Visualization of state distributions indicated that both noise injection and the Ensemble method significantly reduced covariate shift, keeping the learned agent's visited states closer to the expert's demonstrated trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Transforming state representations to an object-centric frame effectively increases data support in critical regions, improving policy generalization and sample efficiency for manipulation tasks.
> - Injecting noise into training data and reusing expert actions provides a non-interactive and 'for free' method for generating corrective labels, effectively mitigating error accumulation in model-free imitation learning.
> - A strategic combination of parametric (BC) and non-parametric (k-NN) imitation learning can leverage the strengths of both, allowing BC for general behavior while using k-NN to guide the agent back to known successful trajectories when significant deviations occur.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly fine manipulation, is a significant area of research with applications ranging from industrial automation to surgery and meal assistance. While complex end effectors are often designed for fine manipulation, simple tools, due to their ubiquity and ease of study, present an alternative approach, especially when human control is involved. This paper specifically focuses on chopsticks as a representative simple tool for fine manipulation, noting their widespread human use and the inherent control complexity arising from their small, curved, and slippery tips. This complexity makes them a suitable testbed for advanced robotic control strategies.

A key challenge in developing autonomous robotic systems, especially for fine manipulation tasks where accurate physical models are difficult to obtain, is the reliance on model-free imitation learning. Imitation learning, where robots learn policies from human demonstrations, is a prominent paradigm. However, a known limitation of model-free imitation learning, particularly methods like behavior cloning, is the phenomenon of covariate shift. Covariate shift occurs when an agent deviates from the expert's demonstrated trajectory, leading it to states not encountered during training. In these "unseen" states, the learned policy can make errors that compound over time, resulting in task failure and poor generalization.

Existing approaches to mitigate covariate shift, such as DAgger and DART, typically require an interactive expert to provide corrective labels for states visited by the learning agent during its own rollouts. This interactive querying can be computationally expensive, time-consuming, and imposes a significant burden on the human expert, especially in fine manipulation tasks that demand high precision. For instance, DART's noise injection during data collection can increase expert data collection time and reduce success rates.

This research contributes to the field by proposing non-interactive methods to address covariate shift in model-free imitation learning for fine manipulation. It operates under the constraint of not having access to an interactive expert or an accurate environmental model, thereby expanding the applicability of imitation learning to scenarios where online expert feedback is impractical or unavailable. The work builds upon the understanding that covariate shift arises from both single-step prediction errors and their accumulation, targeting these two aspects with distinct methodological contributions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an autonomous robotic manipulator equipped with chopsticks that can perform fine manipulation tasks effectively by learning from human demonstrations. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and is broken down into specific technical objectives aimed at overcoming known challenges in imitation learning.

**Motivation for using chopsticks and fine manipulation:**
- Chopsticks are chosen as a simple, yet versatile tool for fine manipulation. Their design requires precise movements, particularly when grasping small, rigid, or slippery objects (e.g., marbles), making them a suitably complex test case for evaluating fine manipulation capabilities in robotics.
- There is a recognized utility for chopstick-like manipulators in various robotic applications such as surgery, micro-manipulation, and meal assistance, but controlling them autonomously presents a significant challenge.
- Leveraging human dexterity, as demonstrated in teleoperating chopstick-equipped robots, provides a rich source of data for learning, which is a core principle of imitation learning.

**Motivation for model-free imitation learning:**
- Accurate physical models for fine manipulation tasks are often difficult or impossible to construct, especially for custom-built robotic systems or dynamic contact-rich interactions. This constraint necessitates the use of model-free approaches, which learn control policies directly from data without requiring an explicit model of the robot or environment.

**Motivation for addressing covariate shift in a non-interactive setting:**
- Model-free imitation learning, particularly simple behavior cloning, is susceptible to covariate shift. This phenomenon causes learned policies to accumulate errors when deployed, leading the robot into states not observed during training and consequently failing the task. For fine manipulation, success often depends on critical, precise movements near the end of a trajectory, making error accumulation particularly detrimental.
- Existing methods to combat covariate shift often require continuous online interaction with an expert (e.g., DAgger, DART). This interactive requirement can be highly demanding, expensive, and impractical for real-world robotic systems, especially for intricate fine manipulation tasks that require significant human attention and effort.
- Therefore, a key motivation is to develop methods that can reduce covariate shift without needing an interactive expert or an accurate model. This non-interactive approach aims to make imitation learning more robust and broadly applicable.

**Specific Technical Objectives:**
To address the aforementioned challenges, the research sets out to:
1. **Enhance data support at critical manipulation stages:** Reduce single-step prediction errors by increasing the density of training data in regions crucial for task success, particularly around the object being grasped.
2. **Generate synthetic corrective labels:** Develop a mechanism to create corrective feedback for the agent when it deviates from the demonstrated trajectory, thereby preventing the accumulation of errors. This must be achieved without relying on an interactive expert or a precise environmental model.
3. **Improve action distribution matching at unseen states:** Propose methods that enable the agent to generate actions that align more closely with expert actions even in states slightly different from those in the training data, thus implicitly guiding the agent back towards successful trajectories and reducing covariate shift.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a custom robotic platform and developed three distinct, yet complementary, strategies to mitigate covariate shift in model-free imitation learning for fine manipulation, all without requiring an interactive expert or an explicit model.

**A. Experimental Setup:**
The robotic system consists of a 6-DOF robot arm equipped with a 1-DOF actuated chopstick end effector. Due to the custom nature and assembly from less rigid parts, the hardware possessed inherent kinematic inaccuracies (4mm average end-effector position error after calibration), comparable to the size of the objects to be manipulated. This setup provided a realistic and challenging environment for fine manipulation.
Demonstration data was collected by a single human expert teleoperating the robot to pick up various small objects: a 1 cm cube, a 2 cm diameter ball, and a 1.4 cm diameter ball. For each object, 500 successful trajectories were recorded at 100 Hz. Each state was an 11-dimensional vector (robot end-effector pose and object's xyz position), and the action was an 8-dimensional target end-effector pose. Success was defined as grasping the object, lifting it above the workstation, and holding it for 1 second. Evaluation involved 25 trials for each object type, with objects placed across a 5x5 grid on the workstation.

**B. Covariate Shift Mitigation Strategies:**

1. **Transform: Increasing Data Support with Object-Centric Frame (ObjC):**
 - **Rationale:** The scarcity of data for states not directly observed in demonstrations can lead to single-step prediction errors. For manipulation tasks, the relative pose between the end effector and the object is often more critical than their absolute positions.
 - **Approach:** Instead of a robot-centric coordinate frame (where the robot base is the origin), the system uses an object-centric frame where the object's location is the origin. The robot's end-effector pose is then represented relative to the object.
 - **Expected Benefits:** This transformation aligns the critical region for grasping (near the object) across all demonstrations, making the training data denser around the origin. This increased data support in the region of interest is intended to improve policy generalization and make the learned policy invariant to the object's initial translation, thereby enhancing sample efficiency.

2. **Noise: Generating Synthetic Corrective Labels:**
 - **Rationale:** Even with increased data support, deviations can occur. Generating corrective labels without an interactive expert or an accurate model is crucial for mitigating error accumulation.
 - **Approach:** During training, a small amount of additive Gaussian noise (ε ~ N(0, σ)) is injected into a subset (20%) of the demonstration states (x_i). The original action (a_i) is then reused as the corrective label for this perturbed state (x'_i = x_i + ε). The magnitude of the noise (σ) is correlated with the variance of the data (η * ˆσ, where η is a fixed noise magnitude and ˆσ is the state dimension's variance).
 - **Justification:** This method assumes that for small deviations, the expert's original action can effectively guide the agent back toward the demonstrated trajectory. It implicitly enforces a form of policy smoothness, assuming actions are Lipschitz continuous with respect to states. Unlike DART/DAgger, which focus on matching the test state distribution by querying an expert, this method generates corrective labels for randomly sampled deviated states "for free," simplifying data generation and reducing expert burden.

3. **Ensemble: Combining Parametric and Non-Parametric Methods:**
 - **Rationale:** Parametric methods like neural network-based Behavior Cloning (BC) can generalize well within the training data distribution but struggle at unseen states. Non-parametric methods like k-Nearest Neighbor (k-NN) inherently stay closer to the training data, potentially reducing covariate shift, but can suffer from non-smooth, jerky motions and are sensitive to their distance function in high-dimensional spaces.
 - **Approach:** A hybrid agent (Ensemble) is proposed, combining a BC agent (specifically, BC+ObjC+Noise) with a k-NN agent (k-NN+ObjC) using a decision tree.
 - **Switching Mechanism:** The decision tree determines which agent to invoke. If the distance of a query state to its k-nearest neighbors in the training data exceeds a predefined threshold (α), indicating a significant deviation, the k-NN agent is invoked. Otherwise, the BC agent is used.
 - **Benefits:** This approach aims to leverage the scalability and generalization capabilities of BC when the agent is operating within the known data distribution, while utilizing k-NN to provide constrained, "known" corrective actions when the agent drifts far from the training data. By invoking k-NN only for far-off states, the method reduces the sensitivity to designing a perfect distance function for k-NN across all states.

**C. Baselines and Comparisons:**
The methods were compared against:
- **Expert:** Human teleoperation performance.
- **Replay:** Direct replay of successful human demonstrations (to assess hardware repeatability).
- **BC+RobotC:** Behavior cloning with a two-layer neural network, using a robot-centric frame.
- **k-NN+RobotC:** A k-nearest neighbor agent, using a robot-centric frame.

The proposed methods evaluated were:
- **BC+ObjC:** Behavior cloning with an object-centric frame.
- **k-NN+ObjC:** k-nearest neighbor with an object-centric frame.
- **BC+ObjC+Noise:** BC+ObjC with the addition of synthetic noise-injected labels during training.
- **Ensemble:** The combined BC+ObjC+Noise and k-NN+ObjC agent.

**D. Evaluation Metrics:**
Performance was primarily measured by the success rate in grasping objects. Covariate shift was qualitatively assessed by visualizing the distribution of visited states during agent rollouts, reduced to 2D using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and comparing them to the distribution of demonstration states. Additionally, the generality of the noise injection method was tested on several MuJoCo simulated environments using expert data generated by reinforcement learning agents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation on the custom chopstick-equipped robot demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed strategies in combating covariate shift and improving fine manipulation success rates.

**A. Success Rates for Fine Manipulation (Table I):**
- **Baselines:**
 - The **BC+RobotC** (Behavior Cloning with Robot-Centric frame) achieved an average success rate of 37.3%. Its failures were often attributed to difficulty reaching distant objects, indicating the impact of covariate shift as errors accumulated over longer trajectories.
 - The **k-NN+RobotC** (k-Nearest Neighbor with Robot-Centric frame) had a similar average success rate of 33.3%. This agent often exhibited jerky motions and abrupt stops, sometimes failing to pick up the object even when reaching its vicinity. This was attributed to k-NN's direct imitation of discrete training data and sensitivity to its distance function, which could not guarantee a smooth policy.
- **Impact of Object-Centric Frame (ObjC):**
 - Transitioning to an object-centric frame improved both baseline methods. **BC+ObjC** saw its success rate increase to 44.0% (a 6.7% improvement over BC+RobotC), demonstrating better success in approaching objects due to the denser data distribution near the object.
 - **k-NN+ObjC** significantly improved to 53.3% (a 20% improvement over k-NN+RobotC). The denser data near the object helped mitigate the jerky motions observed with the robot-centric frame.
- **Impact of Noise Injection:**
 - Adding noise during training further enhanced the behavior cloning agent. **BC+ObjC+Noise** achieved a success rate of 72.0%, representing a substantial 28% increase compared to BC+ObjC. This agent achieved nearly 100% success for objects placed close to the robot. For distant objects, it sometimes successfully grasped the object and at other times rotated the chopsticks without grasping.
 - The MuJoCo experiments also supported the generality of noise injection, showing statistically significant performance improvements (p < 0.05) across various simulated tasks, although the magnitude of improvement was less than observed on the physical robot. This difference was hypothesized to potentially relate to the nature of expert data (RL-generated vs. human multimodal).
- **Impact of Ensemble Method:**
 - The **Ensemble** model, which combined BC+ObjC+Noise and k-NN+ObjC via a decision tree, achieved the highest learning agent success rate of 80.0%. This performance was comparable to the human expert's average success rate of 82.7% and the hardware replay rate of 86.7%.
 - The decision tree mechanism effectively invoked the non-parametric k-NN component whenever the agent's state deviated significantly, guiding the robot back to a "standard" demonstrated pose. Failures in the Ensemble agent typically involved sub-millimeter errors in grasping.

**B. Covariate Shift Across Methods (Fig. 5):**
Visualization of the state distributions visited by different agents (after PCA dimensionality reduction) provided a proxy for covariate shift:
- **BC vs. k-NN:** The states visited by k-NN agents were generally closer to the demonstrated states compared to BC agents, suggesting that k-NN intrinsically suffers less from covariate shift due to its constrained nature (actions derived from training data).
- **Noise Injection:** BC agents trained with noise injection (BC+ObjC+Noise) exhibited less covariate shift than those without noise (BC+ObjC), indicating that synthetic corrective labels were effective in keeping the agent closer to the demonstrated state distribution.
- **Ensemble:** The Ensemble model demonstrated the least covariate shift among the learning agents, suggesting its hybrid approach effectively prevented the agent from drifting far from the expert's state distribution.

In summary, the object-centric transformation provided a foundational improvement by increasing data support. Noise injection directly addressed error accumulation by providing synthetic corrective labels, leading to a substantial performance boost. The ensemble method, by strategically combining the strengths of parametric and non-parametric approaches, achieved robust performance comparable to human experts in challenging fine manipulation tasks by effectively mitigating covariate shift.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation and imitation learning, particularly for applications requiring fine dexterity in environments where detailed models and interactive expert feedback are scarce.

**Advancements in Imitation Learning for Fine Manipulation:** The work addresses the fundamental problem of covariate shift in model-free imitation learning, which is a common impediment to deploying learned policies in complex real-world tasks. By demonstrating effective non-interactive, model-free strategies to combat this issue, the paper expands the practical applicability of imitation learning to challenging domains like fine manipulation with simple tools.

**Reduced Reliance on Expert Interaction and Models:** A key strength of the proposed methods is their ability to reduce covariate shift without requiring an interactive expert or an accurate environmental model. This significantly lowers the barriers to entry for developing autonomous systems, as obtaining such resources can be costly, time-consuming, or technically infeasible. The "for free" generation of synthetic corrective labels via noise injection is a pragmatic solution that offers a viable alternative to interactive data collection paradigms like DAgger or DART.

**Demonstrated Dexterity with Simple Tools:** The research successfully demonstrates a robotic system achieving fine manipulation dexterity with chopsticks, a simple tool, at a level comparable to human experts. An 80% success rate with the Ensemble model is a substantial improvement over baseline methods and approaches human performance of 82.7%, validating the efficacy of the proposed techniques in a real-world, physical robot setting. This outcome suggests that general fine manipulation can be achieved with relatively simple end-effectors, aligning with research on the versatility of basic tools.

**Foundational Work for Future Research:** The empirical success of the proposed methods, based on pragmatic assumptions regarding data support and policy smoothness, opens avenues for further theoretical analysis. Future work could formalize the concept of bounded noise, explore different noise shapes, and investigate adaptive mechanisms for switching between components in ensemble models. The work also highlights the potential for combining model-free and model-based methods, particularly in scenarios where models might be inaccurate but still offer useful information.

**Broad Transferability:** While focused on a non-interactive setting, the techniques proposed (object-centric transformations, noise injection, and parametric/non-parametric ensembles) are generalizable. The authors suggest that these methods could directly transfer to interactive settings, where they could potentially enhance robustness and simultaneously reduce the burden on human users.

**Impact on Robotic Applications:** The ability to teach robots fine manipulation tasks from demonstrations without extensive expert oversight or detailed physical modeling could accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous systems in various domains. This includes areas such as surgical robotics, micro-assembly, and assistive robotics, where precision and adaptability are paramount, and tools might be simpler than traditional multi-fingered grippers. The robust performance on challenging, small objects further underscores the practical potential of this research.
