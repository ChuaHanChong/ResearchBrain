# HypRL: Reinforcement Learning of Control Policies for Hyperproperties

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Michigan State University developed HYPRL, a reinforcement learning framework that enables multi-agent systems to learn control policies satisfying complex relational objectives expressed as HyperLTL specifications. The framework employs Skolemization and quantitative robustness values to automate reward shaping, consistently outperforming baseline methods in efficiency and task completion across diverse multi-agent challenges like safe navigation and resource allocation.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) tasks is challenging due to complex relational dependencies and inter-agent constraints.
- Traditional temporal logics (e.g., LTL) are insufficient for MARL as they specify properties over single execution traces, limiting the expression of conditional or interdependent behaviors and struggling with quantifier alternation.
- Existing methods for specification-guided MARL, such as shield synthesis or compositional MDP-automata, often face scalability issues or are restricted to less expressive property subsets.

## Method

- HYPRL employs a three-step algorithmic approach: ==HyperLTL== Skolemization, quantitative semantics for reward shaping, and reinforcement learning.
- Skolemization transforms HyperLTL formulas with quantifier alternation into a logically equivalent form, allowing policy learning for universally quantified traces and learning Skolem functions as witnesses for existentially quantified traces.
- A quantitative robustness function converts the logical satisfaction of HyperLTL into a numerical reward signal, which is then used by off-the-shelf reinforcement learning algorithms (e.g., DQN, PPO, CQ-Learning) to optimize policies and Skolem functions.

## Results

- In safe RL grid worlds, HYPRL-guided agents achieved goals significantly faster (e.g., **7.58** steps vs. **17.40** for shield synthesis in ISR map) while maintaining low or zero collision rates.
- HYPRL consistently outperformed plain PPO and DQN baselines in the Deep Sea Treasure task, collecting higher total treasures and average treasures per step (e.g., **22.93** vs. **4.31** total treasures for PPO over **500** episodes).
- In complex tasks like the Wildfire scenario, HYPRL enabled agents to successfully complete objectives (fire extinguishing, victim rescue) in fewer steps and larger environments where plain PPO frequently failed due to step-bounds.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hyperproperties, specifically HyperLTL, offer the necessary expressiveness to formally specify complex relational constraints and dependencies among multiple agents, addressing limitations of single-trace temporal logics.
> - Skolemization provides a method to systematically handle quantifier alternation in HyperLTL within an RL framework, enabling the learning of policies that account for inter-agent dependencies.
> - Deriving quantitative robustness values directly from HyperLTL specifications automates the process of reward shaping, allowing RL agents to learn policies that maximize the probability of satisfying complex objectives without manual reward engineering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) faces a significant challenge in the design of reward functions that effectively capture complex desired behaviors. Existing approaches frequently encounter difficulties in identifying optimal solutions or in efficiently addressing such tasks.

Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) methods, particularly those leveraging temporal logics like Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), have demonstrated efficacy in single-agent settings. However, these methods are often insufficient for multi-agent scenarios because LTL typically specifies properties over individual execution traces, implicitly applying universal quantification (e.g., "for all traces..."). This universal scope can be overly restrictive, hindering the exploration of optimal policies in complex environments where agents have relational dependencies.

Extensions of temporal logic-based methods to MARL have been proposed, but these often fail to capture intricate relational dependencies between agents or are restricted to specific subsets of temporal properties, such as co-safety properties. Furthermore, techniques that compose Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with automata representing LTL specifications can suffer from state-space explosion, limiting their scalability in multi-agent contexts.

Another area, shield synthesis for RL, aims to ensure safety properties by evaluating proposed actions before execution. While some work has extended shield synthesis to decentralized multi-agent settings, these methods are typically limited to purely universal specifications (∀*ψ formulas). This limitation prevents them from addressing complex planning tasks that involve dependencies requiring alternating quantifiers (e.g., ∀∃ hyperproperties).

This research positions itself by identifying a gap in existing MARL frameworks: the inability to formally specify and learn policies for multi-agent systems that involve complex relational constraints and dependencies expressed over *sets* of execution traces. The authors propose leveraging hyperproperties, specifically HyperLTL, which offer greater expressiveness for characterizing requirements that span multiple traces, thereby addressing limitations of prior work and enabling the specification of nuanced inter-agent behaviors. The paper specifically highlights the novelty of addressing quantifier alternation in HyperLTL within an RL framework, an aspect not previously studied.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a specification-guided reinforcement learning framework, termed HYPRL, that can learn control policies for multi-agent systems. These policies are designed to maximize the probability of satisfying complex objectives and constraints expressed as hyperproperties using the formal language HyperLTL, particularly in Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with unknown transitions.

The motivation for HYPRL stems from several critical challenges in current MARL paradigms:
1. **Complexity of Reward Function Design**: Crafting reward functions that accurately reflect desirable behaviors in MARL is inherently difficult. As illustrated by the wildfire scenario (Figure 1 in the paper), multi-agent tasks often involve a combination of individual agent objectives (e.g., firefighter extinguishing fires, medical agent rescuing victims), relational constraints between agents (e.g., communication range), and inter-agent dependencies (e.g., medical agent not entering a fire zone before the firefighter has extinguished it). Manually designing such reward functions is error-prone and often leads to sub-optimal policies.
2. **Limitations of Existing Formalisms**: While temporal logics like LTL are effective for single-agent tasks, their inherent universal quantification (e.g., "for *all* traces...") proves too restrictive for multi-agent problems with conditional or interdependent behaviors. For instance, a specification like "for every firefighter path, there *exists* a medical agent path that satisfies certain conditions" cannot be naturally expressed by purely universal LTL. This limitation constrains RL agents to implicitly follow overly conservative strategies, hindering the discovery of more optimal and flexible solutions.
3. **Handling Quantifier Alternation**: Hyperproperties provide a powerful formalism to specify requirements over sets of execution traces, making them suitable for capturing relational and dependent behaviors across agents. However, HyperLTL formulas, especially those with quantifier alternation (e.g., ∀∃), introduce significant logical complexity. Prior to HYPRL, methods for handling such alternating quantifiers in the context of reinforcement learning for policy synthesis had not been extensively explored.
4. **Automating Specification-to-Reward Translation**: The goal is to move beyond manual reward engineering towards an automated synthesis of reward mechanisms directly from formal specifications. By deriving quantitative robustness values from HyperLTL formulas, the framework aims to provide a systematic and principled way to shape rewards, thereby guiding RL algorithms towards policies that maximize satisfaction of the specified hyperproperties.

In essence, HYPRL is motivated by the need for a robust, expressive, and automated approach to enable MARL agents to learn cooperative and constrained behaviors in complex, interdependent settings, particularly where traditional reward shaping or single-trace temporal logics fall short.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HYPRL framework addresses the challenge of learning control policies for multi-agent systems based on HyperLTL specifications by employing a three-step algorithmic approach: Skolemization, quantitative semantics for reward shaping, and reinforcement learning. The system operates on Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with unknown transitions.

**Overall Framework (Figure 4 Overview)**:
The process begins with a HyperLTL formula (φ) and an MDP (M). The formula is first Skolemized. Then, for each learning episode, agents generate execution traces (ζ_1...ζ_n). These traces, along with the Skolemized formula, are fed into a robustness function (ρ) to generate quantitative robustness values. These values serve as rewards for the reinforcement learning algorithm, which then iteratively updates the agent policies (π_1...π_n).

**Step 1: HyperLTL Skolemization**
The initial challenge addressed is the presence of quantifier alternations (e.g., ∀τ.∃τ'.ψ) in HyperLTL formulas, which complicates direct optimization. HYPRL employs Skolemization to transform the formula into a logically equivalent form without quantifier alternations.
- **Process**: For a given HyperLTL formula φ = Q_1τ_1...Q_nτ_n.ψ, where Q_ℓ ∈ {∀, ∃}, Skolemization introduces Skolem functions for each existentially quantified trace variable (τ_i). A Skolem function f_i: T^|Q_∀_i| → T maps a tuple of traces from all preceding universal quantifiers (Q_∀_i) to a specific trace for τ_i.
- **Output**: The formula is transformed into a Skolemized form, Skolem(φ) = ∃f_i (...) ∀τ_j (...).Skolem(ψ). This effectively shifts the problem of finding an existential trace to finding a *function* that, for any given universal traces, provides a witness existential trace.
- **Problem Reformulation**: The original optimization problem (maximizing the probability of satisfying φ) is reformulated to finding a tuple of Skolem witnesses ⟨f_i⟩ for existential quantifiers and a tuple of policies ⟨π*_j⟩ for universal quantifiers that maximize the probability of satisfying Skolem(φ). This means policies are learned only for universally quantified traces, while Skolem functions are learned to act as witnesses for existentially quantified traces.

**Step 2: Policy Learning with Quantitative Semantics**
To enable reinforcement learning, which typically requires numerical rewards, the framework converts the Boolean satisfaction checking of HyperLTL into a quantitative robustness value.
- **Robustness for a Single Trace (LTL)**: A robustness function ρ is defined that maps a finite trace and an LTL formula to a real value. Higher ρ values indicate a greater degree of satisfaction or "robustness" against violation, while lower values suggest potential violations. The function ρ is defined recursively over LTL operators (e.g., min for conjunction, max for disjunction, look-ahead for temporal operators). Predicates like `f(L(s_ℓ)) < c` are translated into `c - f(L(s_ℓ))`, allowing numerical evaluation.
- **Robustness for a Tuple of Traces**: For multi-trace scenarios, a `zip` function combines a tuple of finite traces into a single "zipped trace." The robustness is then evaluated on this zipped trace, allowing a single value to represent the satisfaction level across multiple agents' behaviors.
- **Robustness for Skolemized HyperLTL**: The robustness value for the Skolemized formula Skolem(φ) is computed over a combined tuple of traces. This tuple includes traces generated by policies for universal quantifiers (from `D_π_j`) and traces produced by the learned Skolem functions for existential quantifiers (from `Img(f_i)`). The objective is to maximize the probability that this robustness value converges to a predefined maximum (ρ_max). This captures how universally quantified policies must implicitly account for the optimal choices of existentially quantified Skolem functions.

**Step 3: Reinforcement Learning for HyperLTL**
The final step involves using reinforcement learning algorithms to optimize the policies and Skolem functions based on the robustness values.
- **Reward Signal**: The robustness value derived from Step 2 serves as the immediate reward R(s_k, a_k) for the RL agent. This transforms the logical satisfaction problem into a standard reward maximization problem.
- **Optimizing Expected Reward**: The Bellman equation is used to define the Q-value function, Q_NN*(s, a), which recursively represents the maximum expected reward achievable from state `s` by taking action `a`. The optimal neural network NN* is trained to learn these Q-values.
- **Constructing Policies**: From the learned optimal neural network NN*, the framework constructs the specific policies:
 - For each universally quantified trace variable τ_j, the optimal policy π*_j(ζ_j[0:k]) is directly derived from NN*_j's output for the current state `s_k`.
 - For each existentially quantified trace variable τ_i, a Skolem witness (function) is constructed. The policy for this trace, π*_i(ζ_i[0:k]), is derived from NN*_i, but its action depends on the observed prefixes of all *preceding universal paths* (Tr(ζ_i_1[0:k])...). This is crucial for capturing the dependencies inherent in alternating quantifiers.
- **Algorithms**: HYPRL is designed to be compatible with off-the-shelf RL algorithms such as DQN, PPO, or CQ-Learning.

The theoretical foundation includes proofs (Theorem 1 and 2) demonstrating that an optimal tuple of policies and Skolem witnesses for the Skolemized HyperLTL formula also constitutes an optimal solution for the original HyperLTL specification, maximizing the probability of its satisfaction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HYPRL was conducted on a diverse set of multi-agent learning tasks, comparing its performance against specification-driven baselines and manually designed reward functions.

**Safe RL in Grid Worlds (SRL)**:
- **2-Agent Setting (∀τ₁.∃τ₂.ψ)**: HYPRL, when combined with CQ-Learning, demonstrated superior performance compared to shield synthesis and plain CQ-Learning. For instance, in the ISR map, HYPRL agents reached their goals in an average of 7.58 steps with 0.25 collisions, significantly faster than shield synthesis (17.40 steps, 0.00 collisions) and plain CQ-Learning (27.95 steps, 0.19 collisions). In SUNY and MIT maps, HYPRL either matched (SUNY) or substantially reduced (MIT) the steps to goal compared to shield synthesis while maintaining zero collisions.
- **3-Agent Setting (∀τ₁.∀τ₂.∃τ₃.ψ)**: In more complex 3-agent scenarios, shield synthesis achieved zero collisions but often failed to reach goals within the 100-step bound (marked "S/B"). Plain CQ-Learning had high collision rates. HYPRL, however, significantly reduced the steps to goal (e.g., ISR: 74.18 steps with 7.78 collisions vs. CQ's 98.79 steps with 12.68 collisions, and Shield's S/B with 0.00 collisions), while maintaining lower collision rates than plain CQ-Learning.
- **DQN Comparison**: When combined with DQN, HYPRL consistently achieved a higher number of successful goal completions and required fewer learning episodes to achieve collision avoidance compared to plain DQN across all tested grid-world maps.

**Deep Sea Treasure (DST)**:
- HYPRL, integrated with both PPO and DQN, consistently outperformed the respective baseline algorithms (plain PPO and DQN) in terms of total collected treasures and the average amount of collected treasures per step, regardless of the number of training episodes (500 or 1000). For example, with PPO over 500 episodes, HYPRL achieved 22.93±2.2 total treasures (0.91±0.08 per step) compared to plain PPO's 4.31±1.2 total treasures (0.17±0.04 per step).
- An additional experiment in the appendix indicated that HYPRL generally required fewer steps to achieve specific cumulative treasure targets (e.g., reaching 20 treasures) compared to baselines, demonstrating efficiency.

**Post Correspondence Problem (PCP)**:
- For this undecidable problem, HYPRL (with DQN) was shown to be more efficient in learning optimal policies. It achieved a higher number of successful matches in both 5-domino and 6-domino settings compared to plain DQN, indicating its effectiveness in guiding learning for problems with intricate logical structures.

**Wildfire Scenario**:
- HYPRL (with PPO) consistently outperformed plain PPO across varying grid sizes (3x3, 5x5, 8x8, 10x10). The framework required fewer steps for agents to extinguish fires (O₁), rescue victims (O₂), and maintain a safe communication distance (C₁).
- In larger environments (5x5, 8x8, 10x10), plain PPO frequently failed to complete objectives within the imposed step-bounds, whereas HYPRL successfully guided agents to achieve all tasks. For example, in a 10x10 grid, HYPRL completed O₁ in 21272.8 steps and O₂ in 570.3 steps, while plain PPO timed out for O₁ and took 29023.6 steps for O₂.

**Fair Resource Allocation (Appendix D.5)**:
- In a multi-agent resource allocation task, HYPRL demonstrated the ability to learn policies that not only maximized the average resource utility for all agents but also maintained fairness by minimizing the difference in resource allocation between agents. Baseline methods, while sometimes achieving high average utility, failed to ensure equitable distribution.

In summary, the main findings consistently indicate that HYPRL is more efficient and effective than tested baselines across diverse complex multi-agent tasks. Its ability to leverage quantitative robustness values derived from HyperLTL specifications allows it to handle relational dependencies and quantifier alternations, leading to superior policy learning outcomes compared to methods relying on manually designed rewards or less expressive formalisms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HYPRL framework represents a significant development in the field of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) by integrating the expressive power of hyperproperties with reinforcement learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing the Reward Shaping Challenge**: HYPRL offers a systematic and automated approach to derive reward signals directly from high-level formal specifications expressed in HyperLTL. This mitigates the "notorious stumbling block" of manual reward function design, which is often heuristic-driven, prone to error, and difficult to scale for complex, interdependent multi-agent tasks. By providing a principled method for reward shaping, HYPRL can reduce development time and improve the reliability of MARL systems.

2. **Enhanced Expressiveness for Multi-Agent Systems**: The core strength of HYPRL lies in its utilization of hyperproperties. Unlike traditional temporal logics (e.g., LTL) that typically specify properties over single execution traces, HyperLTL can express requirements over *sets* of traces. This capability is critical for capturing complex relational objectives, safety constraints, and intricate dependencies between multiple agents (e.g., "for every possible path of agent A, there must exist a path for agent B such that..."). By handling quantifier alternation (e.g., ∀∃) through Skolemization, HYPRL enables the specification of more nuanced and less restrictive behaviors than purely universally quantified (∀∀) approaches, leading to the discovery of more optimal and flexible policies.

3. **Improved Performance and Efficiency**: The empirical evaluations consistently demonstrate HYPRL's superior performance across a range of benchmarks, including safety-aware planning, resource management, and even problems like the undecidable Post Correspondence Problem. HYPRL-guided agents achieved goals more efficiently (fewer steps), maintained higher success rates, and better adhered to complex safety constraints compared to baselines using manually designed rewards or other specification-driven methods like shield synthesis. Its ability to outperform baselines even in larger, more complex environments (e.g., wildfire scenarios) underscores its scalability and robustness.

4. **Broader Applicability to Critical Domains**: The capacity of HYPRL to synthesize policies for tasks with relational requirements and dependencies expands the applicability of MARL to complex real-world domains. Potential impacts include applications in:
 - **Disaster Response**: Coordinating search-and-rescue agents, firefighters, and medical personnel, where inter-agent dependencies (e.g., medical agents waiting for fire suppression) and safety constraints are crucial.
 - **Swarm Drones**: Managing large groups of autonomous aerial vehicles for surveillance, delivery, or mapping, requiring coordinated movements and collision avoidance.
 - **Smart Home Automation**: Orchestrating various smart devices to collectively achieve user goals while respecting privacy or energy consumption constraints.
 - **Industrial Resource Management**: Optimizing the allocation and utilization of shared resources among multiple robotic agents or automated systems on a factory floor.

5. **Foundation for Future Research**: The paper acknowledges limitations, such as the current focus on centralized control and fully observable environments (MDPs rather than POMDPs). These limitations highlight avenues for future research, suggesting that HYPRL could serve as a foundational framework for extensions to fully decentralized control, partially observable settings, and potentially incorporating other advanced logical formalisms like HyperATL* for team-level reasoning.

In conclusion, HYPRL advances the state of the art in MARL by providing a robust and expressive framework for policy synthesis from formal specifications. Its ability to automatically handle complex, multi-trace properties and quantifier alternations offers a principled solution to a long-standing challenge, paving the way for more sophisticated and reliable autonomous multi-agent systems.
