# RuCL: Stratified Rubric-Based Curriculum Learning for Multimodal Large Language Model Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The RuCL framework enhances Multimodal Large Language Model reasoning by applying a stratified rubric-based curriculum learning approach that reorients curriculum from data selection to dynamic reward design. This method yielded an average accuracy of 60.06% across seven visual reasoning benchmarks, improving the base model's performance by 7.83% and setting a new state-of-the-art for 7B-scale open-source models while mitigating reward hacking.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for MLLMs suffers from "reward hacking," leading to unreliable reasoning processes despite correct final answers.
- Existing fine-grained, rubric-based supervision methods are computationally costly due to instance-specific rubric generation.
- These methods also exhibit inefficient training dynamics by treating all reasoning skills (rubrics) as equally challenging, generating noisy gradients.

## Method

- The RuCL framework constructs ==generalized rubrics==, filtering and stratifying them into foundational and advanced levels based on empirical model pass rates.
- A ==dynamic, stability-aware curriculum mechanism== progressively weights foundational and advanced rubric rewards during reinforcement learning.
- It employs ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== to optimize a hybrid reward function combining rule-based outcome and curriculum-modulated rubric supervision.

## Results

- Attained state-of-the-art average accuracy of **60.06%** across seven visual reasoning benchmarks, representing a **+7.83%** improvement over the baseline 7B-scale model.
- Showed significant performance enhancements in multimodal mathematical reasoning (**+12.97%** on WeMATH) and complex logical deduction (**+10.40%** on LogicVista).
- Ablation studies confirmed the efficacy of stratified rubrics and dynamic, sigmoid-based curriculum scheduling in improving training stability and overall reasoning performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Curriculum learning can be effectively applied to the reward function itself, dynamically adjusting supervision complexity based on model proficiency.
> - Stratifying reasoning skills (rubrics) by empirically determined difficulty levels improves training stability and mitigates gradient noise from premature penalization.
> - Generalized rubric construction offers a computationally efficient method for providing fine-grained process supervision, crucial for addressing reward hacking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in complex visual reasoning tasks, including mathematical problem-solving and chart understanding. A common approach to enhance these reasoning abilities in post-training is Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). RLVR leverages rule-based verification of final answers, which circumvents the computational expense associated with reward models.

However, a limitation of outcome-based RLVR is its propensity to foster "reward hacking." In this phenomenon, MLLMs may learn spurious reasoning patterns or superficial shortcuts that coincidentally lead to correct final answers, even if intermediate steps are contradictory or hallucinatory. This compromises the reliability of the reasoning process.

To address reward hacking, recent developments have utilized LLM-as-a-Judge frameworks that incorporate structured rubrics to evaluate the validity of intermediate reasoning trajectories. While these rubric-based approaches offer fine-grained supervision, they introduce two main challenges. First, generating instance-level rubrics incurs substantial computational overhead, particularly in online reinforcement learning environments. Second, existing methods typically assign uniform importance to all rubrics throughout the training process, failing to account for varying difficulty levels or learnability across different evaluation criteria. This can result in noisy gradient signals, as models may be penalized for complex logical failures before acquiring foundational skills, hindering efficient convergence.

This research positions itself by drawing inspiration from Curriculum Learning (CL), which traditionally structures training data from easy to hard. In contrast to prior CL applications that primarily focus on data selection, this work proposes a novel application of curriculum learning directly to reward design. By stratifying rubrics based on empirical difficulty and dynamically adjusting their weights, the framework aims to mitigate computational costs and optimize training dynamics in rubric-based MLLM reasoning enhancement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations observed in current methods for enhancing Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) reasoning. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is widely used, but its reliance on outcome-only supervision often leads to "reward hacking," where models generate logically flawed intermediate steps that coincidentally yield correct final answers. This outcome-centric approach overlooks the quality and integrity of the reasoning process itself.

Rubric-based approaches have been introduced to provide more fine-grained supervision by evaluating intermediate reasoning steps. However, these methods face their own set of challenges:
1. **Computational Cost**: Generating rubrics specific to each instance is computationally intensive, particularly in dynamic online reinforcement learning settings. This overhead limits the scalability and practicality of such approaches.
2. **Inefficient Training Dynamics**: Current rubric-based methods typically assign equal weight to all rubrics regardless of their inherent difficulty. This can result in models being penalized for advanced logical errors before they have mastered foundational skills, such as visual perception or basic inference. Such premature penalization generates noisy gradient signals, leading to unstable and inefficient optimization during training.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are to:
- Develop a novel framework, Stratified Rubric-based Curriculum Learning (RuCL), that re-conceptualizes curriculum learning by applying it to reward design rather than merely data selection.
- Reduce computational overhead by generating a set of generalized rubrics that are broadly applicable across various reasoning tasks, thereby avoiding the need for costly instance-specific rubric generation.
- Empirically stratify these generalized rubrics into distinct difficulty levels (e.g., foundational and advanced) based on the model's observed competence.
- Implement a dynamic weighting mechanism that adjusts the influence of different rubric categories throughout training. This mechanism is designed to progressively guide the model, initially focusing on mastering foundational perceptual skills and subsequently advancing to tackle more complex logical reasoning challenges.
- Enhance the overall reasoning capabilities of MLLMs, ensuring that models not only arrive at correct answers but also follow coherent and logically sound reasoning trajectories, thereby mitigating the reward hacking phenomenon.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to deliver a more efficient, stable, and effective paradigm for post-training MLLMs to improve their complex visual reasoning abilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Stratified Rubric-based Curriculum Learning (RuCL), is structured as a two-phase process: Generalized Rubric Construction and Stratification, followed by Dynamic Curriculum Learning.

#### 3.1. Phase I: Generalized Rubric Construction and Stratification

This phase focuses on creating a reusable set of evaluation rubrics and categorizing them by empirical difficulty.
1. **Computational Efficiency Analysis**: The authors highlight that RuCL's approach significantly reduces pre-computation burden compared to instance-specific rubric generation. While online evaluation costs are comparable, instance-level methods scale linearly with dataset size (O(N × C_gen)), whereas RuCL generates a generalized rubric pool once (O(1 × C_gen)).
2. **Candidate Generation & Rollout**: A teacher Large Language Model (LLM), Gemini 3 Pro, is prompted with context including task category, images, query, and ground truth to generate an initial set of 20 diverse rubric candidates. To assess these candidates, a base MLLM performs rollouts on a randomly sampled subset of 2,000 training instances (ViRL-39K dataset), collecting rubric-level evaluation signals.
3. **Applicability-Aware Evaluation**: A specialized Judge mechanism evaluates each rubric candidate `R_j` for a given sample `x_i`. It outputs a tuple `(a_ij, s_ij)`, where `a_ij ∈ {0, 1}` indicates if `R_j` is applicable to `x_i`, and `s_ij ∈ {0, 1}` indicates whether the model's output satisfies the rubric (only if `a_ij = 1`). This decoupling ensures that metrics accurately reflect rubric coverage and model proficiency by preventing non-applicable rubrics from skewing statistics.
4. **Metric-Based Filtering and Stratification**:
 - **Filtering**: The **Applicability Rate (η_j)**, calculated as the proportion of instances where a rubric is applicable, is used to filter out rarely relevant rubrics (e.g., `η_j < 0.99`). This step retained 6 core rubrics from the initial 20 candidates.
 - **Stratification**: For the filtered rubrics, the **Pass Rate (p_j)** is computed as the model's conditional success rate on applicable instances. This metric serves as an empirical proxy for difficulty, with low pass rates indicating higher difficulty. Rubrics are then partitioned:
 - **Foundational Rubrics (R_easy)**: Characterized by high pass rates, targeting prerequisite skills like visual presence and entity extraction (e.g., R01-R03 in Table 1). These provide stable initial supervision signals.
 - **Advanced Rubrics (R_hard)**: Identified by low pass rates, targeting complex reasoning skills like step coherence and evidence grounding (e.g., R04-R06 in Table 1). These address underdeveloped reasoning gaps.
 - **Statistical Justification**: The authors justify using pass rate as a difficulty proxy by showing that as `p_j → 0`, the Coefficient of Variation (CV) of the gradient estimator for that rubric diverges. This indicates that low-pass-rate rubrics (advanced) yield noisy gradients, while high-pass-rate rubrics (foundational) offer reliable signals.
 - **Evaluator Assignment**: Foundational and Advanced rubrics are evaluated by an LLM Judge (Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct), while final answer correctness is determined by a deterministic Answer Verifier.

#### 3.2. Phase II: Dynamic Curriculum Learning

This phase integrates hybrid rewards and dynamically adjusts their emphasis using a stability-aware curriculum.
1. **Hybrid Reward Components**:
 - **Outcome Reward (r_ans)**: A binary reward (0 or 1) based on strict rule-based verification of the final answer against ground truth.
 - **Rubric-based Reward (r_rub)**: Tier-level reasoning scores are aggregated from the binary satisfaction signals of `R_easy` and `R_hard` rubrics.
 - `r_easy(y_i|x)`: Average score across all foundational rubrics applicable to sample `x_i`.
 - `r_hard(y_i|x)`: Average score across all advanced rubrics applicable to sample `x_i`.
2. **Performance-Triggered Curriculum Scheduling**: A curriculum coefficient `λ_t ∈ [0, λ_max]` dynamically adjusts the balance between `r_easy` and `r_hard` contributions to the overall rubric reward `r_rub^(t)`. `λ_t` is regulated through three phases:
 - **Stabilization Phase**: `λ_t` is initially set to 0, prioritizing foundational rubrics. This phase continues until the model demonstrates consistent proficiency on `R_easy` (average foundational rewards `μ_easy^(t)` exceed a threshold `τ_th` of 0.9 over a sliding window `w` of 20 steps).
 - **Curriculum Ramp-up**: Once the stability condition is met, `λ_t` linearly or sigmoidally increases from `λ_base = 0` to `λ_max = 1.0` over a defined duration `T_ramp`. The sigmoid growth function was found to be more effective in experiments.
 - **Advanced Consolidation**: After the ramp-up, `λ_t` is fixed at `λ_max = 1.0`, giving full weight to advanced rubrics.
3. **Total Reward**: The final scalar reward `r^(t)(y_i|x)` used for optimization is a weighted sum of the outcome reward and the curriculum-modulated rubric reward: `r^(t) = α * r_ans + (1 - α) * r_rub^(t)`. The hyperparameter `α` (set to 0.7) controls the trade-off between final correctness and process supervision.
4. **Optimization Algorithm**: The policy `π_θ` is optimized using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a stable policy-gradient method that estimates advantage using group-based statistics from multiple sampled outputs, thereby avoiding the need for a separate value function (critic).

This methodology aims to provide adaptive supervision, reduce gradient noise, and guide the MLLM through a structured learning path from basic perception to complex reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of RuCL was evaluated through extensive experiments on multiple visual reasoning benchmarks, comparing its performance against various MLLM baselines. The base model utilized was Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, trained on the ViRL-39K dataset.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

RuCL achieved an average accuracy of 60.06% across all seven evaluated benchmarks, representing an average improvement of +7.83% over the Qwen2.5-VL-7B base model. This positions RuCL as a competitive performer among 7B-scale open-source reasoning models.

#### 4.2. Mathematical Reasoning Performance

On multimodal mathematical reasoning benchmarks, RuCL demonstrated superior performance:
- **WeMATH**: An increase of 12.97% (from 58.52% to 71.49%).
- **MathVerse**: An increase of 5.16% (from 48.98% to 54.14%).
- **MathVision**: An improvement of 4.70%.
- **MathVista**: An improvement of 3.90%.

These results indicate the efficacy of the rubric-based reward modeling and curriculum learning strategy in enhancing mathematical reasoning abilities, particularly by progressively introducing harder reasoning constraints.

#### 4.3. Generalization to General and Logical Benchmarks

RuCL also exhibited generalization capabilities across broader reasoning tasks:
- **LogicVista**: A 10.40% improvement (from 39.26% to 49.66%), surpassing other open-source 7B competitors in complex logical deduction.
- **MMMU**: A 5.67% increase in multi-disciplinary knowledge reasoning.
- **Super-CLEVR Counting**: A substantial 12.00% gain (reaching 85.50%), indicating enhanced fine-grained visual perception.

These findings suggest that combining intermediate rubric rewards with final outcome supervision improves the model's fundamental reasoning robustness rather than merely overfitting to specific mathematical domains. The model also narrowed the performance gap with proprietary models despite its smaller scale.

#### 4.4. Training Dynamics and Curriculum Efficacy

Analysis of training dynamics (Figure 3 in the paper) showed that the curriculum initially prioritized foundational rubrics, leading to rapid mastery and stabilization of prerequisite skills. As the mechanism detected stable proficiency (scores consistently above 0.9) and progressively introduced advanced reasoning constraints, the model exhibited steady improvement in higher-order tasks while maintaining robust performance on foundational metrics. Qualitative case studies (Appendix G) further illustrated RuCL's ability to mitigate reward hacking by penalizing spurious reasoning chains that produce correct answers, thus enforcing genuine logical consistency.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

Ablation studies validated the contributions of key design choices:
- **Impact of Rubric Aggregation and Scheduling**:
 - **Vanilla GRPO (outcome-only)**: Achieved an average accuracy of 57.13%.
 - **Uniform Averaging (all filtered rubrics, no stratification or scheduling)**: Showed a modest gain at 57.56% due to process supervision.
 - **Linear Stratification (stratified rubrics with a linear ramp for `λ_t`)**: Further improved performance to 58.41% by distinguishing difficulty levels.
 - **RuCL (Sigmoid Stratification)**: Achieved 60.06% accuracy. It significantly outperformed the linear strategy, particularly on perception-heavy (Counting: 85.50% vs. 79.50%) and logic-intensive tasks (LogicVista: 49.66% vs. 47.43%). This was attributed to the Sigmoid schedule's ability to reach maximum difficulty saturation earlier, providing a longer stable period for convergence under hard constraints.
- **Sensitivity to Reward Balancing Hyperparameter α**: Optimal overall performance was observed at `α = 0.7`. Deviations showed performance drops: `α = 0.5` (excessive auxiliary constraints) led to decreases (e.g., Counting to 79.00%), while `α = 0.9` (diminished fine-grained supervision) caused significant degradation in complex reasoning (e.g., WeMath to 53.78%).
- **Sensitivity to Sliding Window Size w**: A window size of `w = 20` for the stability-aware trigger yielded the best performance. Shorter windows (e.g., `w = 10`) underperformed, suggesting susceptibility to transient fluctuations and premature curriculum transitions.

#### 4.6. Out-of-Distribution Robustness

On the specialized EvadeBench (Appendix H), which assesses evasive content detection, RuCL achieved 45.86%, showing a modest improvement over the baseline (43.90%) and Vanilla GRPO (44.47%). This indicates a slight advantage in generalization capability, even though the task presented a considerable challenge for all models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Stratified Rubric-based Curriculum Learning (RuCL) represents a notable advancement in the training of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), particularly in the domain of complex visual reasoning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Mitigation of Reward Hacking**: RuCL directly addresses the critical issue of "reward hacking" inherent in outcome-only reinforcement learning paradigms. By incorporating fine-grained, rubric-based process supervision, the framework guides models to develop logically sound reasoning trajectories rather than merely stumbling upon correct answers. This leads to more robust, reliable, and trustworthy MLLMs, which is crucial for their deployment in applications requiring high integrity, such as scientific analysis, medical diagnostics, or automated decision-making.

- **Enhanced Training Efficiency and Stability**: The proposed methodology improves upon existing rubric-based approaches by making them more computationally efficient and dynamically stable. By generating generalized rubrics upfront, RuCL reduces the high computational overhead associated with instance-level rubric creation. The stratified curriculum, which progressively introduces advanced reasoning challenges only after foundational skills are mastered, minimizes noisy gradients and cold-start instability, leading to more efficient and stable optimization during the reinforcement learning phase.

- **Improved Reasoning Capabilities**: The experimental results demonstrate that RuCL significantly enhances MLLM reasoning performance across a diverse set of visual reasoning benchmarks, including mathematical problem-solving and general logical deduction. Achieving state-of-the-art results among 7B-scale open-source models indicates that this approach fosters both foundational visual perception and advanced logical inference, enabling MLLMs to tackle more intricate tasks with greater accuracy and consistency.

- **Novel Curriculum Learning Paradigm**: RuCL introduces a novel re-conceptualization of curriculum learning by shifting its focus from data selection to reward design. This paradigm offers a new direction for structuring complex learning tasks, suggesting that tailoring the feedback mechanism itself can be a powerful tool for guiding model development. This could inspire future research into more sophisticated and adaptive reward-centric learning strategies across various AI domains.

- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work identifies avenues for further exploration, such as online rubric construction and adaptive updating of rubric difficulties during training. These future directions could lead to even more dynamic, autonomous, and sophisticated MLLM training regimes, potentially reducing reliance on static pre-defined curricula.

- **Contribution to MLLM Safety and Alignment**: By prioritizing verifiable intermediate reasoning, RuCL contributes to the broader goal of aligning AI models with human expectations for explainability and logical coherence. This alignment is vital for developing AI systems that are not only capable but also understandable and controllable, increasing public trust and facilitating responsible AI development.

In summary, RuCL offers a principled and empirically validated approach to enhancing MLLM reasoning by integrating stratified rubric-based rewards with a dynamic curriculum learning strategy. Its contributions extend beyond mere performance gains, impacting the foundational aspects of MLLM training by promoting reliability, efficiency, and the development of truly reasoning-capable AI.
