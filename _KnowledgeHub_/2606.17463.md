# WeaveLA: Event Driven Cross-Subtask Latent Memory Weaving for Repetitive Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> WeaveLA introduces an event-driven, action-side latent memory weaving interface for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, enabling them to effectively handle repetitive manipulation tasks by transferring sub-task completion information. The system improves aggregate success rates by over 5% and achieves substantial gains on challenging multi-repetition tasks, like SWINGXTIMES, where baseline models struggle.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies struggle with repetitive and sequential manipulation tasks due to a lack of explicit mechanisms for cross-subtask information transfer.
- Short-window VLAs cannot effectively track accumulated progress or count repetitions when visual inputs are perceptually similar across different stages of a task.
- Existing memory-augmented VLA approaches often incur high computational costs with per-frame memory writes or lack the specificity to route concise information directly to the subsequent action expert.

## Method

- **WeaveLA** integrates an event-driven, action-side memory mechanism into frozen VLA backbones, triggering memory writes specifically at sub-goal completion events during task rollout.
- A query-driven ==Memory Weaver== module compresses multimodal perceptual features from completed sub-tasks into compact latent memories using attention pooling.
- These latent memories directly condition the action expert of the subsequent sub-task via an additional memory-conditioned AdaRMS step in Gemma blocks, ensuring lightweight modulation and preserving the base policy.

## Results

- WeaveLA improved aggregate average success rates on RoboMME from **19.0%** to **24.7%** (6-task scale) and from **17.3%** to **23.3%** (16-task scale) compared to the Weaver-off baseline.
- On SWINGXTIMES (N=3), a challenging repetition task, success increased from **0%** to **47.8%** (6-task scale) and from **4%** to **30%** (16-task scale) with WeaveLA.
- The system maintained robust performance using a model-internal "latent-shift" event detector, largely replicating oracle trigger results for most tasks, and showed consistent gains in small-scale real-robot experiments on PICKXTIMES.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sub-goal completion events serve as efficient and causally-aligned temporal units for transferring condensed information between sequential sub-tasks.
> - Directly conditioning the action expert with compact latent memories is more effective for repetition-aware control than diluting information through VLM backbones or language prompts.
> - A lightweight, modular memory interface can significantly enhance VLA performance on specific memory-dependent tasks without boosting generic capabilities or requiring full retraining of the base policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements driven by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, such as RT-2, OpenVLA, and $\pi^0/\pi^{0.5}$. These models are proficient in single-step manipulations, conditioning actions on current observations, proprioceptive states, and natural language instructions within short temporal windows. They demonstrate reliability in tasks like grasping objects or pressing buttons in contained environments.

However, a substantial portion of real-world robotic tasks involves repetition and sequential sub-tasks where the current action depends on the outcome of previous stages. Current short-window VLAs are often limited in these scenarios because they lack an explicit mechanism to transfer condensed information across sub-task boundaries. Once one sub-task concludes, the next is initiated without a summary of the preceding events, leading to a "brittleness" in handling cross-subtask dependencies.

Existing memory-augmented VLA policies attempt to address this by incorporating temporal context. These methods vary in terms of what information is stored (e.g., multi-frame summaries, visual traces, semantic traces, recurrent summaries), where it is consumed (e.g., prompt augmentation, planner-level memory, action-path modulation), and critically, *when* the memory write is triggered. Most current approaches either write memory at every frame, utilize a sliding window, or retrieve information from demonstration-time stages. A more recent line of work triggers memory writes at sub-goal events but does not provide a self-contained summary explicitly handed off to the subsequent sub-task's action expert. This deficiency highlights a gap in how VLA policies manage sequential, dependent sub-tasks, particularly those requiring repetition counting or state tracking.

WeaveLA addresses this specific limitation by introducing an event-driven, action-side memory mechanism. It aims to bridge the gap in handling repetitive tasks by identifying and leveraging sub-goal completion events as natural temporal units for information transfer, routing compact summaries directly to the action expert of the next sub-task. This approach distinguishes itself from continuous, per-frame memory writes and static, demonstration-based retrieval by focusing on the discrete, causal boundaries of sequential actions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the capability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies in performing repetitive manipulation tasks that require cross-subtask information flow. The authors identify a fundamental limitation in current short-window VLA policies: their inability to effectively route and utilize information about completed sub-tasks to inform subsequent actions in a sequence. This limitation manifests as brittleness in tasks where the decision or execution of a current sub-task depends causally on what has transpired in the preceding ones.

The motivation stems from the observation that many real-world robotic applications, such as assembly, packaging, or inspection, involve executing the same primitive actions multiple times or making decisions based on accumulated progress. For instance, a robot might need to place *N* items, or swing an object *X* times before pressing a button. In such scenarios, the visual input at different stages of repetition might be perceptually identical, making it impossible for a short-window VLA to infer progress solely from the current observation. Without an explicit mechanism to track the completion of previous repetitions or sub-goals, these policies fail.

The key principles driving WeaveLA's design are:
1. **Sub-goal completion as the natural temporal unit for memory hand-off:** The authors argue that information about a completed sub-goal becomes relevant precisely at the moment of its completion, informing the initiation or modification of the next sub-task. This contrasts with per-frame writing (which incurs high memory cost and requires implicit boundary discovery) or demonstration-time retrieval (which cannot track real-time rollout progress).
2. **Direct routing to the action expert:** The transferred memory should directly influence the motor-execution level of the subsequent sub-task, rather than being diluted through the VLM backbone or language prompt, ensuring its immediate and relevant impact on action generation.

Based on these motivations, the specific objectives of WeaveLA are:
- To design a cross-subtask memory interface that explicitly routes condensed information between sequential sub-tasks.
- To implement this interface on top of existing frozen VLA backbones (specifically $\pi^{0.5}$) with minimal trainable parameters, preserving the base policy's performance on non-repetitive tasks.
- To demonstrate that this event-triggered, action-side memory design leads to significant performance improvements specifically on repetitive and cross-subtask dependent tasks, without being a generic capacity boost for all tasks.
- To validate the proposed "when" axis design (event-triggered writes) by comparing it against alternative memory write triggers and consumption mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

WeaveLA is presented as a cross-subtask latent memory weaving interface designed to augment existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, specifically focusing on repetition-aware robot control. The framework operates on manipulation episodes that are decomposable into ordered sub-tasks.

**Overview:**
WeaveLA integrates a cross-subtask memory channel on top of a frozen VLA backbone. At the completion of each sub-task $\tau_{k-1}$, a compact latent memory $m_{k-1}$ is generated. This latent then conditions the action expert for the subsequent sub-task $\tau_k$. The design is "action-side," meaning the memory directly modulates the action generation path, leaving the VLA's language prompt and visual context processing unchanged. The architecture consists of three main components: (A) a frozen perception backbone, (B) a query-driven Memory Weaver, and (C) action-side memory conditioning.

**Sub-goal-Event Trigger:**
A core design principle of WeaveLA is that memory writes are triggered exclusively at sub-goal completion events during rollout, rather than per-frame or through demonstration-stage retrieval. For simulation experiments, sub-goal boundaries are supplied by the simulator. The memory $m_{k-1}$ is associated with the segment $\tau_{k-1}$ (frames between event $e_{k-1}$ and $e_k$). This event-driven trigger aligns the memory write with the natural causal structure of repetitive tasks, where information from a completed stage becomes relevant for the next.

**Query-Driven Memory Weaver:**
For a completed segment $\tau_{k-1}$, the Memory Weaver extracts perceptual features from the frames within that segment. These features include visual tokens from the frozen vision encoder of the VLA and projected proprioceptive state tokens. These multimodal features are concatenated and layer-normalized into a feature sequence $H \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L \times d_h}$. A fixed set of $N=8$ learnable query latents $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_h}$ then compresses $H$ into a compact latent $m_{k-1}$ via a single attention-pooling step. The memory is segment-local, meaning $m_{k-1}$ is computed solely from features of $\tau_{k-1}$, without feeding back previous memories ($m_{k-2}$). The output memory width is $d=2048$.

**Action-Side Memory Consumption:**
WeaveLA integrates this latent memory into the action expert of the $\pi^{0.5}$ policy, which utilizes Gemma blocks. Each Gemma block already contains two timestep-conditioned AdaRMS (Adaptive Root Mean Square) modules. WeaveLA inserts a third, memory-conditioned AdaRMS step between the self-attention residual and the existing pre-FFN AdaRMS module. The $N=8$ latent memory tokens are projected to the action-expert width, and then used in a cross-attention mechanism with the action hidden states to produce scale and shift parameters for the AdaRMS modulation. This design ensures lightweight memory modulation, adjusting only AdaRMS parameters and allowing the pretrained backbone to remain frozen.

**Staged Training Strategy:**
Training WeaveLA is structured in three stages to ensure stability and proper grounding:
1. **Stage 0 (Action Grounding):** The vision encoder is frozen, and only the base policy's flow-matching action loss is active. This establishes a competent short-window policy on the target tasks.
2. **Stage 1 (Memory Warmup):** The Weaver module and the memory-conditioning pathway are introduced. They are trained solely against the action loss, allowing the memory pathway to stabilize without semantic objectives. This is crucial as a single-stage training combining all objectives was found to lead to representation collapse (monotonically decaying latent norm).
3. **Stage 2 (Full Integration):** The full objective is enabled, including semantic alignment and contrastive auxiliary losses, alongside the action loss.

**Training Objective and Computational Footprint:**
The primary objective is the base policy's flow-matching action loss. Additionally, in Stage 2, two auxiliary losses are incorporated:
- **Memory-text alignment loss ($L_{align}$):** Matches the Weaver latent to a text encoder embedding of the just-completed sub-goal description.
- **Memory contrastive loss ($L_{ctr}$):** Pulls together latent-text pairs from the same trajectory while pushing apart pairs from different trajectories.
These auxiliary losses (weighted at 0.05 and 0.02, respectively) shape the latent space during training, but sub-goal text is not used during inference.

The trainable parameters, approximately 46M, include LoRA adapters for the $\pi^{0.5}$ backbone, Weaver query latents and projections, and action-side memory cross-attention/AdaRMS projections. This constitutes about 1.4% of the frozen $\sim3.4$B parameter base policy. The event-triggered memory write strategy minimizes runtime cost during rollout compared to dense per-frame memory mechanisms.

**Experimental Setup:**
Evaluation is conducted on RoboMME, a benchmark of 16 manipulation tasks. Focus is placed on "repetition" tasks (PICKXTIMES, SWINGXTIMES) and "dependent-stop" tasks (STOPCUBE) that explicitly require tracking sub-goal counts or accumulated progress. The backbone used is $\pi^{0.5}$, and comparisons are made between Weaver-on and Weaver-off conditions, as well as ablations on extractor design and trigger source.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of WeaveLA on the RoboMME benchmark provided evidence for its effectiveness, particularly in scenarios requiring cross-subtask information processing.

**Aggregate Results:**
- On the six tasks used for $\pi^{0.5}$ training, WeaveLA improved the aggregate average success rate from 19.0% (Weaver off) to 24.7% (Weaver on) at the 6-task training scale.
- At the 16-task training scale, aggregate success improved from 17.3% (Weaver off) to 23.3% (Weaver on).
- These aggregate improvements were primarily driven by substantial gains on SWINGXTIMES (32% $\rightarrow$ 56% at 6-task scale) and STOPCUBE (8% $\rightarrow$ 22% at 6-task scale), which are tasks identified as requiring cross-subtask information.

**Repetition-Stratified Analysis:**
This analysis stratified results by the instruction-encoded sub-goal count (N) for repetition tasks (SWINGXTIMES, PICKXTIMES), providing a detailed view of where the mechanism engages:
- For N=1 (single sub-goal, no cross-subtask dependency), both Weaver-on and Weaver-off conditions achieved approximately 100% success, indicating that WeaveLA does not provide a generic capacity boost but specifically targets cross-subtask dependencies.
- For N $\geq$ 2, a performance gap emerged. On SWINGXTIMES with N=3 (the hardest repetition slice), Weaver-off achieved 0% success, while Weaver-on reached 47.8% success. At the 16-task training scale, this slice improved from 4% to 30%.
- Pooling across both repetition tasks at the 6-task scale, Weaver-on succeeded on 24.6% of N $\geq$ 2 episodes compared to 7.2% for Weaver-off, representing a 3.4x relative improvement. Similar trends were observed at the 16-task scale.
- The same pattern of mechanism localization was observed on the STOPCUBE task, which requires dependent stopping based on accumulated events.

**Other Stratified and Paired Analyses:**
- **Difficulty-stratified analysis:** Gains were concentrated on "Hard" episodes (1.4% $\rightarrow$ 12.5%), with marginal changes on "Easy" and "Medium" episodes.
- **Per-episode paired analysis:** On 800 matched episodes, Weaver-on uniquely succeeded on 13 episodes versus 1 for Weaver-off on SWINGXTIMES, and 8 versus 1 on STOPCUBE. For other tasks, unique success counts differed by at most 3, further confirming the mechanism-aligned gains.

**STOPCUBE What/Where/When Ablation:**
Comparison against other RoboMME baselines on the STOPCUBE task (which requires event counting and precise temporal coordination) revealed:
- WeaveLA (22.0% at 6-task, 12.0% at 16-task) outperformed most published MME-VLA variants and prior memory-augmented baselines (MemER, SAM2Act+, both at 0%).
- FrameSamp+Modul (42.0%), which uses a dense perceptual buffer of past frames, remained the strongest published result. The authors suggest that WeaveLA's event-driven, per-event latent and FrameSamp's dense perceptual buffer address orthogonal aspects (counting vs. continuous state awareness) and could potentially be combined.
- The failures of other methods (e.g., recurrent state models diluting the count signal, symbolic subgoal predictors compounding errors, keyframe retrieval lacking temporal precision) were attributed to their different "what," "where," or "when" design choices not suiting the precise counting requirement of STOPCUBE.

**Extractor Design Study (Attention Pooling vs. Q-Former):**
- Replacing the single-step attention-pooling Weaver with a multi-layer Q-Former extractor led to a collapse in 16-task success (from 23.3% to 3.5%). A dominant failure mode for the Q-Former variant was rollout timeouts (56.3% of episodes), attributed to higher inference cost and less stable training. This result supported the use of the simpler attention-pooling extractor.

**Trigger Source Robustness:**
- Replacing the simulator-provided (oracle) sub-goal boundaries with a model-internal "latent-shift" detector (derived from the Stage 2 contrastive auxiliary) demonstrated robust performance for most tasks.
- The 16-task aggregate success remained comparable (latent-shift 17.4% vs. oracle 17.0% vs. no-memory 16.25%).
- The mechanism-localization signature on SWINGXTIMES replicated under latent-shift: N=1 saturated at 100%, and on N=3, latent-shift achieved 21.7% success, recovering 68% of the oracle's 4% $\rightarrow$ 30% gap.
- An exception was STOPCUBE, where latent-shift performance collapsed to the no-memory baseline (2% vs. 12% oracle), indicating that symbolic counting tasks may still require learned symbolic detectors or more robust event detection.

**Real-Robot Sanity Check:**
- A small-scale real-robot experiment on PICKXTIMES (N=3) showed consistent results with simulation, with Weaver-on achieving 70% success (14/20 episodes) compared to Weaver-off's 45% (9/20 episodes).

**Bottleneck Width Ablation:**
- Testing different memory bottleneck widths (4, 8, 16 latents) indicated a binding floor (N=4 performed poorly, aggregating to 2.0%) and saturation at 8 tokens. Increasing capacity to 16 tokens did not improve aggregate success (17.5% vs. 18.12% for N=8), suggesting 8 tokens were sufficient for the tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

WeaveLA addresses a critical limitation of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, which often struggle with tasks requiring memory and sequential decision-making over multiple sub-tasks. By identifying the sub-goal completion event as a natural temporal unit for memory transfer and routing a compact latent summary directly to the action expert, the authors propose a principled approach to imbue VLAs with repetition-awareness.

The significance of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Mechanism-Aligned Gains:** The research provides evidence that WeaveLA's benefits are specifically observed in scenarios where cross-subtask information is necessary. The stratified analyses (by repetition count N, difficulty, and paired episode comparisons) demonstrate that performance improves substantially on tasks like SWINGXTIMES (N=3 from 0% to 47.8%) and STOPCUBE, while performance on tasks not requiring such memory remains largely unchanged. This specificity indicates that the system is not merely increasing overall capacity but effectively addressing the identified bottleneck.
- **Lightweight and Modular Design:** WeaveLA is implemented as a lightweight interface on top of a frozen VLA backbone ($\pi^{0.5}$), with only a small Weaver module and action-side modulation being trained. This modularity minimizes the additional computational cost and preserves the base policy's existing capabilities, making it a practical enhancement for existing large VLA models.
- **Event-Driven Memory Principle:** The concept of event-triggered memory writes, specifically at sub-goal completion, offers an alternative to continuous, per-frame memory storage or static, demonstration-based retrieval. This design is argued to be more aligned with the causal structure of repetitive tasks, allowing for efficient and targeted information transfer.
- **Robustness to Trigger Source:** The demonstration that a model-internal latent-shift detector can largely replicate the performance of an oracle (simulator-provided) trigger suggests potential for practical deployment without requiring external supervision for sub-goal boundary detection in many cases. This enhances the generalizability of the approach.

**Potential Impact:**

The development of WeaveLA has several potential impacts on the field of robotic manipulation and VLA policy design:

- **Expanded Robotic Capabilities:** By enabling VLAs to effectively handle repetitive and long-horizon tasks, WeaveLA could facilitate the deployment of robots in more complex industrial and service environments, such as assembly lines, packaging, or quality control, where sequential and counting tasks are common.
- **Improved Generalization:** The ability to track progress and state across sub-tasks can lead to more robust and generalizable robotic policies, as they would be less prone to failure modes associated with identical visual states at different points in a sequence.
- **New Directions for Memory Design:** The work highlights the importance of the "when" axis in memory design for VLAs, offering a distinct alternative to existing approaches. This could inspire further research into event-driven or causally-aligned memory mechanisms in embodied AI.
- **Building Blocks for More Complex Behaviors:** The ability to weave latent memories across sub-tasks could serve as a foundational component for learning and executing even more hierarchical and abstract robotic skills.

**Limitations acknowledged by the authors include:**

- The latent-shift trigger's collapse on count-critical STOPCUBE, indicating that tasks requiring precise symbolic counting may still need more specialized learned symbolic detectors.
- The need for further characterization of the Q-Former instability observed in the extractor design study.
- The potential for composing WeaveLA with input-side optimizations like FrameSamp, which could lead to combined benefits.

Overall, WeaveLA represents an advancement in designing VLA policies that can effectively manage sequential dependencies, thereby expanding their applicability to a broader range of real-world robotic tasks.
