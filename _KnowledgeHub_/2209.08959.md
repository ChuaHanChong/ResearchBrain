# Latent Plans for Task-Agnostic Offline Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> TACO-RL, a hierarchical self-supervised framework, enables robots to perform complex, long-horizon tasks from high-dimensional visual observations using only offline and unstructured "play" data. This approach demonstrates substantial performance improvements, often by an order of magnitude, over leading baselines in both simulated and real-world multi-task robot manipulation scenarios.

## Problem

- Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods struggle with long-horizon tasks due to cumulative error, making them impractical for complex real-world robot control.
- Learning general-purpose robot skills from high-dimensional sensory inputs (like camera observations) typically requires large, curated datasets or explicit environment models.
- Existing hierarchical approaches often rely on rigid, predefined low-level skills or expert data, limiting their adaptability and scalability for task-agnostic control.

## Method

- **TACO-RL** employs a hierarchical, two-stage approach: a low-level policy learns a continuous space of latent skills from unstructured play data using a ==Conditional Variational Auto-Encoder (CVAE)==.
- A high-level policy, trained with ==Conservative Q-Learning (CQL)==, learns to chain these latent skills for long-horizon goal-conditioned tasks, utilizing ==hindsight relabeling== and ==negative goal sampling==.
- The method operates entirely offline and model-free, learning directly from high-dimensional visual observations without explicit task definitions or environment dynamics models.

## Results

- In the CALVIN simulation environment, TACO-RL achieved an average chain length of **2.7** for 5-instruction chains, significantly outperforming LMP (**1.8**) and CQL+HER (**1.0**).
- For two sequential tasks conditioned on a single goal image, TACO-RL attained a **27%** success rate in simulation, an order-of-magnitude improvement over LMP (**2.7%**) and CQL+HER (**2.4%**).
- On a real Franka Emika Panda robot, TACO-RL demonstrated an average success rate of **61%** across **25** diverse manipulation tasks, notably surpassing LMP (**40%**) and CQL+HER (**11%**), and successfully performed complex sequential tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining imitation learning for short-horizon skill acquisition and offline reinforcement learning for long-horizon goal chaining effectively addresses the limitations of both methods individually.
> - Learning a continuous latent space of behaviors (latent plans) from unstructured 'play data' provides a flexible and task-agnostic action space for a high-level policy.
> - The strategic use of hindsight relabeling with both positive and carefully sampled 'negative' goals is crucial for robustly learning Q-values and distances to goals from sparse, offline data, especially when generalizing to unseen goal images.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of robot control has seen significant advancements through reinforcement learning (RL), particularly offline RL, which allows policies to be learned from pre-collected, fixed datasets without further environmental interaction. This property is appealing for robotics, where data collection can be costly and unsafe. However, current offline RL methods often face limitations when applied to complex, real-world tasks. These tasks frequently involve long horizons, requiring an agent to execute a sequence of multiple implicit subtasks, and typically operate on raw, high-dimensional sensory inputs such as camera observations. Previous research has identified a quadratic relationship between task horizon and the worst-case accumulated error in offline RL, highlighting the difficulty in learning optimal policies for extended sequences of decisions.

Existing approaches to alleviate the long-horizon problem often involve hierarchical policy learning. However, many hierarchical structures are rigid, relying on a fixed set of low-level policies, or require complex model-based planning in latent state spaces, which is itself an active area of research. Other methods rely on expert data or predefined skills, limiting their applicability to more general-purpose robots. Furthermore, imitation learning (IL) has shown promise in learning short-horizon behaviors from unlabeled play data, sometimes outperforming computationally intensive offline RL for distinct tasks. However, IL typically lacks long-term optimality guarantees necessary for chaining skills over extended periods.

This work addresses the intersection of these challenges by proposing a novel hierarchical approach. It combines the data-efficiency and short-horizon skill acquisition capabilities of imitation learning with the long-term optimality reasoning of offline reinforcement learning. Specifically, it focuses on learning task-agnostic control policies from high-dimensional observations using purely offline and unstructured "play" data—datasets of non-goal-directed human demonstrations. This positions the work as an attempt to bridge the gap between learning diverse short-term behaviors from readily available unstructured data and achieving long-horizon, goal-conditioned control without explicit task definitions or environment models.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to perform complex, long-horizon tasks from high-dimensional visual observations in a task-agnostic manner, using only previously collected offline and unstructured interaction data. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

- **Addressing Long-Horizon Tasks in Offline RL**: Current offline RL methods typically struggle with tasks requiring a significant number of consecutive decision steps, largely due to the cumulative error effect over long horizons. A core motivation is to find a practical solution to decompose such complex tasks, making them amenable to offline learning.
- **Leveraging Unstructured Play Data**: Acquiring large, diverse datasets for robot learning is challenging. "Play data" (unsegmented, non-goal-directed teleoperated trajectories) is relatively easy to collect and offers good state coverage and visual variety. The motivation is to fully utilize this readily available, unstructured data to learn a broad repertoire of behaviors without requiring explicit task labels or optimal demonstrations.
- **Combining Strengths of Imitation Learning (IL) and Offline RL**: IL excels at learning short-horizon, multi-modal behaviors directly from demonstrations, including play data, but lacks long-term optimality. Offline RL, conversely, can optimize for long-term rewards but is sensitive to data distribution shifts and struggles with long horizons. The motivation is to create a synergy, where IL learns robust short-term skills, and offline RL stitches these skills together for long-term objectives.
- **Achieving Task-Agnostic Control**: Traditional hierarchical methods often rely on a predefined, discrete set of low-level skills. The ambition here is to develop an agent capable of reaching "any possible target state from a given initial state," implying a continuous and flexible repertoire of behaviors that can be composed dynamically. This contrasts with methods that learn policies for a fixed set of specific tasks.
- **Model-Free Approach**: Many hierarchical or long-horizon planning methods necessitate learning an accurate dynamics model of the environment, which is itself a complex research problem. A motivation is to achieve long-horizon, task-agnostic control without requiring an explicit model of the environment dynamics, relying instead on model-free RL techniques.
- **Learning from High-Dimensional Observations**: The method aims to learn policies directly from raw, high-dimensional camera observations, which is crucial for real-world robot applications where true state information is often unavailable or difficult to extract.

To address these motivations, the proposed solution, TACO-RL, introduces a hierarchical framework. A low-level policy learns continuous latent skills from play data via imitation learning, effectively creating an action space of behaviors. A high-level policy then learns to chain these latent skills using offline reinforcement learning, leveraging hindsight relabeling and a specialized goal sampling strategy to optimize for long-term, goal-conditioned objectives. This decomposition reduces the effective horizon for the high-level policy and simplifies the learning problem.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TACO-RL (Task-AgnostiC Offline Reinforcement Learning) adopts a bottom-up hierarchical approach, consisting of two main stages: learning a low-level policy and then learning a high-level policy that utilizes the low-level policy's learned skills. The entire process relies on an offline, unlabeled, and undirected fixed dataset of robot interactions, denoted as D. The system is designed to learn a single 7-DoF visuomotor policy from high-dimensional camera observations.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The interaction between the agent and environment is modeled as a goal-augmented Markov Decision Process (MDP) M = (S, A, p, r, G, p₀, γ), where S is the state-observation space (visual observations), A is the action space, p is the state-transition function, r is the reward function, G ⊆ S is the goal space, p₀ is the initial state distribution, and γ is the discount factor. The agent operates without access to the true environment state, relying solely on visual observations.

#### 3.2. Learning the Low-Level Policy

The initial phase focuses on extracting a continuous space of meaningful primitive behaviors. A low-level policy, denoted π$_{ω}$(a|s$_{c}$, z), is trained to decode a latent plan `z` into motor-control actions `a`, given the current state `s$_{c}$`. This is achieved using a sequence-to-sequence Conditional Variational Auto-Encoder (seq2seq CVAE), which addresses the multi-modality inherent in unstructured play data (e.g., multiple ways to achieve the same outcome).

The objective for learning π$_{ω}$(a|s$_{c}$, z) minimizes the following:
$$ \min_{ω,φ} E_{τ \sim D,z \sim q_{φ}(z|τ)} \left[ - \sum_{t=0}^{|τ|} \log(π_{ω}(a_{t}|s_{t}, z)) \right] $$
Here, q$_{φ}$(z|τ) is the latent plan encoder, which maps a trajectory `τ` to a distribution over latent plans `z`.
A crucial component is a regularization term that enforces consistency between the latent variables predicted by the encoder q$_{φ}$(z|τ) and a prior policy π$_{δ}$(z|s$_{c}$, s$_{g}$). This prior is conditioned on the initial state `s$_{c}$` and the final state `s$_{g}$` of a sub-trajectory `τ`, preventing the encoder from overfitting to the full trajectory. The overall objective is based on the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) for CVAEs, incorporating a KL-divergence regularization term weighted by β (similar to a β-VAE). KL balancing terms are also used to prevent the prior from being poorly trained. The latent plan `z` effectively serves as a continuous action space for the subsequent high-level policy.

#### 3.3. Offline Reinforcement Learning with Hindsight Relabeling

Once the low-level policy components (encoder q$_{φ}$(z|τ), behavior policy π$_{ω}$(a|s$_{c}$, z), and prior π$_{δ}$(z|s$_{c}$, s$_{g}$)) are trained and fixed, TACO-RL proceeds to learn a general-purpose high-level policy π$_{θ}$(z|s$_{c}$, s$_{g}$) using offline RL. This policy is responsible for chaining the latent skills (plans `z`) to achieve long-horizon goals.

- **Transition Generation**: A trajectory `τ` is sampled from the dataset D. The fixed pre-trained encoder q$_{φ}$(z|τ) is used to sample a latent plan z$_{t}$ for a segment of the trajectory. This generates a high-level interaction transition (s$_{t}$, z$_{t}$, s$_{t+k-1}$), where s$_{t}$ is the initial state, z$_{t}$ is the latent plan, and s$_{t+k-1}$ is the state reached after executing the plan (assuming the low-level policy successfully decodes z$_{t}$ to reach s$_{t+k-1}$). The window size `k` for these transitions can be arbitrary.
- **Reward Function and Hindsight Relabeling**: A sparse reward function is defined as r(s$_{t}$, z$_{t}$, s$_{t+k-1}$, s$_{g}$) = 1 if s$_{t+k-1}$ = s$_{g}$ and 0 otherwise. To mitigate reward sparsity, hindsight relabeling is employed. Goals `s$_{g}$` are sampled from future time steps along the same trajectory as s$_{t}$. Specifically, a discrete-time offset ∆ is sampled from a geometric distribution (∆ ∼ Geom(p)), and the state at time t + ∆ ∗ (k − 1) is used as the goal. If ∆ = 1, the reward for the transition is 1, as the low-level policy is assumed to reach s$_{t+k-1}$.
- **Negative Goal Sampling**: To prevent the Q-function from underestimating distances to unreachable goals, "negative" goal states are sampled. These negative goals are selected from the dataset such that they have similar proprioceptive information to the final state s$_{t+k-1}$ but represent a different scene arrangement. This provides hard negative examples, encouraging the model to focus on object manipulation rather than just end-effector position.
- **Offline RL Algorithm**: The high-level policy is learned using Conservative Q-Learning (CQL), an off-the-shelf offline actor-critic method. The objective minimizes the Bellman error:
 $$ \min_{λ} E_{τ \sim D,z \sim q_{φ}(z|τ),s_{g} \sim S} \left[ Q_{λ}(s_{t}, z_{t}, g) - \hat{Q}(s_{t}, z_{t}, g) \right]^2 $$
 where $\hat{Q}(s_{t}, z_{t}, g) = \left( 1_{s_{t+k-1}=s_{g}} + γ1_{s_{t+k-1} \neq s_{g}} \max_{z_{t+k-1}} Q_{λ}(s_{t+k-1}, z_{t+k-1}, s_{g}) \right)$.
 The actor weights (for π$_{θ}$(z|s$_{c}$, s$_{g}$)) are initialized with the previously learned prior policy π$_{δ}$(z|s$_{c}$, s$_{g}$), which already provides a good starting point for short-horizon tasks.

#### 3.4. Network Architecture and Training Details

- **Visual Encoder**: For visual observations (RGB images from static and gripper cameras), convolutional encoders are used. In simulation, a single static RGB image is processed. In real-world experiments, both static and gripper camera images are used, with the static camera image being processed by a pre-trained R3M Resnet18 network with fixed weights, and the gripper image by a convolutional encoder similar to Play LMP.
- **Low-level Policy (Decoder)**: A recurrent neural network (RNN) (2 layers, 2048 hidden dimension) takes the current state embedding and sampled latent plan `z` to output parameters for a discretized logistic mixture distribution for continuous actions (6 DoF end-effector changes) and a discrete value for gripper action.
- **Prior/Actor**: A 3-layer feed-forward neural network (256 units) takes initial and goal state embeddings to predict parameters of a Gaussian distribution for latent plans `z`.
- **Critic**: A feed-forward network (3 layers, 256 units) takes concatenated embeddings of current state, goal state, and latent plan `z` to predict Q-values.
- **Hyperparameters**: Batch sizes of 64, latent plan dimension of 16. Specific learning rates, target network update rates, and CQL α parameter (1.0 for non-Lagrange version) are detailed. Goal sampling uses a geometric distribution probability `p = 0.3`. In 3D tabletop environments, 90% positive and 10% negative examples are used for goal sampling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TACO-RL was conducted across both simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against state-of-the-art baselines: Conservative Q-learning with Hindsight Experience Replay (CQL+HER), Play-supervised Latent Motor Plan (LMP), and Relay Imitation Learning (RIL).

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (CALVIN Environment)

Experiments in the CALVIN environment involved learning 7-DoF visuomotor robot skills from 6 hours of unstructured play data. The environment presents challenges due to scene changes and reliance on RGB camera images.

- **Long-Horizon Multitask with Visual Observations (LH-MTVis) - Chains of Goals (Table 1)**: This setup tested the ability to solve 500 unique chains of 5 sequential image-based goals. TACO-RL significantly outperformed all baselines in chaining multiple tasks.
 - TACO-RL achieved a 95.4% success rate for 1 instruction, progressively decreasing to 6.9% for 5 instructions, with an average chain length of 2.7.
 - LMP, the next best, achieved 91.4% for 1 instruction but dropped to 0.2% for 5 instructions, with an average chain length of 1.8.
 - RIL and CQL+HER showed notably lower performance, particularly for longer chains.
 - **Ablation Studies**:
 - Removing negative goals ("No neg. goals") reduced performance (average length 2.02), indicating that negative goals are crucial for robust distance estimation.
 - Increasing negative goal ratio to 50% ("50% neg. goals") significantly impaired performance (average length 1.05), suggesting an optimal balance of positive and negative examples is necessary.

- **LH-MTVis - Single Goal Image for Sequential Tasks (Table 2)**: This evaluation assessed the ability to complete two sequential tasks (1000 rollouts) when conditioned only on the final goal image, allowing 300 timesteps.
 - TACO-RL achieved a 67.9% success rate for the first task and 27% for both sequential tasks, with an average length of 0.94. This represents an order-of-magnitude improvement over LMP (2.7%) and CQL+HER (2.4%) for two sequential tasks.
 - RIL, while hierarchical, obtained 13.3% for two sequential tasks, still less than half of TACO-RL's accuracy.
 - Ablations again showed performance degradation without negative goals or with an increased negative goal ratio.

- **Generalization to Unseen Goal Images (Table 3)**: Models were tested on 50 rollouts per task where the goal image did not contain the robot end-effector in its final position, requiring reasoning about scene changes.
 - TACO-RL outperformed baselines across various tasks (e.g., 94% for "Place block in drawer," 87.3% for "Open drawer").
 - The inclusion of negative goals in TACO-RL contributed significantly to its performance in this scenario, allowing it to focus on the entire scene configuration rather than just end-effector imitation.
 - LMP showed a strong bias towards end-effector position, ignoring environmental changes, resulting in lower success rates for tasks like "Open drawer" (12%). CQL+HER also performed poorly.

#### 4.2. Real-Robot Experiments

A Franka Emika Panda robot arm was trained using nine hours of teleoperated play data, including both static and gripper camera RGB images. The goal was to learn a single policy capable of performing multiple goal-conditioned tasks in a 3D tabletop environment (Figure 4).

- **Multi-Task Goal-Conditioned Control (Table 4)**: 20 rollouts were performed for each of 25 diverse manipulation tasks using multiple goal images and start positions.
 - TACO-RL consistently outperformed baselines, especially for tasks requiring extended reasoning or starting far from the goal.
 - It achieved an average success rate of 61% across all tasks.
 - LMP averaged 40%, and CQL+HER averaged 11%.
 - TACO-RL showed strong performance in tasks like "Close drawer" (90%), "Open drawer" (70%), and "Move slider left" (75%).
- **Sequential Tasks and Complex Goals (Qualitative)**: The paper notes that TACO-RL was able to successfully perform sequential tasks in the real world (e.g., "moving the sliding door to the right and then opening the drawer") by inferring transitions between tasks. It also successfully performed complex tasks with a single goal image, such as "lifting the block and moving it to a desired position" and "stacking a block on top of another," indicating its ability to reason in a long-horizon manner beyond just imitating end-effector positions.

#### 4.3. Maze2D Environment (Table 5)

TACO-RL was also evaluated on D4RL Maze2D environments, where it demonstrated superior performance in stitching subtrajectories to reach goals with fewer steps compared to LMP and CQL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces TACO-RL, a hierarchical self-supervised approach that effectively combines imitation learning and offline reinforcement learning to address significant challenges in robot control. The primary contributions and their potential impact are:

- **Effective Long-Horizon Task Learning**: TACO-RL demonstrates an order-of-magnitude improvement in performance for complex, long-horizon tasks compared to leading imitation learning and offline RL baselines. This suggests a scalable method for tackling multi-tier tasks that are typically intractable for existing approaches.
- **Leveraging Unstructured Play Data**: The ability to learn powerful visuomotor policies from purely offline, unstructured, and unlabeled "play data" is a significant step towards practical robot learning. This reduces the burden of meticulously curated datasets, making data collection simpler and more accessible for real-world applications.
- **Task-Agnostic and General-Purpose Control**: By learning a continuous space of latent skills and a high-level policy to chain them, the approach moves towards truly task-agnostic control. The robot is endowed with the capability to reach any possible target state from a given initial state, rather than being confined to a pre-defined set of discrete tasks. This enhances the flexibility and adaptability of robotic systems.
- **Model-Free and Offline Learning**: The method achieves its capabilities without requiring an explicit dynamics model of the environment or online interaction, which is highly advantageous in robotics where model learning can be challenging and real-world exploration costly or unsafe.
- **Robustness to High-Dimensional Observations**: The successful application to high-dimensional camera observations in both simulated and real-world settings indicates the method's potential for deployment in realistic environments where raw sensory inputs are the norm.

The confluence of imitation learning and offline RL, as demonstrated by TACO-RL, offers a promising direction for scaling robot learning, potentially enabling robots to acquire a broad repertoire of skills and compose them flexibly for complex, everyday tasks.

**Limitations**:
Despite its capabilities, TACO-RL presents several limitations:
- **Goal Image Specification**: At test time, a suitable goal image consistent with the current scene must be provided, which can be a practical challenge. Future work may explore natural language interfaces for task specification.
- **Task Progress Tracking**: When sequencing skills with varying time horizons in the real world, the method does not explicitly track task progress, relying on a fixed time horizon for each sub-task. This assumes uniform task completion times.
- **Sequential Training**: The low-level policy must be trained before the high-level policy, increasing the overall training time compared to joint training. However, this is mitigated by the offline nature of the training process, which does not require real-robot rollouts.
- **Data Efficiency**: While leveraging unstructured data, imitation learning derivatives like TACO-RL might exhibit lower data efficiency compared to pure offline RL methods in certain scenarios, as suggested by prior research.
