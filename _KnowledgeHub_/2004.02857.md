# Beyond the Nav-Graph: Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments (VLN-CE), a more realistic benchmark where agents execute low-level actions in 3D environments, moving beyond prior "nav-graph" assumptions. This setting significantly challenges existing models, with the best cross-modal attention agent achieving a 0.30 SPL (32% SR) on val-unseen, revealing a substantial performance gap compared to nav-graph-based methods.

## Problem

- Most Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) tasks operate on sparse "nav-graphs," assuming a known environment topology and perfect, oracle navigation between pre-defined nodes.
- Agents in prior VLN settings often receive their precise location and heading, bypassing the need for real-world localization and collision avoidance.
- These unrealistic assumptions simplify the problem to "visually-guided graph search," inflating performance metrics and hindering the transferability of agents to physical robots.

## Method

- Introduced ==Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments (VLN-CE)== using the ==Habitat simulator== and ==Matterport3D==, allowing free navigation with ==low-level actions== like `move forward 0.25m` and `turn-left/right 15 degrees`.
- Transferred existing **Room-to-Room (R2R)** instruction-path pairs to the continuous Habitat environment, converting ==nav-graph nodes== to ==navigable points== and verifying trajectory traversability.
- Developed and evaluated instruction-guided navigation models, including a ==Seq2Seq== baseline and a ==Cross-Modal Attention== model, incorporating auxiliary losses like ==Dataset Aggregation== and a ==Progress Monitor==.

## Results

- The best-performing `Cross-Modal Attention` model, trained with combined augmentations (PM+DA*+Aug), achieved a **0.30 SPL** (**32% Success Rate**) on val-unseen environments in VLN-CE.
- Input modality ablations demonstrated that both depth (for collision avoidance) and RGB imagery (for instruction grounding) are crucial, with models lacking either performing poorly.
- A significant performance gap exists between VLN-CE agents and state-of-the-art nav-graph VLN models (e.g., **0.21 SPL** for the best VLN-CE agent converted to nav-graph vs. **0.47 SPL** for SOTA VLN), quantitatively showing the impact of nav-graph assumptions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous VLN significantly increases task difficulty, with basic baselines achieving much lower success rates than in nav-graph settings, due to requirements for low-level control, collision avoidance, and continuous perception.
> - Depth perception is critical for effective navigation in continuous environments, as models without it perform poorly, highlighting its importance for collision avoidance.
> - The nav-graph paradigm provides a substantial "prior" to existing VLN models, inflating reported performance and masking the true challenges of embodied navigation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI aims to develop agents that can interact with the physical world, often through natural language instructions. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) is a critical sub-area, focusing on training agents to follow natural language directions (e.g., "Go down the hall and turn left at the wooden desk") in perceptually rich 3D environments.

Prior to this work, most VLN tasks, such as the widely influential Room-to-Room (R2R) dataset for VLN and Touchdown, operated on a "navigation graph" (or "nav-graph") paradigm. In this paradigm, environments are represented as a sparse graph of panoramic images (nodes) with pre-defined connections (edges) indicating navigability. While this approach enabled efficient data collection and leveraged real-world visual data (e.g., Matterport3D, Google StreetView), it introduced several critical, unrealistic assumptions:
1. **Known Topology:** Agents operate on a fixed, pre-defined graph, effectively having prior knowledge of the environment's traversable points.
2. **Oracle Navigation:** Movement between distant nodes is deterministic and perfect, abstracting away low-level visual navigation, collision avoidance, and odometry challenges.
3. **Perfect Localization:** Agents are continuously provided with their precise location and heading, bypassing the complex problem of simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM).

These assumptions reduce the problem to "visually-guided graph search," leading to potentially inflated performance metrics that do not reflect the challenges faced by real robotic systems. Consequently, transferring agents trained in these settings to physical robots is difficult, as the fundamental control and perception challenges are not addressed.

This paper, "Beyond the Nav-Graph," directly confronts these limitations. By introducing **Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments (VLN-CE)**, the authors shift the paradigm from sparse graph traversal to continuous, low-level action execution in realistic 3D spaces. This move aligns VLN more closely with the requirements of real-world robotics, where agents must perform collision-free navigation, maintain localization, and interpret continuous visual streams, all while following high-level language commands. It represents a significant step towards creating more robust and generalizable embodied agents capable of functioning in the wild, thus pushing the boundaries of the embodied AI research landscape towards practical robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop and thoroughly evaluate a new, more realistic Vision-and-Language Navigation task called **Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments (VLN-CE)**. This new task aims to eliminate several unrealistic assumptions inherent in prior nav-graph-based VLN settings.

The key motivations are:
- **Increase Realism in Embodied AI:** The authors are driven by the long-standing promise of general-purpose robotic assistants that can follow natural language instructions. They argue that existing VLN tasks, while valuable, simplify the problem considerably by reducing it to a "visually-guided graph search." By instantiating VLN in continuous 3D environments, they seek to create a testbed that better mirrors the complexities a real robot would encounter.
- **Lift Implicit Assumptions:** Specifically, the paper aims to:
 - Remove the assumption of a **known environment topology**, forcing agents to navigate freely rather than on a pre-defined graph.
 - Eliminate **oracle navigation** between distant points, requiring agents to execute low-level actions, handle obstacles, and navigate without perfect guarantees.
 - Drop the assumption of **perfect localization**, requiring agents to implicitly or explicitly manage their position and heading based on egocentric perception.
- **Uncover True Performance:** The authors hypothesize that performance in prior nav-graph settings might be "inflated" due to these strong implicit assumptions. By introducing VLN-CE, they aim to provide a more accurate measure of progress towards truly instruction-following agents and identify new challenges.
- **Enable Integrative Studies:** The continuous setting allows for future research that integrates high-level language understanding with lower-level control, mapping, and localization systems, which was previously not possible.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide a more challenging, ecologically valid benchmark for VLN that can drive research towards more robust and deployable embodied AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology primarily involves redefining the VLN task in a continuous environment and then evaluating how existing VLN techniques perform in this more challenging setting.

#### 3.1. Task Definition: Vision-and-Language Navigation in Continuous Environments (VLN-CE)

- **Environment:** The task is set in Matterport3D (MP3D) environments, leveraging their mesh-based 3D reconstructions. Crucially, the **Habitat simulator** is used, which allows agents to navigate freely and interact with these continuous 3D spaces, unlike the custom Matterport3D simulator used in original VLN which constrained agents to nav-graph nodes.
- **Observations:** Agents perceive the world through egocentric **RGB-D images** (256x256 resolution, 90-degree horizontal field-of-view), similar to a forward-mounted camera on a robot. This contrasts with the panoramic observations often used in nav-graph VLN.
- **Actions:** Agents execute a small set of **low-level, discrete actions**: `move forward 0.25m`, `turn-left 15 degrees`, `turn-right 15 degrees`, or `stop`. These are designed to be easily implementable on physical robots, unlike the "teleportation" between nodes in nav-graph VLN.
- **Goal:** Navigate from a start position to a described goal, based solely on egocentric perception and natural language instructions, without external localization.

#### 3.2. Dataset Creation: Transferring Room-to-Room (R2R) Trajectories to Habitat

Instead of collecting a new dataset, the authors ingeniously **transfer existing R2R trajectories** (originally defined on nav-graphs) to the continuous Matterport3D environments in Habitat. This allows for direct comparison with previous work.
- **Waypoint Conversion:** Each panorama-based node (v = {I, x, y, z}) from R2R is mapped to a nearest navigable point on the reconstructed mesh in Habitat. This process addresses issues like nodes being in mid-air or on furniture by casting rays downwards and finding the closest navigable floor point. 98.3% of nodes are successfully transferred.
- **Trajectory Navigability Check:** For each converted R2R trajectory (sequence of waypoints), an A*-based heuristic search is run between consecutive waypoints to ensure navigability within Habitat. If the agent cannot reach the next waypoint within 0.5m, the trajectory is deemed non-navigable. This resulted in 77% of R2R trajectories being navigable, revealing discontinuities or differences between the original panoramas and the 3D reconstructions (e.g., holes in meshes, moved objects).
- **VLN-CE Dataset:** The resulting dataset comprises 4475 trajectories (from R2R train/val splits) with their original natural language instructions and pre-computed shortest paths via low-level actions. The average path length in VLN-CE is significantly longer (∼56 actions) compared to R2R (4-6 node hops), making it a truly long-horizon task.

#### 3.3. Instruction-guided Navigation Models

Two types of agent architectures are developed, mirroring advancements in prior VLN settings:
- **Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) Baseline:** A simple recurrent policy (GRU) that takes mean-pooled RGB-D features and the final hidden state of an LSTM-encoded instruction to predict an action. This serves as a foundational baseline for input-modality ablations.
- **Cross-Modal Attention Model:** A more expressive model incorporating:
 - A recurrent network tracking visual observations (`h_t^(attn)`).
 - A bi-directional LSTM for encoding instructions (`S`).
 - Scaled dot-product attention mechanisms to ground the current instruction context (`s_t^`) with relevant spatial features from RGB (`v_t^`) and depth (`d_t^`) observations.
 - A second recurrent network (`h_t^(a)`) combining these attended features with previous action embeddings to predict the next action. This architecture is conceptually similar to strong VLN models like RCM.

#### 3.4. Auxiliary Losses and Training Regimes

The authors adapt several training techniques popular in VLN to VLN-CE:
- **Imitation Learning with Inflection Weighting:** Maximizing likelihood of ground truth trajectories, with higher weights on time-steps where actions change to emphasize critical decision points.
- **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger):** To cope with exposure bias (the train-test mismatch where agents are only exposed to ground-truth paths during training), DAgger collects trajectories from the agent's current policy and iteratively retrains on an aggregated dataset of ground-truth and agent-generated paths.
- **Synthetic Data Augmentation:** Incorporating ∼150k synthetic trajectory-instruction pairs generated by a speaker model (from previous work) to augment the training data.
- **Progress Monitor:** An auxiliary loss that trains the agent to predict its progress (fraction completed) through the trajectory, providing an explicit signal for goal achievement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several important findings regarding the challenges and effectiveness of VLN agents in continuous environments:

#### 4.1. Establishing Baseline Performance for VLN-CE

- **No-Learning Baselines:** Random and simple hand-crafted agents achieve very low success rates (SR) of ~3-4% in val-unseen environments. This contrasts sharply with the 16.3% SR of similar hand-crafted models in nav-graph VLN, highlighting the significantly increased difficulty of VLN-CE due to collision avoidance, continuous perception, and longer horizons.
- **Input Modality Ablations (Seq2Seq Model, Table 2):**
 - The full `RGBD` Seq2Seq baseline achieves 20% SR and 0.18 SPL in val-unseen, significantly outperforming no-learning baselines.
 - **Depth is Critical:** Models without depth (`No Depth`, `No Vision`) perform very poorly (≤1% SR). This suggests depth is essential for basic collision avoidance and traversing environments effectively, a finding that echoes point-goal navigation research but is new to VLN.
 - **Image (RGB) is Important for Instruction Following:** The `No Image` model achieves 17% SR, identical to the `No Instruction` model. This indicates that without RGB, the model primarily navigates based on environmental regularities rather than grounding visual referents in the instructions.
 - **No Instruction Baseline:** A model without instructions still achieves 17% SR, suggesting inherent navigability patterns in the environment that can be exploited even without language. However, this is significantly lower than the full model, confirming the importance of language.

#### 4.2. Model Performance in VLN-CE (Table 3)

- **Cross-Modal Attention vs. Seq2Seq:** The `Cross-Modal Attention` model consistently outperforms the `Seq2Seq` baseline across all training settings. For instance, with basic imitation learning, it improves from 0.18 to 0.22 SPL on val-unseen (22% relative improvement), demonstrating the value of explicit cross-modal grounding.
- **Impact of Training Augmentations:**
 - **DAgger** is found to be consistently impactful, improving SPL by 0.03-0.05 for both models, indicating its effectiveness in mitigating exposure bias in long-horizon continuous tasks.
 - **Progress Monitor (PM) and Data Augmentation (Aug)** alone often show negative or mixed results compared to standard imitation learning. The authors hypothesize that PM over-fits on the non-augmented data.
 - **Combined Augmentations:** The best performance is achieved by combining all three techniques, especially for the `Cross-Modal Attention` model. Specifically, pretraining with imitation learning, data augmentation, and progress monitoring loss, followed by finetuning with DAgger (`Cross-Modal Attention PM+DA*+Aug`), yields an SPL of **0.30** on val-unseen (32% SR), making it the top-performing model. This suggests that while individual augmentations might have drawbacks, their synergistic combination can be powerful.

#### 4.3. Examining the Impact of the Nav-Graph in VLN (Table 4)

- **Significant Performance Gap:** The authors convert their best VLN-CE agent's paths back to nav-graph trajectories and evaluate them on the standard VLN leaderboard. Their `Cross-Modal Attention (PM+DA*+Aug)` model achieves an SPL of **0.21** on the VLN test set. This is significantly lower than state-of-the-art VLN models like RCM (0.38 SPL) or Back-Translation (0.47 SPL), which benefit from the oracle nav-graph during training and inference.
- **Quantifying Nav-Graph Value:** Even after accounting for a ~0.10 SPL drop due to imperfect conversion between continuous and nav-graph paths and unnavigable R2R trajectories, the performance gap remains substantial (roughly 2x difference to SOTA VLN). This strongly supports the hypothesis that the nav-graph provides a significant "prior" and inflates performance in previous VLN benchmarks.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Examples (Figure 5)

- Qualitative examples illustrate the increased difficulty of VLN-CE (e.g., 62 actions in CE vs. 3 hops in VLN for one successful path).
- They also show failure cases, such as agents navigating to wrong visual referents or failing to actively observe the scene for objects mentioned in instructions, highlighting remaining challenges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a profoundly significant contribution to the field of embodied AI by fundamentally reshaping the Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) task.

1. **A More Realistic and Challenging Benchmark (VLN-CE):** The most direct impact is the introduction of VLN-CE, which eliminates the unrealistic "nav-graph" assumptions of known topology, oracle navigation, and perfect localization. This move forces agents to tackle low-level control, collision avoidance, and continuous perception, making it a far more representative testbed for instruction-following robots. This new benchmark will drive research towards more robust and generalizable navigation policies.

2. **Unveiling the "Hidden Cost" of Nav-Graphs:** By demonstrating a significant performance gap between models trained in continuous environments and those leveraging nav-graphs, the paper effectively quantifies the "inflation" of performance metrics in previous VLN work. This insight is crucial; it recontextualizes past progress, highlighting that while impressive, those achievements relied on strong priors not available to real robots. This reframing encourages the community to focus on more foundational, real-world problems.

3. **Prioritizing Foundational Challenges:** The results underscore the critical importance of low-level visual perception (especially depth for collision avoidance) and robust control for embodied navigation. The struggles of single-modality baselines in VLN-CE illustrate that simply adding language understanding to a graph-traversal agent is insufficient; robust, continuous environmental interaction is a prerequisite.

4. **Bridging Sim2Real and Modular Robotics:** By setting the task in the Habitat simulator with low-level actions, the paper creates a direct path for sim2real transfer, a crucial step for deploying AI agents on physical robots. Furthermore, VLN-CE provides a platform for future research into modular architectures, where high-level language understanding can interface with dedicated lower-level control and mapping systems, as suggested by the authors in the discussion.

5. **Shifting Research Focus:** This work is poised to shift the focus of VLN research from purely language grounding and high-level path planning within a known topology, towards developing agents that can truly *learn to navigate* in dynamic, unknown, and continuous environments while integrating language instructions. This paves the way for a new generation of embodied AI research that is more directly applicable to real-world robotics and general-purpose robotic assistants.

In summary, "Beyond the Nav-Graph" is a landmark paper that critically reassesses the foundations of VLN research. By making the task more challenging and realistic, it sets a new standard for progress and opens up exciting avenues for developing embodied AI agents that can truly understand and act upon natural language instructions in the physical world.
