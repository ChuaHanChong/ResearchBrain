# DexDirect: Direct Kinesthetic Arm Guidance for Efficient Dexterous Demonstration Collection

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UCLA developed DexDirect, a hybrid interface that combines direct kinesthetic guidance for the robot arm with vision-based finger retargeting to efficiently collect dexterous manipulation demonstrations. This system achieved 3.2 times higher demonstration throughput and significantly reduced operator workload compared to existing low-setup teleoperation methods, while the collected data successfully trained a robot policy with a 90% success rate on a pick-and-place task.

## Problem

- Collecting large, high-quality, contact-rich dexterous manipulation demonstrations is a major bottleneck for training robust robot policies.
- Existing robot teleoperation interfaces often require a trade-off between control fidelity (e.g., precision, haptic feedback) and the system's cost, setup complexity, or operator workload.
- Controlling high-dimensional multi-fingered hands is particularly challenging for operators, as it requires coordinating global arm movement with intricate local finger articulation without direct physical feedback.

## Method

- **DexDirect** employs a ==hybrid control architecture== that decouples arm control from dexterous hand articulation.
- For arm control, the robot manipulator operates in a ==gravity-compensated drag mode==, allowing direct kinesthetic guidance by the operator via a handle, with trajectories recorded directly from joint encoders.
- For finger articulation, a commodity webcam tracks the operator's right hand, mapping wrist-relative hand landmarks to robot finger joint targets using an ==optimization-based retargeting framework==.

## Results

- DexDirect collected **3.2 times** more successful demonstrations than TeleDex and **17.2 times** more than AnyTeleop across five diverse, contact-rich tasks within equivalent time budgets.
- The system substantially reduced operator workload, achieving an overall Raw TLX score of **2.01**, significantly lower than TeleDex (**3.08**) and AnyTeleop (**3.81**), primarily by reducing mental demand and frustration.
- A diffusion policy trained on 200 demonstrations collected using DexDirect achieved a **90%** success rate on a cube pick-and-place task, validating the data's suitability for end-to-end robot policy learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating arm control (via direct kinesthesia) from finger control (via vision-based retargeting) allows each component to leverage its most effective control modality, optimizing for both precision and low setup.
> - Direct kinesthetic guidance for the robot arm eliminates errors and latency associated with vision-based arm tracking and inverse kinematics, ensuring physically attainable and accurate trajectories while providing passive contact cues.
> - Confining the vision-based finger retargeting to wrist-relative geometry avoids the challenges of monocular metric global wrist-pose estimation and reduces cognitive load by having the arm independently determine the hand's global pose.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of complex, contact-rich behaviors for robots often relies on learning from human demonstrations. The efficacy of these learned policies is significantly influenced by the quantity and quality of the collected demonstration data. A persistent challenge in robot learning, particularly for dexterous manipulation, is the scalable collection of such demonstrations through interfaces that are simultaneously accurate, robot-executable, fast, inexpensive, and easy to deploy. This paper addresses a long-standing trade-off between the fidelity of control offered by an interface and its associated hardware cost, setup complexity, and operator workload.

Existing approaches to demonstration collection can be broadly categorized:

- **Hardware-based interfaces:** These include exoskeletons and isomorphic leader arms. Exoskeletons instrument the operator's hand to retarget measured joint angles, sometimes incorporating contact force feedback. While offering precise control and rich interaction, these systems typically require specialized, costly hardware, extensive setup, and often operator-specific fitting or calibration, limiting their scalability and portability. Isomorphic leader arms, which are scaled or duplicate robot arms, provide intuitive joint-space control but double hardware requirements and increase setup costs.
- **Vision and pose-based teleoperation systems:** These approaches aim to reduce hardware costs by using commodity cameras (webcams, depth cameras) or wearable pose trackers (smartphones, headsets). Examples include AnyTeleop and TeleDex. Such systems eliminate the need for custom mechanical interfaces and simplify deployment. However, they frequently rely on tracking-based control loops that can suffer from tracking latency, pose estimation errors, workspace mismatch, visual occlusion, and increased cognitive burden on the operator due to the need to mentally map human motion to robot actions without direct physical feedback. The absence of haptic feedback in free-space operation can also hinder precise force regulation and contact-rich manipulation.
- **Direct physical demonstration (kinesthetic teaching):** This method involves physically guiding the robot itself, allowing trajectories to be recorded directly from joint encoders. It is often intuitive for non-expert users and ensures that recorded configurations are physically attainable by the robot. However, applying this to multi-fingered hands is mechanically cumbersome, and the operator's hands can occlude critical visual information needed for policy learning, often requiring post-processing or offline re-execution.
- **Handheld capture interfaces:** These decouple data capture from the robot using wearable or handheld devices, sometimes incorporating synthetic haptic feedback. While enabling single-pass collection, the synthetic force feedback is limited by control bandwidth and may not accurately reflect the deployment robot's true physical properties.

The work presented in this paper, DexDirect, positions itself as a hybrid solution that attempts to mitigate the limitations of these existing methods. It seeks to combine the directness and precision of kinesthetic teaching for arm control with the low-setup advantage of vision-based methods for dexterous finger control, thereby reducing overall system cost and operator workload while improving demonstration quality and throughput. This approach aims to provide a more efficient and scalable interface for generating the large, high-quality datasets necessary for advancing robot manipulation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the DexDirect research is to develop an efficient, low-setup, and scalable interface for collecting high-quality dexterous manipulation demonstrations for robot learning. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims to address critical limitations within the field:

1. **Overcome the Demonstration Bottleneck for Dexterous Manipulation:** Robot learning success hinges on large, high-quality datasets of demonstrations. For complex dexterous tasks, collecting these datasets remains a significant bottleneck. The paper aims to provide a system that can generate these demonstrations more effectively than current approaches.
2. **Mitigate the Trade-off Between Interface Fidelity and Cost/Setup:** Existing interfaces typically require a compromise: high-fidelity control often demands expensive, specialized hardware and extensive setup, while low-cost alternatives frequently sacrifice control precision or increase operator workload. DexDirect seeks to bridge this gap by offering a system that provides both high fidelity (particularly for arm control) and low setup cost.
3. **Address Challenges Specific to Multi-fingered Hands:** Extending solutions for parallel-jaw grippers to multi-fingered hands is difficult due to their high dimensionality, kinematic differences from human hands, and the need for operators to simultaneously control global arm motion and high-dimensional finger articulation, often with limited direct haptic feedback. The motivation is to simplify this complex control problem for operators.
4. **Reduce Cognitive Workload and Operator Dependence:** Many low-setup systems rely on visual tracking and proxy control loops, which impose a high cognitive burden on operators due to latency, estimation errors, and the need to mentally map motions. This leads to slow, operator-dependent demonstration collection. A key motivation for DexDirect is to reduce this cognitive load, making the interface more intuitive and accessible to a wider range of users, thereby increasing consistency and speed.
5. **Improve Precision and Robustness of Arm Control:** Vision-based arm tracking can suffer from noise, occlusion, and latency. By implementing direct kinesthetic guidance for the robot arm, the authors aim to eliminate these issues, allowing arm trajectories to be recorded directly from joint encoders, ensuring that all recorded configurations are physically attained by the robot without intermediate pose estimation or retargeting. This also provides passive contact cues to the operator.
6. **Maintain Simplicity and Scalability for Finger Control:** While using kinesthetic guidance for the arm, the paper aims to retain the low-cost and simple deployment advantages of vision-based methods for finger articulation. By isolating finger control to wrist-relative geometry, the system avoids reliance on monocular metric wrist-pose estimation and complex hardware for the hand.
7. **Enable Effective Downstream Policy Learning:** Ultimately, the demonstrations collected must be suitable for training robust robot policies. The motivation is to provide data that is accurate and consistent enough for this purpose, as validated by training a diffusion policy.

In summary, the core motivation is to create a practical, user-friendly, and cost-effective system that can efficiently generate the high-quality, high-volume dexterous manipulation demonstration data required to advance robot learning capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DexDirect implements a hybrid control architecture that separates the control of the robot arm from the articulation of its dexterous hand. This design is based on the rationale that arm motion, requiring global 6-DoF positioning over a large workspace with physical interaction, benefits from direct physical guidance, while finger articulation, largely concerning relative configurations in a hand-local frame, can be effectively commanded through vision-based retargeting.

**A. System Overview and Design Rationale**

The DexDirect system consists of a robot manipulator, an operator handle, and a sensing and operator station.
- **Manipulator:** An Agilex PIPER 6-DoF robot arm is paired with a MIDAS hand, a 16-joint, 13-actuator dexterous robot hand equipped with 283 distributed tactile taxels. The combined payload at the end-effector is 0.75 kg.
- **Operator Handle:** A lightweight 3D-printed handle is rigidly mounted on the dorsal side of the hand assembly, offset by 65 mm from the flange to provide operator hand clearance while limiting the moment arm of applied forces.
- **Sensing and Operator Station:** A wrist-mounted RGB camera (Luxonis OAK-1 Wide FOV) captures visual observations for policy learning. A separate commodity webcam (Logitech C270) tracks the operator’s right hand, positioned 45 cm below the hand and oriented towards the palm, for finger control. The operator rests their right forearm on a fixed support, and a monitor displays the wrist-camera feed. The operator's left hand guides the robot arm via the handle.

**B. Direct Kinesthetic Arm Guidance**

For arm control, the PIPER robot arm operates in a gravity-compensated drag mode. This mode features zero position stiffness and low joint damping, allowing operators to manually move the arm with minimal resistance.
- **System Identification:** Prior to operation, a staged system identification procedure is performed to accurately estimate parameters such as gravity direction, end-effector payload mass and center of mass, joint-wise torque-delivery scaling, and Coulomb friction. These parameters are used to correct gravity torques computed from the robot's MuJoCo model, which are then applied as feedforward torque commands.
- **Direct Trajectory Recording:** Because the physical robot arm is guided directly, arm trajectories are recorded straight from the robot's joint encoders. End-effector poses are derived via forward kinematics. This approach eliminates the need for arm-side pose estimation, retargeting, inverse-kinematics solvers, or Cartesian motion generators during the collection process, ensuring that recorded states correspond to physically attained robot configurations within its limits.
- **Haptic Feedback:** The rigid mechanical connection of the handle transmits interaction forces to the operator, providing passive contact cues without requiring a separate haptic device. Safety is maintained through hardware limits and an emergency stop.

**C. Vision-Based Finger Retargeting**

The operator’s right hand is tracked by a palm-facing webcam at 30 Hz.
- **Landmark Detection:** Each RGB frame from the webcam is processed by MediaPipe Hands, a framework that detects 21 three-dimensional hand landmarks.
- **Robot Hand Mapping:** The wrist-relative landmarks detected from the operator's hand are mapped to the MIDAS hand. This mapping utilizes an optimization-based `dex-retargeting` framework (adapted from AnyTeleop), which computes robot joint targets by matching selected human and robot finger vectors while respecting robot joint limits.
- **Localized Control:** Crucially, no global wrist position or orientation estimated from the webcam is used for robot control. The robot arm, guided kinesthetically, independently determines the global pose of the hand, thereby confining the vision pipeline solely to hand-local finger articulation. This avoids issues associated with monocular metric wrist-pose estimation.
- **Operator Support:** A forearm support helps stabilize the operator's right hand, keeping the palm centered in the camera view and limiting extraneous wrist motion, while allowing the fingers freedom to articulate.

**D. Multimodal Demonstration Recording**

To create comprehensive demonstration datasets, various robot states and sensor readings are synchronized and logged:
- **Data Streams:** Arm joint states, hand actuator states, tactile measurements from the MIDAS hand, and wrist-camera images are all time-stamped using a common monotonic clock on the logging computer. Arm and hand states are logged at 200 Hz, tactile measurements at 60 Hz, and wrist-camera images at 30 Hz.
- **Temporal Alignment:** For policy training, proprioceptive and tactile data streams are aligned to the wrist-camera timestamps to form 30 Hz time-aligned demonstration episodes. Nearest-neighbor timestamp matching is used to select corresponding sensor data for each camera timestamp, without temporal interpolation.

**E. Policy Architecture**

To validate the suitability of DexDirect for robot learning, a diffusion policy is employed:
- **Input:** The policy's condition comprises an embedding derived from a wrist-camera RGB frame (downscaled, cropped, and processed by a DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoder), a 13-dimensional vector representing absolute hand positions, and a 4-dimensional reading from the PaXini tactile sensors (mean thumb, max thumb, mean index, max index).
- **Output:** The policy generates a 19-dimensional action command, consisting of relative positions for the 6-DoF end-effector target and 13 finger targets.

**F. Experimental Design**

- **Baselines:** DexDirect was compared against two established low-setup teleoperation systems: AnyTeleop (reproduced on the same platform using an Intel RealSense RGB-D camera for wrist pose estimation and CuRobo for motion generation) and TeleDex (implemented using its publicly released software and hardware, which uses a smartphone for pose tracking).
- **User Study:** Ten participants without prior experience used all three interfaces. The order of interfaces was counterbalanced. Participants completed five diverse, contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks: Jenga extraction, USB unplugging, Lazy Susan rotation and pick-and-place, Whiteboard erasing, and Keyboard typing. Each task had a fixed time budget (120-180 s) and was preceded by a one-minute practice session.
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed by the number of successful demonstrations collected within the time budget, the completion time for successful executions, and operator workload using an adapted NASA-TLX questionnaire.
- **Policy Training Evaluation:** A diffusion policy was trained using 200 demonstrations collected by a single operator with DexDirect on a cube pick-and-place task. The policy's success rate was evaluated over 20 episodes without human intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DexDirect focused on demonstration throughput, task performance, operator workload, and the viability of collected data for policy learning.

**A. Demonstration Throughput and Task Performance**
DexDirect exhibited a higher rate of successful demonstration collection compared to the baseline systems under equivalent time budgets.
- **Total Successful Demonstrations:** Across all participants and five tasks, DexDirect collected 481 successful demonstrations. This was significantly higher than TeleDex (151 successful demonstrations) and AnyTeleop (28 successful demonstrations).
- **Throughput Ratio:** This translates to a 3.2× higher successful-demonstration throughput compared to TeleDex and a 17.2× higher throughput compared to AnyTeleop.
- **Success Rates per Task:** DexDirect achieved the highest success rate on every evaluated task. Its success rates ranged from 0.71 (keyboard typing) to 0.96 (Lazy Susan rotation and pick-and-place). In contrast, TeleDex's success rates ranged from 0.32 to 0.85, and AnyTeleop's ranged from 0 to 0.50.
- **Task-Specific Failures:** AnyTeleop produced no successful demonstrations for the whiteboard erasing or keyboard typing tasks within the allocated time. This was attributed to difficulties in regulating end-effector depth and delayed robot response, respectively, which hindered stable contact and precise, rapid movements.
- **Completion Times:** DexDirect also demonstrated reduced completion times for successful task executions. Compared to TeleDex, DexDirect achieved speedups ranging from 1.8× (USB unplugging) to 4.8× (Jenga). Relative to AnyTeleop, speedups for tasks where AnyTeleop had successful trials included 3.4× (Jenga), 3.0× (USB unplugging), and 5.6× (Lazy Susan). These results indicate that DexDirect's throughput advantage was a result of both faster individual trial completion and a higher success rate per attempt.

**B. Operator Workload**
The adapted NASA-TLX questionnaire indicated a substantial reduction in perceived operator workload with DexDirect.
- **Overall Workload (Raw TLX Score):** DexDirect achieved an overall Raw TLX score of 2.01, which was lower than TeleDex (3.08) and AnyTeleop (3.81). Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirmed a statistically significant reduction in workload for DexDirect compared to both AnyTeleop (p = 0.00195) and TeleDex (p = 0.01953).
- **Cognitive Dimensions:** The most pronounced reductions were observed in cognitive workload dimensions. Relative to TeleDex, DexDirect lowered mental demand (from 3.19 to 1.77), frustration (from 2.79 to 1.47), and effort (from 3.40 to 2.17). Temporal demand and perceived-performance workload were also lower.
- **Physical Demand:** The only dimension where DexDirect scored higher was physical demand, increasing to 3.23 compared to TeleDex (2.72) and AnyTeleop (2.62). This indicates a trade-off where the physical effort of directly guiding the robot arm replaced the cognitive effort associated with tracking, coordinate remapping, and visual correction. The lower overall workload suggests this trade-off was beneficial for repeated demonstration collection.

**C. Policy Training**
Demonstrations collected using DexDirect were successfully utilized for training robot manipulation policies.
- **Cube Pick-and-Place Task:** A diffusion policy trained on 200 demonstrations collected via DexDirect achieved a 90% success rate (18 out of 20 episodes) on a cube pick-and-place task. This demonstrates that the data collected by DexDirect is suitable for end-to-end policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of DexDirect contributes to the field of robot learning by addressing critical bottlenecks in the collection of dexterous manipulation demonstrations. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Scalable Dexterous Robot Learning:** DexDirect offers a practical and effective path toward generating the large, high-quality datasets essential for training robust robot manipulation policies. By significantly increasing demonstration throughput and success rates, it helps overcome a major data acquisition challenge that limits the capabilities of robot learning systems.
2. **Reduced Barrier to Entry for Robot Demonstration:** The system’s low-setup requirements—leveraging a commodity webcam for hand control and eliminating the need for expensive leader arms or complex exoskeletons for arm guidance—make it more accessible to a broader range of researchers and practitioners. This can democratize access to advanced robot learning techniques.
3. **Improved Operator Experience and Efficiency:** The user study results indicate that DexDirect substantially reduces the cognitive load, frustration, and overall effort required from operators. This suggests that the interface is more intuitive and less demanding, potentially enabling non-expert users to contribute high-quality demonstrations more consistently and for longer durations. The trade-off of increased physical demand for reduced cognitive effort appears to be favorable for overall workload in this context.
4. **Enhanced Demonstration Quality and Reliability:** By employing direct kinesthetic guidance for the robot arm, DexDirect eliminates sources of error inherent in vision-based arm tracking, such as latency, pose estimation errors, and visual occlusion. This ensures that recorded arm trajectories directly reflect physically attainable robot states, leading to more accurate and reliable demonstration data for learning.
5. **Validation of Hybrid Control Paradigms:** The success of DexDirect supports the efficacy of a hybrid control approach, where gross motor skills (arm movement) are managed through direct physical interaction, and fine motor skills (finger articulation) are controlled via vision-based retargeting. This demonstrates a strategic partitioning of control responsibilities that capitalizes on the strengths of different interface modalities.
6. **Potential for Broader Application:** While currently evaluated on a specific robot platform and a set of tasks, the principles of direct kinesthetic arm guidance combined with vision-based finger retargeting could be adapted to other robot systems and more complex manipulation scenarios. The collected data’s proven utility for training a diffusion policy highlights its direct relevance to state-of-the-art robot learning methods.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, which represent areas for future research and development. The system requires co-located operation and a robot capable of safe, low-impedance guidance, precluding remote teleoperation and potentially limiting ergonomics for larger manipulators. The current interface uses both operator hands for a single arm-hand system, which restricts direct single-operator bimananual control. Finger control still relies on monocular landmark detection and morphology-dependent retargeting, making it susceptible to tracking errors, and it lacks distributed fingertip force feedback. Furthermore, the evaluation was limited to ten participants, five short tasks, and one robot platform. Future work could explore longer-term studies, additional robot platforms, extensions for bimanual operation, and controlled ablations to isolate the impact of specific factors like coordinate remapping, latency, and passive contact cues.

Despite these limitations, DexDirect represents a notable advancement in teleoperation interfaces for dexterous robot manipulation, offering a pathway toward more efficient and scalable robot learning from human demonstrations.
