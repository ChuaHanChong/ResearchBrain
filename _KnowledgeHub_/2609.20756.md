# OPTED: On-Policy Fine-Tuning for End-to-End Driving using a Render-Free Teacher

> [!summary] Summary
> OPTED post-trains camera-based end-to-end driving policies by decoupling exploration from rendering: a privileged teacher is trained with PPO on vectorized HD-map and box inputs in a render-free simulator, then labels states the student visits during closed-loop rollouts in a 3DGS simulator. It lifts TransFuser and VaVAM scene scores 1.6x and 9.5x, and in a controlled vectorized study matches RL fine-tuning with roughly 1000x fewer closed-loop episodes.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning supervises end-to-end driving policies only at human-demonstrated states, so closed-loop compounding errors push them into uncovered states that raise safety-critical infraction risk.
- RL post-training of sensor-based policies needs orders of magnitude more interaction than supervision, each step demanding costly 3D rendering, and many rendered frames risk simulator overfitting.
- Closed-loop SFT anchors targets to logged trajectories, biasing supervision as the policy drifts, while privileged-distillation pipelines share one simulator with the teacher and train students from scratch.

## Method

- ==Privileged render-free RL teacher==: PPO in PufferDrive on vectorized ego, agent and road-graph states, using a non-recurrent attention encoder and a dense lane-gated ==route-progress reward==, then frozen after target-domain adaptation.
- Cross-simulator ==DAgger== loop: the pre-trained camera student drives AlpaSim on ==3DGS== reconstructions of real logs; each visited state is synced into the vectorized simulator, labeled by the teacher, and stored in a ==FIFO buffer==.
- **Teacher labeling operator** rolls the teacher K steps from the student's state to convert actions into waypoint targets; output-matched losses use ==forward KL== for action heads or ==horizon-weighted L1== for waypoint heads.

## Results

- On 441 held-out NuRec scenes, OPTED raises scene score from **26.5** to **41.8** for LTFv6 and **3.9** to **37.1** for VaVAM, beating closed-loop SFT RoaD-recovery at **32.9** and **30.7**.
- On WOMD, OPTED reaches **95%** of the teacher's scene score in **6.8k** episodes versus **6.2M** for RL fine-tuning, converging to **93.0** versus **93.9** with closer-to-human jerk.
- Private AlpaSim leaderboard: VaVAM rises **6.7** to **35.8** and LTFv6 **25.1** to **32.2**; higher-scoring LSTM teachers yield students keeping only **56-66%** of teacher km/infraction versus **90%** for the attention teacher.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decouple where exploration and supervision happen: sample-hungry exploration belongs in a cheap privileged simulator, while costly rendered interaction should be spent only on on-policy labeling of the sensor student.
> - A teacher's own return is a poor proxy for its value as a labeler: DAgger teachers should be Markov and stylistically smooth, since distillation transfers style along with competence.
> - Open-loop displacement error poorly predicts closed-loop driving, and waypoint outputs lose teacher competence through the tracking controller, favoring action-predicting students and dynamics-aware controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

End-to-end autonomous-driving systems commonly use behavior cloning (BC) to learn a mapping from camera or other sensor observations to vehicle actions or future waypoints. These models benefit from large human-driving datasets, but BC is fundamentally an open-loop training objective. It supervises the policy only at states encountered by human drivers. During deployment, the policy acts autonomously, and small prediction errors can alter subsequent states. This creates a distribution shift, often called covariate shift, in which the vehicle enters situations that were underrepresented in the training data.

Closed-loop post-training seeks to address this problem. Reinforcement learning (RL) is an attractive approach because it directly optimizes objectives such as route progress, collision avoidance, and staying on the roadway. In recent work, RL has been applied effectively to privileged policies that receive vectorized inputs, including maps, vehicle states, and bounding boxes. These simulators can execute very large numbers of interactions because they do not need to render camera images.

Applying RL directly to camera-based end-to-end policies is considerably more expensive. Every interaction requires sensor rendering, and high-fidelity simulation based on real driving scenes can be computationally demanding. Large-scale rendered RL also risks overfitting to simulator artifacts. Alternative closed-loop supervised approaches, including DAgger and recovery-based methods such as RoaD, use expert or human-derived labels on states visited by the learner. However, these methods require an expert that can label arbitrary states or rely on heuristics tied to logged trajectories.

OPTED is positioned between these approaches. It separates exploration from supervision by training a privileged teacher with RL in a render-free, vectorized simulator, then using that teacher to label states visited by a camera-based student in a rendered simulator. The method combines the scalability of vectorized RL with on-policy supervision and retains the student’s initialization from human driving data.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Addressing Compounding Errors

The primary objective is to improve the closed-loop behavior of pre-trained end-to-end driving policies. A policy can have low open-loop prediction error while still failing during autonomous rollout because it does not know how to recover from states caused by its own previous mistakes. OPTED aims to provide training targets specifically at those states.

Rather than training only against the human trajectory, the method labels the states that the current student actually visits. This creates an on-policy training distribution that follows the evolving behavior of the student.

#### 2.2. Reducing the Cost of Rendered RL

The paper identifies two sources of cost in closed-loop RL for sensor-based policies: the large number of interactions required for exploration and the expense of rendering sensor observations at every interaction. OPTED confines RL exploration to a vectorized simulator, where interactions are cheaper. Rendering is used only when the student is rolled out and requires camera observations.

The authors therefore seek to achieve RL-like closed-loop improvements without requiring the camera-based student to perform millions or billions of exploratory simulator interactions.

#### 2.3. Preserving Human-Driving Behavior

A further objective is to improve safety and route completion without discarding the human-data prior encoded during pre-training. Direct RL can optimize the specified reward while producing behavior that differs substantially from human driving, including abrupt acceleration, braking, or steering. OPTED uses supervised distillation from a teacher but retains the student’s existing parameters and architecture, with the experiments updating only the planning decoder. The study measures whether this preserves aspects of human driving style, including jerk distributions and open-loop trajectory agreement.

#### 2.4. Bridging Input and Output Mismatches

The teacher observes privileged vectorized information and outputs control actions, whereas the student observes rendered camera images and may output waypoints. OPTED is designed to support this mismatch. The teacher is initialized at the student’s current state, rolled out for several steps, and its motion is converted into a waypoint trajectory in the student’s coordinate frame. The student is then trained using a loss appropriate to its output head.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Teacher Training

The teacher is trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in PufferDrive 2.0, a render-free vectorized simulator. Its observations include ego kinematics, surrounding-agent positions and velocities, and local road-graph information. It outputs continuous control actions.

The authors modify the standard PufferDrive teacher in two ways. First, they use an attention-based encoder. An ego embedding separately cross-attends to agent and map-element tokens, and the model does not use recurrence. Second, they replace the primarily sparse reward with a denser route-progress reward. The reward includes progress along the logged route when the vehicle is aligned with the lane, a goal bonus, collision penalties, and off-road penalties. Safety penalties do not immediately terminate the episode, allowing the teacher to learn recovery behavior after an infraction.

The teacher is first trained on a large vectorized driving corpus and then adapted with 500 million additional render-free interactions to the target PAI-AV scene distribution. It is frozen before being used to label student rollouts.

#### 3.2. Student Pre-Training and Simulation

The student models are pre-trained using behavior cloning on human demonstrations. The paper evaluates two camera-based architectures:

- **LTFv6**, also referred to as TransFuser, which uses camera input together with ego speed and acceleration and predicts future waypoints.
- **VaVAM**, a video-based model using a single front camera and a flow-matching waypoint head.

Both models operate at 10 Hz and use an MPC controller to convert predicted waypoints into vehicle actions. In the fine-tuning experiments, the visual backbone is frozen and only the planning decoder is updated. This reduces the risk of fitting to rendering artifacts and allows visual features to be cached.

The end-to-end experiments use AlpaSim, which renders neural 3D Gaussian-splatting reconstructions of real driving logs. The dataset contains highway, urban, and rural scenes, with traffic replayed from recorded logs rather than reacting dynamically to the student. There are 1,669 training scenes and 441 held-out evaluation scenes.

#### 3.3. On-Policy Distillation Loop

OPTED alternates between data collection and student updates:

1. The current student drives the rendered simulator in closed loop and receives camera observations.
2. At each student-visited state, the corresponding vectorized state is synchronized into the render-free teacher simulator.
3. The teacher is stepped for \(K\) actions using its mean policy output. Its future motion is converted into a waypoint trajectory aligned with the student’s prediction horizon.
4. Student observations and teacher-generated targets are stored in a bounded first-in, first-out buffer containing recent rollouts.
5. The student is updated using the appropriate imitation loss, after which a new collection iteration begins.

For action-output students, the loss is a forward Kullback–Leibler divergence from the teacher’s action distribution to the student’s distribution. For waypoint-output students, the loss is a weighted L1 distance between predicted and teacher-converted waypoints. The weights emphasize waypoints that most influence the tracking controller.

#### 3.4. Baselines and Controlled Experiments

The end-to-end comparison includes the original pretrained model, open-loop BC fine-tuning on rendered logged data, and RoaD-recovery-style closed-loop supervised fine-tuning. The authors also compare with direct RL where possible.

A separate controlled study uses vectorized students and teachers in PufferDrive on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset. This permits direct comparison between OPTED and RL fine-tuning of the same student. Both action-output and waypoint-output students are evaluated. Metrics include scene score, distance driven per infraction, and Wasserstein distances between policy and human longitudinal and lateral jerk distributions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. End-to-End Driving Performance

On the 441 held-out NuRec scenes, OPTED raises the LTFv6 scene score from **26.5% to 41.8%**, a factor of approximately **1.6**. For VaVAM, the score increases from **3.9% to 37.1%**, a factor of approximately **9.5**.

For LTFv6, OPTED increases distance per infraction from **0.23 km to 0.43 km**. The off-road rate falls from **20.7% to 11.7%**, and route progress increases from **43.7% to 73.4%**. The at-fault collision percentage rises from 10.2% to 23.4%, but the paper notes that this rate is not normalized by distance traveled; the distance-based metric improves overall.

For VaVAM, OPTED raises distance per infraction from **0.08 km to 0.25 km**, lowers at-fault collisions from **18.6% to 13.5%**, lowers off-road events from **34.1% to 24.8%**, and reduces corridor exits from **48.6% to 28.4%**. Route progress increases from **31.2% to 60.7%**.

OPTED outperforms the tested BC and RoaD-recovery variants at the same two-epoch training budget. BC improves domain adaptation because it uses rendered inputs from the target scenes, but it does not expose the student to its own closed-loop states. RoaD-recovery provides closed-loop training but constrains execution toward logged trajectories and constructs targets through recovery heuristics. OPTED instead allows unconstrained student rollouts and labels those states with the teacher’s behavior.

#### 4.2. Human-Trajectory Agreement

OPTED increases open-loop average displacement error relative to BC and recovery-based fine-tuning. This is expected because the teacher targets route progress and recovery rather than simply reproducing the human trajectory. Nevertheless, the increase is bounded. For example, LTFv6’s ADE at three seconds on NuRec rises to **1.67 m** with OPTED, compared with **0.60 m** after BC and **0.75 m** after RoaD-recovery. On nuPlan, the corresponding value is **1.22 m**, compared with **0.53 m** for BC.

The results indicate that open-loop agreement and closed-loop performance are not equivalent. The BC model can be closer to human trajectories while receiving a lower closed-loop scene score.

#### 4.3. Controller Effects

The paper identifies a controller-related failure mode for VaVAM. Because VaVAM does not use current ego speed and was pre-trained mostly on lower-speed scenes, it can predict a trajectory that is dynamically inconsistent at highway speeds. The default controller may convert the implied braking into lateral swerving. Adding a friction-circle constraint to limit combined longitudinal and lateral acceleration raises the OPTED VaVAM scene score from **37.1% to 50.6%**. This finding shows that policy and controller design jointly affect measured performance.

#### 4.4. Sample Efficiency

In the vectorized WOMD study, the action student starts with a scene score of **68.2%** after BC. OPTED reaches 95% of the teacher’s scene score after approximately **6,800 closed-loop episodes**, while direct RL fine-tuning requires approximately **6.2 million episodes** to reach the same level. This is roughly a three-orders-of-magnitude difference in interaction requirements.

After longer training, OPTED reaches a scene score of **93.0%**, compared with **93.9%** for RL fine-tuning. However, OPTED remains closer to the human prior in some comfort measures. Its longitudinal and lateral jerk distribution distances are **1.00** and **2.04**, respectively, compared with **1.89** and **3.26** for RL fine-tuning.

OPTED also improves the waypoint student, increasing its scene score from **73.8% to 87.8%**, although the transfer is weaker than for the action student. This supports the authors’ conclusion that matching the teacher and student output representations improves knowledge transfer.

#### 4.5. Teacher Design

Teachers with higher standalone scene scores do not necessarily produce better students. Recurrent teachers using sparse rewards obtain slightly higher scene scores, but their distilled students have poorer distance-per-infraction and substantially larger jerk-distribution differences. The authors attribute this to two factors: the teacher’s driving style is transferred along with its competence, and a recurrent teacher’s decisions depend on hidden history that is unavailable when it is queried at an arbitrary student state. A Markov teacher is therefore more compatible with on-policy state labeling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

OPTED provides a framework for post-training sensor-based driving policies while separating expensive rendering from large-scale RL exploration. Its teacher can be trained once and reused across multiple students, and the teacher and student can be pre-trained on separate datasets and in separate simulators. This may reduce the computational barrier to closed-loop improvement for existing camera-based policies.

The method also demonstrates that preserving a human-data initialization can help maintain human-like motion characteristics while adding recovery and route-following competence. Its interface is flexible enough to support different student architectures and output types, although the experiments show that action-to-waypoint conversion introduces a transfer limitation.

Several limitations qualify the results. The teacher has privileged access to maps and agents, while the student relies on camera observations, creating an observability gap. The experiments use reconstructed logs with non-reactive traffic, so they do not fully assess interaction with responsive road users. Only the student decoder is fine-tuned for two epochs, and real-world deployment is not evaluated. Performance also depends on the tracking controller and teacher quality. Consequently, OPTED is best interpreted as a scalable closed-loop simulation and distillation method whose transfer to physical vehicles requires further validation.
