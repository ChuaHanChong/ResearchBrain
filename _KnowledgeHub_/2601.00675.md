# RoboReward: General-Purpose Vision-Language Reward Models for Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stanford University and UC Berkeley introduce ROBOREWARD, a dataset, benchmark, and trained vision-language models to generate automatic reward signals for robotic reinforcement learning. The work demonstrates that targeted VLM training, incorporating diverse failure modes, enables smaller models to surpass frontier VLMs, achieving a 0.665 MAE on a new benchmark and improving real-robot task success rates from 5-10% to 50-80% on unseen tasks.

## Problem

- Robot reinforcement learning faces a critical bottleneck in obtaining accurate, informative, and scalable reward signals.
- Traditional reward engineering relies on brittle, handcrafted functions or labor-intensive human labeling, limiting scalability and generalization.
- Prior VLM-based reward models often struggle with the precision, fine-grained detail, and diverse real-world complexities required for robust robotic applications.

## Method

- Developed **RoboReward**, a large-scale dataset featuring human-labeled 5-level discrete progress scores for diverse real-robot tasks and embodiments.
- Implemented a novel data augmentation pipeline using counterfactual relabeling and negative clipping to synthesize crucial negative and partial-progress examples from success-heavy robot datasets.
- Fine-tuned specialized ==Vision-Language Models== (RoboReward 4B/8B) on this augmented data and created RoboRewardBench, a human-verified benchmark for rigorous evaluation and real-world RL deployment.

## Results

- The RoboReward 8B model achieved the lowest Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of **0.665** on RoboRewardBench, outperforming all 22 evaluated frontier VLMs, including powerful proprietary models.
- When deployed on a real robot, RoboReward 8B improved task success rates from a base of 5-**10%** to 50-**80%** on two unseen tasks, substantially narrowing the gap to human-provided rewards.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the data augmentation pipeline, demonstrating that training without synthetic negative examples significantly degraded model performance across diverse scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Progress-based rewards (discrete 5-level scores) are significantly more effective for robot RL convergence than binary success rewards.
> - Targeted supervision with a balanced distribution of diverse failure modes (achieved via augmentation) allows smaller VLMs to outperform much larger, generalist frontier models in robotic reward prediction.
> - A strong correlation exists between offline reward model accuracy on the benchmark and improved real-world robot policy performance, validating the utility of robust evaluation suites.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The application of reinforcement learning (RL) to real-world robotics holds immense promise for developing autonomous, adaptive robot behaviors. However, a significant practical hurdle remains the **reward specification problem**: how to provide robots with an accurate, informative, and scalable feedback signal to guide their learning.

Historically, this problem has been addressed in two main ways:
1. **Handcrafted Reward Functions:** Engineers painstakingly design analytical reward functions based on domain knowledge, often requiring extensive trial-and-error and fine-tuning. These are brittle, non-transferable, and time-consuming to create, severely limiting the scalability of RL to diverse, complex tasks.
2. **Human Supervision:** Humans label robot trajectories (e.g., success/failure, preferences), which can be accurate but is labor-intensive, slow, and expensive, making it impractical for large-scale, continuous learning.

The emergence of **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**, trained on vast internet-scale multimodal data, presents a compelling alternative. VLMs possess a general understanding of visual scenes and language instructions, theoretically enabling them to automatically assess task progress and assign rewards. Prior work has explored VLMs for reward generation, but their effectiveness and reliability in real-world robotic deployments—especially in providing precise, accurate rewards required for RL training—remain poorly understood. Many existing VLM-based reward models struggle with real-world complexities, fine-grained details, and the diversity of robotic tasks and embodiments.

This paper directly addresses this gap by:
- **Standardizing Evaluation:** Creating RoboRewardBench, the first comprehensive, human-verified benchmark for VLM-based reward models on diverse real-robot tasks. This moves beyond simulation or limited datasets to robustly assess VLM capabilities.
- **Tackling Data Imbalance:** Recognizing that existing large-scale robot datasets are success-heavy (collected from demonstrations), the authors develop an innovative data augmentation pipeline to synthesize crucial negative (failure) and partial-progress examples, which are vital for training robust reward models that can discriminate different levels of task completion. This "inverse-HER" approach is a notable methodological contribution.
- **Developing General-Purpose Models:** Training and releasing RoboReward models that demonstrate superior performance over much larger, proprietary VLMs, proving that targeted supervision on diverse failure modes is key for practical robotics.

By providing open-source resources—a dataset, benchmark, and models—this work aims to catalyze research into general-purpose, VLM-powered reward functions, moving the field closer to truly autonomous and scalable robot learning paradigms. It connects the advancements in large multimodal models with the specific, demanding requirements of real-world robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the RoboReward project are multifaceted, driven by the limitations of current reward engineering in real-world robotics:

1. **Overcome Reward Specification Bottleneck:** The overarching goal is to address the fundamental challenge of acquiring accurate and informative reward signals for real-world robotic reinforcement learning. Current methods, relying on manual human labeling or brittle, handcrafted functions, severely limit the scalability and generality of robotic RL.
2. **Evaluate VLM Effectiveness in Robotics:** Investigate whether state-of-the-art vision-language models (VLMs) can reliably serve as automatic, general-purpose reward models for real robot tasks. Despite VLMs' general vision-language abilities, their performance at the precision and reliability required for RL training in the physical world is largely unconfirmed.
3. **Develop a Comprehensive Dataset and Benchmark:** Create a diverse, large-scale dataset (`RoboReward`) and a human-verified evaluation benchmark (`RoboRewardBench`) for training and rigorously evaluating VLM-based reward models. This includes tackling the prevalent issue of success-heavy robot datasets.
4. **Innovate Data Augmentation for Failure Modes:** Design and implement a data augmentation pipeline to generate calibrated negative examples (failures, near-misses, partial progress) from existing successful robot demonstration datasets. This is crucial for training reward models that can effectively differentiate between various levels of task completion.
5. **Train and Release General-Purpose Reward Models:** Develop and open-source specialized VLM-based reward models (`RoboReward 4B/8B`) trained on the new dataset, aiming to outperform off-the-shelf generalist VLMs on robotic reward prediction.
6. **Validate Downstream RL Performance:** Demonstrate that improvements in offline reward accuracy, as measured by the benchmark, translate into tangible improvements in real-world robot policy learning and task success. This establishes the practical utility and meaningfulness of the proposed reward models.
7. **Accelerate Research in Robotic Reward Modeling:** By open-sourcing the dataset, models, and evaluation suite, the authors aim to provide a standardized platform to facilitate future research and accelerate progress in developing reliable, general-purpose reward models for robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RoboReward project employs a systematic approach combining data curation, novel augmentation techniques, VLM training, and comprehensive evaluation in both simulation and real-world robotics.

#### 3.1. Reward Type Selection

The first methodological step involved determining the optimal reward formulation for downstream RL. Through simulated experiments using the Robomimic benchmark and the DSRL algorithm, the authors compared three episodic reward types:
- **Binary success:** 1 for success, 0 otherwise.
- **Continuous progress:** A continuous value in reflecting task progress.
- **Discrete progress:** 5 bins, with rewards in {1..., 5}.

Findings indicated that **progress-based rewards (both continuous and discrete)** led to significantly faster and more effective RL convergence than binary success rewards. Since discrete progress is easier for human annotators to label consistently than continuous values, the 5-level discrete progress score (No success, Minimal progress, Partial completion, Near completion, Perfect completion) was chosen as the target reward type for RoboReward. Critically, these simulations also demonstrated a strong positive correlation (r = 0.83) between reward model accuracy (lower Mean Absolute Error, MAE) and downstream RL policy performance, justifying the use of MAE as a primary evaluation metric.

#### 3.2. Data Sources and Negative Example Augmentation

To build the `RoboReward` dataset, two primary real-robot data sources were aggregated:
- **Open X-Embodiment (OXE):** A large-scale collection of approximately 1 million successful robot demonstrations across 22 robot embodiments. Due to its success-heavy nature, these episodes were initially assigned the maximum reward score of 5.
- **RoboArena:** A dataset of real-world robot policy evaluations featuring a diverse mix of successful and naturally occurring failed rollouts, with human-assigned progress scores (mapped to the 1-5 scale). This provides organic failure examples.

Recognizing the severe imbalance towards successful demonstrations in OXE, the authors developed a novel **negative examples data augmentation pipeline**:
1. **Counterfactual Relabeling:** For a given successful video, the pipeline synthesizes alternative natural language task commands for which the *same video* would result in only partial success or complete failure. This involves a multi-stage VLM/LLM process:
 - A VLM describes the video and scene.
 - An LLM plans distinct failure modes to enforce a strict ordering (1 < 2 < 3 < 4 < 5).
 - An LLM generates imperative commands for each score (1-4).
 - A final VLM-based validation step filters for coherence, grounding in the video, and rubric consistency, rejecting ambiguous or inconsistent examples.
2. **Negative Clipping:** Successful videos are temporally truncated to earlier endpoints (e.g., early, mid, late cut points). These clipped videos, with the *original task instruction*, often represent minimal or partial progress, creating additional negative and near-miss supervision grounded in the actual trajectory.

This pipeline, combined with prompt rewriting for grammar normalization, generated 54,135 examples in total, creating a training corpus with a balanced distribution of progress scores across diverse tasks and embodiments.

#### 3.3. Model Training and Evaluation Benchmark

- **Training:** The augmented dataset was split into training (45,072), validation (6,232), and test (2,831) sets, ensuring task descriptions were disjoint across splits to test generalization. `Qwen3-VL` (4 billion and 8 billion parameters) was finetuned on this training set. The vision backbone was frozen, while the fusion and LLM layers were fine-tuned for 3 epochs. The best checkpoints (minimizing MAE on the validation set) were selected as `RoboReward 4B` and `RoboReward 8B`.
- **RoboRewardBench:** The test split was rigorously human-verified, with annotators confirming the justification of each end-of-episode reward label given the video and task description. Examples failing this verification were discarded, resulting in `RoboRewardBench`, a high-trust, human-validated evaluation suite of 2,831 examples.
- **Benchmarking:** 22 prominent VLMs (both open-source and proprietary, varying in size) were evaluated on RoboRewardBench using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) as the metric.

#### 3.4. Real-Robot Policy Training

To validate the practical utility of the trained reward models, `RoboReward 8B` was deployed for real-world RL on a WidowX 250 6-DoF robot arm.
- **Algorithm:** DSRL (a state-of-the-art RL fine-tuning algorithm) was used to fine-tune a multi-task diffusion policy pretrained on the BridgeData V2 dataset.
- **Tasks:** Two unseen real-world tasks: "Pick up the brown monkey and move it on top of the yellow towel" and "Pull the drawer out."
- **Comparison:** Performance was compared against a base policy, an oracle human reward, and Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 (a frontier model specifically trained for embodied reasoning). Success rate over 20 trials was the evaluation metric.

#### 3.5. Data Mixture Ablations

To understand the contribution of the data augmentation pipeline, ablation studies were conducted with the `RoboReward 4B` model:
- Training on RoboArena + original (success-only) OXE demonstrations (without negative augmentation).
- Training with counterfactual relabeling but without negative clipping.

These ablations aimed to demonstrate the necessity of diverse negative examples for broad generalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the potential of VLM-based reward models while also highlighting current limitations.

#### 4.1. RoboRewardBench Performance

- **RoboReward Models Outperform Frontiers:** The fine-tuned `RoboReward 8B` achieved the lowest overall Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.665 on RoboRewardBench, outperforming all 22 evaluated frontier VLMs, including powerful proprietary models like GPT-5 mini (0.691) and various Gemini family models (e.g., Gemini 2.5 Pro at 0.902). `RoboReward 4B` also ranked highly (4th, MAE 0.845).
- **Targeted Supervision Yields Large Gains:** The `RoboReward` models significantly improved over their base Qwen3-VL Instruct counterparts of the same parameter size (e.g., `RoboReward 8B` improved by 0.227 MAE over Qwen3-VL Instruct 8B). This underscores the effectiveness of the high-quality, diverse reward supervision provided by the `RoboReward` dataset and its augmentation pipeline.
- **Non-Uniform Generalization Gaps:** The evaluation revealed substantial and non-uniform performance swings across different robot embodiments, scenes, and camera perspectives for many frontier VLMs. Models that performed well on some subsets degraded sharply on others (e.g., GPT-5 performed well on Austin Sirius but poorly on Berkeley RPT). Even `Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5`, a model specifically trained on robotics data, ranked 11th overall, showing that generalist capabilities do not guarantee robust reward modeling across all diverse scenarios. This indicates that real-world physical reasoning remains a challenge even for advanced VLMs.

#### 4.2. Real-World Reinforcement Learning Improvement

- **Effective RL with RoboReward:** When deployed for real-world RL on the WidowX robot, `RoboReward 8B` enabled effective policy improvement. For "Pick-and-place monkey," the success rate increased from 5% (base policy) to 50%, and for "Open drawer," it rose from 10% to 80%.
- **Closing the Gap to Human Rewards:** While not fully matching oracle human-provided rewards (75% and 90% respectively), `RoboReward 8B` substantially narrowed the performance gap, especially for the "Open drawer" task (80% vs. 90%).
- **Outperforming Frontier Robotics VLMs:** `RoboReward 8B` significantly outperformed `Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5` as a reward model, which showed only minimal improvement on "Open drawer" (45% vs. 80%) and no improvement on "Pick-and-place monkey" (10% vs. 50%).
- **Correlation between Offline Accuracy and Online Performance:** The real-world RL results directly aligned with the RoboRewardBench rankings, reinforcing the simulation findings that higher offline reward model accuracy translates to better downstream RL performance.

#### 4.3. Importance of Negative Data Augmentation

The data mixture ablations confirmed the critical role of the negative examples data augmentation pipeline:
- **Insufficiency of Organic Failures Alone:** Training only on RoboArena (which contains organic failures) and original OXE success examples yielded comparable in-domain error on the RoboArena subset but dramatically worse overall performance on RoboRewardBench (1.45 MAE vs. 0.845 MAE with full data). This highlights that organic failures alone are insufficient for broad generalization.
- **Critical Role of Clipping:** Removing negative clipping (while retaining counterfactual relabeling) degraded overall performance (1.075 MAE vs. 0.845 MAE), showing that clipped examples are essential for broadening coverage of failure modes and improving robustness.

These results demonstrate that the combination of diverse organic data, counterfactual relabeling, and negative clipping is crucial for training general-purpose reward models capable of robustly evaluating diverse task outcomes.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Analysis of VLM Failure Modes

A qualitative analysis of `Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5` failures (as a representative frontier VLM) revealed specific pitfalls:
- **Hallucination of Success:** Models sometimes describe a successful outcome (e.g., "fingers close around the handle," "placing the toy onto the cloth") even when the video does not clearly show it, leading to false positives.
- **Under-tracking Late-Stage Events:** Models might accurately describe the initial setup and approach but fail to narrate the decisive task outcome, resulting in false negatives (e.g., stopping at "appearing ready to grasp" when the drawer actually opened).
- **Lack of Fine-Grained Spatial/Temporal Reasoning:** VLMs struggled with subtle details like whether an object was truly placed vs. hovering, or if a grasp was secure.

These errors, while seemingly minor, significantly impact the reliability of the reward signal for RL, explaining the performance gap between VLM-predicted and human-assigned rewards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "ROBOREWARD" paper makes several significant contributions that have a high potential impact on the field of robotic reinforcement learning:

1. **Addressing a Core Bottleneck:** The work tackles one of the most persistent and critical challenges in real-world robotics: the difficulty of specifying accurate, scalable, and transferable reward functions. By providing a pathway to automatic, VLM-driven reward generation, it removes a major barrier to widespread adoption of RL in robotics.

2. **Creation of a Foundational Resource:** The release of the `RoboReward` dataset, `RoboRewardBench` (a human-verified evaluation benchmark), and the trained `RoboReward 4B/8B` models, along with an evaluation suite, represents a substantial open-science contribution. These resources will serve as a standardized platform for researchers worldwide to develop, test, and compare new reward modeling techniques, fostering rapid progress in the field.

3. **Proof-of-Concept for Targeted VLM Supervision:** The demonstration that relatively smaller (4B/8B parameter) models, when subjected to targeted supervision with carefully curated negative examples, can outperform much larger, generalist VLMs (including state-of-the-art proprietary models) is a crucial insight. It suggests that while general VLM capabilities are a good starting point, domain-specific finetuning and data augmentation are essential for achieving the precision required in real-world robotics.

4. **Validation of Offline Metrics for Online Performance:** The strong correlation between offline reward accuracy (MAE on RoboRewardBench) and real-world RL policy performance (task success rates) is a key finding. This validates the utility of such benchmarks as meaningful proxies for real-world usefulness, allowing researchers to iterate and improve reward models more efficiently without constant real-robot deployments.

5. **Highlighting Current VLM Limitations:** By meticulously benchmarking 22 VLMs and providing qualitative failure analyses, the paper clearly articulates the current shortcomings of even frontier VLMs in fine-grained physical reasoning, temporal tracking, and avoiding hallucinations in robotic contexts. This provides clear directions for future research in improving VLM architectures and training methodologies for embodied AI.

6. **Enabling More Generalist Robot Policies:** As the field moves towards generalist robot policies capable of performing a wide array of tasks across diverse embodiments, general-purpose reward models like RoboReward are indispensable. They provide the necessary adaptive feedback loop for fine-tuning and improving these policies in novel, real-world scenarios, reducing reliance on human intervention.

In conclusion, "ROBOREWARD" offers both a practical solution and a comprehensive framework for advancing robotic reward modeling. By open-sourcing its contributions, it sets a new standard for evaluating and developing general-purpose vision-language reward models, promising to accelerate the development of more capable and autonomous robotic systems. Future work, particularly on longer-horizon and multi-stage tasks, will build upon this foundation to address even more complex real-world robotic challenges.
