# FAWAM: Force-Aware World Action Models for Closed-Loop Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> FAWAM introduces a robotic manipulation framework that systematically integrates force information at perception, prediction, and closed-loop execution levels to enable robust contact-rich tasks. The framework achieved an 85% average success rate across diverse tasks, improving performance by 36.25% over vision-only and 21.25% over prior force-aware baselines.

## Problem

- Vision-only approaches are often insufficient for contact-rich manipulation, as critical interaction states like incipient contact or excessive pressure are ambiguous or difficult to infer from visual data alone.
- Existing force integration methods, primarily using force as a passive observation or for low-level reactive control, do not fully leverage its predictive power for future interaction dynamics or explicitly model future contact states.
- Robust manipulation lacks a proactive prediction paradigm and online, closed-loop adjustment mechanisms, unlike human motor control which continuously anticipates interaction outcomes and corrects actions.

## Method

- Developed a ==Force-Envisioned Action Model (FEAM)== that encodes historical force/torque data, generates force-conditioned actions, and jointly predicts future actions and end-effector wrenches.
- Implemented a ==Force-Guided Residual Corrector (FGRC)== as a lightweight, high-frequency module to provide online adjustments to actions based on the discrepancy between predicted and real-time sensed force feedback.
- Utilized a ==two-stage training strategy== for the FEAM (video model fine-tuning then force-aware decoder training) and trained the FGRC separately using human intervention data for corrective learning.

## Results

- FAWAM achieved an average success rate of **85%** across four diverse contact-rich tasks (Erase Whiteboard, Peel Cucumber, Pivot Box, Wipe Vase).
- Performance improved by **36.25%** over vision-only baselines (from **~48%** to **85%**) and **21.25%** over existing force-aware baselines (from **~64%** to **85%**).
- The Force-Envisioned Action Model alone reached a **73.75%** success rate, demonstrating the strength of force-aware prediction, while the Residual Corrector further enhanced robustness and led to faster task completion times.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling future contact forces (wrenches) alongside future actions, through joint prediction, significantly contributes to learning physically grounded robot behaviors.
> - A closed-loop residual correction mechanism, driven by the discrepancy between predicted and sensed forces, is crucial for compensating for unexpected contact variations and improving execution robustness.
> - Systematic integration of force information across perception, predictive modeling, and online corrective execution synergistically enhances robotic manipulation capabilities for contact-rich tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks often involve sustained physical interaction with the environment, commonly referred to as contact-rich manipulation. These tasks, such as wiping, peeling, or pushing, necessitate continuous regulation of interaction forces. Traditionally, robotic systems have relied heavily on visual observations for perception and control. However, the paper identifies a limitation in vision-only approaches for contact-rich scenarios: critical interaction states, such as incipient contact, excessive pressure, or jamming, are frequently ambiguous or difficult to infer from visual data alone. This often leads to brittle performance in dynamic or uncertain environments.

To address these limitations, researchers have explored incorporating complementary sensory modalities, particularly force and tactile feedback. Recent works have integrated force sensing into visuomotor models and vision-language-action (VLA) models, demonstrating improvements in performance on contact-rich tasks (e.g., FoAR, ACP, FACTR, ForceVLA, TA-VLA). These existing approaches typically fall into two categories:
1. **Force as an additional observation modality:** Force signals are used as an extra input to the policy learning process. While this provides more information, the paper argues it often does not fully exploit the predictive power of force for future interaction dynamics.
2. **Force feedback in low-level reactive controllers:** Force information is used for immediate, reactive adjustments at a lower control level. This can compensate for some latency or incompleteness in visual observations but similarly lacks an explicit model of how future contact states will evolve.

A key gap identified by the authors is the absence of explicit modeling of future contact states, such as anticipated contact forces, and the subsequent use of these predictions to guide execution-time feedback corrections. The paper draws an analogy to human behavior, where individuals performing contact-rich tasks continuously anticipate interaction outcomes and adjust their actions when sensed feedback deviates from these expectations. This suggests that robust manipulation requires a proactive prediction paradigm rather than purely reactive observation-to-action mapping.

World models, which are designed to capture future state evolution, offer a natural framework for incorporating contact prediction and generating contact-aware actions. However, current world models predominantly focus on predicting visual or latent state evolution, with force-related prediction and correction remaining relatively underexplored. Furthermore, prediction alone is insufficient; robust execution necessitates that predicted contact signals serve as online references to detect deviations and facilitate corrective actions.

Finally, the work connects to research on on-policy imitation and corrective data aggregation (e.g., DAgger, residual policy learning). Behavior cloning is known to be susceptible to covariate shift. Corrective learning approaches, often involving human intervention, aim to mitigate this by refining policies based on observed errors. For contact-rich tasks, human interventions are challenging due to the need to preserve ongoing contact and avoid abrupt force discontinuities. Residual policy learning, which adds learned corrections to a nominal controller, provides a suitable mechanism for integrating these local execution error corrections. FAWAM integrates these concepts by proposing a systematic, multi-level integration of force signals within a world action model, combining predictive capabilities with online residual correction, thereby pushing the boundaries of robust contact-rich robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and adaptable robotic manipulation framework, termed FAWAM (Force-Aware World Action Model), specifically designed for contact-rich tasks. This framework aims to systematically leverage force information beyond its conventional role as a passive observation.

The key objectives can be broken down into the following:
- **Systematic Force Integration:** To integrate force signals across three distinct levels of robotic control: perception (understanding current contact states), prediction (anticipating future contact outcomes), and closed-loop execution (refining actions based on real-time force feedback).
- **Force-Envisioned Action Model Development:** To design a novel action model capable of encoding historical force/torque data to inform action generation and, critically, to jointly predict both future actions and the corresponding end-effector wrenches. This aims to explicitly model the coupling between robot motions and their physical contact consequences.
- **Force-Guided Residual Corrector Implementation:** To introduce an online residual correction mechanism that uses the discrepancy between predicted wrench trajectories (from the action model) and real-time sensed force feedback. This module is intended to refine actions dynamically during execution, thereby improving robustness to unexpected contact variations.

These objectives are motivated by several factors:
- **Inadequacy of Vision-Only Systems:** Many real-world manipulation tasks involve intricate physical contact where visual information alone is insufficient to discern critical interaction states (e.g., exact contact pressure, subtle sticking, or slippage). Force and tactile sensing provide crucial complementary data that can resolve these ambiguities.
- **Limitations of Existing Force Integration Methods:** Current approaches often incorporate force either as an additional observation modality or as a reactive input for low-level controllers. This passive or purely reactive utilization of force overlooks its potential to model future interaction dynamics proactively, limiting the robot's ability to anticipate and prevent failures.
- **Inspiration from Human Motor Control:** Humans naturally anticipate the contact outcomes of their movements and continuously adjust their actions based on perceived deviations from these expectations. This cognitive process suggests a need for predictive models in robotic control that can forecast both actions and their physical consequences.
- **Need for Closed-Loop Adaptability:** Even with predictive models, real-world environments present uncertainties. A purely predictive model, executing actions open-loop, cannot react to unforeseen disturbances or model inaccuracies during execution. A mechanism for online, closed-loop correction using force feedback is thus essential for robust performance.
- **Bridging World Models and Physical Interaction:** While world models have shown promise in predicting future states and generating actions, their application has largely focused on visual or latent state evolution. There is a clear motivation to extend these powerful predictive capabilities to explicitly model and utilize physical interaction forces, making world models more grounded in the physics of contact-rich manipulation.

By addressing these motivations, FAWAM aims to enable robots to perform complex contact-rich tasks with increased reliability and adaptability in dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FAWAM operates as a closed-loop force-aware world action model, structured into two primary components that function at different frequencies: a Force-Envisioned Action Model (FEAM) operating at 1 Hz, and a Force-Guided Residual Corrector (FGRC) operating at 10 Hz.

#### 3.1. Force-Envisioned Action Model (FEAM)

The FEAM is responsible for contact-state estimation, action generation, and future force prediction. It builds upon a foundation similar to GE-Act for action decoding from future visual latent representations.

- **Contact Feature Encoding:** Recent high-frequency (120 Hz) 6-axis end-effector wrench histories (w_t-H_f+1:t, where H_f is the history window of 0.5 seconds or 60 frames) are encoded into a compact contact feature (c_t) using a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), denoted Enc_f. This MLP is trained jointly with the action decoder.
- **Force-Conditioned Action Generation:** The contact feature c_t is injected into the action generation pathway through force-conditioned AdaLN-Zero modulation. This involves predicting an offset for the standard AdaLN-Zero parameters (shift, scale, and gate values for attention and MLP branches) in each action DiT block using a separate MLP (MLP(i)f(c_t)). This offset is then added to the original parameters. The last layer of MLP(i)f is zero-initialized, allowing the model to initially recover the original action decoder behavior and gradually learn force-adaptive refinements during training.
- **Joint Action and Wrench Prediction:** Instead of having separate heads for action and force prediction, the model uses a single joint linear projection layer that outputs concatenated action and wrench trajectories (Z_t = [A_t, W_t]). This design encourages the decoder to learn a shared representation that captures the inherent coupling between robot motions and their physical contact consequences.
- **Two-Stage Training Strategy:**
 1. **Stage 1: Video Generation Model Fine-tuning:** A video generation model (based on GE-Base) is fine-tuned using a latent-space flow-matching objective. This stage processes historical multi-view visual latents and language instructions to predict future visual latent sequences.
 2. **Stage 2: Force-Aware Action Decoder Training:** The video world model from Stage 1 is frozen. The force-aware action decoder is then trained. Inputs include visual features from the frozen world model, current proprioceptive state, and the contact feature c_t. The decoder predicts joint velocity components corresponding to future action (ˆv_A_t) and future wrench (ˆv_W_t) trajectories. The training objective is a combination of action loss (L_action) and force loss (L_force), balanced by a hyperparameter λ_F, within the same flow-matching framework.

#### 3.2. Force-Guided Residual Corrector (FGRC)

The FGRC is a lightweight module designed to run at a higher frequency (10 Hz) to provide online adjustments to the action chunk generated by the FEAM, reacting to unexpected contact variations.

- **Inputs to the Corrector:** The residual model takes several inputs:
 - **Wrench Tracking Error:** This is a crucial input, calculated as the difference between recent measured 6D force/torque feedback (w_meas_t-H_e+1:t) and the predicted wrench trajectory (w_pred_t-H_e+1:t) from the FEAM, over a history window H_e.
 - **Contact Feature (c_t):** Encoded from recent wrench histories using the same force encoder as the FEAM (frozen during residual training).
 - **Upcoming Base Action Chunk (a_base_t+1:t+H_p):** The nominal action sequence planned by the FEAM.
 - **Predicted Future Wrench Chunk (w_pred_t+1:t+H_p):** The expected contact forces accompanying the nominal action.
 - **Current Proprioceptive State (q_t):** The robot's current configuration.
- **Residual Corrector Architecture:** The FGRC is implemented as a three-layer MLP with a dual-head output:
 - **Residual Action Head:** Outputs a residual action correction (∆a_res_t+1:t+H_r).
 - **Gating Head:** Predicts a scalar residual gate (g_res_t). This gate is converted into a binary activation mask (κ_t) using a conservative threshold (τ = 0.8) during inference. Corrections are applied only if κ_t = 1.
- **Action Correction:** The final corrected action sequence (a_cor_t+1:t+H_r) is computed by adding the activated residual correction to the base action: a_cor_t+1:t+H_r = a_base_t+1:t+H_r + κ_t * ∆a_res_t+1:t+H_r.
- **Correction Dataset Collection:** Data for training the FGRC is collected using FACTR, a force-feedback leader-follower teleoperation system. During autonomous execution, when abnormal contact is observed, a human operator can intervene through the leader arm. The difference between the human-corrected command and the base policy command is recorded as the ground-truth residual target (∆a_gt).
- **Training the Residual Corrector:** The FGRC is trained separately with a balanced batch containing both intervention segments (where ∆a_gt is non-zero and the gate label y_t is 1) and non-intervention policy rollouts (where ∆a_gt is zero and y_t is 0). The loss function combines a mean squared error for residual regression and a Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss for gate classification, balanced by λ_gate. This encourages the model to apply corrections only when necessary and to learn from human interventions.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

Experiments are conducted on a Franka Research 3 (FR3) 7-DoF robotic arm, equipped with an ATI Axia80-M8 6-axis wrist force/torque sensor. Visual observations are captured by three Intel RealSense D435i cameras. Four contact-rich tasks (Erase Whiteboard, Peel Cucumber, Pivot Box, Wipe Vase) are used, with controlled environmental variations to enhance data diversity. The base action model is trained on 90 demonstrations per task, while the residual corrector uses an additional 20 human-intervention episodes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of FAWAM demonstrates its effectiveness in improving robotic manipulation performance in contact-rich tasks.

- **Overall Performance Enhancement:** FAWAM achieved an average success rate of 85% across four diverse contact-rich tasks (Erase Whiteboard, Peel Cucumber, Pivot Box, Wipe Vase). This represents a 36.25% improvement over vision-only baselines (π_0.5 and GE-Act), which achieved an average success rate below 50% (47.50% and 48.75% respectively). Furthermore, FAWAM improved upon existing force-aware baselines (ForceVLA and TA-VLA) by 21.25%, which reached an average success rate of 63.75%.

- **Impact of Core Components:**
 - **Force-Envisioned Action Model (FAWAM w/o Res):** Even without the online residual correction, the base FAWAM policy (which includes force-conditioned action generation and joint action-force prediction) achieved an average success rate of 73.75%. This indicates that the proposed force-aware perception and prediction mechanisms provide a strong foundational policy.
 - **Force-Guided Residual Corrector:** The addition of the residual corrector further enhanced robustness, leading to the full model's 85% success rate. This demonstrates the corrector's ability to compensate for contact deviations during execution and improve overall task completion reliability.

- **Ablation Study (Table 2):**
 - **Force-Conditioned Observation (Obs):** Simply adding wrench histories as an observation yielded a 7.5% improvement over the vision-only baseline, reaching 56.25%. This suggests that passive force conditioning is only partially effective.
 - **Joint Action-Force Prediction (Pre):** Building on force-conditioned observations, the inclusion of joint action-force prediction (Obs + Pre) resulted in a substantial additional 17.5% improvement, bringing the success rate to 73.75%. This indicates that explicitly modeling future wrench evolution alongside actions significantly contributes to learning physically grounded representations.
 - **Residual Correction (Res-only):** When residual correction was applied without the benefit of predicted wrench references (only using sensed force feedback), it still improved performance by 18.75% over the baseline (to 67.50%). However, this was lower than the performance of the full model, highlighting the complementary role of both prediction and correction.
 - **Full FAWAM (Obs + Pre + Res):** The combined contributions of force-conditioned observation, joint action-force prediction, and force-guided residual correction were shown to be synergistic, achieving the highest average success rate of 85%.

- **Execution Time Analysis (Figure 4):** The full FAWAM model demonstrated faster task completion times compared to all ablated variants. This suggests that the force-guided residual correction mechanism not only improves success rates but also leads to more efficient execution by enabling timely adjustments based on sensed and predicted forces, thereby reducing inefficient recovery behaviors.

- **Robustness to Execution-Time Perturbations (Table 3, Figure 5):**
 - To assess the resilience of the correction model, tasks were subjected to unexpected manual perturbations (e.g., tilting the board, changing cucumber height, moving a box, changing vase height).
 - Without the correction model, the policy exhibited limited adaptability, often failing to recover from contact changes, leading to failures like jamming or toppling.
 - With the force-guided correction model, FAWAM consistently improved robustness under these perturbations, successfully recovering stable contact and completing tasks in a higher proportion of trials. This provides evidence that the correction model effectively closes the loop by translating discrepancies between predicted and sensed forces into corrective actions.

- **Residual Activation Threshold (Figure 15, Appendix A.6):** An analysis of the residual activation threshold (τ) showed that an overly low threshold led to decreased success rates due to unnecessary perturbations of nominal execution. Conversely, too high a threshold caused the model to become overly conservative, failing to trigger necessary corrections. An optimal threshold of τ = 0.8 was found to balance selectivity and responsiveness, maximizing performance by selectively applying corrections when significant deviations were detected.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FAWAM framework represents a notable advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring intricate physical interaction. Its significance and potential impact stem from its systematic approach to force integration and its demonstrated ability to enhance robustness and success rates.

- **Elevated Robustness in Contact-Rich Manipulation:** The most direct impact of FAWAM is its ability to significantly improve the reliability of robots performing contact-rich tasks. By achieving an 85% average success rate, FAWAM substantially surpasses both vision-only and existing force-aware methods. This increase in robustness is crucial for deploying robots in real-world scenarios where precise and adaptive physical interaction is necessary, such as domestic assistance, industrial assembly, medical procedures, or handling deformable objects.

- **Novel Paradigm for Force Utilization:** FAWAM moves beyond treating force as a passive observation modality. Its integration of force at three levels—perception, predictive modeling of future interaction dynamics, and closed-loop execution-time correction—introduces a more sophisticated and proactive paradigm for utilizing force information. This shift from purely reactive to a predictive-corrective cycle offers a blueprint for future research in physically grounded robot control.

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Adaptability:** By enabling robots to anticipate contact outcomes and dynamically correct actions based on real-time force feedback, FAWAM contributes to greater robot autonomy. This reduces the robot's susceptibility to uncertainties, external disturbances, and minor execution errors that often plague open-loop or less adaptive systems. The demonstrated ability to recover from unexpected perturbations highlights its potential for more resilient and adaptable robotic systems in unstructured environments.

- **Advancement of World Models for Physical Interaction:** The work extends the utility of world action models by explicitly incorporating force dynamics. While world models traditionally focus on visual or latent state prediction, FAWAM demonstrates the benefit of joint action-force prediction, grounding the predicted motions in their physical consequences. This integration enriches the representational power of world models, making them more suitable for tasks requiring physical understanding.

- **Improved Efficiency and Reduced Failure Modes:** The finding that FAWAM not only improves success rates but also reduces execution times indicates that its corrective mechanisms lead to smoother, more efficient task completion. By preventing small errors from escalating into full failures, the system avoids costly recovery behaviors or task restarts, contributing to overall operational efficiency.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies several limitations that also point towards promising avenues for future work. These include integrating force into video generation models (currently constrained by a lack of large-scale, synchronized force/video datasets), expanding the diversity of human intervention data for the residual corrector to cover more failure modes, and exploring more direct and generalized contact-state representations (e.g., local contact frames, dense tactile signals) beyond end-effector wrenches. Addressing these limitations could lead to even more capable and generalized contact-rich manipulation systems.

In summary, FAWAM provides a compelling demonstration of how a systematic, multi-level integration of force sensing, prediction, and online correction can significantly enhance the capabilities of robots in complex interaction tasks, paving the way for more robust, autonomous, and physically intelligent robotic systems.
