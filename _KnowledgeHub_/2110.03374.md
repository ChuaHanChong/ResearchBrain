# Model Adaptation: Historical Contrastive Learning for Unsupervised Domain Adaptation without Source Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Historical Contrastive Learning (HCL) offers a method for Unsupervised Model Adaptation (UMA), enabling deep neural networks to adapt to new target domains without requiring access to original source data. This approach leverages the model's own historical states to preserve source knowledge and improve target discrimination, achieving state-of-the-art performance across diverse computer vision tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) methods commonly assume access to source-domain data during adaptation, which is often infeasible due to data privacy, portability, or intellectual property concerns.
- Unsupervised Model Adaptation (UMA) aims to adapt a pre-trained source model to an unlabeled target domain without source data, presenting the challenge of adapting effectively without direct supervision or explicit source feature alignment.
- The absence of source data in UMA makes it difficult to prevent catastrophic forgetting of source knowledge while simultaneously learning to fit the target data distribution.

## Method

- The ==Historical Contrastive Learning (HCL)== framework is introduced, leveraging historical versions of the adapting model to compensate for the absence of source data by implicitly preserving source knowledge and guiding adaptation.
- ==Historical Contrastive Instance Discrimination (HCID)== learns instance-discriminative target representations by contrasting current features with reliability-weighted historical embeddings derived from augmented views of the same instance.
- ==Historical Contrastive Category Discrimination (HCCD)== refines category boundaries using ==pseudo-labels==, adaptively weighted by prediction consistency between the current model and historical models for greater robustness.

## Results

- HCL consistently achieved state-of-the-art performance in Unsupervised Model Adaptation across various tasks, including semantic segmentation (GTA5 → Cityscapes, SYNTHIA → Cityscapes), object detection (Cityscapes → Foggy Cityscapes), and image classification (VisDA17, Office-31).
- The method demonstrated performance comparable to, and in some cases surpassed, state-of-the-art UDA methods that do have access to labeled source data during training.
- Ablation studies confirmed the complementary contributions of both HCID and HCCD components, and HCL consistently boosted the performance of existing UMA methods when incorporated.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging historical information from the evolving model itself can serve as a robust proxy for unavailable source data, guiding adaptation in source-free domain adaptation settings.
> - Combining instance-level contrastive learning with category-level self-training, both regularized by historical model states, provides complementary supervision for robust and comprehensive model adaptation.
> - Introducing reliability and consistency weighting based on historical predictions helps to prioritize well-learned knowledge and prevent the propagation of uncertain information during adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical challenge in the field of machine learning, specifically within **domain adaptation**. Deep neural networks, while powerful, often suffer from performance degradation when deployed on data from a different distribution than their training data (known as domain shift or inter-domain discrepancy). **Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA)** is a subfield that aims to mitigate this by adapting a model trained on a labeled "source" domain to an unlabeled "target" domain, typically by aligning their data distributions.

However, UDA commonly assumes access to the original source-domain data during the adaptation process. This assumption often presents practical hurdles in real-world scenarios:
- **Data Privacy**: Source data might contain sensitive information (e.g., medical records, personal images), making it impossible to share or transfer.
- **Data Portability**: Large datasets require significant storage and bandwidth, making their transfer inefficient or infeasible.
- **Intellectual Property**: Source data might be proprietary, preventing its redistribution.

This paper focuses on an increasingly important and challenging variant: **Unsupervised Model Adaptation (UMA)**, also referred to as **Unsupervised Domain Adaptation without Source Data** (or Source-Free Domain Adaptation, SFDA). In UMA, only the pre-trained source model is available; the original source data is explicitly *unavailable* during the adaptation to the target domain. This significantly complicates the adaptation process, as the model lacks direct supervision from labeled source examples and cannot directly align features with them.

Traditional UDA approaches fall into categories like:
- **Adversarial Training**: Using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) or domain discriminators to align source and target feature spaces.
- **Self-training**: Iteratively pseudo-labeling target samples and retraining the model.
- **Image Translation**: Modifying image styles to reduce domain gaps.

Recent UMA efforts have explored various strategies to compensate for the missing source data, such as:
- **Information Maximization**: Freezing the classifier and maximizing information on target data.
- **Conditional GANs**: Generating target-like images with source-alike semantics.
- **Self-Entropy Descent**: Reducing uncertainty in target predictions.
- **Data-free Knowledge Distillation**: Transferring source knowledge without access to the actual source data.

This work distinguishes itself by proposing a novel approach based on **memory-based learning** and **contrastive learning**. While memory networks and temporal ensembling (like Mean Teacher) have been explored in semi-supervised learning and even some UDA settings, this paper claims to be the first to explore a **memory mechanism specifically for UMA**. By leveraging "historical source hypothesis" (i.e., information from previous states of the adapting model), HCL aims to implicitly preserve source-domain knowledge, which is crucial for preventing catastrophic forgetting and guiding adaptation in the absence of explicit source data. This places HCL at the forefront of tackling the source-free domain adaptation problem, offering a unique perspective compared to prior generative or self-training based UMA methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective and robust method for **Unsupervised Model Adaptation (UMA)**. Specifically, the authors aim to adapt deep neural networks, initially trained on a labeled source domain, to perform well on an unlabeled target domain, **without requiring access to the original source-domain data** during the adaptation process.

The core motivation stems from the significant practical limitations of traditional Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) methods, which necessitate access to source data. As detailed previously, concerns regarding data privacy, portability (especially for large datasets), and transmission efficiency make UMA a highly desirable, albeit more challenging, setting for real-world deployment. The absence of labeled source data in UMA makes the adaptation task considerably more difficult, as the model loses the direct ground truth supervision and an explicit reference for aligning target features. This often leads to model collapse or suboptimal performance.

To overcome this challenge, the paper proposes to "make up for the absence of source data by adapting the source-trained model to fit target data distribution without forgetting source hypothesis." The key innovation is the use of **historical information** from the evolving model itself as a proxy for the unavailable source data. The specific objectives for their proposed Historical Contrastive Learning (HCL) technique are:

1. **Preserving Source Hypothesis**: Ensure that the model, while adapting to the target domain, does not forget the knowledge acquired from the source domain.
2. **Learning Instance-Discriminative Target Representations**: Encourage the model to distinguish individual target instances effectively, which is known to improve generalization.
3. **Learning Category-Discriminative Target Representations**: Enable the model to classify target samples accurately into their respective categories, aligning with the downstream task objective.
4. **Robustness and Generalization**: Develop a method that performs consistently and effectively across various visual tasks (e.g., semantic segmentation, object detection, image classification) and different adaptation setups (e.g., partial-set, open-set DA).

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to provide a practical and high-performing solution for adapting models in scenarios where source data is sensitive or inaccessible.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed solution is **Historical Contrastive Learning (HCL)**, which leverages historical versions of the adapting model to compensate for the absence of source data. HCL consists of two complementary components: Historical Contrastive Instance Discrimination (HCID) and Historical Contrastive Category Discrimination (HCCD).

**Overall Idea:**
HCL treats the current model's outputs (features and predictions) as "queries" and the outputs from historical models (previous iterations or snapshots of the adapting model) as "keys." This allows the model to learn from its own evolution, preserving learned knowledge and guiding adaptation towards the target domain without external source supervision.

#### 3.1. Historical Contrastive Instance Discrimination (HCID)

HCID operates at the instance level, aiming to learn discriminative representations for individual target samples. It adapts the principles of contrastive learning to the UMA setting:

- **Mechanism**: For a given target query sample `x_q`, two augmented views (`x'` and `x'''`) are created. The current model `E_t` (encoder) processes `x'` to generate a "query" embedding `q_t`. Simultaneously, historical encoders `E_{t-m}` (previous versions of the encoder) process augmented views (`x'''` or other samples) to generate "key" embeddings `k_{t-m}`.
- **Contrastive Loss (L_HisNCE)**: The goal is to pull the query `q_t` closer to its positive key (an augmented view of the same instance generated by a historical model) and push it away from negative keys (embeddings of other instances or different augmented views).
- **Reliability Weighting**: A crucial innovation in HCID is the introduction of a reliability score `r_{t-m}` for each key. This score is estimated using the classification entropy of the historical model's prediction for that key. This mechanism ensures that the model primarily "memorizes" and learns from well-learnt (low entropy, high confidence) historical embeddings, thus preventing the propagation of uncertain or poorly learned information.
- **Source Hypothesis Preservation**: By contrasting current embeddings with those from historical models (which implicitly retain source-domain knowledge), HCID encourages the current model to learn instance-discriminative target representations while still respecting and preserving the source hypothesis. The paper states that InfoNCE (a standard contrastive loss) can be seen as a special case of HisNCE when historical models are not used, and reliability is constant.

#### 3.2. Historical Contrastive Category Discrimination (HCCD)

HCCD operates at the category level, focusing on learning category-discriminative representations aligned with the objective of visual recognition tasks. It adapts the self-training paradigm for UMA:

- **Pseudo Label Generation**: For an unlabeled target sample `x`, the current model `G_t` (encoder + classifier) generates a prediction `p_t`. A pseudo-label `ŷ` is then derived from `p_t` using a pseudo-label generation function (e.g., taking the class with the highest probability).
- **Historical Consistency (h_con)**: This is the novel aspect of HCCD. It measures the consistency between the current model's prediction `p_t` and historical models' predictions `p_{t-m}` for the same target sample. Specifically, `h_con` is computed as `1 - Sigmoid(||p_t - p_{t-m}||_1)`, where `||.||_1` is the L1 norm. High consistency (small difference) indicates a more reliable pseudo-label.
- **Weighted Self-Training Loss (L_HisST)**: A standard cross-entropy loss is applied using the generated pseudo-labels `ŷ`. However, this loss is adaptively re-weighted by the `h_con` score. This means that samples for which the current and historical models show high prediction consistency are given more weight during self-training, making the learning more robust to noisy pseudo-labels and reinforcing confident predictions.
- **Complementary Role**: HCCD focuses on forming clear class boundaries in the target domain, crucial for downstream classification tasks, while being regularized by the consistency with historical knowledge.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Insights

The paper provides theoretical propositions (detailed in the appendix) suggesting that both HCID and HCCD can be modeled as maximum likelihood problems and are convergent under certain conditions. This provides a theoretical underpinning for the proposed learning mechanisms.

In summary, HCL synergistically combines instance-level contrastive learning (HCID) with category-level self-training (HCCD). HCID ensures that target features are highly discriminative at an individual instance level, which is beneficial for generalization. HCCD refines category boundaries using pseudo-labels, but critically, it uses historical model consistency to weigh these pseudo-labels, ensuring that adaptation is guided by stable and reliable knowledge from past iterations, thereby implicitly retaining the source hypothesis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments across a variety of visual tasks and setups to evaluate the effectiveness of Historical Contrastive Learning (HCL).

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Semantic Segmentation**: Tasks included GTA5 → Cityscapes and SYNTHIA → Cityscapes, using DeepLab-V2 as the segmentation model.
- **Object Detection**: Tasks included Cityscapes → Foggy Cityscapes and Cityscapes → BDD100k, using Faster R-CNN as the detection model.
- **Image Classification**: Tasks included VisDA17 and Office-31 (six adaptation tasks), using ResNet-101/ResNet-50 as classifiers.
- **Learning Setups**: Evaluated not only standard UMA but also partial-set and open-set domain adaptation scenarios on Office-Home.

#### 4.2. Key Results

1. **Consistent State-of-the-Art Performance in UMA**:
 - **Semantic Segmentation (Tables 2 & 3)**: HCL consistently outperformed state-of-the-art UMA methods (e.g., UR, SFDA) by significant margins in mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) for both GTA5 → Cityscapes and SYNTHIA → Cityscapes.
 - **Object Detection (Tables 4 & 5)**: HCL showed clear improvements over existing UMA methods (e.g., SFOD) in mean Average Precision (mAP) on Cityscapes → Foggy Cityscapes and Cityscapes → BDD100k.
 - **Image Classification (Tables 6 & 7)**: HCL demonstrated superior accuracy compared to other UMA methods (e.g., 3C-GAN, SHOT) across VisDA17 and all Office-31 adaptation tasks.

2. **Competitiveness with UDA Methods**: Notably, HCL often achieved performance comparable to, and sometimes even surpassed, state-of-the-art UDA methods (labeled "SF" for source-data free methods, which still access source data) that *do* have access to labeled source data during training. This highlights the effectiveness of HCL in recovering source knowledge without direct access.

3. **Complementarity with Existing UMA Methods**: When HCL was incorporated into existing UMA methods (e.g., "UR+HCL", "SFDA+HCL", "3C-GAN+HCL", "SHOT+HCL"), it consistently boosted their performance across various tasks (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8). This indicates that HCL provides unique and beneficial supervision signals that complement other UMA strategies.

4. **Ablation Studies (Table 2, bottom)**:
 - Both HCID and HCCD individually showed strong performance, contributing significantly to the overall model adaptation.
 - The combination of HCID and HCCD in HCL yielded the best results, confirming their complementary nature. HCID's instance-level discrimination and HCCD's category-level discrimination provide orthogonal supervision, leading to better overall representations.

5. **Generalization Across Tasks and Setups**:
 - The consistent performance across semantic segmentation, object detection, and image classification tasks demonstrated the generalizability of HCL.
 - Experiments on partial-set and open-set domain adaptation (Table 8) further validated HCL's ability to adapt to more complex and realistic transfer learning scenarios, achieving competitive results.

6. **Qualitative Results and Feature Visualization**:
 - **t-SNE visualization (Figure 3)** on GTA → Cityscapes showed that HCL learned more instance-discriminative and category-discriminative representations compared to UR and SFDA. This means that features belonging to the same category are clustered more tightly, while different categories are clearly separated.
 - **Qualitative segmentation examples (Figure 4)** illustrated that HCL produced visually superior segmentation masks, more accurately capturing object boundaries and semantic regions (e.g., sidewalks, roads, sky), outperforming UR and SFDA.

In essence, the findings robustly support the claim that HCL is a highly effective and versatile technique for Unsupervised Model Adaptation, successfully addressing the challenge of source data absence through its novel historical contrastive learning mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research on Historical Contrastive Learning (HCL) for Unsupervised Model Adaptation (UMA) carries significant implications for both academic research and practical applications in AI.

1. **Addresses Critical Real-World Constraints**: The most impactful aspect of this work is its direct assault on the fundamental limitations of traditional domain adaptation concerning data privacy, portability, and intellectual property. By enabling model adaptation without requiring access to source data, HCL removes a major bottleneck for deploying AI systems in sensitive or distributed environments. This is crucial for applications in healthcare (patient data privacy), finance, industrial automation (proprietary manufacturing data), and edge computing (limited bandwidth).

2. **Pioneering Memory-Based Learning for UMA**: The paper explicitly states that it is the "first work that explores memory-based learning for the task of UMA." This represents a novel paradigm shift, moving beyond generative or self-training methods by leveraging the model's own historical states as a rich source of information. This opens up new avenues for research into how memory and temporal consistency can be further exploited in source-free transfer learning scenarios.

3. **Robustness and Generalizability**: The consistent state-of-the-art performance across diverse and challenging computer vision tasks (semantic segmentation, object detection, image classification) and complex adaptation setups (partial-set, open-set DA) highlights the robustness and broad applicability of HCL. This suggests that the proposed method is not merely a task-specific hack but a generalizable technique for adapting deep neural networks.

4. **Enhanced Model Interpretability and Reliability**: The introduction of "reliability" in HCID and "historical consistency" in HCCD not only improves performance but also adds a layer of robustness. By prioritizing well-learned and consistent knowledge from historical models, HCL implicitly guides the adaptation process more reliably, potentially leading to more stable and trustworthy adapted models, especially in critical applications.

5. **Complementary to Existing Methods**: The finding that HCL can consistently boost the performance of other UMA methods ("+HCL" results) is highly significant. It implies that HCL can serve as a powerful add-on or module within existing UMA pipelines, rather than being a standalone replacement. This enhances the practical utility of HCL, allowing researchers and practitioners to easily integrate it to improve their current UMA solutions.

6. **Foundation for Future Research**: By demonstrating the efficacy of historical context in UMA, this work lays a strong foundation for future research. It encourages exploration into more sophisticated memory mechanisms, different ways of defining and utilizing "historical hypothesis," and how this principle can be extended to other transfer learning problems beyond UMA, such as continual learning or few-shot learning where data scarcity or privacy are concerns.

In conclusion, "Model Adaptation: Historical Contrastive Learning" presents a significant advancement in unsupervised model adaptation. By ingeniously leveraging the model's own history to compensate for the absence of source data, HCL addresses critical practical challenges, establishes a new research direction, and offers a robust, generalizable solution with considerable potential for real-world impact.
