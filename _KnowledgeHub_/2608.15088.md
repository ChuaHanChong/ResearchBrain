# Max-Q Selective Imitation for Human-in-the-Loop Online Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Max-Q Selective Imitation for human-in-the-loop robot RL: an MC Q-chunk critic regresses chunk-level Q onto Monte Carlo returns from the replay buffer, and the actor imitates the hard winner between policy action and buffer action by Q value. On real USB pick-and-insertion, reaches 99% success in 30 minutes vs HIL-SERL's 5 hours.

## Problem

- Behavioral cloning of interventions stays conservative and never surpasses demonstration-level performance under limited robot interaction budgets.
- Offline extractors like IQL/AWR always imitate buffer actions, so learning targets never converge to the live policy even as it improves, leaving a policy-target gap.
- SAC-style HIL systems bootstrap TD backups under the current policy rather than sample-average buffer behavior, under-utilizing intervention returns and slowing convergence.

## Method

- **MC Q-chunk critic**: regresses chunk-level action values onto Monte Carlo returns from logged trajectories instead of TD bootstrapping, crediting intervention paths directly as sample-average behavior evaluation.
- **Max-Q selective imitation**: actor regresses toward the ==hard winner (arg max Q)== between the current policy action and a sampled buffer action at each state, with stop-gradient isolating critic from actor.
- ==Critic ensemble mean scoring==: averages M independently initialized critics as a variance-reduction trick to stabilize the hard winner-take-all switching signal without softening targets.

## Results

- On real USB pick-and-insertion with 20 demonstrations, ACT QChunk-MCBC reaches **99%** success within **30 minutes** versus HIL-SERL's roughly 5 hours to converge to 100%.
- In simulation on Peg Insertion, ACT QChunk-MCBC hits **99%** success at 4,386 steps (0.32h) while HIL-SERL needs 100,000 steps (3.19h) for 100%; EXPO saturates at only **30%**.
- On Square task, Flow QChunk-MCBC reaches **96%** by 2,200 steps (0.60h) and ACT QChunk-MCBC **99%** by 1,901 steps (0.38h), while EXPO and E2HiL plateau near **10%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hard winner-take-all rule between policy and buffer actions gives a label-free mechanism that automatically shifts from imitation learning to on-policy self-improvement as competence grows.
> - Soft-mixing or score-gap thresholds between policy and buffer targets can silently reintroduce the policy-target distribution gap that a hard switch is designed to eliminate.
> - Sample-average Monte Carlo value evaluation credits off-policy intervention data more directly than current-policy TD bootstrapping, which is generalizable beyond this specific chunking architecture.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Real-world robot reinforcement learning (RL) faces several challenges, including high interaction costs, potential failure modes in contact-rich tasks, and limitations in safe exploration. Human-in-the-loop (HIL) protocols are designed to address these constraints by integrating autonomous robot rollouts with human interventions. This approach typically generates a replay buffer containing both near-policy transitions from autonomous operation and corrective actions provided by humans, which may deviate significantly from the current policy.

Existing methodologies for HIL online robot learning demonstrate limitations in fully meeting the requirements of this setting. Behavioral cloning, while stable, tends to be conservative and may not facilitate learning beyond the demonstrated performance. Unconstrained maximization of Q-values over continuous action spaces can lead to overestimation in out-of-distribution (OOD) regions where the Q-function's reliability is compromised. Offline RL techniques, such as Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) with Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR), aim to keep updates within the data support. However, in an online HIL fine-tuning context, AWR's continuous targeting of buffer actions can cause the policy to oscillate between heterogeneous sample modes rather than converging towards the live policy. This can result in a persistent gap between the policy distribution and the target-sample distribution, leading to tracking errors during closed-loop execution as the robot encounters state-action regions not adequately represented by the training targets.

Conversely, Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)-style HIL systems, exemplified by HIL-SERL, maintain closer alignment with the policy distribution. Nevertheless, their reliance on Temporal Difference (TD) backups that bootstrap under the current policy (π) rather than the sample-average behavior present in the replay buffer can under-utilize the valuable returns from human intervention trajectories. This work positions itself within this landscape by seeking to develop a method that overcomes these specific limitations, enabling both rapid absorption of human corrections and continuous improvement beyond initial demonstration or intervention levels, while mitigating the policy-target gap.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective training method for Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) online reinforcement learning for real robots. This method aims to enable robots to quickly integrate useful human interventions while simultaneously facilitating continued improvement beyond the performance level provided by demonstrations or interventions.

The motivations for this work stem from identified limitations in current HIL online robot learning approaches:

- **Inefficient Use of Human Interventions:** Current TD-based critics in SAC-style HIL systems (e.g., HIL-SERL) bootstrap Q-values under the current policy (π). This approach can dilute the signal from human interventions because the value estimates are not directly derived from the actual executed behavior, which includes the human corrections. The authors aim to enhance the utilization of intervention returns by evaluating them in a sample-average manner.
- **Policy-Target-Sample Gap:** Methods that solely imitate buffer actions (e.g., AWR in offline RL) continue to pull the policy towards potentially stale or diverse buffer samples, even after the autonomous policy has improved. This creates a discrepancy between the policy distribution used for execution and the target-sample distribution used for training, leading to potential out-of-distribution performance issues and compounding tracking errors during real-world robot operation. A key motivation is to close this gap, ensuring that the learning targets align with the evolving, live policy when it is superior.
- **Balancing Exploration and Exploitation with Safety:** In real-world robot learning, unrestricted exploration is often unsafe or impractical. HIL provides corrective actions, but the learning system must judiciously decide when to follow these interventions and when to pursue self-improvement. The authors are motivated to develop a mechanism that automatically and safely switches between learning from human corrections and on-policy self-improvement without explicit labeling or hand-tuned thresholds.
- **Overestimation and OOD Issues:** Unconstrained maximization of Q(s,a) in continuous action spaces can lead to chasing high Q-values in out-of-distribution regions where the Q-function is unreliable. The goal is to constrain policy improvement to actions that are either within the existing data support or generated by the current policy, thereby maintaining reliable Q-value estimates.
- **Rapid Skill Acquisition and Efficiency:** Real-world robot interaction is costly. The research is motivated by the need to achieve high task success efficiently, within limited robot interaction budgets, particularly for contact-rich manipulation tasks that often require many trials to master.

In summary, the work seeks to build a HIL robot learning system that is sample-efficient, robust to varied data sources, and capable of stable, continuous improvement, addressing the limitations of prior work by fundamentally redesigning the critic evaluation and actor update mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a two-component training method for HIL online robot learning: an MC Q-chunk critic and max-Q selective imitation for the actor. The overall process operates within a standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) setup (S, A, P, r, γ) under a HIL online protocol. The replay buffer, B, is initialized with a small set of demonstrations and is continuously expanded by both autonomous robot rollouts and human interventions. A crucial aspect is that the method does not require explicit labels for intervention data; all transitions are treated uniformly. The overarching objective is to achieve high task success within a constrained robot interaction budget.

#### 3.1. MC Q-Chunk Critic

The critic component, denoted Qθ(st, at:t+h-1), is designed to evaluate action chunks rather than individual actions. An action chunk at:t+h-1 represents a sequence of actions (at..., at+h-1) over a predefined chunk horizon, h. Instead of using Temporal Difference (TD) bootstrapping, which estimates values based on subsequent states and actions sampled from the *current* policy, this critic is trained to regress directly onto the Monte Carlo (MC) return. The MC return, Gt = ∑(k=0 to Tt-1) γ^k * rt+k, is calculated along the complete logged trajectory from the replay buffer.

The critic's loss function is defined as:
L_Q = E(st,at:t+h-1,Gt)∼B [Qθ(st, at:t+h-1) − Gt]²

This approach ensures that Qθ estimates values under the *sample-average behavior policy* that generated the data in the buffer, which includes a mixture of autonomous and human intervention actions. By crediting intervention paths directly through their full returns, this method aims to enhance the visibility and utilization of informative human corrections, rather than diluting their impact through current-policy-bootstrapped TD backups. Chunk-level scoring is also intended to align with the temporal structure often present in human demonstrations and interventions.

An implementation detail is the optional use of a critic ensemble. To mitigate noise in early Q-value estimates, which can negatively affect hard switching decisions, the ranking score for actions is optionally computed as the mean of M independently initialized critics trained on the same MC objective: Q̄(s, a) = (1/M) ∑(i=1 to M) Qθ_i(s, a). This ensemble averaging is presented as a variance-reduction technique rather than a core methodological contribution.

#### 3.2. Max-Q Selective Imitation

The actor, πϕ(s), is updated through a mechanism called max-Q selective imitation. This approach addresses the limitations of pure buffer imitation (which cannot prioritize an improving policy over stale data) and unconstrained Q-maximization (which risks querying Q in unreliable OOD regions).

For a sampled state s, the method compares two distinct action candidates:
1. **Policy Action (aπ):** The action proposed by the current autonomous policy, aπ = πϕ(s).
2. **Buffer Action (ab):** An action sampled from the replay buffer conditioned on the state, ab ∼ B(·|s).

The actor then identifies the action candidate with the higher estimated Q-value, designated as a⋆:
a⋆ = arg max(a∈{aπ,ab}) Qθ(s, a)

The actor is subsequently updated by minimizing a supervised regression loss, moving the policy towards this selected winner:
Lπ = Es∼B [∥πϕ(s) − a⋆∥²]

A critical aspect is that the target a⋆ remains a single, discrete action; the method explicitly avoids mixing aπ and ab with soft weights or gating the switch with hand-tuned score margins. This "hard winner-take-all" rule is designed to prevent the policy from being continuously pulled towards buffer actions even after it has become stronger, which would otherwise reintroduce the policy-target-sample gap. Stop-gradient is applied through a⋆ and the Q comparison to ensure that the actor objective does not inadvertently distort the critic's values.

This design has three operational consequences:
- **Limited OOD Q Queries:** All scored actions (aπ and ab) are within the union of the policy and data support, thereby limiting Q-value evaluations to regions where the critic is expected to be more reliable.
- **Directed Improvement Signal:** The hard max provides a clear, directed signal for policy improvement, guiding the actor solely towards the currently higher-Q candidate.
- **Label-Free Switching:** This mechanism facilitates an automatic, label-free switch between learning from human interventions and on-policy self-improvement. When human interventions or past buffer actions have a higher Q-value, a⋆ will be a buffer action, and the policy will imitate it. As the autonomous policy improves and its actions achieve higher Q-values, aπ will win more often, causing Lπ to regress the policy onto itself. This effectively aligns the target-sample distribution with the policy distribution, consequently reducing the closed-loop exposure to underrepresented regions during execution.

During online interaction, the robot continues to execute (potentially noisy) actions sampled from π, allowing for autonomous exploration when self-generated actions prove superior.

#### 3.3. Training Procedure

The training procedure integrates these components in an iterative loop:
1. Initialize the replay buffer B with demonstrations and initialize the critic Qθ (potentially an ensemble) and actor πϕ.
2. In each online interaction step:
 a. Execute an action a ∼ πϕ (with exploration); allow for human intervention.
 b. Store the resulting transition/chunk in B; compute the Monte Carlo return Gt for completed trajectories.
 c. Sample a minibatch from B.
 d. Update the critic Qθ using MC regression onto Gt.
 e. Determine a⋆ by comparing aπ and ab using Qθ values, and update the actor πϕ by imitating a⋆.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed method, appearing as ACT QChunk-MCBC and Flow QChunk-MCBC (differing in action chunking head), against several baselines: HIL-SERL, plain MCBC (no Q-chunk actor), EXPO, and E2HiL. Experiments were conducted in simulation on Peg Insertion and Square tasks, and on a real-robot USB pick-and-insertion task. Success was measured by autonomous evaluation episodes at specific checkpoints, with curves truncated at each method’s peak performance.

#### 4.1. Peg Insertion (Simulation)

On the Peg Insertion task, the proposed Q-chunk variants demonstrated superior time-to-success:
- **ACT QChunk-MCBC:** Achieved 99% success within 0.32 hours (approximately 4,386 training steps) after the first checkpoint.
- **Flow QChunk-MCBC:** Reached 96% success within 0.50 hours (approximately 5,075 training steps).
- **Plain MCBC:** Also reached 96% success within 0.67 hours (approximately 10,112 training steps), indicating the benefit of selective imitation.
- **HIL-SERL:** Eventually reached 100% success but required a longer training duration of approximately 3.19 hours (100,000 steps). This reflects slower integration of intervention information due to its TD-based evaluation.
- **E2HiL:** Peaked at 90% success after approximately 2.36 hours.
- **EXPO:** Saturated at 30% success, not recovering within the logged timeframe.

These results indicate that the MC Q-chunk critic combined with max-Q selective imitation delivers a more efficient success-time trade-off on this task.

#### 4.2. Square (Simulation)

The Square task replicated the performance trends observed in Peg Insertion:
- **ACT QChunk-MCBC:** Achieved 99% success within 0.38 hours (approximately 1,901 training steps).
- **Flow QChunk-MCBC:** Reached 96% success within 0.60 hours (approximately 2,200 training steps).
- **Plain MCBC:** Achieved 96% success within 0.80 hours (approximately 4,384 training steps).
- **HIL-SERL:** Reached 100% success after a significantly longer period of approximately 3.85 hours (43,350 steps).
- **EXPO and E2HiL:** Remained near failure, peaking at 10% success.

The consistent outperformance on both simulation tasks suggests that the MC Q-chunk and max-Q selective imitation stack is effective in terms of sample and time efficiency for contact-rich assembly tasks.

#### 4.3. Real-robot USB Insertion

A real-robot USB pick-and-insertion task with a Franka-class arm and a wrist camera was used to validate the method. Training involved 20 human demonstrations, autonomous rollouts, and human interventions.
- **ACT QChunk-MCBC:** Achieved 99% success within 30 minutes (0.5 hours) of robot training time.
- **Flow QChunk-MCBC:** Reached 96% success within approximately 0.8 hours.
- **Plain MCBC:** Reached 96% success within about 1 hour.
- **HIL-SERL:** Achieved ceiling success (100%) but required approximately 5 hours of HIL training.
- **E2HiL:** Peaked near 90% after several hours (3.7 hours).
- **EXPO:** Saturated at 30% success after 2.4 hours.

The real-robot experiments confirmed the simulation findings, demonstrating an order-of-magnitude reduction in training time for the proposed methods compared to TD-based HIL baselines.

#### 4.4. Ablations (Planned)

The paper outlines planned ablation studies to further analyze the contributions of each component:
- Comparison of MC Q-chunk with π-bootstrapped TD critics.
- Evaluation of various actor variants, including full buffer imitation, SAC-style actors, unconstrained Q-maximization, and online IQL/AWR.
- Analysis of the frequency at which the policy action (aπ) wins over buffer actions (ab) during training.
- Win rates stratified by intervention versus autonomous buffer actions (for analysis only).
- Sensitivity analysis regarding demonstration count and intervention frequency.
- An optional check on the critic ensemble's effectiveness compared to a single critic.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method for Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) online robot learning that aims to significantly improve training efficiency and robustness for real-world robotic tasks. The key aspects of the proposed approach, the MC Q-chunk critic and max-Q selective imitation, offer several potential benefits:

- **Enhanced Sample and Time Efficiency:** The results across simulated and real-robot tasks demonstrate that the proposed method achieves high success rates in significantly less training time and with fewer environmental interactions compared to existing HIL baselines. For real-world robotics, where interaction costs are high and safety is paramount, this efficiency is a critical factor in enabling broader adoption and practical deployment. The order-of-magnitude reduction in training time observed on the USB insertion task highlights a practical advantage.

- **Effective Utilization of Human Interventions:** By employing an MC Q-chunk critic, the method directly credits the full Monte Carlo returns of intervention trajectories. This approach can lead to a more effective absorption of valuable human corrections, which are not diluted by bootstrapping under potentially suboptimal current policies. This has the potential to make HIL systems more responsive to human guidance and capable of learning from diverse expert demonstrations.

- **Mitigation of the Policy–Target-Sample Gap:** The max-Q selective imitation mechanism, with its hard winner-take-all rule, addresses the issue of the policy-target-sample gap. By allowing the learning target to shift from imitating buffer actions to aligning with the autonomous policy itself once the policy becomes stronger, it aims to reduce execution-time distribution shift. This can contribute to more stable and predictable robot behavior in closed-loop operation, as the robot is less exposed to state-action regions underrepresented by its training targets.

- **Automatic Switching Between Learning Modes:** The label-free, hard switching rule between imitating interventions and on-policy self-improvement provides an adaptive learning strategy. This automatic adjustment can simplify the HIL training process by removing the need for manual tuning or explicit labels for different types of data, potentially making the system more accessible and robust across various stages of learning.

- **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated on contact-rich manipulation tasks, the principles of using Monte Carlo returns for behavior evaluation and a selective imitation strategy could be applicable to other domains requiring efficient learning from mixed data sources, including tasks in fields beyond robotics.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as sensitivity to critic quality and variance of MC returns, provide avenues for future research to further refine and formalize the two-candidate improvement operator, explore adaptive chunk horizons, and conduct broader multi-task evaluations.

In conclusion, this work presents a methodological contribution to the field of robot reinforcement learning, particularly for HIL settings, by offering a system designed for rapid and stable acquisition of skills.
