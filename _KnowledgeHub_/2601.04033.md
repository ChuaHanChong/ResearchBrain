# Thinking with Frames: Generative Video Distortion Evaluation via Frame Reward Model

> [!summary] Summary
> A new frame-level reward model called REACT was developed to accurately evaluate structural distortions in generative videos, such as anatomical inaccuracies or implausible object interactions. It achieved 0.610 accuracy (with ties) in human preference alignment and an F1-score of 0.845 for distorted frame recognition, also demonstrating improved downstream video generation when used for post-training.

## Problem

- Existing video reward models fail to systematically evaluate structural distortions (e.g., deformed body parts, mesh penetration) in generative videos, often assigning high scores despite severe flaws.
- Current video-level evaluation methods, operating at low frame rates, miss localized and frame-specific structural artifacts.
- Direct application of image-based quality assessment models to videos is ineffective due to a significant domain gap, as video distortions manifest differently (e.g., blurriness from motion).

## Method

- Introduces REACT, a ==frame-level reward== model leveraging Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning to specifically identify and evaluate structural distortions.
- Employs a two-stage training framework: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with a masked loss mechanism for domain knowledge injection, followed by reinforcement learning (RL) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with a novel pairwise reward derived from BTT loss.
- Develops a dynamic frame sampling mechanism for inference, adaptively selecting frames most likely to exhibit distortions to optimize evaluation efficiency.

## Results

- REACT achieved significantly higher accuracy in human preference alignment on REACT-Video, obtaining **0.610** (with ties) and **0.813** (without ties), outperforming existing video evaluators by 20-**40%** relative improvement.
- Demonstrated superior performance in distortion recognition on REACT-Frame, achieving the highest F1-score of **0.845** for distorted frames and **0.671** for normal frames.
- Integrating REACT into video generation post-training paradigms (Best-of-N and Flow-DPO) improved visual fidelity and aesthetic quality metrics, and fusing REACT with existing reward models achieved the best overall performance in refining video generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fine-grained, frame-level analysis is crucial for accurately detecting localized structural distortions in generative videos, which are often missed by video-level and image-based evaluators.
> - Synthesizing high-quality Chain-of-Thought data using MLLMs like Gemini Pro significantly reduces manual annotation costs while enabling robust reasoning capabilities for distortion analysis.
> - A combined SFT and RL approach, particularly with masked SFT and pairwise preference rewards, is effective for training MLLMs to perform complex visual reasoning and align with human judgments on video distortion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in text-to-video (T2V) generation have been significantly propelled by video reward models, which guide generative models to enhance visual quality, motion dynamics, and text alignment through reinforcement learning strategies. Existing models such as VideoScore, VideoReward, and UnifiedReward primarily focus on these general aspects of video quality. However, a notable gap persists in the systematic evaluation of *structural distortions*, which encompass abnormalities in object appearances (e.g., incomplete, duplicated, or deformed body parts) and interactions (e.g., mesh penetration) within generative videos. Consequently, videos containing severe structural flaws can still receive high quality scores from current evaluation paradigms.

This research positions itself by addressing this specific limitation. The paper highlights that existing video-level evaluation methods, often operating at low frame rates, may miss localized, frame-specific structural artifacts. Furthermore, directly applying image-based quality assessment models to videos is challenged by a significant domain gap. Image artifacts tend to be sharp and well-defined, whereas video distortions often manifest as blurry or fragmented regions due to temporal inconsistencies and motion dynamics. This domain divergence limits the effectiveness of image evaluators when transferred to video content.

The presented work introduces a frame-level reward model to specifically target structural distortions, thereby complementing existing video reward systems. It leverages methodologies from large language models (LLMs) and multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs), particularly Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning and reinforcement learning techniques like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which have been instrumental in enhancing reasoning capabilities and aligning model outputs with human preferences in various visual understanding and generation tasks. By focusing on fine-grained, frame-level analysis of structural integrity, this research aims to provide a more comprehensive and accurate evaluation of generative video quality.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observation that while current text-to-video (T2V) generation models have made considerable progress in producing visually appealing and temporally consistent videos, they frequently exhibit significant structural distortions. These distortions, such as anatomical inaccuracies (e.g., deformed limbs, extra body parts) or implausible object interactions (e.g., mesh penetration), are often overlooked by existing video reward models. These models tend to assign high scores based on aesthetic appeal or overall motion fluidity, even when fundamental structural integrity is compromised. Furthermore, image-based quality assessment tools are not directly applicable due to a critical domain gap, as video-specific distortions present different characteristics (e.g., blurriness due to motion) compared to static image artifacts.

To address this deficiency, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Frame-Level Reward Model (REACT)**: To create a specialized reward model that operates at the frame level, designed to accurately identify and evaluate structural distortions in generative videos. This frame-level approach aims to overcome the limitations of video-level models (which might miss localized artifacts) and image-based models (which struggle with video-specific distortion characteristics).
2. **Provide Detailed Distortion Analysis**: To enable REACT to generate not only point-wise quality scores but also interpretable attribution labels, detailing the specific types of structural distortions present in a video frame.
3. **Establish a Comprehensive Structural Distortion Taxonomy**: To define and categorize various types of structural distortions commonly found in generative videos, providing a structured framework for fine-grained assessment.
4. **Construct a Large-Scale, High-Quality Dataset**: To build a human preference dataset annotated according to the proposed taxonomy, including ground-truth structural distortion labels and pairwise human preferences for training the reward model.
5. **Propose an Efficient Data Synthesis Pipeline**: To develop a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) synthesis pipeline that efficiently generates detailed reasoning data, crucial for fine-tuning multi-modal language models (MLLMs) to recognize and reason about structural distortions, while reducing manual annotation costs.
6. **Implement a Robust Training Framework**: To design and implement a two-stage training framework comprising supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with a masked loss mechanism for domain knowledge injection, followed by reinforcement learning (RL) using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with pairwise rewards to enhance reasoning capabilities and align scores with human preferences.
7. **Introduce a Dynamic Frame Sampling Mechanism**: To develop an adaptive inference strategy that intelligently selects frames most likely to exhibit distortions, optimizing the evaluation process within fixed computational constraints.
8. **Create a Dedicated Benchmark (REACT-Bench)**: To establish a new human preference benchmark specifically for evaluating structural distortions in generative videos, facilitating standardized assessment and future research.
9. **Demonstrate Complementary Value**: To experimentally validate that REACT provides accurate quantitative evaluations and interpretable attribution analysis, thereby complementing existing reward models in assessing generative video quality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for REACT involves a multi-faceted approach encompassing data preparation, a two-stage reward model learning framework, and a dynamic sampling mechanism for inference.

#### 3.1. Data Preparation

The foundation of REACT lies in a meticulously constructed dataset tailored for structural distortion evaluation.
- **Taxonomy of Structural Distortions**: The authors developed a detailed taxonomy categorizing structural distortions into two main aspects: abnormal object appearance and abnormal object interaction. Abnormal object appearance is further subdivided into animal-related (limb deformation, extra limbs, limb incompleteness, torso deformation, facial deformation), non-animal (non-animal collapse and distortion), and motion blur. Abnormal object interaction primarily focuses on mesh penetration. This results in eight distinct distortion categories, each with detailed definitions and visual examples provided in the supplementary material.
- **Data Collection**: Real-world videos with complex motions were collected from social media platforms and converted into text prompts. State-of-the-art T2V models (e.g., Kling, HaiLuo, Sora) were then used to generate videos from these prompts, often resulting in structural distortions. Frame pairs were constructed by pairing frames from different generative models responding to the same prompt, or by leveraging image-to-video (I2V) generation paradigms. This process yielded over 15,000 frame pairs (approximately 30,000 frames).
- **Human Annotation**: A team of 34 professional evaluators (20 annotators and 14 reviewers) annotated the collected data. The annotation process involved three components: distortion recognition, spatial grounding of distortion labels with bounding boxes, and human preference (Good/Same/Bad – GSB) for frame pairs. Stringent quality control measures, including annotator training, multiple review rounds, and random sampling for final checks, ensured high accuracy (over 95% for bounding boxes, over 90% for attribution labels). Special rules were established for prioritizing animal-centric labels, distinguishing motion blur from other distortions, and differentiating limb incompleteness from deformation.
- **Efficient Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Synthesis**: To facilitate reasoning capabilities in the MLLM, high-quality CoT data was synthesized. Instead of costly manual generation, annotators drew bounding boxes around distorted regions. Gemini 2.5 Pro, a closed-source MLLM, was then prompted (using specific templates detailed in Appendix C) to simulate the reasoning process that would lead to the observed attribution labels and bounding box localizations. The generated CoT samples were filtered based on label and region accuracy, resulting in 6,000 high-quality instances. Additionally, pseudo point-wise scores were assigned to each CoT sample based on the number of distortion labels (e.g., [4.0, 5.0] for no distortions, [1.0, 2.0] for three or more distortions) to provide numerical supervision and promote score diversity during fine-tuning.

#### 3.2. Reward Model Learning

REACT adopts Qwen2.5-VL-7B as its base model and employs a two-stage training paradigm:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: This initial stage aims to inject domain knowledge into the MLLM, enabling it to recognize structural distortions and generate corresponding attribution labels and point-wise scores. To prevent overfitting to training data and preserve diverse reasoning trajectories for the subsequent RL stage, a **masked SFT strategy** was introduced. Initially, the model was fine-tuned on complete CoT data (including reasoning, labels, and scores). Subsequently, in a second epoch, loss computation was applied only to the final attribution labels and scores, masking the reasoning traces. This approach refined label and score accuracy while mitigating over-reliance on predefined reasoning paths.
- **Reinforcement Learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**: To further enhance reasoning and improve the accuracy of point-wise scores and attribution labels, GRPO was applied. GRPO refines the policy through group-wise relative comparisons of alternative reasoning trajectories. A key innovation was the introduction of a **pairwise reward based on BTT loss**. Since the dataset provided human preference pairs rather than explicit point-wise scores, a reward was allocated to each rollout within a group by calculating pairwise scores. The total reward for each rollout comprised three components:
 - **Format Reward ($R_{fmt}$)**: A binary reward (1 or 0) for adhering to the specified output JSON format (i.e., reasoning within `<think></think>` and output within `<answer></answer>`).
 - **Attribution Accuracy Reward ($R_{attr}$)**: Calculated based on the number of correctly identified, incorrectly identified, and missed attribution labels.
 - **Preference Reward ($R_{pref}$)**: Derived from the Bradley-Terry-Terry (BTT) model, this component computed preference probabilities between two rollouts for a given frame pair based on their predicted point-wise scores. The log-probabilities corresponding to the ground-truth human preference were used for the reward.
 The final reward was a weighted sum of these three components, guiding the GRPO process to align REACT's outputs with human preferences and improve its reasoning ability.

#### 3.3. Dynamic Sampling Mechanism

During inference, a dynamic frame sampling mechanism was introduced to adaptively select frames most likely to exhibit distortions, optimizing analysis within fixed sampling constraints (e.g., 2 fps). This two-stage process involved:
- **Initial Sampling**: Frames were first sampled at half the video's original frame rate and analyzed by REACT.
- **Adaptive Resampling**: Based on the scores from the initial stage:
 - If all initial scores were high (above a high threshold), indicating likely distortion-free content, remaining frames were sampled farther apart.
 - If scores were low (below a low threshold), indicating structural distortions, adjacent frames within a 1/4 fps interval were sampled around the low-scoring frames.
 - If scores were mixed, frames with scores lower than the mean were prioritized, and two frames were randomly sampled within a 1/4 fps interval around these.
The overall video score was then computed by averaging scores from both sampling stages. This dynamic approach increased the probability of identifying problematic frames without increasing the total sampling count.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of REACT was conducted on a newly introduced benchmark, REACT-Bench, which comprises two subsets: REACT-Video for human preference alignment and REACT-Frame for structural distortion recognition.

#### 4.1. Human Preference Alignment

Experiments on REACT-Video assessed how well REACT's scores aligned with human pairwise preferences regarding the severity of structural distortions.
- **Superior Performance**: REACT achieved significantly higher accuracy in human preference alignment compared to state-of-the-art video evaluators (VideoReward, VideoScore2, UnifiedReward), image evaluators (Q-Insight, VisualQuality-R1), and general multimodal large language models (MLLMs). Specifically, REACT obtained 0.610 accuracy with ties and 0.813 accuracy without ties. In contrast, the best existing video evaluator, UnifiedReward, achieved 0.416 with ties and 0.701 without ties. This indicated a 20-40% relative improvement over existing methods.
- **Addressing the Gap**: The substantial performance difference suggested that current video evaluators insufficiently account for structural distortions, often assigning high scores even when such defects are present, prioritizing aesthetic or temporal consistency. Image evaluators and general MLLMs performed even lower, underscoring the domain gap between image and video distortions and their limited capacity for fine-grained structural defect identification in videos.
- **Generalizability**: While REACT specifically targets structural distortions, additional experiments on broader benchmarks (GenAI-Bench and VideoGen-RewardBench, detailed in Appendix D.3) showed that REACT was competitive with, though slightly inferior to, video-based evaluators in *overall* video quality assessment, while still outperforming image-based evaluators. This suggests its specialized focus complements general quality assessment.

#### 4.2. Distortion Recognition

Evaluation on REACT-Frame assessed REACT's ability to accurately recognize structural distortions at the frame level.
- **Highest F1-Score**: REACT demonstrated superior performance in identifying distorted frames and distinguishing them from normal frames. It achieved the highest F1-score for both distorted frames (0.845) and normal frames (0.671).
- **Baseline Limitations**: General MLLMs and existing image evaluators often exhibited high precision for distorted frames and high recall for normal frames, but their overall low F1-scores indicated a tendency to misclassify distorted frames as normal. This highlighted the difficulty these models face in accurately recognizing structural distortions, especially considering the domain gap for image evaluators when applied to video frames. REACT's performance confirmed its specialized capability in this task.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

A series of ablation studies investigated the contribution of different components to REACT's performance.
- **Necessity of SFT**: Training the reward model directly with RL without an initial supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage resulted in a significant performance drop in both human preference alignment (Acc w/o Tie: 0.513) and distortion recognition (Distorted F1-Score: 0.467). This highlighted that SFT with pseudo-scores was crucial for injecting domain knowledge and enabling the MLLM to generate diverse, high-quality rollouts necessary for effective GRPO.
- **Importance of Preference Reward**: Omitting the pairwise preference reward ($R_{pref}$) in the RL stage led to a substantial degradation in human preference alignment (Acc w/o Tie: 0.514), emphasizing its role in aligning the model's scores with human judgments.
- **Effectiveness of Masked SFT**: For distortion recognition, progressively improving the SFT stage from one epoch to two epochs, and then incorporating masked loss in the second epoch, consistently improved F1-scores for distorted frames (from 0.557 to 0.690 without masked loss, and further to 0.764 with masked loss). This confirmed that the masked SFT strategy effectively balanced domain knowledge injection with preventing overfitting.
- **Impact of Dynamic Sampling**: Removing the dynamic sampling mechanism from REACT resulted in a decrease in human preference alignment accuracy (Acc w/o Tie: 0.725), demonstrating that the adaptive frame selection strategy enhanced performance by focusing on potentially problematic frames.

#### 4.4. Performance on Improving Video Generation

To evaluate practical utility, REACT was integrated into two video generation post-training paradigms: Best-of-N sampling and Flow-DPO, using the Wan-2.1-1.3B model and VBench metrics.
- **Improved Visual Fidelity**: In Best-of-N sampling, REACT achieved performance competitive with UnifiedReward, slightly outperforming it in Imaging Quality and Aesthetic Quality while maintaining similar Background Consistency and Subject Consistency.
- **Enhanced Supervision for RL**: Under Flow-DPO post-training, REACT further surpassed UnifiedReward in Imaging Quality and Aesthetic Quality, indicating that its accurate assessment of structural distortions provided a more reliable supervision signal for refining video generation.
- **Complementary Value**: A simple reward fusion strategy, averaging scores from REACT and UnifiedReward, yielded additional gains and achieved the best overall performance across all VBench metrics. This suggested that REACT captures distinct structural cues that are complementary to those captured by existing reward models, and their combination can further improve overall video generation quality.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Results

Case studies (Figure 6) qualitatively demonstrated REACT's capabilities, showing its ability to accurately identify various structural distortions (e.g., limb deformation, torso deformation, facial deformation) within video frames, provide detailed reasoning, and assign appropriate point-wise scores reflecting the visual quality. Similarly, it correctly identified high-quality, distortion-free frames and assigned high scores.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of generative video evaluation and generation by specifically addressing the overlooked issue of structural distortions. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several aspects:

1. **Enhanced Generative Video Evaluation**: REACT provides a refined and more comprehensive method for assessing the quality of generative videos. By explicitly evaluating structural distortions – a common failure mode in current T2V models – it complements existing reward models that primarily focus on broader visual quality, motion, and text alignment. This leads to more accurate and reliable evaluation scores that better reflect human perception of video realism and integrity.

2. **Interpretable Feedback for Model Developers**: The model's ability to provide both point-wise scores and detailed attribution labels for specific distortion types (e.g., "limb deformation," "mesh penetration") offers interpretable feedback. This fine-grained analysis can guide developers of T2V models in identifying and rectifying specific weaknesses in their generation architectures, leading to more robust and higher-quality outputs.

3. **Improved Generative Model Training and Alignment**: The demonstrated utility of REACT in improving video generation quality through post-training strategies (Best-of-N sampling and Flow-DPO) highlights its direct applicability. By providing a stronger, distortion-aware supervision signal, REACT can facilitate the alignment of generative models with human preferences, pushing the boundaries of what T2V models can achieve in terms of structural coherence. The successful fusion with existing reward models further underscores its complementary value.

4. **Creation of Valuable Resources**: The introduction of a detailed taxonomy of structural distortions in generative videos, coupled with a large-scale human-annotated dataset and the REACT-Bench benchmark, provides crucial resources for the research community. These resources can foster standardized evaluation, enable direct comparisons, and accelerate future research into understanding and mitigating distortions in generative media. The efficient Chain-of-Thought (CoT) synthesis pipeline is also a methodological contribution for MLLM-based data generation.

5. **Advancement in Multi-Modal Learning**: The two-stage training framework, combining masked supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for domain knowledge injection and reinforcement learning (RL) with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) using a novel pairwise reward, represents a methodological advancement in training robust multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) for complex visual reasoning tasks. The dynamic sampling mechanism further optimizes inference for video content.

6. **Future Research Directions**: The work lays a clear foundation for future research, particularly in extending REACT's capabilities to spatio-temporal reasoning. This would enable the detection of more complex, temporally-dependent issues like flash effects or object disappearances that current frame-level models cannot fully capture, thus paving the way for even more sophisticated generative video evaluation systems.

In summary, REACT represents a significant step towards more comprehensive and reliable evaluation of generative videos, with direct implications for improving the quality and realism of content produced by advanced T2V models.
