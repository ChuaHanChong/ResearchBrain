# AutoSpeed: Annotation-Free Stage-Adaptive Motion Speed Learning for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> AutoSpeed introduces an annotation-free framework that enables visuomotor policies to learn stage-adaptive motion speeds during end-to-end training for robotic manipulation. It achieves up to a 1.7x speedup in task execution and enhanced success rates across various simulation and real-world tasks, demonstrating implicit adaptation to task complexity without explicit speed or stage annotations.

## Problem

- Imitation learning policies often mimic suboptimal motion speeds from expert demonstrations, limiting efficiency and throughput in robotic manipulation.
- Robotic tasks demand variable motion speeds and prediction horizons across different stages, but current policies typically use fixed speeds and horizons, leading to inflexibility.
- Prior methods for modulating motion speed rely on costly external annotations or proxy models, or infer speeds only at test time, preventing end-to-end optimization.

## Method

- The **AutoSpeed** framework formulates policy training as a ==cost-aware multi-target selective optimization== problem, implicitly inferring stage-dependent motion speed ratios based on a ==composite cost== that balances prediction error and temporal horizon.
- ==Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)== is used to smoothly modulate motion speed in the ==frequency domain==, enabling non-integer acceleration/deceleration while preserving high-frequency action details.
- ==Nonlinear Temporal Aggregation (NTA)== is developed to robustly combine overlapping, variable-speed ==action chunk== predictions, improving deployment smoothness and reactivity.

## Results

- AutoSpeed consistently reduced task execution time by up to **1.7x** (e.g., from **272** to **160** steps in Transfer Cube) while improving or maintaining task success rates.
- In multi-task settings (Meta-World, LIBERO-10), AutoSpeed-trained policies showed significant success rate increases (e.g., **19.0%** for BAKU-DiT) and substantial reductions in average episode lengths.
- The framework autonomously inferred stage-dependent motion patterns, decelerating during precision-critical phases (e.g., grasping, insertion) and accelerating during simpler free-space movements, without requiring any annotations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal motion speed and effective temporal prediction horizon are positively correlated and should dynamically adjust to task stages, with easier stages allowing faster execution and longer reliable predictions.
> - Stage-dependent motion speeds can be implicitly learned during end-to-end policy training by optimizing a composite cost that rewards longer prediction horizons when prediction accuracy is high.
> - Frequency-domain action transformation allows for flexible and smooth re-timing of trajectories, maintaining critical action details necessary for fine-grained manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL) has become a prominent methodology for developing visuomotor policies in robotic manipulation, leading to the development of various policy architectures, including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. A common practice in existing IL-based approaches is for policies to mimic the motion speed observed in expert demonstrations and to operate with a fixed temporal prediction horizon, often achieved through fixed-length action chunking.

However, the motion speeds in expert demonstrations are frequently suboptimal in terms of task efficiency. This can limit the overall throughput and performance of learned policies, particularly in scenarios where task completion efficiency is critical, such as industrial applications. Robotic manipulation tasks are characterized by varying levels of difficulty across different stages; for instance, free-space movements may be less complex than precision-critical operations like insertion. This variability suggests that optimal motion speed and temporal prediction horizon should adapt to the specific task stage rather than remaining fixed. Prior research in human motor control also supports the notion that motion speed and effective prediction horizon are positively correlated, with humans adjusting speed based on task demands.

Previous work on motion speed modulation in robotics can be categorized by the timing of speed ratio inference:
1. **Pre-training inference**: Methods like DemoSpeedUp and ESPADA infer speed ratios before training, using them to annotate datasets. These approaches often rely on proxy policies, VLM/LLM-based annotations, or manual rules to identify regions suitable for acceleration. SpeedAug also uses tempo-augmented trajectories and RL-based fine-tuning.
2. **Test-time inference**: Approaches such as SAIL, SRIL, and VFIL infer speed ratios during model deployment. These methods integrate complexity-aware speed modulation with tracking and scheduling, or condition the policy on sampling frequency.

A significant limitation of annotation-based methods is the cost and effort associated with generating external annotations or training additional models. Furthermore, the quality and consistency of these annotations directly influence policy performance but are not optimized end-to-end with the policy itself. The current research landscape has largely left the paradigm of "training-time emergence from unlabeled data" underexplored for motion speed inference.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing an annotation-free framework that implicitly infers stage-dependent motion speed ratios during end-to-end policy training. It unifies acceleration and deceleration within a single speed-modulation perspective. Additionally, while action chunking is a standard practice, its fixed horizon can limit reactivity. Some works (MoH, AutoHorizon, GBC) have explored flexible action chunking or adaptive horizons. AutoSpeed aligns with this by dynamically adjusting the effective prediction horizon in conjunction with motion speed, without altering the model's output dimensionality, thereby contributing to more adaptable and efficient visuomotor control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations in how current imitation learning (IL) policies handle motion speed and temporal prediction horizons in robotic manipulation. Existing IL-based visuomotor policies generally imitate the execution speed present in expert demonstrations and operate with a fixed temporal prediction horizon. This approach has several drawbacks:
- **Suboptimal Efficiency**: Expert demonstrations often exhibit suboptimal speeds, leading to learned policies that are not as efficient as they could be, particularly in industrial settings where task throughput is a critical performance metric.
- **Lack of Flexibility**: Manipulation tasks inherently vary in difficulty across different stages. For instance, free-space movements require less precision and can be executed quickly, while fine-grained tasks like insertion demand high precision and slower, more deliberate movements. Fixed-speed policies fail to adapt to these stage-dependent demands, leading to either overly slow execution in simple stages or overly fast, error-prone execution in complex stages.
- **Fixed Horizon Limitations**: The use of a fixed temporal prediction horizon, common in action chunking, limits the policy's ability to leverage long-horizon predictability in easy stages or to react quickly with a shorter horizon in precision-critical moments.

Building upon these observations, the authors hypothesize that the optimal motion speed and, consequently, the effective temporal prediction horizon should be dynamically adjusted based on the current task stage. They identify a consistent positive relationship between motion speed and effective prediction horizon: easier stages allow faster execution with longer reliable predictions, while complex stages benefit from slower execution with shorter, more responsive horizons.

The key objectives of introducing AutoSpeed are:
1. **Annotation-Free Stage-Adaptive Speed Learning**: To develop a model-agnostic learning framework that enables existing visuomotor policies to predict trajectories with stage-adaptive motion speeds without requiring explicit speed or stage annotations from the expert demonstrations. This aims to overcome the cost and potential biases of manual or proxy-model-based annotation methods.
2. **Implicit Inference of Speed Ratios**: To implicitly infer the stage-dependent speed ratio during end-to-end policy training. This involves making the speed ratio an endogenous signal learned directly from the interaction between prediction error and prediction horizon.
3. **Joint Optimization**: To jointly optimize the quality of this implicit speed ratio inference and the accuracy of trajectory prediction within a single, unified end-to-end training pipeline.
4. **Smooth Speed Modulation**: To implement speed modulation in the frequency domain using the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT). This technique aims to enable smooth, non-integer speed scaling while preserving critical high-frequency action details necessary for fine-grained manipulation.
5. **Improved Task Performance**: To substantially reduce task execution time while simultaneously improving or maintaining task success rates across a diverse range of single-task and multi-task learning scenarios in both simulation and real-world environments.
6. **Broad Applicability**: To ensure that the framework is compatible with various types of visuomotor policies, including both non-generative (e.g., MLP-based) and generative (e.g., diffusion- and flow-based) action prediction models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The AutoSpeed framework proposes a model-agnostic approach to enable visuomotor policies to learn stage-adaptive motion speeds without requiring explicit speed or stage annotations. This is achieved by formulating policy training as a cost-aware multi-target selective optimization problem.

**Problem Setup and Motion Speed Definition:**
The framework defines a motion speed ratio, `r_t`, at each time step `t`. A larger `r_t` corresponds to easier task stages, enabling faster execution and a longer effective temporal prediction horizon. Conversely, a smaller `r_t` is associated with precision-critical stages, demanding slower execution and a shorter effective horizon. The temporal prediction horizon `h_t` is directly correlated with the motion speed ratio: `h_t = H * r_t`, where `H` is a fixed chunk size, keeping the model's output tensor shape consistent.

**Motion Speed Transform via Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT):**
To generate trajectories at different speeds, AutoSpeed employs the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT). This method transforms time-domain action signals into the frequency domain. Temporal scaling (stretching or compression along the time axis) is then performed in this domain. Finally, an inverse-DCT is used to reconstruct the retimed action chunk in the time domain. This frequency-domain approach enables non-integer acceleration and deceleration, which is described as preserving high-frequency action details crucial for fine-grained manipulation and maintaining motion continuity. During training, a set of candidate future trajectories `{A_t^(m)}` is generated by applying this transform with a user-defined discrete set of motion-speed ratios `{r^(m)}`.

**Multi-Target Selective Optimization:**
Training is formulated as a cost-aware multi-target optimization problem. For each time step `t`, a set of candidate future trajectories at different speeds, `{A_t^(m)}`, is considered. Each candidate is evaluated using a composite cost `J`, which balances prediction error against the temporal prediction horizon:
`J(A_t^(m)) = E_t^(m) / (w + log(h_t^(m)))`
- `E_t^(m)` represents the model's mean-squared prediction error for candidate `m` at time `t`. For non-generative models, `E_t^(m)` is the MSE between the predicted action chunk and `A_t^(m)`. For generative models (diffusion or flow-matching), it is the MSE of the predicted noise or velocity field, respectively.
- The denominator `(w + log(h_t^(m)))` acts as an adaptive normalization term. It penalizes candidates with shorter prediction horizons more heavily, implicitly encouraging the policy to favor faster speeds (longer horizons) when prediction is reliable.
- `w` is a constant term that can be tuned to control the overall motion style; a smaller `w` promotes a more aggressive, faster policy.

The policy is then trained by minimizing the loss on the candidate `m*` that yields the minimum composite cost: `m* = argmin_m J(A_t^(m))`. This process implicitly infers the most appropriate speed ratio for the current task stage by selecting the optimal trade-off between prediction accuracy and temporal horizon.

**Ratio Head:**
A lightweight "Ratio Head" is introduced and trained jointly with the policy. This head predicts the speed ratio `r_t` directly from the latent observation features. This predicted ratio is subsequently utilized during inference by the Nonlinear Temporal Aggregation (NTA) module.

**Training Recipe for Generative Models (Three-Stage Strategy):**
To enhance training stability and reduce computational cost for generative models, a three-stage training strategy is proposed:
1. **Stage 1 (Early Training)**: The model is optimized by minimizing the average loss across all candidate speeds. This stage helps in learning smooth and generalizable structures.
2. **Stage 2 (Multi-Objective Optimization)**: The core multi-target selective optimization process, as described above (Sec. 2.2 in the paper), is applied.
3. **Stage 3 (Fine-tuning with Ratio Head)**: The Ratio Head is frozen, and the model is optimized directly using the motion speed predicted by the frozen Ratio Head. This allows the generation process to converge more rapidly to the selected mode.

**Nonlinear Temporal Aggregation (NTA):**
Conventional temporal aggregation, often used with action chunking, assumes uniform time intervals, making it incompatible with the variable-speed predictions of AutoSpeed. NTA addresses this by aggregating overlapping predictions from action chunks that operate at different temporal scales. Instead of linear time, NTA uses task progress (episode step) as a reference. For a given control step, NTA collects predicted actions from previous chunks that cover the next effective episode step. It then computes a weighted average of these candidate actions using an exponential-decay weighting scheme. Temporal misalignments are corrected via frequency-domain transformation before aggregation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AutoSpeed involved extensive experiments across diverse simulation benchmarks and real-world robotic manipulation tasks. The findings consistently demonstrate the framework's effectiveness in enhancing task efficiency and robustness.

**Overall Performance (Efficiency and Success Rates):**
AutoSpeed consistently reduced task execution time while either improving or maintaining task success rates. This indicates that the framework enables policies to achieve faster execution without compromising manipulation precision, suggesting that reducing task execution time and improving success rates are not mutually exclusive goals.

**Single-Task Learning (ALOHA Benchmark):**
- **Transfer Cube Task**: AutoSpeed substantially reduced the average episode length from 272 to 160 steps, achieving a maximum speedup of 1.7x compared to the vanilla ACT baseline. While the success rate initially saw a slight decrease (from 72% to 64%) due to heightened sensitivity with faster interactions, coupling AutoSpeed with a high-gain controller mitigated this, leading to a 6% improvement in success rate (to 78%) while maintaining execution efficiency.
- **Insertion Task (Contact-rich)**: AutoSpeed simultaneously improved the success rate (from 22% to 24%) and reduced execution length (from 353 to 296 steps with a high-gain controller), demonstrating its robust performance in precision-critical, contact-rich scenarios.

**Multi-Task Learning (LIBERO-10 and Meta-World Benchmarks):**
- **Meta-World Benchmark**: AutoSpeed consistently outperformed baselines, showing substantial improvements in success rates regardless of the underlying action head (BAKU-DiT and BAKU-Flow). BAKU-DiT (AutoSpeed) exhibited a 19.0% increase in success rate, while BAKU-Flow (AutoSpeed) improved by 7.8% to achieve the highest overall success rate of 65%. These gains were accompanied by significant reductions in average episode lengths (e.g., from 87 to 70 steps for BAKU-DiT).
- **LIBERO-10 Suite**: AutoSpeed-trained policies compressed execution time with an approximate maximum acceleration of 40% compared to their vanilla counterparts. This highlights AutoSpeed's ability to dynamically adjust to task complexity in multi-task settings, leading to superior efficiency.

**Real-World Tasks (Agilex Piper Bimanual Robot Platform):**
- Across four distinct tabletop manipulation tasks, AutoSpeed consistently outperformed baselines. When integrated with the Nonlinear Temporal Aggregation (NTA) module, AutoSpeed-trained policies achieved an average speedup of approximately 1.78x across the task suite.
- This acceleration was accompanied by consistent absolute improvements in task success rates. For example, in the Place the Toy task, AutoSpeed increased the success rate by 10%.
- Physical deployments confirmed AutoSpeed's capacity to autonomously extract stage-dependent motion speed patterns from unannotated demonstrations, translating these into superior task efficiency and robust real-world performance.
- Action oversampling (e.g., 2x) was shown to be an optional method to improve fine-grained action fidelity under AutoSpeed, further enhancing performance.

**Inferred Speed Ratios and Task Stages:**
A key finding is that the motion speeds inferred by AutoSpeed exhibit a strong correspondence with task stages. Qualitative analysis (illustrated in figures) demonstrates that the policies autonomously decelerate during complex, interaction-critical task phases (e.g., grasping, alignment, insertion, button pressing) and accelerate during simpler, unconstrained stages (e.g., free-space reaching). This results in distinct "valleys" in the predicted speed curves during critical stages. This implicitly learned stage awareness was also found to be consistent with action entropy, where lower predicted speeds generally corresponded to higher action-entropy stages. However, AutoSpeed was noted to distinguish uncertainty from multi-modality more effectively, thereby identifying safely acceleratable stages more accurately.

**Model Agnosticism and Training Overhead:**
AutoSpeed demonstrated compatibility with various policy classes, including non-generative models like ACT and generative models like BAKU-DiT and BAKU-Flow. For non-generative models, AutoSpeed introduces no additional computational overhead. For generative models, the overhead is described as small due to the action head constituting a minor fraction of the overall model and the potential for parallel denoising of trajectory candidates. Wall-clock training times across benchmarks showed only marginal increases (e.g., from 7.6 to 7.9 hours for ALOHA Transfer).

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Speed Range Bounds**: Evaluations with different predefined speed ranges (e.g., [1x, 2x], [1x, 4x], [2x, 4x]) revealed that all AutoSpeed variants exhibited consistent phase-aware motion patterns. Policies decelerated during complex interactions and accelerated during simpler stages. A clear trade-off emerged: conservative ranges achieved higher success rates, while aggressive ranges maximized speedup while maintaining competitive success rates, all outperforming the vanilla baseline.
- **Sensitivity to `w` (Length Penalty Coefficient)**: Varying the `w` parameter in the composite cost function showed that a smaller `w` (lower penalty for shorter horizons) biases the policy toward higher speeds. However, different `w` values consistently preserved the phase-aware pattern of accelerating in predictable stages and decelerating in critical interaction stages.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AutoSpeed framework introduces a method for learning stage-adaptive motion speeds in robotic manipulation, offering several contributions and potential impacts on the field.

**Novelty and Technical Contributions:**
- **Annotation-Free Learning**: AutoSpeed is presented as the first annotation-free, stage-aware framework that implicitly infers motion speed ratios directly within an end-to-end policy learning process. This addresses a limitation of previous methods that relied on explicit annotations or external proxy models.
- **Unified Control Objective**: The framework unifies speed control and temporal reasoning under a single composite cost objective. This allows the policy to select an optimal balance between prediction error and effective temporal prediction horizon, aligning motion speed with task complexity.
- **Frequency-Domain Modulation**: The use of Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) for frequency-domain scaling enables smooth, non-integer acceleration and deceleration. This technique helps preserve high-frequency action details, which are crucial for fine-grained manipulation, and maintains motion continuity.
- **Robust Inference Mechanism**: The development of Nonlinear Temporal Aggregation (NTA) provides a mechanism for robustly ensembling overlapping predictions from variable-speed action chunks, thereby improving the smoothness and reactivity of policy deployment.

**Practical Benefits and Impact:**
- **Enhanced Efficiency and Throughput**: By autonomously adapting motion speeds, AutoSpeed substantially reduces task completion times. This is particularly beneficial for industrial applications and other scenarios where high throughput and operational efficiency are paramount.
- **Improved Task Success Rates**: The dynamic adjustment of speed and effective prediction horizon to suit task demands leads to more robust and precise manipulation, consequently improving task success rates, especially in precision-critical stages. This demonstrates that efficiency gains do not necessarily come at the expense of performance.
- **Reduced Annotation Burden**: Eliminating the need for manual or proxy-model-generated speed or stage annotations simplifies the data collection and training pipeline for imitation learning, making it more scalable and less labor-intensive.
- **Broad Applicability**: The model-agnostic nature of AutoSpeed allows it to be integrated with a wide range of existing visuomotor policies, including both non-generative (e.g., ACT) and generative (e.g., diffusion- and flow-based) models, and across various learning paradigms (single-task and multi-task). This broad compatibility expands its potential adoption.
- **Controllable Motion Style**: The `w` parameter in the composite cost function provides a tunable knob for researchers and practitioners to control the overall motion style, enabling a trade-off between aggressive speed and cautious precision depending on specific application requirements.
- **Foundation for Future Work**: The implicitly inferred stage-aware motion speed annotations could potentially be stored and reused to streamline future training or fine-tuning of larger-scale models, further reducing computational costs. Future research might also explore adaptive `w` parameters, refined retiming methods, and more robust handling of specific failure modes such as delayed controller response or distortion under extreme deceleration.

In summary, AutoSpeed offers a practical and broadly applicable framework for enhancing the efficiency, robustness, and adaptability of visuomotor policies in robotic manipulation. By enabling policies to autonomously infer and adapt motion speeds to different task stages, it provides a route toward faster, safer, and more reliable robot deployments, advancing the capabilities of imitation learning in complex real-world environments.
