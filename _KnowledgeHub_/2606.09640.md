# Physics-Aware Sparse Learning and Selective Online Adaptation for Euler-Lagrange Robot Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for Euler-Lagrange robot dynamics integrates physics-aware sparse learning and selective online adaptation to address unmodeled effects and time-varying conditions while preserving mechanical structure. This approach consistently yielded the lowest prediction error and superior trajectory tracking performance across five diverse robotic platforms.

## Problem

- Nominal first-principles Euler-Lagrange models become inaccurate due to unmodeled effects (e.g., payload variations, friction) and nonstationary operating conditions.
- Many learning-based dynamics correction methods disregard the fundamental mechanical structure of Euler-Lagrange systems, leading to physically inconsistent predictions (e.g., violating inertia matrix symmetry or positive-definiteness).
- Existing online adaptation techniques often incur high computational costs, unnecessarily modify mechanically structured components, or lack robustness for efficiently coping with time-evolving model mismatches.

## Method

- A structure-preserving ==residual decomposition== is introduced, explicitly correcting inertia to maintain symmetric positive-definiteness, inducing consistent Coriolis terms, and modeling generalized-force residuals.
- A sparse, history-dependent latent representation is utilized via a temporal encoder and soft-thresholding to capture a limited number of active interaction patterns for compact residual modeling.
- A selective Bayesian online adaptation mechanism is developed that updates only the disturbance-sensitive generalized-force component within its active latent subspace, keeping mechanically structured components fixed.

## Results

- The proposed method consistently achieved the lowest normalized prediction error across all five robotic platforms (differential-drive robot, 6-DoF manipulator, quadrotor, mobile manipulator, aerial manipulator) compared to Sparse SysID, PI-TCN, and DMRAC.
- It demonstrated superior trajectory tracking performance, yielding the best normalized RMSE on figure-eight trajectories across all platforms, outperforming nominal models and various learning-based baselines including DNN, Diffusion models, Meta-learning, and Active MLPs.
- Ablation studies confirmed that moderate sparsity improves prediction accuracy and sample efficiency, while the selective online adaptation facilitates faster error reduction and smoother convergence after disturbances like payload release, by dynamically activating relevant latent modes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decomposing and modeling residual dynamics to preserve the intrinsic Euler-Lagrange mechanical structure leads to physically consistent predictions and enhanced reliability in model-based control.
> - Employing sparsity in latent representations significantly improves data efficiency and computational tractability, enabling compact modeling and efficient online adaptation by focusing on relevant interaction patterns.
> - Selectively adapting only the generalized-force residual component, which is most susceptible to external disturbances, using uncertainty-aware Bayesian updates ensures stable and effective online performance without compromising learned mechanical structures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Accurate dynamics models, often based on Euler–Lagrange (EL) equations, are foundational for model-based robotic control. However, practical robotic systems frequently exhibit unmodeled effects such as payload variations, intra-system coupling, friction, and aerodynamic disturbances, which cause nominal first-principles models to become inaccurate.

The broader research landscape has seen the emergence of learning-based dynamics correction methods to address these inaccuracies without entirely abandoning the analytical structure of first-principles models. Many existing learning-based approaches, including additive residual learning methods and full-model learning methods, aim to improve prediction accuracy by introducing a single additive residual term. These methods typically operate by treating model mismatch as an undifferentiated component, often without explicitly preserving the internal mechanical structure of Euler–Lagrange systems. This can lead to models that do not maintain properties such as symmetry, positive-definiteness of the inertia matrix, or the intrinsic coupling between inertia and velocity-dependent terms. Such structural violations can result in physically inconsistent predictions, reduced reliability when embedded in model-based controllers, and limited interpretability.

A sub-area of research focuses on structure-aware neural dynamics models, which embed Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, or manipulator structure directly into learned dynamics. While these approaches enhance physical plausibility and sample efficiency, they are generally trained as fixed models and do not primarily address online adaptation to changing operating conditions. For example, enables structured learning but does not explicitly model non-conservative effects like friction or drag. Physically consistent identification methods infer global physical parameters, but their utility is limited for handling time-evolving phenomena such as payload changes or external disturbances.

To cope with nonstationary dynamics, some methods update learned models during deployment. These include adapting only the final layer of a neural network using online gradient-based updates, which can be sensitive to hyperparameter tuning, or employing meta-learned low-dimensional adaptation, which ties online adaptability to the expressiveness of pre-trained representations. Nonparametric and system identification approaches offer online refinement but typically adapt generic local models or global parameter blocks, rather than distinguishing between fast-changing disturbance effects and mechanically structured dynamics.

Furthermore, compactness is a relevant factor for learned dynamics models integrated into control loops. Sparse modeling techniques have been utilized in system identification to select relevant dynamics terms, recover parsimonious governing equations, and simplify models for computational tractability in control applications. These works underscore the value of compact representations but generally focus on model discovery or simplification rather than the specific problem of combining structured residual learning with selective online adaptation.

The current work addresses a gap in the literature by proposing a framework that integrates structure preservation, selective online adaptation, and compact modeling. It aims to develop a residual learning approach for Euler–Lagrange systems that explicitly decomposes model mismatch into components that preserve mechanical structure while enabling efficient online adaptation of only the disturbance-sensitive parts of the dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and accurate dynamics modeling framework for Euler–Lagrange robotic systems that can operate reliably under real-world, time-varying conditions. This overarching goal is motivated by several specific challenges observed in existing methods:

1. **Preservation of Euler–Lagrange Mechanical Structure:** Nominal first-principles Euler–Lagrange models often deviate from true system dynamics due to unmodeled effects such as payload variations, friction, and aerodynamic influences. While learning-based methods can correct these deviations, many existing approaches simply add an unstructured residual term to improve prediction accuracy. This practice is a significant limitation because it disregards the fundamental mechanical properties of Euler–Lagrange systems. By collapsing diverse unmodeled effects into a single component, these methods fail to preserve crucial structural couplings, such as the relationship between inertia and velocity-dependent (Coriolis/centrifugal) terms. Consequently, the resulting models may violate physical principles like symmetry and positive-definiteness of the inertia matrix. Such violations lead to physically inconsistent predictions, reduced reliability, and diminished interpretability, particularly when these models are integrated into model-based controllers. The motivation is to formulate residual learning in a way that explicitly decomposes model mismatch into components that maintain the intrinsic mechanical structure of EL systems.

2. **Effective Online Adaptation to Time-Varying Dynamics:** Robotic systems in real-world deployment encounter nonstationary conditions, where dominant model mismatch can evolve over time (e.g., changing payloads, varying external disturbances, or dynamic coupling effects as illustrated in Figure 1). Offline-trained models are susceptible to distribution shifts, which can lead to accumulating prediction errors and degradation of closed-loop control performance. Therefore, online adaptation is essential for maintaining model accuracy and controller robustness. However, existing online adaptation methods often update generic local models or large parameter blocks, potentially modifying mechanically structured components unnecessarily or incurring high computational costs. The motivation here is to develop an online adaptation mechanism that is responsive to changing conditions, focusing updates only on the parts of the dynamics most affected by disturbances, thereby preserving the learned mechanical structure.

3. **Parsimonious and Compact Representation for Efficiency and Control Compatibility:** The authors observe that residual dynamics are often governed by a limited number of active interaction patterns, suggesting an inherent sparsity. Deploying large, dense learned models within real-time control loops can be computationally expensive and data-inefficient for online adaptation. Many control systems require compact dynamics representations for computational tractability and compatibility. The motivation is to achieve a parsimonious representation of residual dynamics that exploits this sparsity, enabling compact modeling of both structured mechanical mismatch and disturbance-related effects. This parsimony aims to improve computational efficiency and data efficiency, facilitating selective online adaptation by restricting updates to a small, active latent subspace.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Formulate a structure-preserving residual decomposition for Euler–Lagrange systems that explicitly corrects inertia, induces consistent Coriolis terms, and models generalized-force residuals.
- Introduce a sparse, history-dependent latent representation to capture a limited number of active interaction patterns for compact residual modeling.
- Develop a selective Bayesian online adaptation mechanism that updates only the disturbance-sensitive generalized-force component within its active latent subspace, while keeping the mechanically structured components fixed.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a structure-preserving residual learning framework for Euler–Lagrange robotic systems, designed to address the limitations of conventional learning-based dynamics correction. The core idea is to decompose model mismatch into components that are mechanically consistent and adaptable, leveraging sparse latent representations and selective online adaptation.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system dynamics are described by the Euler–Lagrange equation: $M (\chi) ¨χ + C(\chi, ˙χ) ˙χ + f (\chi, ˙χ) = τ$. A nominal model, $\bar{M}$, $\bar{C}$, $\bar{f}$, is assumed to be available. Due to unmodeled effects, the true system deviates, leading to a residual $\delta_t$. The objective is to learn a structured residual model $\delta_t = \Delta M_t ¨χ_t + \Delta C_t ˙χ_t + \Delta f_t$ that adheres to Euler–Lagrange mechanical properties (e.g., corrected inertia must be symmetric positive definite, velocity-dependent coupling consistent with inertia), while allowing efficient online adaptation of the generalized force component.

#### 3.2. Temporal History Encoder

To capture short-horizon dependencies in residual dynamics, a temporal history encoder is employed. At each time step $t$, a per-step observation $\zeta_t = [\chi_t^\top, \dot{\chi}_t^\top, \ddot{\chi}_t^\top, \tau_t^\top]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{4n}$ is constructed. A history window $H_t$ of length $K$ is formed from recent observations. This history tensor $H_t$ is then mapped to a compact latent representation $\tilde{z}_t = \phi(H_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ using a lightweight temporal convolutional network (TCN) for $\phi(\cdot)$.

#### 3.3. Sparse Latent Interaction Selection

The dense latent feature $\tilde{z}_t$ is further processed to extract sparse latent representations, reflecting the hypothesis that residual dynamics are often governed by a limited number of active interaction modes. Two branch-specific sparse codes are generated from $\tilde{z}_t$ using an elementwise soft-thresholding operator $S_\lambda(\cdot)$:
- $z_M_t = S_{\lambda_M}(\tilde{z}_t)$ for parameterizing inertia corrections (structural branch).
- $z_f_t = S_{\lambda_f}(\tilde{z}_t)$ for driving the generalized force model (adaptive branch).
The soft-thresholding operator $S_\lambda(v)_i = \text{sign}(v_i) \max\{|v_i| - \lambda, 0\}$ enforces sparsity, meaning that only latent dimensions exceeding a certain threshold contribute. The set of active indices $S_t = \text{supp}(z_f_t)$ is identified for the adaptive branch, indicating the dimensions that contribute to prediction and are updated online.

#### 3.4. Positive-Definite Parameterization of the Inertia Matrix

To ensure that the corrected inertia matrix remains symmetric positive definite (SPD), the correction $\Delta M_{\theta_M}(\chi_t, z_M_t)$ is parameterized using a specific neural decoder $g_{\theta_M}(\cdot)$. The corrected inertia matrix is $\hat{M}(\chi_t, z_M_t) = \bar{M}(\chi_t) + \Delta M_{\theta_M}(\chi_t, z_M_t)$. The decoder outputs two components: $b(\chi_t, z_M_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{nr}$ and $d(\chi_t, z_M_t) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. $b$ is reshaped into a matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$. The inertia correction is then defined as $\Delta M_{\theta_M} = B B^\top + D(\chi_t, z_M_t)$, where $D = \text{diag}(\text{softplus}(d))$. This construction guarantees positive definiteness: $B B^\top$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, and $D$ is a diagonal matrix with strictly positive entries (due to the softplus activation), making $\Delta M_{\theta_M} \succ 0$ and thus $\hat{M} \succ 0$.

#### 3.5. Structure-Preserving Coriolis Matrix Construction

The Coriolis and centrifugal terms are inherently linked to the inertia matrix in Euler–Lagrange systems. To preserve this coupling, the Coriolis matrix $\hat{C}$ is not learned independently but constructed directly from the corrected inertia matrix $\hat{M}(\chi_t, z_M_t)$. This is achieved by computing the Christoffel symbols of the first kind: $\Gamma_{ijk} = \frac{1}{2} (\frac{\partial \hat{M}_{ij}}{\partial \chi_k} + \frac{\partial \hat{M}_{ik}}{\partial \chi_j} - \frac{\partial \hat{M}_{jk}}{\partial \chi_i})$, and then forming $\hat{C}_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n \Gamma_{ijk} \dot{\chi}_{t,k}$. The correction $\Delta C_{\theta_M} = \hat{C} - \bar{C}$ introduces no new parameters, and derivatives of $\hat{M}$ are computed via automatic differentiation, approximating the skew-symmetry property of $\dot{M} - 2C$.

#### 3.6. Generalized Force Residual Modeling

The remaining model mismatch, after accounting for inertia and Coriolis corrections, is attributed to generalized forces, which represent non-conservative and unmodeled effects. This component is modeled as $\hat{f}(\chi_t, \dot{\chi}_t, z_f_t) = \bar{f}(\chi_t, \dot{\chi}_t) + \Delta f_{\theta_f}(z_f_t)$, where $\Delta f_{\theta_f}(z_f_t) = \Theta z_f_t$. Here, $\Theta \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_z}$ is a linear force decoder, driven by the adaptive sparse latent code $z_f_t$. This linear mapping is used because the nonlinear history-dependent interactions are already captured by the encoder $\phi(H_t)$.

#### 3.7. Training Objective

The model is trained using a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ to minimize a combined objective function $L$:
- **Data Fidelity:** $L_{data} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N ||\delta_t - \hat{\delta}_t||_2^2$, which minimizes the difference between the true and predicted residual dynamics.
- **Sparsity Regularization:** $L_{sparse} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^N (\lambda_M ||z_M_t||_1 + \lambda_f ||z_f_t||_1)$, which promotes consistent activation of a small number of latent modes by penalizing the $\ell_1$ norm of the sparse codes.
The total objective is $L = L_{data} + L_{sparse}$.

#### 3.8. Online Adaptation

For online deployment, only the generalized force decoder $\Theta$ is adapted, while the inertia and Coriolis components remain fixed. This strategy preserves the learned mechanical structure and focuses adaptation on disturbance-sensitive terms.
1. **Residual Isolation:** The force residual $r_t$ is computed by subtracting the structured inertia and Coriolis corrections from the total residual: $r_t = \delta_t - \Delta M_{\theta_M}(\chi_t, z_M_t) ¨χ_t - \Delta C_{\theta_M}(\chi_t, ˙χ_t, z_M_t) ˙χ_t$. This yields a linear model $r_t = \Theta z_f_t + \epsilon_t$.
2. **Bayesian Linear Regression:** Each output dimension $j$ of the force residual $r_t^{(j)}$ is modeled independently as $r_t^{(j)} = (z_f_t)^\top \theta_j + \epsilon_{t,j}$, where $\theta_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ is the parameter vector for that dimension. A Gaussian posterior ($\theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{t,j}, P_{t,j})$) is maintained for each $\theta_j$, initialized from the offline-trained parameters.
3. **Bayesian Update:** Recursive Bayesian linear regression updates for $\mu_{t,j}$ and $P_{t,j}$ are performed using Kalman filter-like equations, incorporating the sparse latent code $z_f_t$. Although the updates occur in the full $d_z$-dimensional space, the sparsity of $z_f_t$ (i.e., only elements in the active set $S_t$ are non-zero) ensures that adaptation is effectively restricted to a low-dimensional subspace.

#### 3.9. Control Law

A computed torque controller is employed using the learned dynamics model. The control input $\tau$ is computed as $\tau = \hat{M}(\chi_t, z_M_t)u + \hat{C}(\chi_t, ˙χ_t, z_M_t) ˙χ + \hat{f}(\chi_t, ˙χ_t, z_f_t)$, where $u = ¨χ_d - K_d ˙e - K_p e$ uses feedback gains $K_p, K_d$ to minimize tracking error $e = \chi - \chi_d$. For underactuated systems, a Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse is used to map desired generalized forces to actuator inputs.

#### 3.10. Implementation Details

The model is implemented in JAX. A temporal CNN with 2 layers, kernel size 3, and 64 hidden dimensions is used for the encoder. The latent dimension $d_z$ is set to 16, with sparsity thresholds $\lambda_M = \lambda_f = 0.01$ and soft-threshold $\lambda=0.2$. The inertia decoder is a 2-layer MLP with 64 hidden dimensions and a low-rank dimension $r = \lceil 3n/4 \rceil$. Training uses Adam optimizer with a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$, batch size of 256, 100 epochs, and a weight decay of $1 \times 10^{-5}$. For online adaptation, the initial mean of the force decoder parameters is derived from offline training, initial covariance is $0.1I$, and observation noise is $1 \times 10^{-3}$. Controller gains are tuned per platform.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed physics-aware sparse learning and selective online adaptation framework across five robotic platforms, comparing its performance against various baselines in terms of model prediction accuracy and trajectory tracking.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on five distinct robotic systems: a differential-drive mobile robot (RP1), a 6-DoF serial manipulator (RP2), a quadrotor (RP3), a mobile manipulator with a holonomic base and 6-DoF arm (RP4), and an aerial manipulator with a 2-DoF serial arm (RP5). All systems utilized an onboard NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX for real-time computation. Nominal dynamics models were provided for each platform, with coupling effects neglected for composite systems like the manipulators. Training data consisted of 5 minutes of randomly generated, smooth trajectories collected at 100 Hz, including variations in payload. Evaluation was performed under unseen conditions, encompassing novel trajectories, payload variations, and changes in operating regimes.

#### 4.2. Model Validation (Predictive Accuracy)

The predictive accuracy of the residual dynamics model was assessed by commanding each robot to track a smooth reference trajectory for 2 minutes using a PID controller, under operating conditions not encountered during training (e.g., payload variations). Prediction error was quantified by the normalized Root Mean Square (RMS) error of the residual dynamics.
- **Baselines:** Sparse System Identification (SysID), Physics-Inspired Temporal Convolutional Networks (PI-TCN), and Deep Model Reference Adaptive Control (DMRAC).
- **Results (Figure 3):**
 - Sparse SysID exhibited the highest prediction error, attributed to its reliance on a fixed dictionary of basis functions which may not capture complex residual dynamics.
 - DMRAC and PI-TCN improved performance over Sparse SysID, with PI-TCN benefiting from temporal encoding and physics-inspired regularization, and DMRAC showing improvement through online adaptation. However, these methods treated model mismatch as an unstructured function.
 - All baseline methods displayed larger errors on the mobile manipulator (RP4) and aerial manipulator (RP5), where strong configuration-dependent coupling is present, indicating their limitations in capturing such structured effects with purely additive residual models.
 - The **proposed method** consistently achieved the lowest normalized prediction error across all five robotic platforms. This outcome suggests that explicitly modeling structured corrections to inertia and velocity coupling, combined with selective online adaptation, contributes to improved prediction accuracy, particularly in systems with complex, coupled dynamics.

#### 4.3. Trajectory Tracking Performance

Trajectory tracking performance was evaluated for figure-eight-shaped trajectories (Figure 2) on all platforms, with control implemented using the law described in the methodology. Performance was quantified by the normalized Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the tracking signal $e(t) = \chi(t) - \chi_d(t)$.
- **Baselines:** Nominal model, Deep Neural Networks (DNN), Diffusion-based models, Meta-learning approaches, and Active MLPs.
- **Results (Figure 4):**
 - The nominal model yielded the worst tracking performance due to its inability to account for unmodeled effects and dynamic changes.
 - Offline methods like DNN and Diffusion models improved performance by learning residual dynamics, with Diffusion models marginally outperforming DNNs by capturing multimodal and stochastic dynamics. However, both were limited by distribution shift due to the absence of online adaptation.
 - Meta-learning further enhanced tracking by enabling rapid adaptation through shared representations, but its adaptation was constrained by the expressiveness of pre-trained basis functions.
 - Active MLP, incorporating online updates, showed stronger performance but suffered from instability and sensitivity to noise inherent in batch-based SGD updates in fast, nonlinear regimes.
 - The **proposed method** demonstrated the best trajectory tracking performance across all platforms. This performance is attributed to the combination of structured dynamics learning and recursive, uncertainty-aware Bayesian updates, which facilitates stable and sample-efficient adaptation while preserving physical consistency.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study: Effect of Sparsity and Adaptation

An ablation study was conducted on the aerial manipulator (RP5) to analyze the impact of sparsity on prediction accuracy, data efficiency, and adaptation characteristics.
- **Sparsity Threshold vs. Prediction Error (Figure 5, left):**
 - Dense models showed a tendency to over-parameterize.
 - Moderate sparsity improved performance by effectively suppressing spurious interactions.
 - Excessive sparsity led to underfitting by removing relevant dynamics.
 - This indicates an optimal intermediate sparsity level that balances expressiveness and robustness.
- **Training Data Size vs. Prediction Error (Figure 5, middle):**
 - Sparse models achieved consistently lower error in low-data regimes compared to dense or overly sparse models.
 - This highlights improved sample efficiency, as sparsity reduces the effective model dimension, leading to faster convergence and better generalization with less data.
- **Payload Release Disturbance (Figure 5, top-right):**
 - Following a simulated payload release, the sparse model exhibited faster error reduction and smoother convergence in tracking, demonstrating the advantage of adapting within a reduced latent subspace.
- **Evolution of Active Latent Dimension (Figure 5, bottom-right):**
 - During nominal operation, only a small number of latent modes were active.
 - Upon disturbance (payload release), additional modes were dynamically activated to capture transient dynamics.
 - Subsequently, the model returned to a lower-dimensional representation as conditions stabilized.
 - This result indicates that the model adaptively adjusts its complexity based on the operating conditions, leveraging sparsity to activate relevant interaction patterns only when needed.

These ablation results collectively suggest that sparsity is crucial for enhancing data efficiency and adaptation capabilities while maintaining sufficient expressiveness, particularly beneficial for systems characterized by strong dynamic coupling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for Euler–Lagrange robotic dynamics that combines physics-aware modeling with sparse learning and selective online adaptation. The findings contribute to the field of robotic control and dynamics learning in several ways:

1. **Addressing Model Inconsistency:** A primary significance of this work is its direct approach to mitigating the physically inconsistent predictions often produced by conventional learning-based dynamics models. By explicitly decomposing model mismatch into structured mechanical corrections (inertia and induced Coriolis terms) and a generalized-force residual, the framework ensures that fundamental physical properties like symmetric positive-definiteness of the inertia matrix and the coupling between inertial and velocity-dependent forces are preserved. This is crucial for developing robust and reliable model-based controllers.

2. **Enhanced Performance in Complex Systems:** The demonstrated improvements in predictive accuracy and trajectory tracking across diverse and complex robotic platforms—including mobile manipulators and aerial manipulators—highlight the framework's effectiveness. These systems often exhibit significant and time-varying coupling, which current methods struggle to model accurately without compromising physical consistency. The ability of the proposed method to perform well under such conditions indicates its potential for broad applicability in advanced robotic systems.

3. **Practical Online Adaptability:** The selective online adaptation mechanism, which updates only the disturbance-sensitive generalized-force component using Bayesian linear regression, offers a practical solution for real-world deployment. This approach allows the model to respond to changing operating conditions (e.g., varying payloads, environmental disturbances) without destabilizing or altering the learned mechanical structure. This selective adaptation ensures that the model remains current and accurate, which is critical for sustained performance in dynamic environments.

4. **Computational and Data Efficiency:** The integration of sparse latent representations significantly contributes to the framework's efficiency. By activating only a limited number of interaction patterns, the model maintains compactness, which is advantageous for real-time inference and reduces the computational cost and data requirements for online adaptation. This parsimonious representation is particularly valuable for resource-constrained robotic platforms.

5. **Improved Interpretability and Reliability:** Models that adhere to physical principles are generally more interpretable and reliable, especially when extrapolating to unseen conditions or states. By preserving the mechanical structure, the proposed framework potentially enhances the transparency and trustworthiness of learned dynamics models, which is important for safety-critical applications and for easier analysis in control design.

**Potential Impact:**
The methodologies presented in this paper could lead to more robust and versatile model-based controllers for a wide range of robotic applications, particularly those operating in dynamic and unpredictable environments. This could include improved performance in areas such as autonomous aerial manipulation, mobile robotics in unstructured terrain, and complex industrial tasks involving variable payloads. By offering a principled way to integrate learning with physics, the work contributes to bridging the gap between data-driven and first-principles modeling in robotics.

**Future Work:**
The authors identify a current limitation in keeping the mechanically structured component fixed during deployment, which could be restrictive under very large or persistent changes in inertial properties. Future research directions include:
- Investigating joint adaptation of structured mechanical terms.
- Developing richer uncertainty-aware updates.
- Extending the framework to contact-rich and higher-dimensional manipulation tasks, which present additional modeling challenges.

These future endeavors suggest a path toward even more comprehensive and adaptable physics-aware learning for robotic systems.
