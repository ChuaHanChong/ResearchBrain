# Synthesizing Physically Plausible Human Motions in 3D Scenes

> [!summary] Summary
> InterScene is a physics-based character control framework that synthesizes long-term human motion in cluttered 3D indoor scenes. It decouples human-scene interaction into two reusable controllers: InterCon, with complementary sit and get-up policies trained via adversarial motion priors, and NavCon, a trajectory-following policy for collision-free paths. A rule-based finite-state scheduler composes them, reaching 98.8% sit success without scene-specific training.

## Problem

- Kinematic data-driven scene-interaction models produce physical artifacts such as object penetration, floating bodies, and foot sliding that break plausibility of generated motion.
- Existing physics-based interaction controllers are trained in environments holding one isolated object, so they cannot handle realistic multi-object cluttered rooms.
- Prior controllers model only entering an interaction, never leaving it, and lack obstacle avoidance, blocking chained long-term tasks across several objects.

## Method

- **InterCon** pairs complementary sit and get-up policies, conditioned on object pose, facing direction, bounding-box vertices, and an explicit root target location, forming a closed-loop interaction skill.
- ==Interaction early termination== ends an episode once root-target overlap exceeds a step threshold, rebalancing sample distribution; seated pose sampling uses the trained sit policy to synthesize penetration-free seated initial states.
- **NavCon** trains a trajectory-following policy on ten future 2D root waypoints; a rule-based ==finite-state scheduler== sequences sit, get-up, and A*-planned path-following actions from user instructions.

## Results

- Sit policy reaches **98.8%** success with 2.5 s execution time, versus **93.7%** for the prior physics-based interaction baseline, **87.5%** for plain adversarial motion priors, and **17.0%** for hierarchical control.
- Interaction early termination drives success monotonically: **87.5%** without it, rising to **92.3%**, **93.9%**, and **98.8%** as the step parameter drops from 90 to 60 to 30, with smaller training variance.
- Get-up policy succeeds **94.6%** from sampled seated states; full-system success across three unseen cluttered scenes ranges **57.0-99.2%** per plan, degrading with scene and plan complexity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing interaction into reusable enter and leave skills plus navigation lets policies trained in simple single-object settings generalize to unseen cluttered scenes without retraining.
> - Early termination is a cheap sample-distribution fix for sparse-reward interaction tasks, improving both final performance and training stability more than richer reward shaping.
> - Bootstrapping data with an upstream policy sidesteps costly motion capture, but open-loop scheduling without replanning or global scene awareness leaves performance brittle in complex layouts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The generation of virtual human motions in diverse daily scenarios constitutes a fundamental area of research within computer vision and graphics. Previous approaches in this field can broadly be categorized into kinematics-based and physics-based methods, each with distinct characteristics and limitations.

Kinematics-based methods, which leverage large-scale motion capture datasets, have been instrumental in generating long-term human motions in 3D indoor scenes. Techniques such as phase-based neural networks (e.g., PFNNs, local motion phases), conditional variational autoencoders (cVAEs), motion diffusion models (MDMs), and reinforcement learning (RL) based generative models have been explored. While these methods can produce complex motion patterns, they frequently encounter physical artifacts, including character penetration with objects, floating above surfaces, and foot sliding, due to their inherent lack of explicit physical enforcement.

In response to these challenges, recent research has explored physics-based character control. These methods utilize physics simulations and motion control techniques, often powered by deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and adversarial motion priors (AMP), to achieve enhanced physical realism. Examples include frameworks for tasks like carrying boxes, sports, and human-scene interactions such as sitting. However, existing physics-based approaches have largely been constrained to simplified environments containing only a single, isolated object. The policies trained with reinforcement learning in these setups exhibit limited modeling capacity, which restricts their applicability and generalization to more complex, multi-object 3D scenes and long-term interaction sequences.

This work positions itself to bridge this gap by extending the capabilities of physics-based character control. It aims to enable physically simulated characters to perform long-term interaction tasks within diverse, cluttered, and previously unseen 3D scenes. The approach seeks to overcome the limitations of prior physics-based methods by allowing for complex interactions with multiple objects and seamless navigation within intricate environments, thereby advancing the realism and applicability of virtual human animation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a physics-based character control framework capable of synthesizing physically plausible long-term motion sequences involving human-scene interactions in complex 3D indoor environments. This overarching goal is motivated by several specific challenges and desiderata in the field of virtual human animation:

1. **Mitigating Physical Artifacts:** A core motivation stems from the persistent issue of physical implausibility in motions generated by data-driven kinematic approaches. Artifacts such as interpenetration between character and scene objects, characters floating above surfaces, and foot sliding reduce the realism of virtual humans. Physics simulation is adopted to inherently address these issues by enforcing physical constraints.

2. **Addressing Limitations of Existing Physics-Based Methods:** While physics-based methods improve realism, prior work has been largely restricted to scenarios involving a single interaction object. These methods struggle to synthesize complex, long-term motion sequences in realistic 3D scenes populated with multiple objects due to the limited modeling capacity of reinforcement learning policies when faced with high-dimensional, cluttered environments. The motivation here is to extend physics-based control to multi-object, dynamic interaction scenarios.

3. **Enabling Long-Term, Chained Interactions:** Realistic human behavior often involves sequences of actions rather than isolated events. Existing methods often focus on specific interaction instances (e.g., sitting down). This work aims to enable characters to perform long-term tasks that require multiple sequential interactions, such as navigating through a room, sitting on one chair, getting up, walking to another, and interacting with it. This necessitates modeling complete interaction cycles, including both entering and leaving an interaction state.

4. **Generalization to Diverse, Cluttered, and Unseen Scenes:** Training robust policies for every possible scene configuration is impractical. A key motivation is to develop a framework where policies trained in relatively simpler, controlled environments can generalize effectively to novel, complex, and cluttered 3D scenes without requiring additional training data for each new environment.

5. **Efficient Learning and Data Utilization:** Learning complex motor skills through reinforcement learning can be challenging due to sparse rewards and the difficulty of obtaining high-quality training data for specific interaction phases. For instance, obtaining diverse, physically plausible seated poses for training "getting up" behaviors is difficult. The research is motivated to introduce techniques that improve training stability and leverage synthetic data generation to overcome data scarcity.

To achieve these objectives, the central idea is to decouple complex human-scene interactions into two fundamental processes: "Interacting" and "Navigating." This decomposition leads to the development of two specialized, reusable controllers, an Interaction Controller (InterCon) and a Navigation Controller (NavCon), orchestrated by a rule-based scheduler. This modular strategy is intended to simplify the learning problem and facilitate generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named InterScene, is designed to synthesize physically plausible long-term human motions in complex 3D environments by decoupling human-scene interactions into distinct, manageable processes.

**System Architecture:**
The system comprises three main components:
1. **Interaction Controller (InterCon):** Manages goal-conditioned interaction behaviors.
2. **Navigation Controller (NavCon):** Manages collision-free locomotion.
3. **Rule-based Action Scheduler:** A high-level planner that coordinates InterCon and NavCon based on user-defined instructions.

The system operates on a physics-based character within the Isaac Gym parallel physics simulator, with the simulation running at 60 Hz and the control policies at 30 Hz. The character model consists of 15 rigid bodies, 12 internal joints, and 28 degrees of freedom (DoF) actuators controlled by proportional-derivative (PD) controllers that convert policy actions into torques. Character states (`s_t`) are represented in a maximal coordinate system (223 dimensions), while states for the discriminator are in a reduced coordinate system (125 dimensions).

**Policy Training Paradigm:**
All control policies are trained using a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning framework combined with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP). The policy `π(a_t | s_t, g_t)` predicts an action `a_t` based on the character state `s_t` and a task-specific goal `g_t`. The reward function `r = w_G r_G + w_S r_S` combines a human-designed task reward (`r_G`) with a naturalness reward (`r_S`) derived from a motion discriminator. Policies, value networks, and discriminators are implemented as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with ReLU activations, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training.

**Interaction Controller (InterCon):**
InterCon is responsible for enabling the character to enter or leave interaction states with objects. It comprises two complementary control policies:
- **Sit Policy (π_s):** Guides the character to sit on a target object.
- **Get-up Policy (π_g):** Enables the character to rise from a seated position.

- **Task-specific Goal State (`g_t`):** This includes `g_obj_t` (object features) and `g_tar_t` (target location). `g_obj_t` contains the target object's position, rotation, facing direction, and bounding box vertices, making the policy and discriminator aware of the object's state. `g_tar_t` is a 3D point representing the desired root location; for sitting, it is 10 cm above the seat center, and for getting up, it is computed online based on foot states.
- **Motion and Object Datasets:** The SAMP dataset provides 111 motion sequences (walking, sitting, getting up), which are manually split for training sit and get-up policies. Objects (chairs, stools, sofas) are sourced from the 3D-Front dataset (90 for training, 30 for testing) and coarsely fitted to motions.
- **Initialization:** For the sit task, Reference State Initialization (RSI) is used, sampling interaction states and randomizing object configurations. Frames with penetration are skipped. For the get-up task, initialization from plausible seated states is crucial.
- **Interaction Early Termination (IET):** To stabilize RL training and balance data distribution, IET is introduced. An episode terminates if the cumulative overlap time between the character's root and the target location exceeds a fixed threshold, in addition to standard fall detection.
- **Training Scheme (Fig 4):**
 1. **Sit Policy Training:** The sit policy is trained first using AMP, IET, and an object-conditioned discriminator.
 2. **Seated Pose Sampling:** The pre-trained sit policy is then used to generate a synthetic database of diverse, high-quality seated poses (300 poses per training object, totaling 300 × 90 poses). This addresses the challenge of obtaining varied seated reference states for the get-up policy.
 3. **Get-up Policy Training:** The get-up policy is trained from scratch, initializing episodes from the synthetically generated seated pose database.
- **Task Rewards (`r_G_t`):** For sitting, the reward combines terms for proximity to the target (`r_near_t`) and velocity alignment towards the object (`r_far_t`), weighted based on distance. For getting up, `r_near_t` is primarily used.

**Navigation Controller (NavCon):**
NavCon provides collision-free locomotion in cluttered environments, complementing InterCon's interaction skills.
- **Trajectory Following Policy (π_f):** Based on prior work, this policy enables the character to track pre-planned paths.
- **Task-specific Goal State (`g_traj_t`):** Consists of a short future path (10 consecutive 2D target positions for the character's root, sampled at 0.1s intervals over 1.0s).
- **Motion and Trajectory Datasets:** Around 200 sequences from the AMASS dataset are used for motion. Target trajectories are procedurally generated and interpolated.
- **Task Reward (`r_G_t`):** An exponential decay function based on the horizontal distance between the character's root and the desired trajectory point (Eq 5).

**Rule-based Action Scheduler:**
This component acts as a high-level planner, translating user instructions (sequences of actions like "sit on object W1", "get up from W1", "follow path H1") into a series of explicit control signals. It schedules the execution of InterCon and NavCon policies and manages transitions between actions (e.g., terminating an action when the character's root overlaps with the target for a sufficient duration).

**Extensibility (Supplementary Material):**
The modularity of the system is demonstrated by integrating a new "lie down" interaction controller. This involves training separate lie-down and get-up policies for sofas, using a similar training scheme and adapted task rewards, showcasing the framework's capacity to incorporate additional interaction skills.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides quantitative and qualitative evaluations of the proposed system's effectiveness across individual tasks and long-term motion synthesis in complex environments.

**Individual Tasks (Evaluated in single-object environments across 3 models, each with 4096 trials):**

- **Sit Task:**
 - The proposed method achieved a success rate of 98.8%, an average execution time of 2.5 seconds, and a target error of 36.8 mm.
 - This performance demonstrated an improvement over existing physics-based methods: AMP yielded 87.5% success, 4.0s execution, and 36.5mm error, while InterPhys achieved 93.7% success, 3.7s execution, and 90.0mm error. The results suggest that the integration of interaction early termination (IET) and an object-conditioned discriminator contributes to enhanced performance.
- **Get-up Task:**
 - The get-up policy, trained using the synthetic seated pose database generated by the pre-trained sit policy, achieved a success rate of 94.6%. This validates the effectiveness of the "seated pose sampling" strategy in addressing the challenge of acquiring diverse, high-quality seated reference motions for training.
- **Ablation Studies on IET:**
 - Experiments with varying IET step parameters (30, 60, 90 steps, and no IET) indicated that IET improves task performance. A smaller IET step parameter (30 steps) resulted in the highest success rate (98.8%) and most stable training, characterized by a reduced fluctuation margin in the learning curves. This suggests that IET effectively balances the data distribution during reinforcement learning.

**Long-term Motion Synthesis (Evaluated in multi-object, cluttered 3D scenes):**

- **Qualitative Evaluation:** The complete system successfully generated long-term motion sequences in three challenging 3D indoor scenes (e.g., a man sitting on multiple chairs, getting up, and resting on a sofa). This outcome supports the proposed "divide and conquer" strategy, demonstrating that policies trained in simplified environments can generalize to unseen and complex multi-object scenes.
- **Quantitative Evaluation:** Success rates for complete plans in multi-object scenes varied, ranging from 57.0% to 99.2% depending on the specific scene and plan complexity (Table 3). The performance was noted to be influenced by the intricacy of the environment and the sequence of actions.
- **Ablation Studies on NavCon:**
 - A qualitative comparison between the complete system and a variant without NavCon highlighted the importance of the navigation controller. Without NavCon, characters attempting to reach a target object in a cluttered scene frequently became stuck due to collisions with obstacles, as the interaction controller alone tends to follow the shortest path. With NavCon, the character successfully navigated around obstacles to perform interactions.

**Extensibility:**
- The supplementary material demonstrated the system's extensibility by introducing new "lie down" and "get up" policies specifically for sofas. These policies were successfully trained and seamlessly integrated into the existing framework. This allowed the extended system to synthesize more diverse long-term interactions, such as lying on a sofa, then sitting on a chair, and finally lying on a bed (Fig 10).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for synthesizing physically plausible human motions in 3D scenes, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts on the fields of computer vision, graphics, and robotics.

**Key Contributions:**
1. **Enabling Long-Term Interactions in Complex Scenes:** The primary contribution is a system that allows physically simulated characters to execute long-term, multi-action interaction tasks within diverse, cluttered, and previously unseen 3D indoor environments. This addresses a significant limitation of prior physics-based methods that were often confined to single-object scenarios.
2. **Decoupled Control Architecture:** The introduction of two reusable controllers—InterCon for interaction and NavCon for navigation—effectively decouples complex human-scene interactions. This modular approach simplifies the learning problem, as individual policies can be trained in simpler environments.
3. **Generalization through Modular Design:** The divide-and-conquer strategy, combined with the rule-based scheduler, enables the trained policies to generalize to complex multi-object scenes without requiring additional, expensive training for each new environment.
4. **Enhanced Training Stability and Data Efficiency:** Techniques such as Interaction Early Termination (IET) and Seated Pose Sampling contribute to more stable reinforcement learning training and mitigate data scarcity issues, particularly for challenging states like getting up from a seated position.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Virtual Characters and Animation:** The framework can lead to more realistic, natural, and diverse behaviors for virtual characters in applications such as video games, virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and film production, where physically accurate interactions are crucial for immersion.
- **Robotics and Human-Robot Interaction:** The learned skills for robust human-scene interaction and navigation could potentially inform the development of more capable and adaptable physical robots or virtual agents in shared human environments.
- **Simulation and Training:** The ability to simulate physically plausible human behaviors in complex environments could be valuable for training AI agents, testing architectural designs, or studying human movement in various contexts.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The modular architecture and the techniques developed for training and generalization provide a foundation for further research into incorporating more complex skills, improving robustness in dynamic or abnormal situations, and enhancing global environmental awareness for autonomous agents.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors identify several areas for future investigation. The current system's reliance on AMP might limit the diversity of learned behaviors, suggesting a need for conditional generation capabilities. The robustness in abnormal situations, where characters might deviate from pre-planned paths and collide, is a challenge that could be addressed by closed-loop systems with periodic re-planning. Furthermore, policies currently only observe the immediate target object, indicating that incorporating more global representations of the cluttered environment would enhance performance. The framework also currently does not account for more complex real-world environments, such as dynamic scenes, uneven terrains, multi-floor buildings, or narrow spaces, which represent avenues for expansion towards more autonomous and versatile agents.
