# R2S-Eval: Robot Evaluation with Real-to-Sim Calibration via Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> R2S-Eval evaluates robot manipulation policies by combining real-to-sim calibration with vision-language-model preference judging. A calibrated Isaac Sim replica of the real setup generates closed-loop rollout videos cheaply; a VLM writes structured behavior descriptions, emits anonymized pairwise preferences, and Bradley-Terry aggregation turns them into policy rankings. Across seven real tasks and six VLAs, rankings reach Spearman 0.957 and 91.9% human agreement while eliminating repeated hardware trials.

## Problem

- Hardware evaluation loops demand per-trial scene setup, resets, and operator monitoring, so manual effort grows linearly with tasks and trials per policy.
- Repeated real-world rankings are unstable: small shifts in object placement, contact, perception, or robot state flip rollout outcomes and reorder policies.
- Success rate is too coarse; policies with identical binary labels can differ sharply in motion quality, recovery behavior, or progress made before failing.

## Method

- ==Real-to-sim calibration== matches robot kinematics, cameras, objects, and initializations, then acquires simulated data via a retrying ==action-replay loop== and trains simulation-adapted counterpart policies.
- ==Structured VLM judgment==: the model first writes a behavior description covering smoothness, temporal continuity, progress, and contact control, then outputs a binary preference over anonymized, position-randomized video pairs.
- ==Bradley-Terry aggregation== fits latent policy strengths from the pairwise preference matrix by ==minorization-maximization== with a sum-to-one constraint, yielding scores and rankings plus bootstrap confidence intervals.

## Results

- Calibrated simulation reproduced real success rates within **2.13 points** mean absolute difference (max **7.0**) across six VLAs and seven tasks, preserving the same overall policy ranking.
- Real-to-sim preference evaluation averaged Spearman **0.957**, Pearson **0.978**, MMRV **0.005**, and **91.9%** human agreement over eight VLM judges; LIBERO gave **0.823** / **0.924** / **82.9%**.
- Conventional hardware evaluation cost **26.9 operator hours** at 20 trials across 7 tasks and scaled linearly, while order-swapped A/B judgments stayed consistent **91.5%** of the time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pairwise preference over full rollout videos recovers execution-quality distinctions that binary success discards, separating a clean grasp from a retry-then-succeed trajectory.
> - Judge quality was insensitive to VLM scale and family: 4B models matched 27-32B ones, so evaluation cost need not scale with judge size.
> - Calibrated simulation need not be photorealistic or behaviorally identical; it only has to preserve comparative ordering, which is a weaker and more attainable requirement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the broader landscape of robot learning and evaluation, specifically addressing challenges in assessing the performance of generalist manipulation policies, such as vision-language-action (VLA) models, when deployed on physical robots.

Current methodologies for evaluating robot policies typically involve fine-tuning on task-specific data and deploying policies in real-world environments. Performance is predominantly measured by task success rates, often collected through repeated hardware trials. This approach has been identified as labor-intensive, susceptible to variability due to environmental factors, and limited in the information it provides, as a binary success outcome does not capture the qualitative aspects of execution.

Existing research has explored various forms of robot evaluation, including standardized simulation benchmarks (e.g., LIBERO, RLBench, RoboSuite), automated real-world testing systems (e.g., AutoEval, RoboArena), and proxy evaluations using simulators or learned world models. While these efforts aim to streamline or scale evaluation, they frequently still prioritize success rates or scalar scores, often neglecting nuanced behavioral differences and the stability of policy rankings. Large-scale embodied benchmarks, such such as BEHAVIOR-1K, have expanded the scope to long-horizon activities, yet continue to largely rely on task completion metrics.

The paper identifies a gap in existing methods: the lack of an efficient, stable, and informative evaluation pipeline that aligns with human judgment of robot behavior quality. Humans evaluate robot performance by observing and comparing complete behaviors, discerning quality differences even among successful or failed rollouts, a capability that binary success metrics do not provide. While vision-language models (VLMs) have been used for tasks like success detection, progress estimation, and failure diagnosis, their application in aggregating pairwise preferences into statistically grounded and validated policy rankings, reflecting execution quality beyond mere success, remains an underexplored area. Real-to-sim (R2S) methods are widely used for environment reconstruction and policy training, but their integration into a validated, quality-aware evaluation pipeline for generating real-world consistent behavior videos efficiently is a key contribution of this work.

R2S-Eval integrates real-to-sim calibration techniques for efficient behavior video generation with VLM-based preference evaluation and a multi-axis validation protocol. This approach addresses the limitations of conventional evaluation by providing a method to assess execution quality, stability, and human agreement, thereby advancing robot evaluation toward a more automated, statistically robust, and behaviorally informative paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of generalist manipulation policies, particularly vision-language-action (VLA) models, on physical robots. Effective evaluation of these policies is crucial for understanding their performance, identifying strengths and weaknesses, guiding data collection, and informing policy improvements. However, the authors identify several critical limitations with conventional real-world robot evaluation methods:

1. **Labor-Intensiveness**: Current real-world evaluation protocols require substantial manual effort. Each trial necessitates scene setup, robot execution, object resetting, and continuous operator monitoring. This manual overhead becomes a significant bottleneck as the complexity and scale of robot tasks and policies increase.
2. **Instability and Lack of Reproducibility**: Policy rankings obtained from real-world evaluations can be inconsistent. Minor variations in environmental conditions—such as object placement, contact dynamics, perception noise, or initial robot state—can lead to different rollout outcomes, making it challenging to repeat rankings reliably.
3. **Limited Informativeness**: The standard metric of binary task success rate (success/failure) provides a coarse understanding of policy performance. It offers little insight into the quality of execution, such as motion smoothness, efficiency, corrective behaviors, or the extent of progress made before a failure. Policies with similar success rates might exhibit substantially different behavioral characteristics, which are overlooked by binary metrics.

These limitations highlight a disparity between current evaluation practices and human assessment. Humans naturally observe and compare entire robot behaviors, distinguishing qualitative differences even when the final outcome (success or failure) is the same. This observation motivates the need for an evaluation approach that captures a more complete view of execution quality.

To address these challenges, the research sets forth the following key objectives:

- **Reformulate Evaluation**: To shift the paradigm of robot manipulation policy evaluation from solely counting binary successes to estimating policy rankings based on preferences over complete rollout behaviors. This aims to capture execution quality beyond simple task completion.
- **Develop an Integrated Pipeline (R2S-Eval)**: To propose and implement a novel evaluation pipeline that addresses two core practical problems of video-based preference evaluation:
 1. **Efficient Behavior Video Acquisition**: To develop a "real-to-sim calibration" component that generates rollout videos in a simulator effectively calibrated to the real-world evaluation setting. This aims to significantly reduce the reliance on costly and time-consuming repeated hardware trials.
 2. **Automated Quality Judgment**: To integrate a "Vision-Language Model (VLM) preference evaluation" component capable of automatically assessing the execution quality of rollout videos and generating pairwise preferences, which are then aggregated into robust policy rankings.
- **Establish a Validation Protocol**: To introduce a multi-axis validation protocol to rigorously assess the proposed evaluation pipeline. This protocol specifically examines the reliability and stability of policy conclusions, agreement with human preferences, reduction in manual hardware-operation effort, and the capacity to reveal behavioral information beyond binary success counts.
- **Advance Robot Evaluation**: Ultimately, to contribute to the advancement of robot evaluation methodologies by moving towards an automated, statistically stable, and quality-aware assessment of robot manipulation behavior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The R2S-Eval pipeline is designed to evaluate robot manipulation policies by integrating real-to-sim calibration with VLM-based preference evaluation. The methodology is structured into problem formulation, pipeline components, and a validation protocol.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is defined as ranking a set of candidate manipulation policies, Π = {π_1..., π_N}, under a target evaluation distribution D.
- **Conventional Evaluation**: Policies are ranked by their empirical success rates, `ˆs_succ_i`, derived from binary success outcomes `c(τ) ∈ {0, 1}` for each rollout `τ`. This method disregards behavioral nuances.
- **R2S-Eval Approach**: Policy evaluation is reframed as preference estimation over rollout behaviors. For each policy `π_i`, a set of rollout videos `V_i = {v_1_i, ..., v_K_i_i}` is generated. A preference judge provides a binary preference `y_ab_ij ∈ {i ≻ j, j ≻ i}` for sampled video pairs `(v_a_i, v_b_j)`. These preferences are aggregated into a matrix `M`, where `M_ij` counts preferences for `π_i` over `π_j`.
- **Statistical Preference Model**: The Bradley–Terry (BT) model is used to infer a policy ranking from `M`. This model posits that the probability `Pr_θ(π_i ≻ π_j)` is proportional to `exp(θ_i) / (exp(θ_i) + exp(θ_j))`, where `θ_i` is the latent quality score of `π_i`. Policy scores `ˆθ` are estimated by maximizing the log-likelihood of `M`, and the final ranking `r_pref` is derived from these scores.

#### 3.2. Real-to-Sim Calibration

This component aims to efficiently generate behavior videos that are consistent with real-world scenarios, thereby reducing the need for extensive real-world hardware trials.
- **Goal**: To establish a simulated evaluation distribution `D_S` that is calibrated to the real-world evaluation distribution `D_R`. This does not require a perfect digital twin but focuses on calibrating factors critical to policy behavior.
- **Calibration Factors**:
 - **Robot**: Simulated robot geometry, kinematics, joint limits, and control interface are matched to the physical robot.
 - **Tasks and Objects**: Simulated tasks replicate real-world counterparts. Objects are reconstructed as 3D assets (e.g., USD meshes from 3D scanning) and placed according to pose estimates.
 - **Cameras**: Camera viewpoints are calibrated to match the real-world setup.
- **Video Acquisition Stages**:
 1. **Adaptive Policy Training**: To mitigate residual real-to-sim discrepancies (e.g., friction, compliance, sensing noise), calibrated simulation trajectories are obtained through action replay, motion planning, or teleoperation. Candidate real-world policies (`π_R_i`) are adapted to generate simulation-side counterparts (`π_S_i`). The comparative ranking of `{π_S_i}` in simulation serves as a proxy for the hardware ranking of `{π_R_i}`.
 2. **Closed-Loop Simulation Deployment**: Each `π_S_i` is deployed in the calibrated simulation environment to execute tasks autonomously.
 3. **Rollout Video Collection**: Videos `V_i` are recorded directly from these closed-loop simulation executions.

#### 3.3. VLM Preference Evaluation

This component focuses on automating the judgment of behavior quality from videos, aiming to align with human preferences.
- **Pairwise Video Sampling**: For each policy pair `(π_i, π_j)`, a video pair `(v_a_i, v_b_j)` is sampled, with `v_a_i ∈ V_i` and `v_b_j ∈ V_j`. Critically, comparisons are made for videos depicting the same task and initial configuration, and each task contributes equally to the total comparisons to avoid confounding factors.
- **Structured VLM Judgment**: A Vision-Language Model acts as an automated judge.
 1. **Description Generation**: For each individual video `v_a_i` and its instruction `x`, the VLM (`G_desc`) generates a structured textual description `d_a_i`. This description details motion smoothness, temporal continuity, task progress, and observable force/effort control.
 2. **Preference Judgment**: For a pair of anonymized videos (`v_A`, `v_B`), the VLM (`G_pref`) compares their corresponding descriptions (`d_A`, `d_B`) based on the predefined criteria. It outputs a binary preference `y_AB ∈ {A ≻ B, B ≻ A}`. This two-step process grounds the judgment in explicit execution evidence rather than solely on task success. The task-level win counts are then pooled into the preference matrix `M`.
- **Bradley–Terry Preference Aggregation**: The collected preferences in `M` are aggregated using the Bradley–Terry model. The model fits strength parameters `b_i` (where `θ_i = log b_i`) to represent the latent quality of each policy, with the sum of `b_i` normalized to 1 for identifiability. The Minorization-Maximization algorithm is used for fitting, and the estimated `ˆθ` values determine the final policy ranking `r_pref`.

#### 3.4. Validation Protocol

A multi-axis validation protocol is introduced to systematically evaluate the performance of the entire R2S-Eval pipeline:
1. **Reliability**: Assesses whether R2S-Eval produces policy conclusions consistent with a reference ranking that reflects actual policy capabilities in the target setting.
2. **Stability**: Examines if estimated policy scores and rankings converge and exhibit low variance as more evaluation evidence accumulates.
3. **Human Agreement**: Measures the alignment between VLM-generated preferences and human judgments, ensuring the VLM reflects human assessment.
4. **Human Effort**: Quantifies the reduction in manual labor required for hardware execution, scene resetting, and monitoring compared to conventional real-world evaluation.
5. **Behavioral Informativeness**: Determines if the evaluation captures nuanced differences in progress, recovery, or control quality, particularly among rollouts with identical binary success/failure outcomes.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform**: SharpaNorth, a dual-arm humanoid with 63 DoFs, equipped with head and wrist-mounted RGB cameras.
- **Task Suite**: Seven tabletop manipulation tasks (e.g., "Cover Ball with Cup," "Stack Blocks") with random object initializations.
- **VLA Policies**: Six different VLA models are evaluated: π_0, π_0.5, OpenVLA, NORA-Long, SmolVLA, and X-VLA.
- **Data Collection**: 3,525 real-world teleoperated trajectories were collected using an exoskeleton and VR headset.
- **Simulation Environment**: Built in NVIDIA Isaac Sim. Real-world action sequences were replayed in the calibrated simulator with an automatic replay loop to manage sensitivity to initial object poses. Policies `π_R_i` (real) and `π_S_i` (simulated counterpart) were trained independently using identical recipes.
- **VLM Evaluators**: Eight different VLMs were tested, including Qwen3-VL (4B, 8B, 32B), LLaVA-OV-1.5 (4B, 8B), and Gemma 3 (4B, 12B, 27B).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted both in simulation (LIBERO benchmark) and real-to-sim settings addressed the research questions and validated the R2S-Eval pipeline across its defined axes.

#### 4.1. Real-to-Sim Calibration Results (Q1: Hardware-consistent policy conclusions)

- **Action Replay Success**: The real-to-sim calibration achieved a high average success rate of 97.8% for replaying real-world action sequences in the simulated environment. This indicates that the simulated environment largely replicated the conditions for successful task execution.
- **Closed-Loop Real–Sim Consistency**: Independent closed-loop rollouts of real-world policies (`π_R_i`) and their simulation-adapted counterparts (`π_S_i`) showed closely matched task-level success rates. The mean absolute difference in success rates across all policy-task pairs was 2.13 percentage points, with a maximum discrepancy of 7.0 percentage points.
- **Ranking Agreement**: Crucially, the real-world and simulated evaluations produced the same overall policy ranking. Policies that performed well in the real world (π_0, π_0.5, X-VLA) also clearly outperformed others in simulation. Qualitatively similar failure modes were observed in both domains.
- **Conclusion**: The calibrated simulation proved sufficient to preserve comparative policy conclusions, confirming its utility as a proxy for real-world evaluation regarding policy ranking.

#### 4.2. VLM Preference Evaluation Results (Q2: Reflects actual performance; Q3: Stability; Q4: Human agreement)

- **Reliability (Q2)**:
 - **LIBERO (Simulation)**: VLM-derived rankings showed high consistency with success-rate rankings. The average Spearman's ρ was 0.823, and Pearson r was 0.924, with a low Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV) of 0.018. Minor inversions occurred only among policies with very close performance.
 - **Real-to-Sim**: In this setting, where policies exhibited larger performance differences on the real robot, the inferred VLM rankings were even more consistent. Average Spearman's ρ reached 0.957, Pearson r was 0.978, and MMRV was 0.005. The VLM successfully distinguished small performance gaps, such as between π_0.5 and X-VLA, which had nearly identical real-world success rates.
- **Stability (Q3)**:
 - The Bradley–Terry (BT) confidence intervals were narrow (average 1.337% for LIBERO, 1.757% for real-to-sim), indicating that policy scores were estimated robustly with low uncertainty. Convergence curves demonstrated that policy scores and rankings stabilized effectively with increasing video comparisons.
- **Human Agreement (Q4)**:
 - VLM judgments demonstrated strong agreement with human preferences. The average human agreement rate was 82.9% for LIBERO and 91.9% for real-to-sim, suggesting that VLMs can effectively capture human-like qualitative assessments of robot behavior.
- **Generalizability of VLMs**: All eight evaluated VLMs, despite architectural and scale differences, exhibited similar performance in terms of reliability and human agreement, indicating the pipeline's robustness across diverse VLM backbones.
- **Order-Swap Consistency**: When the A/B presentation order of video pairs was reversed, the preferred video remained unchanged in 91.5% of cases, suggesting low positional bias from VLMs, though a slight bias was noted for nearly tied pairs.

#### 4.3. Hardware-Operation Effort (Q3: Mitigation of manual cost)

- **Reduction in Manual Effort**: The study quantified the repeated operator time for conventional real-world evaluation. It showed a roughly linear increase in operator hours with more tasks or trials (e.g., approximately 27 hours for 20 trials per task across 7 tasks). In contrast, R2S-Eval significantly reduces this burden by requiring real-world hardware effort primarily for initial data collection and calibration, not for repeated policy evaluations.

#### 4.4. Behavior Case Study (Q5: Behavioral informativeness)

- **Beyond Binary Success**: Case studies demonstrated R2S-Eval's ability to differentiate execution quality even when binary success labels were identical.
 - **Both-Success Case (LIBERO)**: The VLM preferred a rollout that grasped and placed an object smoothly in a single continuous execution over one that succeeded only after an initial failed grasp and a retry.
 - **Both-Failure Case (Real World)**: The VLM preferred a failed rollout that showed progress by attempting manipulation inside a box over another failed rollout that exhibited only small, non-progressive oscillatory motions.
- **Conclusion**: VLM preference evaluation leverages cues from the entire execution process, including task progress, motion continuity, and control quality, providing behavioral insights that binary success rates cannot.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The R2S-Eval pipeline presents a significant advancement in the evaluation of robot manipulation policies, with several potential impacts on the field:

- **Enhanced Evaluation Paradigm**: R2S-Eval shifts the focus of robot policy assessment from a coarse, binary success/failure metric to a more nuanced, quality-aware preference-based ranking. This aligns robot evaluation more closely with human judgment, providing richer insights into behavioral aspects like motion smoothness, temporal continuity, and effort control, which are crucial for developing robust and dexterous robots.
- **Reduced Resource Burden and Increased Scalability**: By leveraging real-to-sim calibration for efficient video generation, R2S-Eval substantially reduces the reliance on laborious and costly real-world hardware trials. This lowers the barrier to entry for conducting extensive evaluations, enabling researchers and developers to iterate on policies more rapidly and cost-effectively. This is particularly impactful for generalist VLA models, which require broad and continuous evaluation.
- **Improved Evaluation Stability and Reliability**: The statistical preference aggregation via the Bradley–Terry model, combined with validation metrics like narrow confidence intervals and low rank violation, demonstrates that R2S-Eval yields more stable and reliable policy conclusions compared to conventional methods. This consistency is critical for making informed decisions about policy selection and improvement.
- **Accelerated Policy Development**: A more informative and efficient evaluation system can accelerate the development cycle of robot policies. By clearly identifying strengths, subtle failure modes, and qualitative performance differences, R2S-Eval can guide data collection, policy training adjustments, and architectural improvements more effectively than traditional success-rate tracking.
- **Validation of Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The work rigorously validates the consistency between simulated and real-world policy conclusions for comparative ranking. This finding reinforces the potential of calibrated simulations not just for training but also for reliable evaluation, paving the way for more trust in sim-to-real transfer strategies.
- **Broader Applicability of VLMs**: The demonstrated high agreement between VLM judgments and human preferences, along with the pipeline's generalization across various VLM backbones, suggests a powerful and versatile application of vision-language models as objective, automated evaluators for complex robotic behaviors. This could inspire further research into VLM-driven assessment across various domains.
- **Future Research Directions**: The work lays a foundation for future research, including extending R2S-Eval to a wider array of tasks and robot embodiments, systematically analyzing and minimizing real-to-sim discrepancies, and developing higher-fidelity simulations that further close the reality gap. These extensions could expand the reach and accuracy of automated robot evaluation.

In summary, R2S-Eval represents a significant step towards a scalable, automated, and behavior-aware evaluation framework for robot manipulation policies, with the potential to fundamentally improve how generalist robots are developed and deployed.
