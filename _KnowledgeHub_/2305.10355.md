# Evaluating Object Hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This work presents the first systematic study of object hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), demonstrating that this issue is severe and linked to biases in visual instruction tuning data. The authors introduce POPE, a polling-based evaluation method, which offers a more stable and reliable assessment of hallucination, revealing that some LVLMs exhibit significant overconfidence and higher hallucination rates than previous models.

## Problem

- Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) frequently generate descriptions that include objects not present in the image, a phenomenon known as object hallucination.
- There was a lack of systematic empirical study specifically addressing object hallucination in the new generation of LVLMs.
- Existing evaluation methods for object hallucination, such as CHAIR, were found to be unstable, highly sensitive to prompt variations, and unsuitable for the open-ended nature of LVLMs.

## Method

- Conducted the first systematic empirical study on ==object hallucination== across several representative LVLMs using both existing (==CHAIR==) and novel evaluation methods.
- Investigated the underlying causes of object hallucination by analyzing the influence of ==object frequency== and ==co-occurrence== within visual instruction tuning datasets.
- Proposed a novel and robust evaluation method named **POPE** (==Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation==) that reformulates hallucination assessment into a ==binary (Yes/No) classification task== using targeted object probes with random, popular, and adversarial ==negative sampling==.

## Results

- Most instruction-tuned LVLMs (e.g., LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT) suffer from severe object hallucination, with some exhibiting higher rates than smaller VLPMs; InstructBLIP was an exception with significantly lower hallucination.
- LVLMs show a strong tendency to hallucinate objects that frequently appear (top 30 most frequent objects accounted for **~70%** of all hallucinated objects) or frequently co-occur with ground-truth objects in their training data.
- POPE demonstrated superior stability (LLaVA F1 std **0.78** vs. CHAIR_I std **3.22**) and consistently identified hallucination trends across models, also revealing an extreme overconfidence bias in some LVLMs to answer 'Yes' (near **99%**) to probing questions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object hallucination is a severe and widespread problem in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), with some models showing even higher hallucination rates than smaller, traditional vision-language models.
> - Object hallucination in LVLMs is strongly correlated with the frequency of objects and their co-occurrence patterns within the visual instruction tuning data.
> - The proposed Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation (POPE) method offers a more stable, flexible, and reliable way to assess object hallucination compared to prior methods, accurately reflecting model tendencies and biases.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper is situated at the forefront of the rapidly evolving field of **Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs)**, which represents a convergence of advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and traditional Vision-Language Pre-trained Models (VLPMs). The success of LLMs in processing and generating human-like text, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in complex tasks like instruction following and zero-shot learning, has inspired researchers to extend these capabilities to the multi-modal domain. Models like GPT-4, Flamingo, LLaVA, MiniGPT-4, and InstructBLIP exemplify this trend, integrating powerful LLMs with visual encoders to enhance visual semantics understanding and multi-modal reasoning.

Despite the impressive progress and performance gains demonstrated by LVLMs across tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering, a critical challenge persists: **hallucination**. Hallucination, broadly defined as the generation of content inconsistent with ground truth, is a known issue in both LLMs (leading to factually incorrect or nonsensical text) and VLPMs (manifesting as non-existent objects in image descriptions). The paper notes that VLPMs, in particular, suffer from "object hallucination," where they generate descriptions containing objects not present in the image. Given that LVLMs integrate these components, it becomes a crucial question whether they inherit or even exacerbate these hallucination tendencies.

Prior to this work, a systematic investigation into object hallucination specifically within LVLMs was largely missing. Existing evaluation methods, such as CHAIR (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance), primarily designed for traditional image captioning models (VLPMs), have limitations when applied to the open-ended and instruction-sensitive nature of LVLMs. These limitations include sensitivity to prompt variations, reliance on complex text parsing, and potential bias towards caption length.

This paper fills a significant gap by presenting the **first systematic study on object hallucination in LVLMs**. It moves beyond merely acknowledging the problem to quantitatively assessing its severity across several representative state-of-the-art LVLMs. Furthermore, it delves into the *causes* of hallucination in these models, particularly investigating the influence of visual instruction tuning data, which is a hallmark of LVLM training. By proposing a novel, more robust evaluation method (POPE), the work also contributes a valuable tool for the research community to consistently and reliably measure this critical reliability aspect. Therefore, this paper not only sheds light on a prevalent and dangerous flaw in cutting-edge multi-modal AI but also provides new methodologies and insights to guide future research towards more reliable and human-aligned LVLMs. It directly contributes to the ongoing efforts to make AI systems more trustworthy and deployable in real-world applications where hallucination could have severe consequences.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observed phenomenon of "object hallucination" in newly developed Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), despite their otherwise impressive capabilities. While LLMs and even previous Vision-Language Pre-trained Models (VLPMs) were known to suffer from various forms of hallucination, a systematic evaluation of this specific issue in the context of LVLMs was lacking. The authors identify this as a critical problem because hallucinated content—descriptions or objects inconsistent with the target image—can significantly degrade model performance and, more importantly, severely compromise user experience and safety in real-world applications (e.g., an autonomous driving system hallucinating an obstacle could lead to dangerous decisions).

Against this backdrop, the paper sets forth three key objectives:

1. **To conduct the first systematic empirical study on object hallucination in representative LVLMs:** The authors aim to quantitatively assess the prevalence and severity of object hallucination across several leading LVLMs. This involves using existing metrics like CHAIR to establish a baseline understanding of how pervasive this problem is in the current generation of models. The initial hypothesis, driven by observations of LLM and VLPM hallucination, is that LVLMs might also exhibit significant object hallucination, potentially even more so due to the complexity introduced by large language models.

2. **To investigate the potential reasons behind object hallucination in LVLMs, particularly the influence of visual instruction tuning data:** Moving beyond mere observation, a core objective is to understand *why* LVLMs hallucinate. The authors hypothesize that the visual instruction-tuning process, crucial for LVLMs' superior performance, might inadvertently exacerbate hallucination. Specifically, they investigate if the frequency of object appearance and co-occurrence within the instruction datasets biases LVLMs towards hallucinating common or contextually associated objects, even when absent from the image. This objective seeks to uncover mechanistic insights into the problem, which can inform future model development.

3. **To propose a more stable, flexible, and reliable evaluation method for object hallucination in LVLMs:** The authors recognize that existing evaluation methods, such as CHAIR, are not optimally suited for LVLMs. They are found to be highly sensitive to instruction design, dependent on caption length, and require complex parsing rules to extract objects, leading to instability and potential misclassification. Therefore, a crucial objective is to design a novel evaluation approach, one that is less susceptible to these external factors, easier to implement, and more accurately reflects an LVLM's tendency to hallucinate. This new method should ideally be stable across different prompts and scalable to unannotated datasets.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims not only to highlight a critical reliability challenge in LVLMs but also to provide foundational insights and practical tools for the AI community to understand, measure, and ultimately mitigate object hallucination, thereby paving the way for safer and more trustworthy multi-modal AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach, combining empirical evaluation with a novel methodological contribution, to systematically study object hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs).

#### 3.1. Initial Evaluation of Hallucination Severity (Section 3)

The study begins by evaluating the severity of object hallucination in several representative LVLMs: mPLUG-Owl, LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT, MiniGPT-4, and InstructBLIP.
- **Metric:** The established CHAIR (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance) metric is used, specifically its two variants: CHAIR_I (object instance level) and CHAIR_S (sentence level). CHAIR measures the proportion of objects mentioned in a generated caption that are not present in the ground-truth image.
- **Dataset:** Experiments are conducted on the MSCOCO dataset, a widely used benchmark for image understanding tasks.
- **Prompting:** LVLMs are prompted with two different instructions (I1: "Generate a short caption of the image." and I2: "Provide a brief description of the given image.") to generate captions, which are then analyzed.
- **Analysis of Limitations:** This initial evaluation also serves to expose the weaknesses of CHAIR when applied to LVLMs, specifically its instability due to instruction variations and its susceptibility to caption length. The reliance on complex human-crafted parsing rules for exact object matching is also highlighted as a disadvantage.

#### 3.2. Investigating the Influence of Instruction Data (Section 4)

To understand *why* LVLMs hallucinate, the authors propose and verify two hypotheses related to the visual instruction datasets used for training:
- **Hypothesis 1:** LVLMs are prone to hallucinate objects that frequently appear in the visual instruction datasets.
- **Hypothesis 2:** LVLMs are prone to hallucinate objects that frequently co-occur with ground-truth objects present in the image.
- **Verification:**
 - **Qualitative Analysis:** Bar charts are used to visualize the hallucination times of frequently appearing objects and objects frequently co-occurring with a specific object ("dining table") in MSCOCO, demonstrating a clear correlation.
 - **Quantitative Analysis:** The "top-k hit ratio" (HR@k) is introduced as a metric to quantify these correlations. HR_A@k measures the proportion of hallucinated objects that are among the top-k most frequently appearing objects. HR_C@k(o) measures the proportion of hallucinated objects that are among the top-k most frequent co-occurring objects with a given ground-truth object 'o'. This provides statistical evidence for the hypotheses.

#### 3.3. Developing POPE: Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation (Section 5)

Based on the identified limitations of CHAIR and insights into hallucination causes, the authors propose a novel evaluation method called POPE.
- **Core Idea:** POPE transforms the object hallucination evaluation into a binary (Yes/No) classification task. Instead of generating a full caption and then parsing it, LVLMs are directly asked simple questions like "Is there a <object> in the image?".
- **Definition:** An evaluation triple `⟨x, {q(oi), ai}⟩` is constructed, consisting of an image (x), probing questions q(oi) about object oi, and the corresponding "Yes" or "No" answer (ai).
- **Object Sourcing:** Ground-truth objects are obtained from human annotations. Non-existent (negative) objects are sampled based on specific strategies.
- **Negative Sampling Strategies:** Three strategies are designed to probe different aspects of hallucination:
 - **Random Sampling:** Non-existent objects are chosen randomly.
 - **Popular Sampling:** Top-k most frequent objects in the dataset that are *not* in the image are selected. This tests the hypothesis about frequently appearing objects.
 - **Adversarial Sampling:** Top-k objects that frequently co-occur with ground-truth objects *but are not* in the image are selected. This tests the hypothesis about co-occurrence.
- **Metrics:** Standard classification metrics are used: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 score (as the primary metric), and the percentage of "Yes" answers to analyze model bias.
- **Advantages of POPE:**
 - **Stability:** Less sensitive to variations in prompt templates due to the simple closed-ended "Yes/No" format.
 - **Scalability:** Can be extended to unannotated datasets by utilizing automatic segmentation tools like SEEM to identify objects.
 - **Consistency:** The "Yes/No" responses are shown to align well with objects mentioned in model-generated captions.

#### 3.4. Impact on Other Vision Tasks (Section 5.4)

Finally, the study investigates the relationship between object hallucination (as measured by POPE) and performance on other vision-language tasks, specifically Visual Question Answering (VQA) and image captioning.
- **VQA Evaluation:** SEEM-based POPE and VQA scores are compared on A-OKVQA and GQA datasets, with ChatGPT assisting in parsing open-ended VQA responses.
- **Image Captioning Evaluation:** Traditional image captioning metrics (BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE-L) are used to assess the quality of generated captions on the same subset of images used for POPE.

This comprehensive methodological framework allows for both a deep understanding of the hallucination problem in LVLMs and the development of a robust new tool for its evaluation, contributing significantly to the field's ability to measure and address model reliability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yields several significant findings regarding object hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), its underlying causes, and the efficacy of the proposed evaluation method.

#### 4.1. Severity of Object Hallucination in LVLMs (Initial CHAIR Evaluation)

- **Widespread Problem:** Most instruction-tuned LVLMs (mPLUG-Owl, LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT, MiniGPT-4) suffer from severe object hallucination.
- **Worse than Smaller Models:** Surprisingly, some LVLMs exhibit *more severe* hallucination than smaller, traditional Vision-Language Pre-trained Models (VLPMs). For instance, LLaVA had a CHAIR_S of 32.7% compared to OSCAR_base's 13.0%, indicating a higher tendency to generate captions with hallucinated objects.
- **InstructBLIP as an Exception:** InstructBLIP demonstrates significantly lower hallucination compared to other LVLMs. The authors suggest this might be due to its visual instructions being collected from a wide variety of public datasets, contrasting with others that often rely on synthetic instructions generated by unimodal LLMs, which may inherently contain hallucinated information.
- **CHAIR Limitations Confirmed:** The CHAIR metric proves to be unstable and highly sensitive to instruction wording and caption length. Different prompts with similar semantic meanings lead to widely varying CHAIR scores (e.g., LLaVA's CHAIR_I ranging from 10.5% to 18.8% for similar captioning prompts), highlighting its unsuitability for consistent LVLM evaluation.

#### 4.2. Influence of Instruction Data on Hallucination

The empirical analyses strongly support the hypotheses regarding the impact of instruction data characteristics on hallucination:
- **Frequency Bias:** Both qualitative (bar charts in Figure 2a) and quantitative (HR_A@k in Table 2) analyses show a clear correlation: LVLMs are significantly more prone to hallucinate objects that frequently appear in the MSCOCO dataset (which often forms the basis for their instruction tuning). The top 30 most frequent objects accounted for approximately 70% of all hallucinated objects across models.
- **Co-occurrence Bias:** Similarly, LVLMs tend to hallucinate objects that frequently co-occur with ground-truth objects present in the image (Figure 2b, HR_C@k in Table 2 and 10). For example, over half of hallucinated objects were among the top 10 frequently co-occurring objects with "dining table" in images where a dining table was present. This suggests models are "completing" a scene based on learned correlations rather than strict visual evidence.

#### 4.3. Efficacy and Advantages of POPE

The proposed Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation (POPE) method demonstrates several advantages:
- **Consistent Hallucination Assessment:** POPE results generally align with the CHAIR findings, confirming that LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT, and mPLUG-Owl suffer more severe hallucination (F1 scores below 70%) compared to InstructBLIP (F1 scores above 80%).
- **Overconfidence Bias:** LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT, and mPLUG-Owl show an extreme tendency to answer "Yes" (near 99%) to probing questions, regardless of the object's presence. This indicates significant overconfidence and contributes to their high hallucination rates on negative (non-existent) objects.
- **Difficulty Gradient:** Model performance on POPE consistently decreases from "Random" to "Popular" to "Adversarial" sampling settings (Table 3). This gradient validates that frequently appearing and co-occurring objects represent "harder" hallucination cases for LVLMs, as they are more likely to be hallucinated.
- **Superior Stability:** POPE exhibits significantly higher stability compared to CHAIR across different prompt templates. The standard deviation of LLaVA's F1 score on POPE prompts was 0.78, while CHAIR_I showed a much higher standard deviation of 3.22 (Table 4).
- **Scalability:** POPE, when combined with automatic segmentation tools like SEEM, proves effective for evaluating object hallucination on unannotated datasets (Table 5, 11). While SEEM-based evaluation leads to slightly lower F1 scores (suggesting finer granularity and increased challenge), the performance trends across models and sampling strategies remain consistent with annotation-based POPE.
- **High Consistency:** A strong correlation is observed between objects mentioned in model-generated captions and objects receiving "Yes" responses in POPE questions, validating POPE's reliability as an assessment tool.

#### 4.4. Impact of Hallucination on Other Vision Tasks

- **Not Always Direct Correlation with VQA:** The study finds that the degree of object hallucination (POPE F1 score) is not always directly proportional to VQA performance (Table 6). For example, MiniGPT-4 achieves a higher POPE F1 score than LLaVA but performs worse on VQA tasks. This suggests that object hallucination and VQA capability measure different aspects of an LVLM's overall performance. The authors hypothesize that differences in instruction tuning data (e.g., caption-only vs. complex VQA instructions) might explain this divergence.
- **General Alignment with Image Captioning:** Generally, better POPE scores tend to align with better traditional image captioning metrics (BLEU, METEOR, ROUGE-L) (Table 12), implying that reduced hallucination positively contributes to caption quality.

In summary, the findings paint a clear picture: object hallucination is a severe and prevalent issue in LVLMs, exacerbated by biases in their instruction tuning data. The proposed POPE method offers a more stable and flexible alternative for evaluating this critical problem, providing valuable insights for the development of more reliable multi-modal AI.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper makes several significant contributions to the field of Artificial Intelligence, particularly in the realm of multi-modal models, with far-reaching potential impacts.

#### 5.1. Pioneering Systematic Study of LVLM Hallucination

The paper presents the **first systematic and empirical study** specifically focused on object hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models. Before this work, while hallucination was acknowledged in LLMs and earlier VLPMs, its distinct manifestation and severity in the new generation of LVLMs remained underexplored. By rigorously evaluating several state-of-the-art LVLMs, the paper unequivocally demonstrates that object hallucination is a severe and widespread problem, often even more pronounced than in their smaller predecessors. This establishes a critical benchmark and draws urgent attention to a reliability challenge that is often overshadowed by LVLMs' impressive generative capabilities.

#### 5.2. Uncovering the Roots of Hallucination in Instruction Tuning

A key insight provided by this research is the empirical link between the characteristics of visual instruction tuning data and the propensity for object hallucination. The findings that LVLMs tend to hallucinate objects based on their **frequency of appearance** or **co-occurrence patterns** in the training corpus offer concrete explanations for this behavior. This understanding is invaluable for model developers, suggesting that simply scaling up training data or model size might not inherently solve hallucination and could even exacerbate it if the data is biased. It prompts a shift in focus from merely increasing data volume to curating data for *representativeness and fidelity*, potentially leading to new data augmentation strategies or debiasing techniques for instruction tuning.

#### 5.3. Advancing Evaluation Methodology with POPE

The introduction of **POPE (Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation)** is a significant methodological advancement. By reframing hallucination assessment as a simple binary classification task ("Yes/No" questions), POPE overcomes critical limitations of previous methods like CHAIR, which were unstable, sensitive to instruction wording, and required complex parsing. POPE's inherent stability, flexibility, and scalability (especially when combined with automatic segmentation tools like SEEM) make it a superior tool for consistent and reliable evaluation. This new benchmark could become a standard for assessing object hallucination in LVLMs, fostering more meaningful comparisons across models and accelerating research into mitigation strategies.

#### 5.4. Guiding Future Model Development and Robust AI

The findings serve as crucial guidance for the future development of LVLMs. Knowing *what* objects are most prone to hallucination (frequent and co-occurring ones) and *why* (data biases) provides actionable insights for designing more robust models. This could lead to:
- **Targeted Data Curation:** Developing visual instruction datasets that are more balanced and less prone to inducing hallucination.
- **Novel Architectural Designs:** Exploring new architectures or training objectives that explicitly penalize or mitigate hallucination, perhaps by incorporating stronger grounding mechanisms or uncertainty quantification.
- **Bias Mitigation Techniques:** Applying techniques from bias mitigation in other AI domains to reduce the model's tendency to rely on statistical priors from the training data over actual visual evidence.

#### 5.5. Enhancing Real-World Reliability and Safety

Perhaps the most critical impact of this work lies in its contribution to building more **reliable and safe AI systems**. Hallucination, particularly object hallucination, is not merely an academic curiosity but a significant barrier to the trustworthy deployment of LVLMs in high-stakes applications such as autonomous vehicles, medical diagnosis, content moderation, or assistive technologies. In these contexts, misidentifying a non-existent object or missing a crucial real one due to hallucination can have severe, even life-threatening, consequences. By systematically identifying, quantifying, and providing a better tool for evaluating hallucination, this paper lays foundational groundwork for developing LVLMs that are not only capable but also dependable and safe for real-world integration. This emphasis on reliability is paramount for fostering public trust and enabling the widespread adoption of multi-modal AI technologies.
