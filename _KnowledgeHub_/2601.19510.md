# ALRM: Agentic LLM for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ALRM is a modular agentic framework for robotic manipulation. A ReAct-style task planner decomposes instructions into subtasks, and an executor runs them either as generated Python code (Code-as-Policy) or iterative tool calls (Tool-as-Policy) through a ROS/MoveIt API server. A new 56-task benchmark with paraphrased instructions tests ten LLMs. Claude-4.1-Opus reaches 93.5% success under Tool-as-Policy.

## Problem

- Prior LLM-based robot controllers rely on static, one-shot planning and lack modular agentic execution that plans, reflects on outcomes, and revises actions in a closed loop.
- Vision-language-action models map perception directly to motor commands, needing massive data and offering no explicit reasoning, planning, or reflection, while Code-as-Policy generation is one-shot and brittle.
- Existing manipulation benchmarks such as RLBench and RoboCasa emphasize low-level control, with simple tasks and little linguistic variation, so they poorly test multistep reasoning and instruction paraphrasing.

## Method

- A three-module architecture: a ==ReAct-style Task Planner Agent== issuing ==thought-action-observation cycles==, a ==Task Executor Agent==, and a ==RESTful API server== bridging ROS, MoveIt, and Gazebo for the Interbotix wx250s arm.
- The executor exposes eight predefined actions (pick, place, move_to, get_objects, compute_grasp, and others) in two modes: **Tool-as-Policy** makes ==iterative nested tool calls==, while **Code-as-Policy** writes one Python program per subtask.
- A benchmark of three environments (utensils, boxes, fruits) with three canonical multi-object tasks, each ==paraphrased into lexical, syntactic, semantic, and high-level-reasoning variants==, scored by a ==three-LLM majority-vote judge==.

## Results

- Claude-4.1-Opus was the best closed model: **93.5%** success under Tool-as-Policy versus **92.6%** under Code-as-Policy, but latency rose from **33.44s** to **82.60s**. GPT-5 was the exception, scoring **90.7%** CaP versus **85.2%** TaP.
- Falcon-H1-7B reached **84.3%** under Code-as-Policy at **24.89s**, matching DeepSeek-V3.1 (**84.3%**, **69.83s**) and beating Gemini-2.5-Pro (**73.1%**). Under Tool-as-Policy it collapsed to **14.8%**, and Granite-3.3-8B and Mistral-7B scored **0%**.
- Large models averaged **92.3%** on lexical paraphrases and about **84%** on the reasoning, semantic, and syntactic categories. Small models under CaP averaged just **45.8%** on high-level reasoning, their weakest category.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Tool-based execution lets large models self-correct mid-subtask, but costs more LLM calls and latency. Code generation is faster and works for small models without tool-calling support.
> - Small open models can be competitive on robot code generation, but reliable nested tool calling is a distinct capability they lack. It should be a training target for local deployment.
> - Large LLMs are robust to phrasing changes but struggle more with task difficulty, so benchmarks should stress multistep reasoning over object properties rather than only wording variation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional robotic systems typically rely on explicit, pre-programmed task specifications and control logic, involving multi-layered software stacks such as ROS, hardware abstraction APIs, and middleware like MoveIt. While these systems offer optimized modules for control and perception, they tend to be rigid, domain-specific, and lack adaptability to novel tasks or dynamic environmental changes.

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have introduced capabilities beyond natural language generation, encompassing code synthesis, image interpretation, tool use, and symbolic reasoning. These multi-modal capabilities position LLMs as potential foundations for embodied AI, where perception, reasoning, and action are integrated within dynamic environments.

In the context of robotics, LLMs are being explored for interpreting unstructured language, reasoning over symbolic goals, and interfacing with APIs for automated control. Approaches such as Code-as-Policy (CaP) leverage LLMs as policy generators to translate high-level goals into executable code, facilitating zero-shot task automation. LLMs also assist with low-level control grounding and abstract task decomposition. However, existing LLM-robot integration efforts often exhibit limitations, including static planning, a lack of interpretability, and the absence of closed-loop mechanisms for reflection during task execution.

Concurrently, agentic AI frameworks, like ReAct and Reflexion, have demonstrated effective architectures for planning, acting, and refining actions. These frameworks combine structured reasoning with interactive execution, enabling task decomposition, tool invocation, code generation, and adaptive actions based on feedback.

Furthermore, current simulation benchmarks for robotic manipulation, such as ACRV, RLBench, and RoboCasa, often concentrate on low-level control, lack tasks requiring complex reasoning over environments or objects, and rarely involve multiple sequential steps. These benchmarks also frequently lack systematic evaluation of linguistic variation, leaving task instructions untested under diverse wordings, syntactic structures, or reasoning demands.

The ALRM framework addresses these identified gaps by integrating LLM-based action generation with agentic execution for robotic manipulation. It introduces a modular, agentic execution mechanism with a ReAct-style reasoning loop, supporting both direct executable code generation (CaP) and iterative planning with tool-based action execution (TaP). Additionally, ALRM introduces a new benchmark specifically designed to evaluate multistep reasoning and linguistic diversity in manipulation tasks, thereby extending the capabilities and evaluative scope of LLM-driven robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to develop and evaluate an LLM-driven agentic framework that enhances the integration of natural language reasoning with reliable robotic execution, and to establish a systematic evaluation methodology for complex robotic manipulation tasks.

The motivation behind this work stems from two main limitations observed in the current landscape of LLM-robotics integration:

1. **Limited Modular, Agentic Execution Mechanisms:** Prior LLM-based approaches in robotics frequently lack modular and agentic execution capabilities. This deficiency restricts their ability to autonomously plan tasks, reflect on the outcomes of their actions, and revise plans in a closed-loop manner. Existing systems often rely on static planning or one-shot code generation, which can be brittle and less adaptable to dynamic environments or unexpected execution failures. The absence of iterative reasoning and feedback loops limits the robustness and generalization of LLM-controlled robots, hindering their ability to perform complex, multi-step tasks that require adaptation and self-correction. The researchers are motivated to introduce a framework that incorporates structured reasoning (inspired by agentic AI) and interactive execution to overcome these limitations.

2. **Inadequate Benchmarks for Multistep Reasoning and Linguistic Variation:** Current benchmarks for robotic manipulation tasks predominantly focus on low-level control, such as precise joint movements or simple pick-and-place operations, and do not adequately evaluate tasks requiring complex, multi-step reasoning or significant linguistic understanding. Many existing benchmarks also lack systematic assessment of linguistic variation, meaning that task instructions are not tested under different wordings, syntactic structures, or levels of abstraction. This limitation makes it challenging to thoroughly assess the reasoning capabilities, robustness to natural language diversity, and generalization of LLM-based agents in more realistic and complex scenarios. The researchers are motivated to create a novel simulation benchmark that specifically addresses these shortcomings, emphasizing high-level, multi-step tasks with rich linguistic diversity to provide a more comprehensive testbed for LLM-driven robotic agents.

By addressing these motivations, ALRM aims to provide a scalable, interpretable, and modular approach that bridges the gap between high-level natural language instructions and reliable robotic execution, thereby advancing the capabilities of embodied AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ALRM (Agentic LLM for Robot Manipulation) is an LLM-driven agentic framework designed to bridge natural language reasoning with robotic execution. Its architecture consists of three main modules: the Task Planner Agent, the Task Executor Agent, and the API Server.

**A. LLM-Based Robotic Agent Architecture**

1. **Task Planner Agent:**
 - Designed based on the ReAct framework, it generates iterative cycles of "thought" and "action" to decompose high-level user requests into subtasks.
 - It dynamically creates subtasks by interacting with the environment and the robot, using observations from the Task Executor Agent as feedback. This closed-loop feedback allows the planner to adapt plans in real-time.
 - The LLM is instructed to generate subtasks according to high-level templates (e.g., "Get the position of objects," "Execute atomic manipulation tasks like Pick up [object] and place it [spatial relation] [destination]").
 - Guidelines ensure that the LLM generates one action per step, focuses on one object at a time, and generates actions only for objects present and relevant to the user's request.

2. **Task Executor Agent:**
 - Receives high-level natural language subtasks from the planner (e.g., "Pick the lemon and place it in the trash") and translates them into a sequence of executable actions.
 - It has access to eight predefined actions: `pick`, `place`, `move_to_home_pos`, `move_to` (for robot control); `get_objects`, `get_reference_names` (for perception); and `compute_grasp`, `get_pose` (for pose retrieval).
 - Actions can be executed via two complementary modes:
 - **Tool-as-Policy (TaP):** Leverages the LLM's tool-calling capabilities. It operates in interactive steps, typically producing a single tool call per step. The result is appended to the conversation history and fed back to the LLM, enabling reasoning over intermediate results and error correction without replanning by the Task Planner Agent. This mode increases the number of LLM calls and relies on robust tool calling.
 - **Code-as-Policy (CaP):** Generates a complete Python code script that calls the available functions to fulfill the subtask in a single run. This approach is faster due to fewer LLM calls. However, it is more brittle; an error in any part of the generated code can lead to subtask failure, increasing reliance on the Task Planner for accurate action descriptions.
 - Both modes provide LLMs with best practice instructions and action definitions (method descriptions, input parameters, expected outputs). CaP also includes a one-shot code example. Observations returned to the Task Planner Agent summarize execution results (e.g., "The lemon was successfully picked," "Object orange not found").

3. **API Server for Robotic Control:**
 - A RESTful API server controls an Interbotix wx250s arm in a simulated environment.
 - It provides endpoints for pick-and-place, motion commands, and perception queries.
 - Two internal modules, `wx250sRobot` (an abstraction layer for MoveIt and ROS) and `SimPerception` (retrieves object positions and grasp poses from Gazebo), connect the API to the robotics backend.
 - This design allows the LLM to generate high-level actions without needing to manage low-level ROS commands, reducing complexity and improving generalization.

**B. Benchmark**

To evaluate ALRM, a novel benchmark dataset was designed, comprising 56 tasks across multiple simulated environments with linguistically diverse instructions.

1. **Environments:** Three distinct simulated environments were developed, each with a robotic manipulator and a specific set of objects:
 - **Kitchen utensils:** spoon, spatula, can of coke, basket.
 - **Boxes:** cardboard box, wooden box, metal box, container.
 - **Fruits:** strawberry, plum, lemon, peach, bowl, trash.
 - These environments require reasoning about object properties and positions in different semantic domains.

2. **Tasks:** For each environment, three canonical multi-object pick-and-place tasks are defined (e.g., "Move the spoon, the coke, and the spatula to the basket"). These tasks involve manipulating at least two objects, may require specific execution orders or multiple reference positions, and sometimes actions based on object properties.

3. **Paraphrase Categories:** To test robustness to language variation, each canonical task is paraphrased five times across six linguistic categories:
 - **Canonical (CAN):** The original, direct imperative command.
 - **Lexical (LEX):** Nouns and verbs replaced with synonyms.
 - **Syntactical (SYN):** Sentences rephrased with different syntactic structures (e.g., declarative, interrogative).
 - **Semantics (SEM):** Emphasizes contextual or stylistic changes, including typos, informal phrasing, or chitchat.
 - **High-level reasoning (HLR):** Abstract instructions requiring inference or reasoning to identify objects or define actions (e.g., "Pick up the sourest and the biggest fruits"). Two HLR paraphrases are created per task, totaling 56 instructions.
 - Paraphrases are designed to be solvable with the same underlying actions as the canonical task without ambiguity.

**C. Experimental Setup**

1. **Large Language Models:** Ten LLMs were selected for evaluation across both CaP and TaP modes:
 - **Large-scale (closed-source):** GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-4.1-Opus, DeepSeek-V3.1 (non-thinking mode).
 - **Small-scale (open-source, up to 8B parameters):** Falcon-H1-7B, Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B, DeepSeek-R1-7B, Granite-3.3-8B, Mistral-7B.
 - Closed-source models were run via official APIs; open-source models were run locally on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU.

2. **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate:** Measures whether the LLM-generated action sequence (tool calls or code) correctly solves a task. An LLM-as-a-judge approach was used, with three different LLMs (GPT-4.1, Claude-Sonnet-4, Gemini-2.5-Flash) acting as judges.
 - Scoring Rubric: 0 (no subtask solved), 1 (at least one subtask solved, but others contain errors/are missing), 2 (all subtasks fully solved).
 - Final score determined by majority vote; if scores differed, the average was taken. Temperature was fixed at 0.0 for consistency.
 - Results reported as average success rate across all 56 instructions and a breakdown by paraphrase category.
 - **Latency:** Measures the time (in seconds) taken by an LLM to complete a task, reported as an average across all tasks.

3. **Evaluation Design:**
 - A lightweight simulation environment provided APIs for robot movement and perception, returning placeholder poses to mimic real robot/simulator outputs. This prioritizes assessing the quality of generated actions.
 - Ground-truth code and tool calls were validated in Gazebo using the Interbotix wx250s arm to ensure physical realism of the framework's actions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the ALRM framework across various LLMs, operation modes, and paraphrase categories yielded distinct performance characteristics.

**A. Operation Mode Comparison**
The research found a general trend where large-scale LLMs achieved higher success rates in Tool-as-Policy (TaP) mode compared to Code-as-Policy (CaP) mode, albeit with increased latency. For instance, Claude-4.1-Opus demonstrated a 0.9% improvement in average success rate (93.5% in TaP vs. 92.6% in CaP) but experienced a latency increase from 33.44s to 82.60s. Similar patterns were observed for DeepSeek-V3.1 and Gemini-2.5-Pro. GPT-5 was an exception, with CaP outperforming TaP (90.7% vs 85.2%). This indicates a trade-off where TaP leverages nested reasoning capabilities more effectively for large LLMs but demands more processing time.

Conversely, small-scale LLMs (up to 8B parameters) generally struggled with TaP mode, often experiencing significant drops in success rates. However, some small-scale models achieved competitive success rates with CaP while maintaining lower latency. For example, Falcon-H1-7B achieved an 84.3% success rate in CaP with a latency of 24.89 seconds. The qualitative analysis indicated that small-scale models frequently encountered difficulties in handling multiple consecutive nested calls in TaP, leading to partial or failed completions.

**B. LLMs Comparison**
Among the large-scale LLMs, Claude-4.1-Opus exhibited the highest overall performance, achieving the best success rates in both TaP (93.5%) and CaP (92.6%) modes. GPT-5 also showed high success rates, particularly in CaP (90.7%), but its latency was substantially higher (145.59s in CaP). Gemini-2.5-Pro and DeepSeek-V3.1 performed similarly, with Gemini-2.5-Pro showing a slight advantage in success rate and lower latency in TaP. Notably, no large-scale LLMs received a score of 0, indicating their general robustness across multi-step robotic manipulation tasks.

For small-scale LLMs, Falcon-H1-7B was the top performer in CaP mode, achieving an 84.3% success rate. This performance was comparable to DeepSeek-V3.1 (84.3%) and surpassed Gemini-2.5-Pro (73.1%) in CaP, despite Falcon-H1-7B being a much smaller model and exhibiting one of the lowest processing times (24.89s). Qwen3-8B achieved a moderate 64.8% success rate in CaP, but its practicality was limited by high latency (343.33s). Llama-3.1-8B also performed relatively well in CaP, with a 68.5% success rate. Other small-scale models, including Granite-3.3-8B (53.7%), DeepSeek-R1-7B (21.3%), and Mistral-7B (8.3%), performed poorly in CaP, primarily due to difficulties in generating meaningful and complete action sequences. DeepSeek-R1-7B does not support tool calling, and other small-scale models struggled with TaP, with several failing completely (e.g., Granite-3.3-8B and Mistral-7B achieved 0.0% success in TaP).

**C. Task Categories**
Large-scale LLMs generally performed consistently across all task categories in both CaP and TaP modes. The lexical category demonstrated the highest average success rate (92.3%) among these models, indicating their strong ability to understand diverse wordings and synonyms. Canonical tasks achieved an average success rate of 88.8%. High-level reasoning (average 84.3%), semantics (average 84.7%), and syntactical tasks (average 84.0%) showed similar performance, suggesting that large-scale LLMs are more affected by overall task complexity than by specific types of natural language variation.

For small-scale LLMs operating in CaP mode, canonical tasks achieved the highest average success rate (54.6%), reflecting their ability to handle standard imperative commands. Syntactical tasks followed closely (average 53.7%), with Falcon-H1-7B and Qwen3-8B both achieving 88.9%. Lexical and semantics tasks performed similarly (average 51%). However, high-level reasoning tasks presented the greatest challenge, yielding the weakest performance (average 45.8%) for small-scale models. This highlights a significant difficulty for smaller models in performing inference and abstract reasoning to define actions and generate corresponding code from implicit instructions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ALRM framework presents a contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a modular and agentic approach that integrates LLM-based reasoning with robotic execution. Its design, incorporating a ReAct-style reasoning loop and supporting both Code-as-Policy (CaP) and Tool-as-Policy (TaP) execution modes, addresses identified limitations in previous LLM-robotics integration efforts, particularly the lack of closed-loop reflection and dynamic plan revision. This capability enables robotic systems to plan, monitor execution outcomes, and iteratively revise actions, which could lead to more robust and adaptable robotic behaviors in complex and dynamic environments.

The introduction of a novel simulation benchmark, comprising 56 tasks across multiple environments with an emphasis on multistep, high-level reasoning, and rich linguistic diversity, is also significant. This benchmark provides a structured and comprehensive testbed that moves beyond low-level control tasks and simple linguistic variations. Its public availability supports future research by offering a standardized tool for systematically assessing the capabilities of LLM-based agents in robotic manipulation, particularly their ability to interpret diverse natural language instructions and perform complex, sequential actions.

The experimental findings demonstrate a clear trade-off between model scale, execution mode, performance, and latency. The superior performance of large closed-source LLMs like Claude-4.1-Opus, especially in the TaP mode, suggests that advanced reasoning and tool-calling capabilities are beneficial for complex manipulation tasks, despite the increased latency. The competitive performance of open-source models like Falcon-H1-7B in CaP mode, even matching larger models in success rate with significantly lower latency, indicates the potential for deploying smaller, more efficient models for certain robotic applications, particularly where computational resources are constrained. The observed struggles of small-scale LLMs with TaP and high-level reasoning tasks also highlight specific areas for future model development, particularly in enhancing nested tool-calling abilities and abstract inference.

In terms of potential impact, ALRM offers a scalable, interpretable, and modular architecture that facilitates the development of more intelligent and autonomous robotic systems. By enabling robots to understand and execute tasks from varied natural language instructions, it could broaden the accessibility of robotics to non-expert users and automate tasks in more unstructured settings. The insights derived from the evaluation, regarding model performance characteristics across different operational modes and linguistic complexities, can guide developers in selecting appropriate LLMs and execution strategies for specific robotic applications, balancing performance requirements with computational costs. The identified limitations, such as the dependence on prompt engineering and the current scope of the benchmark, also delineate clear directions for further research, including expanding task diversity, integrating real-world perception, and optimizing prompting strategies.
