# PACE: Physics Augmentation for Coordinated End-to-end Reinforcement Learning toward Versatile Humanoid Table Tennis

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Purdue University developed an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework enabling a humanoid robot to play table tennis with coordinated whole-body motions. The system integrates a learned ball trajectory predictor and physics-based predictive rewards, achieving a 94.1% success rate in simulation and a 61.3% success rate on a physical Booster T1 robot while generating average outbound ball speeds of 6.9 m/s.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to react to fast-moving objects with versatile, coordinated whole-body behaviors in dynamic, interactive tasks like table tennis (TT).
- Traditional model-based TT approaches often rely on restrictive 'virtual hitting-plane' assumptions, limiting adaptability and making it difficult to scale to high-DoF humanoids.
- End-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) for legged humanoids in dynamic tasks faces challenges such as high-dimensional action spaces, locomotion instability, and sparse ball-hitting rewards, which hinder efficient exploration and training.

## Method

- An end-to-end asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning framework directly maps ball-position observations and robot proprioception to whole-body joint commands for both arm striking and leg locomotion.
- A lightweight ==Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)== network is trained online to predict the future post-bounce apex of the ball, augmenting the actor's observations to enable proactive decision-making and anticipatory movements.
- Dense, continuous, physics-based 'reaching' and 'returning' rewards are designed to provide immediate feedback, guiding efficient learning and overcoming the sparsity of binary success/failure rewards.

## Results

- In simulation, the policy achieved a **97.6%** hit rate and a **94.1%** success rate for mixed-range serves (**5.2** to **6.5** m/s ball speeds), demonstrating versatile strike and footwork strategies adaptable to varied ball trajectories.
- The learned policy transferred zero-shot to a physical Booster T1 humanoid robot, achieving a **93.5%** hit rate and a **61.3%** success rate across 31 randomized serves.
- The physical robot exhibited coordinated upper and lower body movements, including 2D footwork (lateral and forward-backward), and returned balls at an average outbound speed of **6.9** m/s immediately post-impact, exceeding the incoming ball speed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a learned ball trajectory predictor into the actor's observation space provides crucial proactive guidance, enabling the humanoid to anticipate and position its whole body effectively for fast-paced interactions.
> - Dense, physics-based predictive rewards are essential for efficient exploration and the emergence of coordinated whole-body behaviors in complex tasks with sparse natural rewards.
> - An end-to-end RL approach can successfully learn intricate arm-leg coordination for high-DoF humanoid robots in dynamic sports without relying on restrictive model-based planning or virtual hitting-plane assumptions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose embodied intelligent agents capable of complex physical interactions is a current focus in robotics. Humanoid robots have demonstrated advanced capabilities in tasks such as walking, jumping, kicking, and dancing, primarily in free space or with static object interaction. However, reacting to fast-moving objects with versatile, coordinated whole-body behaviors remains a challenge. Table tennis (TT) represents a particularly demanding instance of this challenge due to its requirement for rapid perception, proactive whole-body motion, agile footwork, and precise timing.

Previous research in robotic TT has largely relied on model-based planning and control. These approaches typically use analytical physics-based models for ball trajectory prediction combined with inverse kinematics for robot motion planning. Many have adopted a "virtual hitting-plane" assumption, where a specific hitting point is computed, simplifying the task to a machine-like operation and limiting the need for adaptive footwork and arm-leg coordination. While some model-based methods have relaxed this assumption to achieve competitive success rates and varied swing types, they have predominantly focused on robotic arms with fewer degrees of freedom (DoFs), making direct scaling to humanoid whole-body movements difficult. For instance, humanoid robots playing TT using impedance control have often been constrained to standing positions, limiting their effective hitting range.

Learning-based control methods, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), have shown progress in robotic TT. Some work has integrated analytical interception planners with RL controllers for humanoid whole-body TT, demonstrating agile lateral footwork and accurate strikes, but often still constrained by an underlying virtual-hitting-plane assumption. End-to-end, model-free RL frameworks have been applied to robot arms with sliding bases, expanding reachable workspaces and enabling diverse strokes without the virtual hitting-plane constraint. However, applying these end-to-end frameworks to legged humanoids introduces challenges related to high-dimensional action spaces, locomotion instability, and the sparsity of ball-hitting rewards, which hinder efficient exploration and training.

This work addresses the aforementioned limitations by presenting an end-to-end RL framework for humanoid TT that jointly coordinates locomotion and arm striking directly from ball-position observations and robot proprioception. It aims to overcome the reliance on modular decomposition or hierarchical planning and the restrictive virtual hitting-plane assumption. By integrating a learned future ball-state predictor and physics-based predictive rewards, the framework targets improved learning efficiency and the emergence of coordinated whole-body movements for versatile TT play in humanoids, a capability that has been challenging for prior methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework capable of controlling a humanoid robot to play table tennis (TT) with coordinated whole-body motions, including both arm striking and agile footwork. This framework aims to achieve high success rates across varied serve ranges and ball speeds, without relying on traditional model-based planning or restrictive assumptions like a virtual hitting plane.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several challenges inherent in enabling humanoids to perform dynamic, interactive tasks like TT:

- **Complexity of Whole-Body Coordination:** Competitive TT demands rapid eye-hand-leg coordination, core stabilization, and precisely timed arm swings, along with agile footwork to cover the table. Integrating these diverse physical capabilities into a unified control policy for a high-DoF humanoid robot is a complex problem.
- **Limitations of Traditional Approaches:**
 - **Model-based planning:** While effective for simpler tasks, model-based methods in TT often rely on the "virtual hitting-plane" assumption, which simplifies the task to a predictable machine operation and limits the robot's ability to adapt to varying ball trajectories or develop versatile stroke strategies. These methods also struggle to scale directly to the high DoF of humanoid robots.
 - **Learning-based methods (arms-only):** Existing end-to-end RL for TT has demonstrated success on robotic arms, particularly those with additional translational DoFs. However, transferring this to legged humanoids introduces significant challenges due to their inherent locomotion instability, higher-dimensional action spaces, and the difficulty of efficient exploration in the presence of sparse ball-hitting rewards.
- **Need for Proactive Control:** The speed of a TT ball (crossing the table in less than 0.5 seconds) necessitates proactive rather than reactive movements. A robot must anticipate the ball's trajectory and position its entire body effectively before the ball arrives. Explicitly incorporating prediction into the control loop is motivated by this timing constraint.
- **Inefficiency of Sparse Rewards:** In complex tasks like TT, binary success/failure rewards (e.g., whether the ball was successfully returned) are sparse and delayed. Such sparsity makes it difficult for RL agents to learn intricate, continuous behaviors like coordinated locomotion and arm swings efficiently. Dense, continuous feedback is required to guide exploration effectively.
- **Generalization to Diverse Play:** A versatile TT player needs to adapt to different serve types (long, short, wide, deep) and return the ball effectively. The objective is to enable the humanoid to exhibit diverse strike and footwork strategies rather than being confined to a narrow operational envelope.

In summary, the key motivation is to push the boundaries of humanoid whole-body control in highly dynamic and interactive environments, using TT as a benchmark. The research aims to overcome the limitations of prior approaches by introducing a novel RL framework that explicitly integrates predictive mechanisms and dense, physics-guided rewards to foster proactive behavior, efficient learning, and versatile, coordinated motions in a humanoid robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes an end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework that maps ball-position observations and robot proprioception directly to whole-body joint commands for coordinated arm striking and leg locomotion. This framework is designed to address the challenges of proactive action and sparse rewards in humanoid table tennis (TT) control. The problem is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP).

**A. Overall Framework Architecture**

The core of the methodology integrates a learnable ball-hitting position predictor with an asymmetric actor-critic RL architecture, as depicted in Figure 2 of the paper. This integration provides proactive decision-making and dense training supervision.

**B. Predictor-augmented Actor Policy**

To enable proactive behavior, a ball trajectory predictor is incorporated into the RL pipeline:

1. **Predictor Training:** A lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) network is trained online during policy updates. It receives five historical ball positions as input and predicts the post-bounce apex of the ball, denoted as $\tilde{p}_{ball}$. The ground truth for training, $\hat{p}_{ball}$, is derived from physics-based simulation augmented with aerodynamic models. The predictor is optimized using a root-mean-square-error regression loss between $\tilde{p}_{ball}$ and $\hat{p}_{ball}$. To enhance robustness during deployment, Gaussian noise is injected into training ball trajectories.
2. **Augmented Actor Observation:** The output of the learned predictor, $\tilde{p}_{ball}$, is used to generate a target base/trunk shift command, $\Delta\tilde{p}_{base,xy}$. This command indicates the horizontal difference between the current base position and the desired base position for intercepting the ball. Both $\Delta\tilde{p}_{base,xy}$ and $\tilde{p}_{ball}$ are concatenated with other standard observations and fed to the actor policy.
3. **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** The actor utilizes the learned predictor's output ($\tilde{p}_{ball}$, $\Delta\tilde{p}_{base,xy}$) as part of its observation space to inform its actions. In contrast, the critic, which is used only during training to evaluate states, receives more precise "privileged information." This includes the physics-based ground truth prediction ($\hat{p}_{ball}$, $\Delta\hat{p}_{base,xy}$) and other unobservable simulation states like ball linear velocity, end-effector position, time until ball arrival, and episode progress. This asymmetric design allows the critic to learn a more accurate value function, guiding the actor's learning while the actor trains on information available during real-world deployment.

**C. Prediction-based Reward Design**

To overcome the sparsity of binary success/failure rewards, dense, continuous reward functions are constructed using physics-based predictions of the ball's future trajectory (Figure 3):

1. **Reaching Reward (Hit Guidance):**
 - The target ball hitting position, $\hat{p}_{ball}$, is defined as the ball's highest point after a bounce, predicted using simulation physics.
 - **End-effector Position Penalty:** A term penalizes the distance between the robot's end effector ($p_{ee}$) and the target hitting point (i.e., $||\text{p}_{ee} - \hat{p}_{ball}||$).
 - **Robot Base Position Penalty:** Another term penalizes the difference between the robot's actual base position projected onto the horizontal plane ($\text{p}_{base,xy}$) plus arm's relative position ($\text{p}_{arm,xy}$) and the target hitting point's horizontal coordinates ($\hat{p}_{ball,xy}$). This encourages the robot's base to move to a position where its arm can comfortably reach the target.
 - **Base Velocity Penalty:** The robot is penalized for mismatch between its base velocity ($\text{v}_{base}$) and a pseudo-velocity command ($\hat{v}_{ball}$), which is proportional to the base position tracking error.
 - **Reward Clamping:** Rewards are clamped when errors fall within a tolerance range, and prediction-based rewards are disabled near the end of the ball's flight to prevent overfitting to exact hitting points and encourage flexible strike strategies.

2. **Returning Reward (Return Guidance):**
 - To provide immediate feedback after a hit, simulation physics predict the ball's landing position ($\hat{x}_{ball}, \hat{y}_{ball}$) and height ($\hat{z}_{ball}$) as it crosses the net.
 - **Landing Position Penalty:** Penalizes the distance of the predicted landing position from the desired landing area on the opponent's side of the table.
 - **Net Clearance Reward:** Encourages the ball's trajectory to pass over the net at a specified margin.
 - These rewards are computed at the moment of contact, providing immediate feedback that improves learning efficiency compared to waiting for the ball to land or cross the net.

**D. Design of Observation and Action Space**

1. **Observation Space:** The actor's observation history consists of five past measurements. It includes proprioceptive information (joint positions and velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity vector), external perception (robot base position, heading direction, ball position), the robot's previous action, and the learned predictor's output ($\tilde{p}_{ball}$, $\Delta\tilde{p}_{base,xy}$). The critic receives all actor observations without injected noise, along with privileged ground-truth simulation data (Table I).
2. **Action Space:** The policy outputs 21-dimensional residual displacements ($a_t$) from a stable nominal joint configuration ($q^{nom}_t$), representing the robot's standing pose with the right hand raised. The target joint positions are computed as $q^{target}_t = q^{nom}_t + \alpha a_t$, where $\alpha$ is a scaling factor. A low-level PD controller tracks these target joint positions.

**E. Training and Experimental Setup**

1. **Simulation Environment:** Built on LeggedLab using IsaacLab, incorporating domain randomization for robot physical properties, noise, and delays in perception signals to minimize the sim-to-real gap.
2. **Air Drag Modeling:** A custom external-force module accounts for aerodynamic drag on the ball, calibrated using real-world pre-bounce trajectories and fitted to a quadratic-in-speed drag model.
3. **Training Episodes:** Each episode begins with a ball served from the opponent's side with randomized initial velocities and trajectories, focusing on the forehand side. Episodes terminate early if the robot falls, and the robot's initial position is randomized for exploration.
4. **Network Architecture and Optimization:** Actor and critic networks are MLPs with hidden layers of. They are optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with 4096 parallel environments on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs.
5. **Hardware Setup:** A Booster T1 humanoid robot (1.2m tall, 30kg, 23 DoFs) is used. Its forearm is customized with a 3D-printed PLA tube to mount a TT paddle. A Vicon Vero motion-capture system tracks the robot's trunk, table, and ball pose at 150 Hz with 5mm accuracy, using reflective markers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the proposed framework's performance in simulation and on a physical humanoid robot, focusing on hit rate (intercepting the ball with the paddle) and success rate (making a valid return to the opponent's table).

**A. Training Results from Simulations**

1. **High Success Rates for Different Serves:** The policy was evaluated across over 250,000 serves in simulation, with ball speeds ranging from 5.2 m/s to 6.5 m/s, categorized into long, mid-long, short, and mixed serves.
 - For **long serves** (initial forward velocity: -6.5 to -6.2 m/s), the hit rate was 96.1% and the success rate was 92.3%.
 - For **mid-long serves** (-6.2 to -5.7 m/s), the hit rate was 97.0% and the success rate was 95.0%.
 - For **short serves** (-5.7 to -5.2 m/s), the hit rate was 99.3% and the success rate was 94.8%.
 - In the **mixed-range setting** (-6.5 to -5.2 m/s), which combines long and short serves randomly, the policy achieved a hit rate of 97.6% and a success rate of 94.1%.
 These results indicate the policy's ability to handle variations in serve depth and speed without a predefined hitting plane, utilizing whole-body coordination for successful returns.

2. **Diverse Strike and Footwork Strategies:** Analysis of successful strike positions (Figure 4) revealed that the policy adapts its hitting strategy based on the incoming serve. Short serves resulted in strikes closer to the table, requiring rapid forward movement. Long serves were intercepted farther back, involving coordinated backward footwork. This adaptability demonstrates that the policy does not adhere to a virtual hitting plane but rather leverages locomotion, torso adjustments, and arm swing for effective returns at varied locations, exhibiting versatile strike behaviors.

3. **Predictor Training Accuracy:** The lightweight MLP predictor, trained with approximately 10 hours of simulated ball trajectories, achieved an average root-mean-square error of about 3 cm in predicting the post-bounce apex of the ball. This level of accuracy was determined to be sufficient for guiding the policy and enabling high return success rates.

**B. Ablation Studies**

Ablation studies confirmed the contribution of the predictor and prediction-based reward designs to learning performance (Figure 5):

- **Impact of Predictor:** Removing the future ball position ($\tilde{p}_{ball}$) and prediction-based base shift ($\Delta\tilde{p}_{base,xy}$) from the actor's observations (the "no predictor" setting) led to a substantial decrease in hitting performance. While the policy could still move to intercept the ball, it achieved almost no successful returns, indicating the predictor's necessity for establishing a stable stance and executing effective strikes.
- **Impact of Prediction-based Rewards:**
 - **Hit-guidance Reward:** This reward was crucial for efficient exploration, enabling the policy to achieve a high hit rate within the first 4000 training updates. Its removal resulted in a slower increase in the hit rate.
 - **Return-guidance Reward:** This reward was essential for learning effective strike motions. Without it, relying solely on sparse success feedback (ball landing on opponent's side), the policy failed to learn appropriate returning velocity or orientation.

**C. Deployment (Sim2Real)**

The learned policy was deployed zero-shot on the physical Booster T1 humanoid robot:

- **Hardware Performance:** Out of 31 balls launched by a serving machine with randomized initial velocities within the training distribution, the robot intercepted 29 balls and made 19 valid returns. This corresponds to a hit rate of 93.5% and a success rate of 61.3%.
- **Sim2Real Gap Factors:** The observed decrease in return success rate compared to simulation testing was attributed to several factors:
 - Differences between the simulated serial-ankle actuation model and the physical T1's parallel-link ankle mechanism, leading to pronounced end-effector position errors from small ankle tracking errors.
 - Unmodeled motor dynamics.
 - Imperfectly simulated contact dynamics.
- **Coordinated Movement and Ball Speed:** Consistent with simulation findings, the policy produced coordinated upper- and lower-body movements on the physical robot. Versatile lateral and forward-backward footwork emerged in response to serves with diverse directions and speeds (Figures 6 and 7). The policy returned the ball at competitive speeds, with a mean outbound ball speed of 6.9 m/s immediately post-impact across the 19 successful returns, exceeding the initial incoming ball speed. This indicates the policy's ability to generate dynamic, forceful returns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) framework for humanoid table tennis (TT) control, offering advancements in how robots can interact with fast-moving objects in dynamic environments.

The primary significance of this work lies in its demonstration of a unified RL policy that bypasses traditional constraints in robotic TT. By moving beyond the "virtual hitting-plane" assumption, the framework enables the humanoid robot to concurrently generate complex joint reference motions for both arm swing and agile footwork, a capability not widely demonstrated in prior humanoid TT research. This approach suggests a practical pathway toward developing versatile and competitive humanoid TT players.

The integration of a learning-based ball-hitting predictor for actor policy augmentation is a key technical contribution. This predictor provides the necessary proactive decision-making capability, allowing the robot to anticipate ball trajectories and position its whole body effectively within the constrained timing of a TT game. The use of dense, physics-based predictive rewards during training addresses the challenge of sparse reward signals, improving learning efficiency and enabling the emergence of consistent and effective ball-return behaviors. Ablation studies reinforce the critical role of both the learned predictor and the predictive reward design in achieving these outcomes.

The successful zero-shot transfer of the learned RL policy to a physical 23-DoF Booster T1 humanoid robot is a notable finding. Despite the inherent Sim2Real gap, the robot exhibited coordinated upper- and lower-body movements, including two-dimensional footwork (lateral and forward-backward movements) in response to varied serves. This demonstrates the framework's ability to develop generalizable whole-body control strategies for complex physical interactions, which is an important step toward robust humanoid autonomy in unstructured environments. The capability to return balls at competitive speeds also indicates the policy's potential for dynamic and forceful actions.

The potential impact of this research extends beyond table tennis. The methodologies for integrating predictive learning, dense reward shaping, and end-to-end whole-body control could be applied to other dynamic human-robot interaction tasks and sports, where rapid perception, anticipation, and coordinated full-body movements are crucial. This work contributes to the broader objective of developing general-purpose embodied intelligent agents capable of performing diverse real-world tasks that require high-speed reaction and versatile physical skills. Future work involving dexterous arm designs and curriculum learning could further expand the repertoire of skills, potentially enabling more advanced stroke types and strategic play, moving closer to human-level athletic intelligence in robotics.
