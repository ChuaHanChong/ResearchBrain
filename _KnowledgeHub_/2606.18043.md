# Uncertainty Quantification for Flow-Based Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A new epistemic uncertainty estimator, Velocity Field Disagreement (VFD), quantifies model confidence in flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Coupled with the SAVE active fine-tuning framework, it improves VLA reliability and dramatically reduces the expert demonstrations needed for adaptation.

## Problem

- Current flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models lack intrinsic mechanisms to quantify their prediction confidence.
- VLAs deployed in real-world, out-of-distribution environments can fail without warning, posing safety risks due to confident execution of erroneous actions.
- Adapting VLAs to new tasks or domains requires extensive and costly human expert demonstrations, hindering data efficiency.

## Method

- The ==Velocity Field Disagreement== (**VFD**) method estimates epistemic uncertainty in flow-based VLAs by leveraging disagreements in an ensemble's learned velocity fields, theoretically linking KL divergence to L2 velocity field differences.
- The Sample-efficient Active fine-tuning via Velocity-field Epistemic uncertainty (SAVE) framework uses VFD scores to prioritize the most uncertain tasks and initial observations for expert demonstration acquisition.
- SAVE iteratively fine-tunes a VLA ensemble using newly collected data and a replay of pre-training data, optimizing the data acquisition process for multitask adaptation.

## Results

- VFD provided superior uncertainty calibration, showing a negative Spearman rank correlation of **0.71** with task success rates, and remained well-calibrated with a small ensemble size (M=2).
- The SAVE framework, guided by VFD, achieved a 22-**50%** reduction in expert demonstrations needed for multitask adaptation and reached a higher overall success rate of **67.1%** (**13.2%**).
- VFD effectively predicted task failures during deployment, achieving a detection accuracy of **67%** and a True-Positive-Rate of **79%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Disagreements in the velocity fields of flow-matching model ensembles provide a mathematically grounded and effective measure of epistemic uncertainty.
> - Quantifying epistemic uncertainty enables the creation of more reliable robotic systems that can detect impending failures and signal unreliability.
> - Uncertainty-guided active learning significantly improves data efficiency, reducing the need for extensive expert demonstrations during VLA adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a recent development in robotics, extending the large-scale pre-training paradigm from deep learning into physical systems. These models, often employing vision-language backbones coupled with generative action heads trained via flow matching, have demonstrated capabilities in multitask learning, robustness, and emergent zero-shot performance in robotic manipulation. Examples include architectures like those utilizing large pre-trained vision-language models for semantic understanding combined with flow matching for generating continuous robot actions, offering stable training, relatively fast inference, and expressivity for multimodal action distributions.

However, a critical limitation for the real-world deployment of current flow-based VLAs is their inability to quantify confidence in their predictions or detect situations where their actions might be unreliable. When deployed in non-stationary or novel environments, these models can encounter scenarios outside their pretraining distribution, potentially leading to failures without warning. This lack of "failure awareness" or ability to "know what they don't know" raises safety concerns, as VLAs may confidently execute erroneous actions instead of abstaining or seeking assistance. Furthermore, robustly adapting VLAs to new domains currently requires substantial and costly human expert demonstrations.

This research addresses this gap by focusing on uncertainty quantification specifically for flow-based VLAs. It aims to develop efficient methods for estimating epistemic uncertainty—the uncertainty attributable to the model's limited knowledge, which can be reduced by acquiring more data. This contrasts with aleatoric uncertainty, which is inherent noise in the data. The work builds on existing research in uncertainty quantification for generative models, which often employs techniques like deep ensembles, test-time dropout, or Laplace approximations, but adapts them to the specific generative mechanism of flow matching. Additionally, the paper connects uncertainty estimation to active learning, a field focused on intelligent data selection to reduce the need for extensive labeling, thereby aiming to improve the data efficiency of VLA adaptation.

By providing a method to quantify epistemic uncertainty and leveraging it for active learning and failure detection, this work contributes to making VLAs more reliable, adaptable, and suitable for deployment in dynamic, real-world robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce an efficient method for quantifying epistemic uncertainty in flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This objective is motivated by several challenges and limitations inherent in current VLA deployment:

1. **Lack of Confidence Mechanisms in Current VLAs:** Modern flow-based VLAs, despite their empirical performance in robotic manipulation, do not inherently provide a measure of confidence in their predicted actions. They operate as deterministic or probabilistic generative models without an intrinsic mechanism to signal when their knowledge is insufficient or their predictions are likely to be unreliable.

2. **Challenges in Real-World Deployment:** When deployed in dynamic and non-stationary real-world environments, VLAs inevitably encounter scenarios that deviate from their pre-training data distribution. These "out-of-distribution" (OOD) scenarios can lead to model failures. Without uncertainty quantification, VLAs may execute erratic or unsafe actions with high confidence, rather than recognizing their limitations, abstaining from action, or requesting human intervention. This poses significant safety concerns for autonomous robotic systems.

3. **Inefficient Adaptation to New Tasks:** Adapting pre-trained VLAs to novel tasks or evolving environments currently necessitates collecting a large volume of human expert demonstrations. This process is time-consuming and expensive. There is a need for more efficient data acquisition strategies that can intelligently identify which new data points would be most beneficial for model improvement. The ability to estimate epistemic uncertainty can guide this process by indicating where the model's knowledge is weakest.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth three specific contributions:

- **Develop a Mathematically Grounded Epistemic Uncertainty Estimator:** The first objective is to derive an efficient and mathematically sound method for estimating epistemic uncertainty specifically tailored for flow-matching models. This estimator, termed Velocity Field Disagreement (VFD), aims to leverage the generative architecture of flow-matching to quantify model uncertainty.

- **Propose an Uncertainty-Guided Active Fine-Tuning Framework:** The second objective is to build upon the VFD estimator to create a framework for sample-efficient active multitask fine-tuning of flow-based VLAs. This framework, named SAVE (Sample-efficient Active fine-tuning via Velocity-field Epistemic uncertainty), is designed to use VFD scores to prioritize the selection of tasks and specific initial states for which expert demonstrations should be collected, thereby optimizing the data acquisition process.

- **Empirically Validate the Effectiveness of the Proposed Methods:** The final objective involves conducting comprehensive empirical evaluations to demonstrate that VFD produces better-calibrated uncertainty estimates correlated with downstream performance, that it is effective in detecting deployment failures, and that the SAVE framework significantly reduces the number of expert demonstrations required for multitask adaptation compared to existing baselines.

In essence, the research aims to enhance the reliability, safety, and data efficiency of flow-based VLAs, making them more practical for real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research centers on deriving and applying a novel epistemic uncertainty estimator, Velocity Field Disagreement (VFD), for flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, and subsequently integrating this estimator into an active learning framework called SAVE for data-efficient adaptation.

#### 3.1. Epistemic Uncertainty Estimation in Flow-Matching Models (VFD)

The core of the methodology lies in quantifying epistemic uncertainty, which represents the model's uncertainty due to limited knowledge and can be reduced with more data. The approach utilizes a Bayesian framework, where the total predictive uncertainty can be decomposed into epistemic and aleatoric components.

- **Bayesian Approximation with Ensembles:** Directly computing the posterior predictive distribution for deep neural networks is intractable. The authors approximate this by employing a small ensemble of `M` flow-matching models, `θ_1..., θ_M`, obtained by fine-tuning a base VLA multiple times on randomly shuffled subsets of a diverse dataset. Each ensemble member `p_θ_i(x|y)` represents a different hypothesis about the true distribution given the data.

- **Connecting Epistemic Uncertainty to KL Divergence:** Epistemic uncertainty is formally defined as the mutual information between the output `x` and the model parameters `θ` given the input `y` and data `D`. This can be expressed as the expected Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between each individual model's prediction and the average predictive distribution of the ensemble (Equation 2). The authors further approximate this by an upper bound derived from Jensen's inequality, which involves the average pairwise KL divergence between ensemble members (Equation 4c).

- **Theorem 4.1: KL Divergence and Velocity Field Disagreement:** A key theoretical contribution is Theorem 4.1, which establishes a direct relationship between the KL divergence between two flow-matching models and the squared `L_2` difference of their learned velocity fields. For two distributions `p_θ_1(x|y)` and `p_θ_2(x|y)` induced by velocity fields `v_θ_1_s(x,y)` and `v_θ_2_s(x,y)` of flow-matching models with optimal transport (OT) Gaussian conditional probability paths, the KL divergence is given by:
 `D_KL(p_θ_1(x|y) || p_θ_2(x|y)) = ∫_0^1 κ_s E_{x_s~p_θ_1_s(x|y)} [ ||v_θ_1_s(x_s,y) - v_θ_2_s(x_s,y)||^2_2 ] ds`
 where `κ_s = s / (1-s)`. This formula indicates that disagreements in velocity fields at higher flow-matching times (`s` closer to 1, where samples contain less noise and are closer to the data distribution) are more indicative of epistemic uncertainty.

- **Practical VFD Score Calculation:** The integral in Theorem 4.1 is approximated discretely by sampling `x_0` from the base noise distribution, performing Euler integration using one ensemble member's velocity field, and calculating the squared velocity-field disagreement at intermediate ODE states. The final VFD score (Equation 7) for an input `y` averages this scaled disagreement over all non-identical pairs of ensemble members and over the discrete time steps. For practical implementation, the expectation is approximated by forward integrating a small batch of actions, leveraging GPU parallelization.

#### 3.2. Uncertainty-Guided Active Multitask Fine-Tuning (SAVE)

The SAVE framework leverages the VFD uncertainty estimates to actively acquire expert demonstrations for fine-tuning VLAs.

- **VLA Ensemble Initialization:** Instead of training multiple VLAs from scratch (which is computationally prohibitive), the framework starts with a single pre-trained base VLA `π_b`. It then fine-tunes this base VLA `M` times on randomly shuffled versions of a diverse pre-training dataset `D_pre` to create an initial VLA ensemble `Π_0 = (π_0^(1)..., π_0^(M))` for uncertainty estimation.

- **Iterative Active Learning Loop:** The ensemble is fine-tuned over `R` rounds. In each round `r`:
 1. **Uncertainty Evaluation:** The current ensemble `Π_r` evaluates all candidate initial observations `o_kl` (from a pool `O_k` for each task `T_k`) by computing their VFD uncertainty score `u^(r)_kl = u_e(o_kl; Π_r)`.
 2. **Task-Level Uncertainty Aggregation:** Individual observation uncertainties are aggregated to a task level by computing the mean VFD score for each task `U^(r)_k = (1/L) Σ_l u^(r)_kl`.
 3. **Uncertainty-Guided Task Prioritization:** To balance exploitation (focusing on very uncertain tasks) and exploration (ensuring coverage across tasks), tasks are sampled from a categorical distribution `Cat(W^(r))` where weights `w^(r)_k` are proportional to `(U^(r)_k)^τ`. The temperature parameter `τ ≥ 0` controls this balance; higher `τ` concentrates sampling on more uncertain tasks.
 4. **Most Uncertain Observation Selection:** Once a task `T_k` is sampled, the framework selects the single most uncertain initial observation `o*_k = argmax_{o_kl ∈ O_k} u^(r)_kl` within that task.
 5. **Expert Query:** An expert demonstrator `π*` is queried to provide a demonstration `τ_e` starting from the selected initial observation `o*_k`. These new demonstrations are added to a buffer `D_new`.
 6. **Iterative Fine-tuning:** The VLA ensemble `Π_r` is fine-tuned to `Π_{r+1}` using a training mixture `D^(r)_train`. This mixture combines newly collected demonstrations `D_new` with a replay of pre-training data `D_pre` (Equation 11) using a replay ratio `λ ∈`. This strategy is designed to prevent catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired skills while integrating new knowledge.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Environment:** Experiments are conducted on the LIBERO benchmark, simulating a Franka manipulator in a kitchen environment, focusing on 10 long-horizon tasks from LIBERO-10 for active fine-tuning.
- **VLA Model:** SmolVLA, with a SmolVLM-2 backbone and a flow-matching action expert, is used.
- **Baselines:** VFD is compared against six baselines for uncertainty estimation (Action-L2, ACE, DECU, Generative Uncertainty (GU), Entropy, Perplexity) and a diversity-based active learning baseline.
- **Metrics:** Calibration is evaluated using Spearman's and Pearson's rank correlation. Active fine-tuning performance is measured by sample efficiency (rounds to reach success rate thresholds) and final success rate. Failure detection is assessed using accuracy, True Positive Rate (TPR), and detection time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness of VFD for uncertainty quantification and its application in the SAVE framework for active fine-tuning and failure detection.

#### 4.1. Calibration of Uncertainty Estimates (Q1)

- **Superior Calibration of VFD:** VFD demonstrated better calibration than all compared baselines. It achieved a negative Spearman rank correlation of 0.71 (with a standard deviation of 0.03) between mean uncertainty and per-task success rates, which was 0.09 higher than the next best method, Generative Uncertainty (GU) (0.62 ± 0.00). Similarly, VFD showed a higher negative Pearson correlation (0.71 ± 0.02). This indicates a stronger and more consistent inverse relationship between VFD uncertainty and task success probability.
- **Small Ensemble Sufficiency:** Experiments showed that VLA ensembles remain well-calibrated even with a small size of `M=2`. This is significant as it reduces the computational cost associated with training and maintaining multiple ensemble members, making the method more practical.
- **Modality Agnostic Calibration:** VFD was observed to be well-calibrated not only for variations in visual and proprioceptive inputs but also for changes in language instructions, implying its ability to capture epistemic uncertainty across different input modalities.

#### 4.2. Active Fine-Tuning Performance (Q2, Q3)

- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency with SAVE (VFD-guided):** SAVE, when guided by VFD uncertainty, exhibited superior sample efficiency in multitask adaptation. It required at least 22% fewer expert demonstrations to achieve similar performance levels compared to other uncertainty estimation baselines (Action-L2, GU). Specifically, it required 50% fewer samples than random selection and 45% fewer than diversity-based selection to reach the same success rates in the later rounds of fine-tuning.
- **Higher Overall Success Rate:** After 15 rounds of active fine-tuning, SAVE with VFD-guided data acquisition achieved the highest overall success rate of 67.1% (±3.2%) across tasks. This outperformed other methods by 3% to 12% given the same budget of expert demonstrations. The performance of active fine-tuning was directly correlated with the calibration quality of the underlying uncertainty signal.
- **Impact of Uncertainty Guidance:** The research indicated that most of the performance gain over random acquisition originated from prioritizing tasks based on uncertainty. Uncertainty-based selection of initial observations within a sampled task provided additional, albeit smaller, improvements. This hierarchical selection strategy effectively allocated the demonstration budget to tasks where the model's knowledge was weakest.
- **Exploration-Exploitation Trade-off:** The temperature parameter `τ` in the task-sampling distribution allowed for controlling an exploration-exploitation trade-off. Moderate `τ` values (e.g., `τ=2.5`) led to faster improvement in multitask success by focusing on high-uncertainty tasks, while higher `τ` values could lead to insufficient exploration of less uncertain but still important tasks. VFD-based sampling rapidly reduced the concentration of uncertainty in the most uncertain task, promoting a more balanced improvement across tasks.

#### 4.3. Failure Detection During Deployment (Q4)

- **Effective Failure Prediction:** High VFD scores during policy deployment were found to be a strong indicator of imminent task failure.
- **Detection Performance:** VFD achieved a detection accuracy of 67% and a True-Positive-Rate (TPR) of 79% (correctly predicting 79% of all failures). These results compare favorably against baselines specifically designed for failure detection (ACE, STAC, RND-OE), demonstrating the generalizability of VFD as a reliable uncertainty signal for runtime monitoring.

In summary, the empirical results confirm that VFD provides well-calibrated epistemic uncertainty estimates for flow-based VLAs, which can be effectively utilized to guide sample-efficient active fine-tuning and to detect failures during deployment, contributing to enhanced reliability and adaptability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation by addressing a critical limitation of modern flow-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: their inability to quantify prediction confidence. The introduction of Velocity Field Disagreement (VFD) and the SAVE framework has several implications and potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Enhanced Reliability and Safety in Robotic Systems

The primary significance lies in enabling VLAs to express their epistemic uncertainty. This capability is crucial for deploying robotic systems in non-stationary, real-world environments where models inevitably encounter out-of-distribution scenarios. By providing a calibrated measure of "what the model does not know," VFD allows for:
- **Failure Detection:** High VFD scores can indicate imminent task failure during deployment, enabling timely human intervention or activation of safety fallback policies. This moves beyond models that confidently execute erratic actions, towards robots that can signal unreliability.
- **Informed Decision Making:** Robots equipped with VFD can potentially make more informed decisions, such as abstaining from an uncertain action, asking for human assistance, or initiating a self-repair or re-planning process when faced with high uncertainty. This contributes directly to the safety and trustworthiness of autonomous systems.

#### 5.2. Improved Data Efficiency for VLA Adaptation

The SAVE framework leverages VFD to guide active data acquisition, which has a substantial impact on the cost and scalability of adapting VLAs.
- **Reduced Expert Demonstration Costs:** By prioritizing tasks and initial observations where the VLA is most uncertain, SAVE significantly reduces the number of costly human expert demonstrations required to fine-tune models for new tasks or domains. This makes the adaptation process more economical and accessible, especially for complex robotic skills.
- **Faster Model Improvement:** The targeted data collection leads to faster improvement in multitask performance compared to random or diversity-based data acquisition strategies. This accelerates the deployment pipeline for new robotic applications.

#### 5.3. Advancements in Uncertainty Quantification for Generative Models

The mathematical grounding of VFD, linking KL divergence to velocity field disagreement in flow-matching models, contributes to the theoretical understanding of uncertainty in modern generative architectures. This theoretical insight, combined with an efficient computational method, provides a practical tool that can be extended or adapted for other flow-based generative models beyond VLAs.

#### 5.4. Facilitating Real-World Deployment of VLAs

The combination of failure awareness and data-efficient adaptation addresses two major hurdles for the robust deployment of VLAs in open-world settings. By enhancing both the reliability during operation and the efficiency of learning new behaviors, this work moves VLA research closer to practical applications in diverse fields, from industrial automation to service robotics.

**Potential Limitations and Future Work:**
While impactful, the authors acknowledge limitations, such as the reliance on maintaining an ensemble of models (even if small) and the use of initial observation uncertainty as a proxy for overall task difficulty. Future work could explore single-model uncertainty estimation techniques, more sophisticated uncertainty signals beyond initial states, and methods to account for informational dependencies between tasks to further optimize data acquisition. Nonetheless, this research provides a foundational step towards more intelligent and trustworthy robotic systems.
