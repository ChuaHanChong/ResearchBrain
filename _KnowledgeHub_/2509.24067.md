# In-Context Compositional Q-Learning for Offline Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> In-context Compositional Q-Learning (ICQL) develops an offline reinforcement learning framework that leverages linear Transformers and in-context learning to infer adaptive local Q-functions for complex, compositional tasks. This approach yielded average performance increases of 8.8% on MuJoCo, 6.3% on Adroit, and 16.4% on Kitchen D4RL benchmarks, while generating Q-value estimates that more closely align with online reinforcement learning methods.

## Problem

- Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces the distributional shift problem, leading to unreliable Q-value estimates and suboptimal policies when queried for state-action pairs not sufficiently represented in the static dataset.
- Existing offline RL methods often rely on a single, global Q-function, which is inefficient and insufficient for tasks with inherent compositional structures or distinct local dynamics.
- Global function approximators struggle to generalize to novel, high-performing behaviors or guarantee optimality with biased or sparse datasets, as knowledge from one subtask may not directly transfer to another.

## Method

- **ICQL** reframes offline Q-learning as a ==contextual inference== problem, defining local Q-function approximations that adapt to specific state regions rather than a single global function.
- A linear Transformer-based in-context learning (ICL) module is employed to dynamically infer context-dependent weights for these local Q-functions, leveraging a small, retrieved set of state-similar transitions.
- The framework integrates with existing offline RL algorithms (e.g., IQL, TD3+BC) by using expectile regression for critic training and advantage-weighted regression for policy optimization, all based on locally estimated Q-values.

## Results

- ICQL achieved average performance gains of **8.8%** over CQL on MuJoCo, **6.3%** over IQL on Adroit, and a notable **16.4%** over DT on Kitchen D4RL benchmarks.
- The framework produced Q-value estimates with a higher correlation (e.g., **0.69**) to online RL values (SAC) compared to baseline offline methods (e.g., IQL's **0.29** correlation) on the Walker2d-Medium dataset.
- Ablation studies confirmed that state-similar retrieval, optimal context length (around 20 transitions), and the linear Transformer architecture are critical for effective and computationally efficient local Q-function inference, outperforming random retrieval and other in-context modeling choices.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Many complex RL tasks exhibit compositional structures, making locally adaptive Q-functions more effective than a single global Q-function for accurate value estimation and policy learning.
> - In-context learning with linear Transformers can effectively infer these local Q-functions by processing relevant contextual information, offering a flexible mechanism for addressing localized dynamics without explicit subtask labels.
> - Theoretical analysis demonstrates that accurate local Q-function approximations, under realistic assumptions about feature representation and context coverage, can lead to bounded policy sub-optimality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Offline Reinforcement Learning (Offline RL) is a subfield of reinforcement learning focused on learning optimal policies from static datasets without further interaction with the environment. This paradigm is particularly relevant for real-world applications where data collection is costly, risky, or impractical, such as robotics, logistics, and operations research. A fundamental challenge in Offline RL is the **distributional shift** problem, where a learned policy may query state-action pairs not sufficiently represented in the training dataset. This can lead to unreliable value function estimations, often manifesting as overestimation, and consequently, to suboptimal or unsafe policy performance.

Existing approaches to mitigate distributional shift primarily fall into two categories: **policy constraint methods** and **value regularization methods**. Policy constraint methods, such as Behavior Cloning (BC), Decision Transformer (DT), and TD3+BC, aim to keep the learned policy close to the data-collecting behavior policy. However, their performance can be limited by the quality and coverage of the offline data, potentially restricting generalization to novel, higher-performing behaviors. Value regularization methods, including Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) and Implicit Q-Learning (IQL), attempt to provide conservative estimates of Q-values for out-of-distribution actions, imposing penalties to prevent overestimation. While these methods offer softer constraints, their optimality is not universally guaranteed, especially with biased or sparse datasets.

This paper identifies a limitation within these existing frameworks: their reliance on a **shared global Q-function**. Many complex RL tasks inherently possess a **compositional structure**, meaning they can be naturally broken down into diverse subtasks. A global Q-function may struggle to effectively capture the distinct local dynamics and value patterns associated with these different subtasks. For instance, in locomotion tasks, knowledge for increasing speed might not directly transfer to recovery from an unexpected fall. The paper illustrates this with dimensionality-reduced state distributions where geometrically similar clusters can have semantically different behaviors and future returns, a phenomenon not adequately addressed by global value functions in data-limited offline settings.

Into this landscape, the work introduces **in-context learning (ICL)**, typically associated with large language models and Transformers, as a mechanism for compositional value estimation in Offline RL. While Transformers have been applied to Offline RL through sequence modeling for return-conditioned policy learning (e.g., Decision Transformer) or behavior cloning, they have not been extensively explored for explicit value function estimation, particularly with a focus on local and compositional adaptation. This research extends the theoretical and empirical understanding of ICL to the domain of value-based Offline RL, proposing it as a method to infer local Q-functions adaptively without requiring explicit subtask labels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an offline reinforcement learning framework that can accurately estimate Q-functions in tasks exhibiting compositional structures, thereby overcoming the limitations of approaches that rely on a single, global Q-function.

The motivation for this work stems from several detailed observations and perceived gaps in the current Offline RL landscape:

- **Inadequacy of Global Q-functions for Compositional Tasks:** The authors observe that many real-world control tasks are inherently compositional, consisting of multiple distinct subtasks. Current Offline RL methods typically learn a single, global Q-function to cover the entire state-action space. This global approach is often inefficient and insufficient because knowledge gained from one subtask may not be directly transferable or applicable to another. For example, the optimal Q-values and dynamics in one region of the state space might differ significantly from another, even if the states appear geometrically similar after dimensionality reduction. A global function approximator struggles to capture these localized, distinct patterns, especially when offline data is limited and exploration is not possible.

- **Addressing Distributional Shift through Local Adaptation:** While existing methods tackle distributional shift through policy constraints or value regularization, they primarily operate on a global scale. The motivation here is to provide a more granular solution by allowing the Q-function to adapt locally to the specific characteristics of different state regions. By inferring local Q-functions, the framework aims to provide more accurate and reliable value estimates, particularly for state-action pairs that might be out-of-distribution with respect to the global dataset, but well-represented within a local context.

- **Leveraging In-Context Learning for Flexible Q-function Approximation:** The authors are motivated by the emergent capabilities of Transformers and in-context learning in other domains (e.g., natural language processing, supervised learning). They posit that these capabilities can be adapted to Offline RL to infer local Q-functions. This approach aims to dynamically adjust value estimation based on a small, retrieved set of relevant transitions (context) without needing predefined subtask labels or explicit task decomposition. This provides a flexible and adaptive mechanism for Q-function approximation that can capture local variations.

- **Establishing Theoretical Guarantees:** Beyond empirical performance, a key motivation is to provide a theoretical foundation for this novel approach. The goal is to formally analyze how accurate local Q-function approximations contribute to overall policy optimality, under realistic assumptions about feature representation and context coverage. This aims to provide justification for the importance of accurate local value estimation in achieving strong policy performance in offline settings.

- **Empirical Validation across Diverse Benchmarks:** The motivation also includes demonstrating the practical effectiveness and general applicability of the proposed framework. This involves evaluating its performance on a wide array of challenging D4RL benchmarks, including MuJoCo locomotion, Adroit dexterous manipulation, and Kitchen long-horizon compositional tasks, to highlight its robustness and advantages over established baselines.

In summary, the work is motivated by the need for more nuanced and adaptive Q-function estimation in Offline RL, particularly for compositional tasks, and proposes to fulfill this need by integrating in-context learning with local value approximation, supported by theoretical analysis and extensive empirical validation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, In-context Compositional Q-Learning (ICQL), reframes offline Q-learning as a contextual inference problem. It leverages the in-context learning capabilities of linear Transformers to adaptively infer local Q-functions.

#### 3.1. Local Q-Functions

ICQL begins by defining a "local Q-function approximation" for a given state `s`. For any query transition `(s, a, r, s', a')`, a neighborhood `Ω(d, d_bar)_s` is defined as the set of transitions in the offline dataset `D` whose states `s_i` are within an `l2`-distance `d` of `s`, and whose state-action transitions `||s'_i - s_i||` are bounded by `d_bar`. Within this local domain, it is assumed that an optimal uniform local weight vector `w*_s` exists such that the local Q-function can be approximated linearly: `Q_Ω(d, d_bar)_s (s_bar, a_bar) ≈ w*_s^T ϕ(s_bar, a_bar)`, where `ϕ` is a feature function mapping state-action pairs to `R^d`. This assumption allows for a family of value functions, adapting locally to different state regions, rather than a single global approximator.

#### 3.2. Retrieval Methods

Since the ideal radius `d` is not directly tunable, ICQL employs a retrieval mechanism with a size parameter `k` to control locality. For a given query state `s_query`, `k` transitions are retrieved from the offline dataset `D` to form a local context set `Ω_d_k_s_query`. Three retrieval strategies are explored:
- **State-Similar Retrieval (main method):** Retrieves `k` transitions where the `l2`-distance between the retrieved state `s_i` and `s_query` is minimized. This method aims to capture geographically similar states.
- **Random Retrieval:** Selects `k` transitions uniformly at random from `D`, serving as a baseline to evaluate the importance of contextual relevance.
- **State-Similar-with-High-Rewards Retrieval:** Filters state-similar candidates by selecting those with higher rewards, intending to prioritize high-quality transitions within the local neighborhood.

#### 3.3. In-Context Compositional Q-Learning (ICQL)

The core of ICQL is a linear Transformer that infers context-dependent weights `w_s` for local Q-function estimation.
1. **Input Prompt Construction:** For a query state-action pair `(s_query, a_query)` and its retrieved context `Ω_d_k_s_query` (containing `N=k` transitions), an input "prompt" matrix `Z_0` is constructed. This matrix concatenates the feature embeddings `ϕ(s_i, a_i)` and `γϕ(s'_i, a'_i)` along with the rewards `r_i` for each retrieved transition, and includes the query feature `ϕ(s_query, a_query)` with a masked return.
2. **Linear Transformer for Weight Inference:** An L-layer linear Transformer `TF_Q_θ` processes `Z_0`. Each layer performs a linear attention operation `LinAttn(Z; P, G)`, where `P` and `G` are learnable parameter matrices. Crucially, the paper demonstrates (Theorem D.2) that the linear Transformer iteratively updates the weight vector `w_l_s_query(Ω_d_k_s_query)` across its layers in a manner analogous to pre-conditioned SARSA:
 `w_(l+1)_s_query = w_l_s_query + (1/N) * C_l * Σ (r_j + γw_l^T ϕ(s'_j, a'_j) - w_l^T ϕ(s_j, a_j)) * ϕ(s_j, a_j)`.
 The final output of the Transformer provides the context-dependent Q-function estimate: `Q_hat(s_query, a_query | Ω_d_k_s_query) = w_L_s_query(Ω_d_k_s_query)^T ϕ(s_query, a_query)`.
3. **Training Procedure:** ICQL integrates into existing offline RL frameworks. The paper primarily demonstrates its integration with IQL (Implicit Q-Learning) and TD3+BC (Twin Delayed DDPG + Behavior Cloning).
 - **Critic Loss:** The critic `TF_Q_θ` is trained using expectile regression on the local Q-function estimates, similar to IQL: `L_critic = E_(s,a,r,s')~D [ρ_τ (Q_hat(s, a | Ω_d_k_s) - y)]`, where `y = r + γV(s' | Ω_d_k_s')` and `V` is a context-dependent value estimator.
 - **Policy Loss:** The policy `π` is optimized via advantage-weighted regression (similar to IQL) or behavior cloning regularization (similar to TD3+BC), using advantages derived from the locally estimated Q-values: `L_policy = E_s~D [E_a~π [exp(β · (Q_hat(s, a | Ω_d_k_s) - V(s | Ω_d_k_s))) log π(a|s)]]`.
 - The feature extractor `ϕ` is jointly trained with the linear Transformer. After training, the policy is evaluated independently without further retrieval.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides theoretical guarantees for ICQL, establishing pointwise and expected bounds on the local Q-function approximation error and how this error propagates to policy sub-optimality. Key assumptions include:
- **Bounded Feature Norm (Assumption 3.1):** The norm of the feature vectors `ϕ(s, a)` is bounded.
- **Set Coverage (Assumption 3.3):** The retrieved set `Ω_d_k_s_query` covers a certain proportion `σ` of an "ideal" local transition set `Ω*_s_query`.
Under these assumptions, Theorem 3.5 shows that the performance gap between the optimal policy `π*` and the greedy policy `π` (with respect to `Q_hat`) is bounded. This bound depends on the local approximation error `ε_s_approx` and a term related to `d + log(1/δ) / (σ |Ω_d_k_s_query|)`, highlighting the trade-off between feature expressiveness, retrieval quality (coverage `σ` and size `k`), and approximation accuracy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ICQL was conducted on D4RL benchmarks, encompassing MuJoCo, Adroit, and Kitchen tasks, and compared against several established offline RL algorithms.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Significant Performance Gains:** ICQL demonstrated performance improvements across the tested domains.
 - On **MuJoCo tasks**, ICQL achieved an average gain of 8.8% over the second-best baseline, CQL.
 - On **Adroit tasks**, ICQL improved IQL's average score by 6.3%.
 - On **Kitchen tasks**, ICQL exhibited a notable 16.4% improvement over DT, underscoring its effectiveness in environments requiring compositional reasoning.
- **Competitive with Extended Baselines:** When compared to additional advanced baselines (RA-DT, ReBRAC, DMG, FQL, QC), ICQL generally maintained competitive or superior performance across most tasks (Table 7).
- **Task-Specific Underperformance:** ICQL's performance on the `Hammer-Human-v1` dataset was lower than some baselines. The authors attribute this to the dataset's small size, sparse coverage, and low-quality, noisy reward signals, which hinder effective local Q-function learning (Appendix H.4).

#### 4.2. Value Estimation Accuracy

- **Closer to Online RL Estimates:** Analysis of the learned Q-functions showed that ICQL produced Q-value estimates that had higher similarity (e.g., correlation score of 0.69) with those of an online RL algorithm (SAC) compared to IQL (e.g., correlation score of 0.29) on the `Walker2d-Medium` dataset (Figure 3, Figure 8, Figure 9). This suggests that ICQL's local Q-function estimation strategy contributes to more accurate value predictions.
- **Support for Compositional Hypothesis:** The t-SNE visualizations of Q-value distributions across states revealed distinct, partitioned value patterns, which supports the paper's hypothesis that Q-functions are inherently compositional in nature.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Number of In-Context Learning Layers:** Increasing the number of linear Transformer layers (representing more in-context learning steps) generally led to higher normalized scores on MuJoCo tasks (Figure 4, Table 8), indicating that more iterative refinement benefits value learning.
- **Influence of Context Length:** Optimal performance was typically observed at a moderate context length (e.g., 20 transitions) in Gym tasks. Both excessively short or long contexts resulted in suboptimal performance (Figure 5, Table 8), highlighting the importance of balancing local relevance with sufficient information and avoiding noise.
- **Context Retrieval Strategies:**
 - **State-Similar Retrieval** provided the strongest overall performance, validating the benefit of constructing context from locally similar states (Table 2).
 - **Random Retrieval** was generally less effective, confirming the importance of context relevance.
 - **State-Similar-with-High-Rewards Retrieval** outperformed other strategies on some tasks (e.g., `Walker2d-Medium`, `HalfCheetah-Medium`, `Kitchen-Mixed`), suggesting that incorporating reward information can be beneficial when high-value transitions are particularly informative.
- **In-Context Modeling Choices:** The **linear Transformer** architecture for the in-context module consistently achieved the best performance across most tasks compared to a simple linear MLP and a standard self-attention-based Transformer (Table 3). The linear Transformer showed particular advantages on replay-style and long-horizon datasets, indicating its empirical importance for stable and effective local Q-function estimation.

#### 4.4. Computational Overhead

- **Moderate Cost:** While ICQL introduced moderate additional computational cost compared to some baselines (e.g., IQL, TD3+BC), it remained more efficient than sequential models like DT and RA-DT in terms of training time, inference time, and GFLOPs (Table 9).
- **Scalability:** The training GFLOPs and memory consumption scaled approximately linearly with both the context length and the number of linear Transformer layers (Tables 10, 11). Pre-computing retrieval indices significantly reduced per-step retrieval overhead during training, making the approach practical (Tables 12, 13, 14).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper, introducing In-context Compositional Q-Learning (ICQL), demonstrates several points of significance and offers potential impact within the field of offline reinforcement learning.

Firstly, the paper's primary significance lies in its **novel formulation of Q-learning as a contextual inference problem** within the offline RL setting. By leveraging in-context learning with linear Transformers to adaptively infer local Q-functions, ICQL moves beyond the conventional reliance on a shared global Q-function. This approach directly addresses the challenge of capturing the compositional structure inherent in many complex tasks without requiring explicit subtask labels or predefined task hierarchies, which are often unavailable in real-world offline scenarios.

Secondly, the **theoretical analysis** provided for ICQL contributes to the foundational understanding of in-context learning in value-based RL. By showing that ICQL can achieve a bounded approximation error for the Q-function under realistic assumptions (linear approximability of local Q-functions and accurate weight inference from retrieved context), and that it enables near-optimal policy extraction, the paper provides formal guarantees for its empirical effectiveness. This theoretical underpinning strengthens the credibility of in-context learning as a principled approach for robust value estimation.

Thirdly, the **empirical results** demonstrate substantial performance improvements across diverse and challenging D4RL benchmarks, including MuJoCo locomotion, Adroit dexterous manipulation, and particularly on long-horizon, compositional Kitchen tasks. The observed gains (up to 16.4% on Kitchen tasks and 8.8% on MuJoCo) indicate that the compositional value estimation provided by ICQL is effective in real-world-relevant environments. Furthermore, the finding that ICQL produces Q-value estimates that are more aligned with those of online RL algorithms (like SAC) than strong offline baselines (like IQL) suggests that the method generates more accurate and reliable value functions, which is crucial for safe and effective policy learning from static data.

Fourthly, the research highlights the **underexplored potential of in-context learning and linear attention mechanisms** for value estimation in RL. While Transformers have gained prominence in sequence modeling for policy generation, their application to adaptive, compositional value function approximation in Offline RL has been limited. ICQL serves as a proof-of-concept for how these architectural inductive biases can be leveraged to enhance value-based methods, opening new avenues for research at the intersection of large models and reinforcement learning.

Finally, the **ablation studies** provide insights into the critical components of ICQL, such as the importance of context length, retrieval strategy, and the choice of in-context modeling architecture. These analyses not only validate the design choices of ICQL but also offer guidance for future work on retrieval-augmented and compositional RL systems. For instance, the superior performance of state-similar retrieval over random retrieval underscores the necessity of relevant context, while the effectiveness of linear Transformers over standard self-attention or MLPs points to the computational efficiency and stability benefits of this specific architecture for the task.

In summary, ICQL advances the state of the art in offline RL by introducing a theoretically grounded and empirically strong framework for compositional Q-learning. Its ability to infer local Q-functions adaptively without explicit supervision holds significant promise for tackling complex, multi-faceted tasks in data-limited settings, and it broadens the scope of in-context learning applications in AI.
