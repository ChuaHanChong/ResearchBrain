# Towards Understanding Self-play for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> An empirical analysis of self-play post-training for LLM reasoning, using the Absolute Zero Reasoner on Qwen2.5-Coder 3B and 7B. It measures pass@k capacity, policy entropy by proposer and solver role, parameter update sparsity against SFT and RLVR checkpoints, and an alternative proposer reward, and proves self-play preserves base-model support. Self-play sharpens sampling but stays bounded by the base model, with the proposer as the key driver.

## Problem

- Self-play post-training shows strong in-domain and out-of-domain reasoning gains, yet the mechanisms producing those gains remain poorly understood and largely unexamined.
- Prior RLVR analyses argue gains come from distributional sharpening, reduced entropy and sparse updates, but nobody checked whether self-play shares these dynamics.
- Proposer design choices, such as difficulty-targeting reward functions, are credited for gains in self-evolving systems without isolating their causal contribution.

## Method

- ==Unbiased pass@k estimation== on MBPP+, HumanEval+ and AIME compares self-play checkpoints against their base models to test whether reasoning capacity, not just sampling efficiency, grows.
- ==Training-dynamics probes==: ==policy-entropy== tracking decomposed by proposer and solver role, a ==frozen-proposer ablation==, and an ==update-sparsity metric== counting parameters unchanged versus the base checkpoint.
- A modified ==learnability reward== peaking at a 0.5 average solve rate replaces the default proposer reward, plus a balanced 800-question deductive probe set measuring response length and solve rate across training iterations.

## Results

- Self-play wins at small k but the base model overtakes it at large k on all four benchmarks, though the large-k gap is not statistically significant, unlike standard RLVR.
- Update sparsity is intermediate: about **45.7%** for the 7B self-play model versus **3.4%** for SFT and **94-98%** for RLVR checkpoints on the same base.
- Entropy collapses under self-play, faster for the trainable-proposer 3B than the frozen-proposer variant; the 0.5-peak proposer reward barely moved entropy and cut validation accuracy **2%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-play is RLVR over a self-generated task distribution, so on-policy updates without exploration or off-policy data cannot add probability mass outside base-model support.
> - Reward shaping toward 50% solve rate is not the active ingredient in self-evolving systems; credit likely belongs to other architectural components.
> - Proposer entropy stays above solver entropy, so promoting proposer diversity, not solver regularization, is the more promising lever against entropy collapse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper situates itself within the rapidly evolving landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning, a field profoundly impacted by advanced post-training techniques. Historically, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has been a dominant paradigm for enhancing reasoning capabilities in LLMs, especially in domains like mathematics and coding where correctness can be objectively verified. Techniques like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and its variants (e.g., DAPO) have shown significant success. However, the true nature of RLVR's improvements has been a subject of ongoing debate. Prior studies have questioned whether RLVR fosters genuine reasoning or primarily achieves "distributional sharpening"—making models more likely to sample correct answers from their existing knowledge without necessarily expanding their underlying reasoning capacity. Concerns have also been raised about RLVR's training dynamics, including phenomena like "entropy collapse" (where models reduce exploration by converging to a narrow set of highly rewarded behaviors) and "parameter update sparsity" (where only a small fraction of parameters are updated). A critical theoretical limitation, termed the "Invisible Leash" principle, suggests that standard on-policy RLVR methods cannot generate solutions outside the base model's initial probability support, thus bounding its reasoning capacity.

Against this backdrop, self-play has emerged as a compelling alternative paradigm. Inspired by its successes in game theory and complex problem-solving (e.g., AlphaGo), self-play for LLMs involves models generating and solving their own problems, often without additional human-curated data. Notable examples include frameworks where an LLM acts as both a proposer of coding problems and a solver, demonstrating strong gains on out-of-domain benchmarks. This "Absolute Zero" paradigm, as exemplified by the Absolute Zero Reasoner (AZR) framework, represents a significant step towards enabling models to improve autonomously.

This paper directly addresses a critical gap in the understanding of self-play: while its empirical success is evident, the underlying mechanisms driving these improvements remain poorly understood. By systematically analyzing the training dynamics of AZR and comparing them against RLVR and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), this work aims to shed light on how self-play influences model behavior, reasoning capacity, and internal representations. It directly engages with debates surrounding distributional sharpening versus genuine reasoning, the role of exploration and entropy, and the nature of parameter updates, thus contributing to a more nuanced understanding of post-training strategies for LLMs. The focus on math reasoning, a domain intrinsically verifiable, further solidifies its contribution to a field striving for robust and interpretable AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation of this research is to move "Towards Understanding Self-play for LLM Reasoning" by dissecting the mechanisms through which self-play post-training improves LLMs. Despite the demonstrated successes of self-play in various reasoning tasks, particularly in mathematics and coding, the specific ways these improvements are achieved and their inherent limitations are not well-understood. The authors aim to clarify how self-play differs from other established post-training strategies, namely RLVR and SFT, and to identify pathways for its further enhancement.

The research is guided by five specific research questions (RQs):

- **RQ1: Does self-play in AZR enable novel reasoning beyond the capacity of the base model?** This question probes the fundamental impact of self-play on an LLM's reasoning capabilities, specifically addressing whether it can overcome the "Invisible Leash" limitation often associated with RLVR.
- **RQ2: How does the difficulty of the questions proposed evolve over the course of training?** This investigates the implicit curriculum learning aspect of self-play, exploring if the model's "proposer" component can generate progressively challenging tasks, a critical factor for sustained learning.
- **RQ3: Does AZR experience entropy collapse during training like RLVR-trained models?** This examines the model's exploration-exploitation trade-off, a known issue in RL, to see if self-play similarly leads to a reduction in policy entropy and potential stagnation.
- **RQ4: How does parameter update sparsity in self-play compare with RLVR and SFT?** This question delves into the micro-level training dynamics, analyzing which parts of the model's parameters are updated during self-play, offering insights into its learning efficiency and potential impact on catastrophic forgetting.
- **RQ5: Does lowering the proposer’s target question difficulty improve model performance?** This explores the impact of explicit reward shaping on the proposer's behavior, testing a hypothesis that encouraging tasks of intermediate difficulty might optimize learning signals.

By addressing these questions, the authors seek to provide a comprehensive analysis that not only characterizes self-play but also points towards its inherent limitations and future directions for improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts the **Absolute Zero Reasoner (AZR)** framework as its primary self-play mechanism. AZR is a single-model self-play paradigm where a unified LLM acts as both a "proposer" (generating tasks) and a "solver" (learning to solve them). It is designed to handle three families of coding tasks: deductive (predicting output from program and input), abductive (inferring input from program and output), and inductive (synthesizing a program from input-output pairs). Task validation is conducted via environment execution, creating problems with gold solutions. The model is optimized using a multitask advantage estimator, with rewards for task quality (for the proposer) and solution accuracy (for the solver). Rewards also include a format-aware penalty.

Due to computational constraints, the experiments are conducted on **QWEN2.5-CODER-3B** and **QWEN2.5-CODER-7B** models, which serve as the base LLMs. The study employs a rigorous empirical approach, comparing AZR's dynamics and performance against established methods like standard RLVR and SFT.

The methodology for each research question is as follows:

- **RQ1 (Reasoning Capacity):** The authors employ an unbiased `pass@k` estimator to measure reasoning capacity, following the experimental setup of Yue et al.. Benchmarks include MBPP+, HumanEval+, AIME 2024, and AIME 2025. `pass@k` measures the probability that at least one out of `k` independent samples from the model correctly solves a problem.
- **RQ2 (Evolution of Question Difficulty):** To track question difficulty, a balanced dataset of 800 deductive questions is sampled from different training iterations. For each question, 8 responses are generated using both AZR-CODER-7B and its base model, QWEN2.5-CODER-7B. The average response length and average solve rate are measured as proxies for difficulty, leveraging the observation that longer responses often correlate with more complex reasoning.
- **RQ3 (Entropy Collapse):** Policy entropy is tracked over training steps to determine if self-play leads to entropy collapse, a phenomenon observed in RLVR. Policy entropy is defined as the negative expected log-probability of tokens generated by the policy across a dataset. The study also examines the entropy dynamics of the proposer and solver roles separately. An ablation study is performed with a "frozen proposer" variant to isolate the effects of proposer updates.
- **RQ4 (Parameter Update Sparsity):** Parameter update sparsity is quantified as `1 - (||θ1 - θ0||0 / n)`, where `||.||0` counts non-zero elements, `θ0` and `θ1` are model parameters at different checkpoints, and `n` is the total number of parameters. This metric measures the proportion of unchanged parameters. AZR-CODER models are compared against publicly available SFT and RLVR checkpoints using the same base models.
- **RQ5 (Proposer Reward Function):** A modified proposer reward function is tested. The default AZR proposer reward incentivizes tasks of moderate difficulty (rewards `1 - r_solve` if `r_solve` is not 0 or 1). The modified function explicitly targets an average solve rate of 0.5, giving maximum reward at `r_solve = 0.5` (`1 - 2|r_solve - 0.5|`). The impact on policy entropy and validation accuracy is then observed.

The approach combines performance evaluation on external benchmarks with internal dynamic analyses, offering a holistic view of self-play's effects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings, each addressing one of the defined research questions:

- **RQ1: Reasoning Capacity - Bounded by the Base Model:** The results from `pass@k` evaluations (Figure 1) demonstrate that self-play significantly improves performance at small `k` values on benchmarks like MBPP+, HumanEval+, and AIME. This suggests effective "distributional sharpening," where the model becomes more confident and precise in its top-ranked solutions. However, at larger `k` values, the base model often performs better than its self-play-trained counterpart. This indicates that AZR, like standard RLVR, remains **bounded by the reasoning capacity of the base model**, confirming the "Invisible Leash" principle (as further formalized in Appendix C). Self-play does not enable the model to discover entirely novel reasoning pathways beyond its initial support. Nonetheless, the performance drop at large `k` for AZR is less pronounced than typically observed with standard RLVR, which the authors hypothesize is due to the implicit data diversity generated by the co-evolutionary nature of self-play.

- **RQ2: Evolution of Question Difficulty - Adaptive Curriculum Generation:** The analysis of question difficulty (Figure 2) reveals that the proposer component in AZR generates **increasingly difficult questions** over the course of training. Correspondingly, AZR-CODER-7B produces longer responses for these more challenging questions, a trait often associated with deeper reasoning. For easier questions, AZR generates shorter responses. This suggests that self-play implicitly teaches the model to **adapt its response length based on the perceived difficulty** of a question, a mechanism that might help prevent both "underthinking" and "overthinking."

- **RQ3: Entropy Collapse - Present but Differentiated:** Self-play with AZR **does exhibit entropy collapse**, similar to RLVR-trained models (Figure 3). The rate of decay in policy entropy varies with model size and the experimental setup. Notably, a "frozen proposer" variant (where the proposer role does not receive PPO updates) maintains higher entropy than the standard 3B model. This suggests that the dual optimization pressure on both proposer and solver roles in standard self-play accelerates the collapse. When decomposed by role, **proposer entropy consistently remains higher than solver entropy** (Figure 4), implying that the proposer maintains a higher degree of exploration in its output distribution compared to the solver.

- **RQ4: Parameter Update Sparsity - Intermediate Sparsity:** The analysis of parameter update sparsity (Figure 5) reveals that self-play induces an **intermediate level of sparsity**. Self-play models exhibit denser updates than RLVR models but sparser updates than SFT models. For example, AZR-CODER-7B showed approximately 45.7% sparsity. This distinct pattern is attributed to the dual nature of self-play, where the model simultaneously generates new data (which might involve some exploration) and learns from solving it (leading to more focused updates).

- **RQ5: Proposer Reward Function - Limited Impact of Simple Reshaping:** Modifying the proposer's reward function to explicitly target a 50% average solve rate (a strategy sometimes suggested to maximize gradient signals in RL) had **little effect on entropy dynamics and slightly reduced final validation accuracy by 2%** (Figure 6). This finding suggests that simply altering the proposer's reward function in this manner is insufficient to significantly improve AZR performance, implying that other system components or more complex reward shaping strategies are crucial for self-play gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides crucial insights into the internal workings of self-play for LLM reasoning, a burgeoning area with immense potential. By systematically comparing AZR against RLVR and SFT, the study clarifies both the strengths and inherent limitations of self-play, contributing significantly to the theoretical and practical understanding of post-training methodologies for LLMs.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Clarifying Self-play Mechanisms:** The detailed analysis of training dynamics (entropy, sparsity, curriculum evolution) moves beyond empirical observations of performance gains to explain *how* self-play operates. It confirms that distributional sharpening is a primary mechanism, similar to RLVR, but highlights how self-play's unique dynamics might mitigate some of RLVR's drawbacks (e.g., less severe pass@k drop).
2. **Identifying the Proposer as a Critical Component:** The finding that the proposer generates an adaptive, increasingly difficult curriculum and maintains higher entropy than the solver underscores its vital role. This suggests that the proposer is not merely a data generator but an active driver of the learning process, shaping the educational experience of the solver.
3. **Reinforcing the "Invisible Leash" Limitation:** The confirmation that AZR remains bounded by the base model's reasoning capacity (RQ1) is a significant theoretical contribution. It extends the "Invisible Leash" principle to self-play, emphasizing that current on-policy self-play methods are unlikely to discover truly novel reasoning skills that were entirely absent in the base model's initial probability distribution. This sets clear expectations for what self-play can achieve and guides future research towards methods that can "break the leash."
4. **Novel Insights into Parameter Updates:** The discovery of an intermediate level of parameter update sparsity for self-play (RQ4) distinguishes it from both dense SFT updates and sparse RLVR updates. This unique characteristic warrants further investigation, potentially offering insights into how self-play balances exploration and exploitation, and its implications for phenomena like catastrophic forgetting—a crucial concern in continuous learning.
5. **Guiding Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines several promising avenues for future work:
 - **Expanding Reasoning Support:** Developing methods to overcome the "Invisible Leash" by injecting off-support mass or integrating new tools/capacities is essential for achieving genuinely novel reasoning.
 - **Advanced Curriculum Learning:** Designing more effective automatic curriculum learning strategies within self-play could unlock new capabilities by tailoring problem difficulty more precisely.
 - **Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting:** Investigating the link between intermediate sparsity and catastrophic forgetting is important for ensuring the stability and robustness of self-play models.
 - **Sustaining Exploration:** Addressing entropy collapse through proposer modifications or explicit entropy regularization is critical for preventing stagnation and ensuring continuous learning.
 - **Refining Proposer Rewards:** Further exploration of sophisticated reward functions for the proposer, beyond simple target solve rates, is needed to provide stronger and more nuanced learning signals.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Improved LLM Training:** These findings can inform the design of more effective and robust post-training strategies for LLMs, especially in domains requiring strong reasoning capabilities like scientific discovery, coding, and mathematical problem-solving.
- **Foundation for Autonomous AI:** A deeper understanding of self-play dynamics is fundamental for developing truly autonomous AI systems that can continuously learn and improve without constant human supervision.
- **Better Benchmarking and Evaluation:** The insights into `pass@k` limitations and distributional sharpening emphasize the need for comprehensive evaluation metrics that can differentiate between superficial performance gains and genuine increases in reasoning capacity.
- **Theoretical Advancement:** The work contributes to the theoretical understanding of reinforcement learning and language model fine-tuning, particularly concerning policy entropy, parameter updates, and support limitations.

In conclusion, this research provides a foundational understanding of self-play in LLMs, highlighting its strengths as an adaptive curriculum generator and its limitations in expanding raw reasoning capacity. It sets a clear agenda for future work, emphasizing the critical role of the proposer and the need to address exploration and support boundaries to realize the full potential of self-play for advanced LLM reasoning.
