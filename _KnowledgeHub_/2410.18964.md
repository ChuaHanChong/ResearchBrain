# Learning to Look: Seeking Information for Decision Making via Policy Factorization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Texas at Austin introduce DISaM, a dual-policy framework enabling robots to learn to actively seek task-relevant information and then use it for manipulation. This method, leveraging an intrinsic reward for information-seeking, consistently achieved higher success rates than baseline approaches across various complex simulated and real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic agents often assume that all necessary task information is directly available from sensors or requires human pre-specification.
- End-to-end reinforcement learning for Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) or Contextual MDPs (CMDPs) struggles with long action sequences, sparse rewards, and computational complexity in information-gathering tasks.
- Existing active and interactive perception solutions typically require human-defined specifications for what information needs to be gathered, limiting robot autonomy in novel or dynamic environments.

## Method

- The work formalizes tasks requiring active information gathering as ==factorized Contextual Markov Decision Processes (fCMDPs)==, where actions are separated into information-seeking (IS) and information-receiving (IR) categories.
- It introduces **DISaM**, a ==dual-policy architecture== with an ==Information-Seeking (IS) policy== and an ==Information-Receiving (IR) policy==, learned separately but collaboratively.
- An ==intrinsic reward== mechanism is used for the IS policy, derived from the IR policy's predicted action discrepancy (based on inferred vs. ground-truth context), and a ==dynamic uncertainty-based switching== mechanism decides when to perform IS or IR actions.

## Results

- **DISaM** consistently outperformed all baseline methods in overall success rates across **five** diverse tasks, including complex multi-stage tasks in simulation and real-world settings (e.g., Cooking, Walls, Teatime).
- The intrinsic reward mechanism was shown to be critical for the IS agent's learning, with an ablation using sparse task rewards performing significantly worse than the full **DISaM**.
- The framework demonstrated robustness to different context representations, effectively learning with both one-hot encodings and continuous language (CLIP) embeddings, albeit with a slight performance drop for the latter in more complex tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Factorizing the problem into distinct information-seeking and manipulation policies simplifies learning and addresses the challenges of sparse rewards in long-horizon, partially observable tasks.
> - An intrinsic reward signal, generated by comparing the manipulation policy's actions based on inferred versus ground-truth context, provides a dense and effective learning signal for the information-seeking agent.
> - Dynamically switching between information-seeking and manipulation actions based on the manipulation policy's uncertainty enables efficient and adaptive task execution, allowing the robot to gather information only when needed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in robotics where agents operate in environments requiring active or interactive exploration to gather necessary information for task completion. This contrasts with traditional approaches that often assume task-relevant information is readily available through sensors or rely on predefined state representations.

Current methods in **active perception** enable agents to adjust sensor parameters (e.g., camera pose) to infer information like object pose or shape. **Interactive perception** extends this by allowing agents to change the environment state to create information-rich signals for perception. However, these solutions typically factorize perception and manipulation, requiring human specification of the information to be gathered.

**Belief space planning** approaches jointly reason about uncertainty reduction and action selection but often require accurate forward and observation models, leading to computational complexity in intricate state and sensor spaces. Solutions to **Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs)**, including model-free reinforcement learning (RL) with memory, face challenges with long action sequences and sparse rewards, limiting their applicability to simpler settings.

The paper frames its problem within **Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs)** or Latent MDPs, which are a class of POMDPs where reward and transition dynamics depend on an unobserved context. Existing CMDP theory has been developed in multi-task, model-based, continual, and inverse RL settings. This research builds upon CMDPs by proposing a model of active perception where one agent seeks information to determine the latent context, and another acts based on that context.

This work contributes to the landscape by offering a factorized approach that aims to overcome the limitations of specifying information a priori, handling sparse rewards, and managing the complexity of joint learning in long-horizon, partially observable tasks. It distinguishes itself by enabling agents to *learn* what information needs to be inferred and by decoupling information-seeking from manipulation through a dual-policy architecture.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop robotic agents capable of *learning* to perform information-seeking actions to discover task-relevant information, and then utilizing this information to execute manipulation goals. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current learning robots:

- **Assumption of Readily Available Information:** Many contemporary robot learning systems operate under the assumption that all necessary information for manipulation is directly accessible from their sensor signals (e.g., a fixed camera view) or through human-defined low-dimensional state representations. This restricts robots' autonomy in dynamic or partially observable environments where critical information might be hidden or require active exploration.
- **Human-Specified Information:** Existing active and interactive perception solutions often necessitate a human to pre-specify *what* information the robot should gather (e.g., "find the object's pose"). This limits the robot's ability to adapt to novel situations where the required information is not predefined or changes dynamically.
- **Challenges with Joint Optimization in POMDPs/CMDPs:** While POMDPs and CMDPs provide frameworks for decision-making under partial observability, solving them with a single, unified policy using reinforcement learning is computationally challenging. This is particularly true for long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards, where finding sequences of actions to first explore for information and then exploit it for a task goal becomes prohibitive.
- **Need for Context-Dependent Behavior:** Many real-world tasks require actions to be conditioned on an unknown context (e.g., a diner's preference, the time of day, a specific instruction). Robots need a robust mechanism to identify this context to make informed decisions.

To address these issues, the research aims to:
1. **Formalize Information-Seeking Tasks:** Introduce a general formalism, "factorized Contextual Markov Decision Processes (fCMDPs)," to model tasks requiring active information gathering for subsequent manipulation.
2. **Develop a Dual-Policy Solution:** Propose a dual-policy architecture, DISaM (Dual Information-Seeking and Manipulation), that separates the problem into two collaborative policies: one for actively seeking contextual information and another for manipulating based on that information.
3. **Enable Efficient Learning:** Design a training methodology that leverages this factorization to make the learning process more efficient and tractable, particularly by using the information-receiving policy to provide intrinsic rewards for the information-seeking policy.
4. **Facilitate Dynamic Exploration-Exploitation:** Implement a mechanism for the dual agent to autonomously balance between information-seeking (exploration) and task execution (exploitation) based on the uncertainty of the manipulation policy regarding its next action.

The motivation is to endow robots with greater autonomy, allowing them to adapt to unstructured environments by actively seeking and interpreting information, rather than passively receiving it or relying on human pre-programming for every piece of contextual data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the introduction of a new problem formalism, **factorized Contextual Markov Decision Processes (fCMDPs)**, and a dual-policy solution named **DISaM (Dual Information-Seeking and Manipulation)** to address it.

#### 3.1. Factorized Contextual Markov Decision Processes (fCMDPs)

The authors frame tasks requiring information-seeking as Contextual Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs), where dynamics and rewards depend on a hidden contextual variable *c*. A CMDP is defined by (C, S, A, O, M(c)), where C is the set of contexts, S states, A actions, O observations, and M(c) is an MDP for context *c*.
The key insight is that in many robotic manipulation problems, the agent's action space A can be naturally factored into two parts: A = A_IS ∪ A_IR.
- **A_IS (Information-Seeking Actions):** Actions primarily useful for revealing context (e.g., moving a camera, navigating, interacting with specific scene elements).
- **A_IR (Information-Receiving/Manipulation Actions):** Actions sufficient to complete the task given the correct context (e.g., robot arm movements for grasping or placing).

This factorization leads to two collaborative policies:
- **Information-Seeking (IS) Policy (π_IS):** Maps observations O_IS to information-seeking actions A_IS (π_IS: O_IS → A_IS). It provides context information *c_IS* ∈ C to the IR policy.
- **Information-Receiving (IR) Policy (π_IR):** Consumes the retrieved context and maps observations O_IR and context *c* to manipulation actions A_IR (π_IR: O_IR × C → A_IR).

An observation encoder, E_ϕ: O_IS → C, learns to convert observations from the IS agent into contexts for the IR agent. The policies can share the same embodiment or operate on separate ones. The method benefits when there is minimal overlap in the action spaces of the two policies.

#### 3.2. DISaM: Dual Information-Seeking and Manipulation Policies

DISaM employs a two-phase learning procedure:

**Phase 1: Training the Information-Receiving (IR) Agent**
- **Objective:** Train π_IR to perform manipulation tasks assuming access to the *ground truth context* (c_GT).
- **Method:** The IR policy (π_IR: O_IR × C → A_IR) is trained using **imitation learning (IL)**. A dataset of expert human demonstrations (D = {τ_1..., τ_n}), labeled with ground truth context, is collected. The policy is trained via behavior cloning.
- **Observations:** O_IR typically consists of images from a stationary camera overlooking the workspace and robot proprioception.
- **Context:** The context *c* provides information about the task goal, often as task IDs or natural language instructions (represented as CLIP embeddings in some experiments).

**Phase 2: Training the Information-Seeking (IS) Agent and Encoder**
- **Objective:** Train the IS policy (π_IS_θ) to take actions that reveal the context, and an observation encoder (E_ϕ) to accurately map IS observations into context for the IR policy.
- **Challenges:** Maximizing the original fCMDP task reward for the IS agent directly is difficult due to its sparsity.
- **Intrinsic Reward Mechanism:** DISaM addresses sparsity by leveraging the already trained IR policy. The IS agent's objective is to seek and encode context that minimizes the difference between:
 1. Actions taken by π_IR conditioned on the *IS-provided context* (a_IR ~ π_IR(o_IR, c_IS)).
 2. Actions taken by π_IR conditioned on the *ground truth context* (a_GT ~ π_IR(o_IR, c_GT)).
 - The loss L (e.g., cross-entropy or negative log-likelihood) between these two action distributions defines an **intrinsic reward** for the IS policy: R_IS = α - β ⋅ L(π_IR(o_IR, c_IS), π_IR(o_IR, c_GT)). This denser reward signal facilitates learning for the IS agent via reinforcement learning.
- **Iterative Optimization:** E_ϕ and π_IS_θ are optimized iteratively.
 - **Encoder Training:** E_ϕ is trained via supervised learning using (o_IS, c_GT) pairs collected during rollouts, minimizing an encoder loss (e.g., cross-entropy for one-hot contexts, negative log-likelihood for language embeddings modeled by Mixture Density Networks).
 - **IS Policy Training:** π_IS_θ is trained using reinforcement learning (PPO) with the intrinsic reward R_IS.

#### 3.3. Deciding Between IS and IR Actions at Test Time

During deployment, DISaM dynamically switches between IS and IR policies based on the IR agent's uncertainty regarding its next action.
- **Uncertainty Measure:** An ensemble of trained encoders E_ϕ(o_IS_t) is used to sample *n* contexts ({c_i_t}_(i=1)^n). These contexts are fed to the IR policy to generate *n* action distributions ({π_IR(o, c_i_IS)}_(i=1)^n). The average KL-divergence between pairs of these action distributions quantifies the IR agent's uncertainty.
- **Switching Mechanism:**
 - If uncertainty is **above a threshold δ**, DISaM activates the IS policy to seek more information.
 - If uncertainty is **below δ**, DISaM assumes sufficient context and executes the IR policy's action.
The threshold δ is adapted to the action space but is found to be robust (e.g., δ = 0.5 for discrete skills, δ = 1e5 for continuous visuomotor actions).

#### 3.4. Real-World Training Leveraging Synced Simulation (Mixed Sim-Real)

For real-world tasks, a mixed sim-real setup is used to reduce training complexity:
- A simulated version of the real-world task is created.
- IR policies are trained using data from both environments.
- For IS policy training, the IS policy and encoder operate in the real world to generate real-world context vectors. However, the *simulator* interprets these context vectors with the IR policy to provide rewards back to the real IS policy. This avoids rolling out the real IR policy repeatedly, minimizing resets and human supervision.
- To facilitate context updates in real-world environments (e.g., changing time or person identity), visual cues are displayed on screens, allowing programmatic alteration.
- In real-world tasks, both IS and IR agents may share the same physical camera (e.g., a robot's head camera). A resting head position for the IR agent is maintained, and the system resets to it when switching from IS to IR control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted on five tasks: three in simulation (Cooking, Walls, Assembly) and two in the real world with a Tiago robot (Button, Teatime). These tasks required diverse information-gathering strategies, including active camera control, navigation, and interactive manipulation.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness in Learning to Seek, Infer, and Exploit Contextual Information

- **Superior Performance:** DISaM consistently outperformed all baselines across all five tasks in terms of overall success rate (Figure 5). In multi-stage, longer-horizon tasks like Cooking, Walls, and Teatime, where information must be gathered at multiple points, DISaM achieved high success rates while baselines struggled.
- **Qualitative Observations:**
 - **Full RL** often used the IS policy infrequently, leading to inadequate context recovery and suboptimal performance, suggesting difficulty in learning long-horizon exploration with sparse rewards.
 - **Random Cam** showed some reasonable performance but struggled with large action spaces and required more time to obtain relevant observations, highlighting the benefit of a learned IS policy.
 - **Sampled Context** performed poorly, indicating that simply guessing the context is insufficient.
- **Stage-wise Success:** An analysis of stage-wise success rates (Appendix A.5, Figure 6) further supported DISaM's effectiveness. While some baselines achieved limited success in early or simpler stages, DISaM maintained robust performance across all stages, demonstrating its ability to dynamically switch between information-seeking and manipulation as required by the task progression.

#### 4.2. Importance of the Intrinsic Reward Mechanism

- **DISaM (task reward)**, an ablation that trained the IS agent using hand-designed stage rewards instead of the proposed intrinsic reward, showed significantly lower success rates compared to the full DISaM.
- This finding indicates that directly optimizing from even carefully designed stage-wise sparse rewards remains challenging for the IS agent. The intrinsic reward, derived from the discrepancy between IR actions conditioned on predicted vs. ground-truth context, provided a denser and more effective learning signal, substantially boosting DISaM's performance.

#### 4.3. Handling Different Representations of Context (Language as Context)

- **DISaM (language)**, a variant that used CLIP embeddings of natural language sentences as context vectors instead of one-hot encodings, was evaluated.
- In the Cooking task, a relatively small performance drop was observed, suggesting that DISaM can effectively learn to map observations to continuous language embeddings.
- In the more challenging Walls task, which required camera movement to see around obstacles, a modest but more pronounced drop in performance was noted.
- Despite these challenges, DISaM (language) still outperformed all other baselines. This demonstrates the framework's potential to integrate more complex, semantic context representations like natural language.

#### 4.4. Failure Modes and Robustness

- **Failure Modes (Appendix A.9):** The primary failure modes observed were the IS policy failing to observe the correct visual cue (especially in tasks with long action sequences like Walls), and IR policy failures (particularly in real-world settings due to limited training data). Encoder failures were less common due to lower data requirements.
- **Robustness to Noise (Appendix A.10):** In simulation experiments with varying levels of Gaussian noise added to observations, DISaM demonstrated robustness at low noise levels. However, as sensor noise increased, failures to correctly encode images into context became predominant, leading to reduced performance. This suggests that robust perception under noisy conditions remains a critical area for improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents DISaM, a novel framework that contributes to the field of robotic learning by enabling agents to *learn* to seek and utilize contextual information for complex manipulation tasks. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Addressing Partial Observability in Robotics:** The work formalizes a class of problems as factorized Contextual Markov Decision Processes (fCMDPs), which better represent real-world scenarios where critical task information is not always readily available and must be actively discovered. This provides a structured approach to a common challenge in embodied AI.
- **Enabling Autonomous Information Gathering:** By decoupling information-seeking from manipulation and allowing the robot to learn what information to gather, DISaM moves beyond approaches that require human pre-specification of context. This capability is fundamental for robots operating in dynamic, unstructured, and novel environments where explicit instruction for every piece of information is impractical.
- **Efficient Learning in Long-Horizon Tasks:** The dual-policy factorization and the use of an intrinsic reward signal derived from the IR policy's action discrepancy significantly mitigate the challenges of sparse rewards and long horizons typically faced by end-to-end reinforcement learning for POMDPs. This makes training more efficient and tractable for complex tasks.
- **Dynamic Exploration-Exploitation Balancing:** The uncertainty-based switching mechanism allows the robot to autonomously decide when to explore for information and when to exploit known information for manipulation. This adaptive behavior enhances the robot's reactive capabilities in dynamic environments, enabling it to collect information only when necessary.
- **Generalizability of Context Representation:** The demonstration of using both one-hot encodings and continuous language embeddings for context suggests the framework's adaptability to various forms of task specification, including high-level semantic instructions. This can facilitate more intuitive human-robot interaction.
- **Applicability to Single and Multi-Robot Systems:** The fCMDP formalism and DISaM solution are applicable to both single-robot systems (where a robot uses its own sensors/effectors to seek information and manipulate) and multi-robot systems (e.g., a scout robot informing an operator robot). This broadens its potential application scope.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations (e.g., lack of observation history for IS agent, potential for action space overlap between policies, long training times for some tasks, robustness to noise) provide clear avenues for future work, encouraging further advancements in active perception and manipulation.

Overall, DISaM represents a step towards more autonomous and adaptive robots that can proactively engage with their environment to gather the knowledge required for decision-making, rather than relying on fully observable states or predefined information.
