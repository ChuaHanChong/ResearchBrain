# Learning Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation Skills via Privileged Action

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Edinburgh developed a framework that uses "privileged actions" within a curriculum learning strategy to enable robots to acquire long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation skills. This approach allows robots to autonomously learn complex behaviors like pushing-to-grasp and pivot grasping from ungraspable poses, demonstrating improved performance over baselines and successful transfer to real-world robots without extensive reward engineering or human demonstrations.

## Problem

- Training robots for long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation tasks from scratch using reinforcement learning is challenging due to compounded exploration difficulties and sparse rewards.
- Existing methods often rely on manual stitching of policies, labor-intensive human demonstrations, complex reward engineering, or curriculum learning that still requires significant human guidance.
- Non-prehensile manipulation tasks are particularly difficult to learn due to the need for precise contact and interaction dynamics without explicit grasping.

## Method

- A three-stage ==curriculum learning== framework was developed, leveraging "**privileged actions**" in simulation to simplify learning and enhance exploration.
- Stage 1 relaxes collision constraints between the robot and table, gradually reintroducing them via a ==virtual table== as the policy improves.
- Stage 2 introduces a ==virtual force== directly applied to the object when the end-effector is near, which is progressively attenuated to encourage physical interaction, followed by Stage 3 which trains in a normal, physically realistic setting using a parsimonious reward.

## Results

- The Franka robot successfully learned complex push-to-grasp and pivot-grasp behaviors from ungraspable poses, achieving significantly higher rewards (e.g., **(2.34 ± 0.13) 5 10^4** for push-and-grasp) compared to vanilla PPO (**65 1 10**).
- The framework generalized to dexterous manipulation with a Kuka robot and AllegroHand, successfully grasping and reorienting challenging YCB objects (scissors, stapler, wrench), outperforming DexPBT and SAPG.
- Learned policies robustly transferred to the real world, with both push-and-grasp and pivot grasp executed successfully on a physical Franka robot, confirmed by ablation studies that validated the importance of each curriculum stage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - "Privileged actions" (e.g., relaxed collision constraints, virtual forces) in simulation can effectively expand the accessible state-action space, accelerating exploration and preventing policies from getting stuck in local optima.
> - A structured curriculum that gradually phases out these privileged actions allows for efficient learning of complex, multi-stage manipulation skills without requiring dense, task-specific rewards or human demonstrations.
> - Policies learned with this approach exhibit emergent non-prehensile behaviors, adapting to various object poses and environmental constraints to achieve grasping, such as pushing objects to an edge or performing pivot grasps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Training robots to perform long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation tasks from scratch using reinforcement learning (RL) presents a substantial challenge within the field of robotics. Existing research in RL has demonstrated success in various locomotion and manipulation tasks, but often focuses on optimizing short-horizon behaviors. The difficulty in scaling RL to longer time horizons stems primarily from compounded exploration challenges in high-dimensional state spaces and the prevalence of sparse rewards, which can lead the learning process to become trapped in local optima.

Previous approaches to address these challenges include:
- **Stitching multiple policies:** This involves combining several short-horizon policies to achieve a longer task. However, this method is often considered undesirable as it requires manual crafting and combination of primitive behaviors, limiting the robot's autonomous discovery of optimal solutions.
- **Reference trajectories or demonstrations:** Human demonstrations or pre-generated trajectories can provide a warm start, reducing the need for extensive exploration. While effective for specific scenarios, collecting demonstrations for diverse environments is labor-intensive and impractical at scale. Furthermore, human biases or limitations in teleoperation devices can introduce suboptimal behaviors when translated to robots.
- **Curriculum learning:** This strategy guides the learning process by gradually increasing task complexity, starting from simpler tasks. While widely used, many existing curriculum-based methods still rely on task-specific reward engineering or human demonstrations. For instance, some works combine demonstrations with auto-curricula for dexterous manipulation, or use complex reward functions with relaxed collision constraints.
- **Privileged information:** Simulations offer access to information (e.g., precise object states, internal forces) that is unavailable in the real world. Leveraging such "privileged information" has been shown to improve sample efficiency and policy performance by simplifying the state-action mapping. However, traditional privileged information does not inherently enable the discovery of novel or complex behaviors.
- **Non-prehensile manipulation specifics:** Tasks involving non-prehensile manipulation (e.g., pushing to reorient an object) are particularly challenging due to the need for precise contact and interaction dynamics without explicit grasping. Prior methods often require pre-generated grasp poses, complex and finely tuned reward functions, or decomposition into pre- and post-contact policies.

This work addresses the limitations of these existing methods by proposing a novel framework that focuses on enhancing exploration through "privileged actions"—actions that are feasible only in simulation but simplify the learning problem. By combining these privileged actions with a structured curriculum learning approach, the authors aim to enable efficient acquisition of long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation skills from scratch, without relying on extensive reward engineering, reference trajectories, or pre-defined task primitives. The approach specifically targets scenarios where policies need to adapt to environmental changes and autonomously discover complex behaviors.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to efficiently learn long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation skills from scratch using reinforcement learning, without requiring extensive reward engineering, human demonstrations, or predefined task primitives.

The specific motivations and objectives that underpin this work are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Ineffective Exploration:** A core challenge in learning long-horizon, contact-rich tasks is the ineffective exploration of high-dimensional state-action spaces, particularly when rewards are sparse. The authors aim to overcome this by introducing mechanisms that simplify the learning environment and expand the accessible state-action space, thereby preventing the policy from getting trapped in local optima.
- **Minimizing Reward Engineering:** Many complex manipulation tasks necessitate detailed and task-specific reward functions, which require significant human effort to design and fine-tune. Such finely tuned rewards also limit the generalizability of learned policies across diverse environmental settings. This work is motivated to reduce or eliminate the reliance on complex reward shaping, opting instead for a parsimonious, general reward structure typically used for basic grasping and lifting.
- **Eliminating Reliance on Reference Trajectories:** Collecting human demonstrations or reference trajectories is often labor-intensive and becomes impractical when robots need to operate in varied environments where optimal behavior changes significantly. The research aims to develop a framework where robots can autonomously discover optimal behaviors, thereby removing the need for such pre-provided guidance.
- **Leveraging Simulation for Enhanced Exploration:** Simulation environments offer opportunities beyond what is feasible in the real world. The authors are motivated to capitalize on these unique simulation capabilities by introducing the novel concept of "privileged actions." These actions, which are physically infeasible in reality (e.g., relaxed collision constraints, virtual forces directly applied to objects), are hypothesized to significantly enhance policy exploration by simplifying interactions and opening up paths to high-reward regions that would otherwise be inaccessible or difficult to discover.
- **Facilitating Complex Multi-Stage Skill Acquisition:** The research specifically targets tasks that naturally combine distinct non-prehensile manipulation (e.g., pushing, pivoting) with grasping to lift objects from initially non-graspable poses. The objective is to enable the policy to autonomously learn these multi-stage behaviors, adapting to various object poses and environmental constraints.
- **Ensuring Robustness and Generalizability:** The authors aim for the learned policies to be robust to environmental changes and to converge to diverse, physically acceptable behaviors across various environments, even without task-specific reward modifications. Furthermore, a key motivation is to demonstrate that these skills acquired in simulation are transferable and exhibit robust performance in real-world scenarios.
- **Outperforming State-of-the-Art Methods:** By addressing the fundamental challenges of exploration and reward sparsity with a novel approach, the researchers aim to develop a method that outperforms existing state-of-the-art techniques in terms of convergence speed, success rate, and the complexity of skills learned, especially in scenarios where other methods fail.

In summary, the motivation is to advance robot learning for complex, long-horizon tasks by intelligently using simulation-exclusive "privileges" in a structured curriculum to overcome the inherent difficulties of reinforcement learning in contact-rich and sparse-reward environments, leading to more autonomous and generalizable robot behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is a structured framework that employs privileged actions in conjunction with curriculum learning to enable a policy to acquire long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation skills. The approach does not rely on extensive reward shaping or human priors such as reference trajectories. The framework progresses through three distinct stages: constraint relaxation, virtual forces, and the normal (realistic) setting, as guided by a curriculum strategy.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The work frames the problem within a model-free reinforcement learning setting. The robot manipulates objects on a tabletop, with the state `x` comprising the positions and velocities of the robot and object (`q_R,t, q_O,t, ˙q_R,t, ˙q_O,t`). The policy `π_θ` predicts actions `u` based on the current observation. The objective is to maximize the discounted return `J(θ)` over an episode. A key challenge highlighted is that direct forces on the object via the policy are typically nullified (represented by zeros in the control influence matrix `g(x_t)`), meaning object state changes can only occur through physical contact, which complicates learning non-prehensile manipulation from sparse rewards.

**B. Privileged Actions and Curriculum Stages**

The framework leverages two types of privileged actions, introduced gradually through a three-stage curriculum (Algorithm 1):

1. **Stage 1: Constraint Relaxation with Collision Management**
 - **Purpose:** To simplify the initial learning process for grasping objects from ungraspable poses, especially when the table surface acts as an impediment.
 - **Mechanism:** The collision constraints between the robot and the table are relaxed. This is achieved by increasing the distance `Δ_R` at which contact between the robot and table is triggered (i.e., allowing the robot to "penetrate" the table surface up to `Δ_R` without incurring full collision forces).
 - **Mitigation of Undesirable Behaviors:** To prevent the robot from learning physically unrealistic behaviors (e.g., lifting objects using the arm through the table), a "virtual table" is introduced. This virtual table enforces collision constraints and is initially set significantly below the actual table surface (`∆_R = 0.3m`). As the policy achieves a predefined success rate (e.g., >70%), the height of this virtual table is progressively increased (`∆_R` is decreased by `∆_i = 0.1`) until it aligns with the actual table surface, gradually reintroducing physical constraints.

2. **Stage 2: Virtual Force**
 - **Purpose:** To promote interaction between the robot's end-effector and the object, particularly for non-prehensile manipulation tasks where establishing contact and maintaining it for effective manipulation is challenging under sparse rewards.
 - **Mechanism:** The policy is designed to predict an additional virtual force `u_O,t` that can be applied directly to the object. This virtual force is gated by a matrix `B(x_t)`. `B(x_t)` is an identity matrix (allowing the force to act) only if the robot's end-effector is sufficiently close to the object in both position (`q_O,t − q_EE,t < δ_p · α`) and velocity (`˙q_O,t − ˙q_EE,t < δ_v · α`). Otherwise, `B(x_t)` is a zero matrix, and the object is not directly actuated by this virtual force. Initially, `δ_p = 10`, `δ_v = 5`, and `α = 0.85`, meaning there are minimal restrictions, and the virtual force can strongly influence the object.
 - **Curriculum:** As the policy achieves a success rate threshold (>70%), the thresholds `δ_p` and `δ_v` are progressively reduced by multiplying `α` with a decay factor (`α_d = 0.9`) until a minimum value (`α_min = 0.06`) is reached. This process encourages the robot's physical movements and actual contact forces to gradually replace the reliance on the virtual force.

3. **Stage 3: Normal Setting (No Privileged Actions)**
 - **Purpose:** Once the policy has adapted to the constraints of the virtual table and has learned to interact with the object without significant reliance on virtual forces, it transitions to the normal, physically realistic training environment.
 - **Mechanism:** In this final stage, `∆_R` is zero, and `α` has reached its minimum, effectively disabling the privileged actions. The policy is further trained under standard physical constraints, ensuring the learned behaviors are physically valid and robust.

**C. Reward Setting**
A deliberate choice was made to use a parsimonious and general reward function, specifically the default grasping and lifting reward provided by IsaacGym, without any additional task-specific modifications. This contrasts with prior work that often employed dense, finely-tuned rewards. The reward function (`r_total`) includes:
- `r_f`: Distance reward between the robot end-effector and the object.
- `r_l`: Lifting reward.
- `r_k`: Distance reward between the object and the goal.
- `r_p`: Penalty term for jerk motion (sudden changes in movement).
- `r_b`: Bonus reward for successfully reaching the goal.
Notably, no explicit rewards are provided to guide non-prehensile manipulation behavior, making the task challenging and relying heavily on the exploration benefits of privileged actions.

**D. Sim-to-Real Transfer**
To ensure the learned policies are robust and transferable to the real world, domain randomization techniques are applied during simulation training. This includes randomizing object pose and shape, and adding noise to observations. For real-world deployment, the robot's movement trajectories learned in simulation are distilled and replicated in the physical environment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across various manipulation tasks, demonstrating the efficacy, generality, and transferability of the proposed framework.

**A. Grasp and Lift Object from Non-Graspable Pose (Franka Robot)**

- **Complex Long-Horizon Behavior:** The Franka robot successfully learned complex long-horizon behaviors. When the object was in an initially ungraspable flat pose (due to gripper width limitations), the policy autonomously discovered how to push the object to the table edge, then grasp it from the side, and finally lift it. The policy also exhibited adaptive behavior, reorienting the object first if its initial pose was not suitable for a direct push-and-grasp action.
- **Adaptation to Constrained Environments (Pivot Grasp):** In a more constrained environment featuring small walls blocking direct pushing, the policy adapted to a novel strategy. It learned to leverage the robot's own base as support to perform a "pivot grasp." This complex, emergent behavior was achieved without any human guidance in the observation or reward function, contrasting with traditional methods that would require specific reward engineering for such a skill.
- **Comparison with Baseline:** The proposed method significantly outperformed vanilla Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). While PPO's end-effector often remained at the center of the object, failing to lift it (a local optimum of minimizing end-effector-object distance), the proposed method converged to successful, complete task solutions. Quantitative reward comparisons (Table II) showed rewards in the order of `(2.34 ± 0.13) × 10^4` for push-and-grasp and `(2.17 ± 0.29) × 10^4` for pivot grasp, significantly higher than PPO's `65 ± 10` and `52 ± 17` respectively.

**B. Dexterous Manipulation of Challenging YCB Objects (Kuka Robot Arm with AllegroHand)**

- **Generalization to Dexterous Hands and Complex Objects:** The framework demonstrated its generalization capability by enabling a Kuka robot arm with an AllegroHand to grasp and reorient challenging YCB objects (scissors, stapler, wrench) from a tabletop. These objects were specifically chosen because previous state-of-the-art methods struggled to grasp them successfully.
- **Emergent Non-Prehensile Skills:** For objects like scissors, which are thin and difficult to grasp directly, the robot learned to maneuver them to the edge of the table before grasping, showcasing the emergence of non-prehensile skills without explicit reward.
- **Comparison with State-of-the-Art (DexPBT, SAPG):** The proposed method consistently achieved higher rewards and better convergence across all three challenging YCB objects compared to DexPBT and SAPG (Figure 5). DexPBT, while effective for randomly sized cubes, converged to a low reward value for the stapler and failed entirely on the scissors and wrench. SAPG also struggled to achieve high rewards for these objects. The results indicate the proposed framework's ability to handle the extensive long-horizon exploration needed for stable grasping, lifting, and accurate reorientation even for challenging geometries.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer**

- **Robustness and Physical Reliability:** Real-world experiments validated that the policies learned in simulation were robust and physically reliable, not exploiting simulation-specific shortcuts.
- **Successful Real-World Execution:** Both the push-and-grasp (adapting to various object poses, including reorientation) and the pivot grasp (using the robot base as support in a constrained environment) behaviors were successfully transferred and executed on a real Franka robot. This confirms the practical applicability of the learned skills.

**D. Ablation Study**

- **Importance of Stage 2 (Virtual Force):** Removing Stage 2 (virtual force) from the curriculum led to the policy failing to converge on the challenging scissors task. This indicates that the virtual force is crucial for providing sufficient initial exploration and guidance for object-robot interactions, enabling the policy to find a feasible solution for grasping under realistic collision constraints.
- **Importance of Stage 1 (Constraint Relaxation):** Training without Stage 1 (constraint relaxation) allowed the policy to eventually converge to a successful grasping behavior, but it required significantly more training time. This suggests that while not strictly necessary for eventual success with virtual forces, constraint relaxation substantially aids in the early discovery of stable grasp strategies and facilitates long-horizon planning by simplifying the environment.
- **Overall Contribution of the Curriculum:** The ablation study demonstrated that each stage of the three-stage curriculum (constraint relaxation, virtual force, normal setting) plays a distinct and important role in the efficient and effective learning of long-horizon manipulation skills, contributing to rapid convergence and preventing the policy from getting trapped in local optima.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Learning Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation Skills via Privileged Action" presents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly for complex manipulation tasks. Its potential impact spans several areas:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy in Complex Tasks:** The framework enables robots to autonomously learn long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation skills, including non-prehensile actions (e.g., pushing, pivoting) combined with grasping, without extensive human intervention. This moves towards greater robot autonomy in unstructured and diverse environments.
- **Reduced Human Engineering Effort:** By demonstrating success with a parsimonious, general reward function and eliminating the need for reference trajectories or complex reward engineering, the research substantially reduces the human effort typically required to develop robot skills for new tasks or environments. This has implications for faster deployment and broader applicability of robotic systems.
- **Novelty of Privileged Actions for Exploration:** The introduction of "privileged actions"—actions feasible only in simulation (like relaxed collisions and virtual forces)—as a means to enhance exploration is a novel concept. This approach effectively expands the feasible state-action space during initial learning, preventing local optima and accelerating the discovery of complex behaviors.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency and Convergence:** The structured curriculum combined with privileged actions leads to more efficient policy exploration and faster convergence to robust solutions, as evidenced by the superior performance compared to state-of-the-art methods on challenging tasks.
- **Generality and Adaptability:** The framework demonstrates generality across different robotic platforms (Franka, Kuka with AllegroHand) and various objects, as well as adaptability to environmental changes (e.g., constrained environments requiring pivot grasps). This suggests a path towards more versatile and robust robot policies.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful transfer of complex learned behaviors from simulation to the real world, supported by domain randomization, validates the practical utility of the approach and strengthens the case for sim-to-real learning in robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The concept of privileged actions and their structured integration into a curriculum provides a foundation for future research in robot learning. It opens avenues for exploring how simulation-specific capabilities can be optimally leveraged to overcome real-world learning challenges beyond tabletop manipulation. A noted limitation, the lack of automatic optimization for privileged actions, also points towards a key direction for future work to further generalize the framework.

In conclusion, this research offers a methodical approach to tackling a long-standing challenge in robot manipulation, providing both a theoretical contribution through the concept of privileged actions and practical demonstrations of robust, adaptable, and autonomously learned skills in both simulated and real-world settings.
