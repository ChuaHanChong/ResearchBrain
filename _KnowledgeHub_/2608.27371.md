# Embodied Scene Rearrangement Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> ESRP is a new embodied task where an agent rearranges room furniture in 3D scenes to match a top-down target layout image, using only egocentric RGB and a grasp indicator. It ships ESRP-Bench on OmniGibson with 5,495 scene pairs, 8,213 movable objects, three metrics, and four baselines spanning planning, VLM, imitation, and reinforcement learning. The best baseline reaches only 30.20% success, dropping to 0% on hard scenes.

## Problem

- Tabletop rearrangement work repositions small objects in confined workspaces, ignoring room-scale challenges like long-horizon navigation and spatial reasoning in cluttered environments.
- Scene-level rearrangement research typically abstracts rooms to a 2D plane with full global state access, discarding vertical geometry and partial-observability constraints of real robots.
- Standard task-and-motion planning formulations do not handle the interdependent long-horizon action sequences and mutual occlusions that furniture-scale rearrangement in 3D creates.

## Method

- **ESRP-Bench** builds 5,495 3D-FRONT scenes in OmniGibson with ==backward-constructed initial layouts== (agent-embodied perturbation of the goal), yielding guaranteed-feasible pairs plus reverse expert trajectories for imitation learning.
- Three multi-level metrics: ==Success Rate==, ==Object Success Rate==, and ==Remaining Distance Ratio==, scored against a 0.3 horizontal bounding-box IoU threshold, stratified into easy, medium, and hard difficulty by object count.
- Four baselines: **ESRP-BC** diffusion policy with denoising plus cross-entropy loss, **ESRP-PPO** with dual visual encoders and LSTM memory, **ESRP-VLM** as a ==ReAct== prompting agent, and **ESRP-PLAN**, a privileged-state hierarchical ==TAMP== using BFS and A*.

## Results

- Privileged-perception ESRP-PLAN leads at **30.20%** SR, **36.59%** OSR, **0.6916** RDR; learning baselines cluster near **20%** SR, and ESRP-PPO edges ESRP-BC on RDR, **0.9845** versus **1.0170**.
- Performance collapses with object count: ESRP-PPO falls from **33.33%** SR on easy to **0.54%** on medium and **0%** on hard, while even ESRP-PLAN scores **0%** SR on hard scenes.
- Failure decomposition attributes **60.85%** to **66.55%** of rollouts to placement failure rather than navigation (**11-13%**) or grasping (**4-6%**); swapping in a stronger VLM lifts SR from **17.64%** to **23.01%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Removing global state access, not manipulation difficulty, is what breaks rearrangement agents: precise goal-aligned placement from egocentric views is the dominant bottleneck across every paradigm tested.
> - Long-horizon combinatorial ordering is a separate failure axis from perception, since even a perfect-perception planner collapses once four or more objects must be moved.
> - Stronger general-purpose vision-language priors help only marginally; in-context prompting alone cannot substitute for grounded spatial memory in non-stationary, self-modified environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper addresses a recognized gap within the field of robotic rearrangement planning, specifically concerning the challenges of deploying embodied agents in realistic 3D environments. Existing research in rearrangement planning typically falls into two main categories:

- **Tabletop Rearrangement:** This category focuses on using robotic manipulators to reposition small objects within constrained workspaces. While methods in this area, such as those by King et al. (2016), Labbé et al. (2020), Goyal et al. (2022), and Ren and Qureshi (2024), have advanced object manipulation, they are limited to small-scale objects and confined environments, failing to address the complexities of larger, room-level tasks that involve extensive navigation and spatial reasoning.

- **Scene-Level Object Rearrangement (Simplified):** This research stream, including works by Xiong et al. (2020), Wang et al. (2024), Batra et al. (2020), and Wang et al. (2020), deals with larger items like furniture but often relies on simplified 2D planar abstractions. These simplifications overlook critical aspects of real-world 3D environments, such as intricate spatial relationships, vertical geometric constraints, and the impact of partial observations on rearrangement feasibility. Consequently, their applicability to embodied agents operating in realistic 3D settings is limited.

The authors identify a substantial disparity between current research capabilities and the practical requirements for furniture rearrangement in real-world 3D scenes, such as those found in domestic or hospitality settings. The proposed Embodied Scene Rearrangement Planning (ESRP) task directly seeks to bridge these gaps by introducing realistic constraints that are often overlooked.

Furthermore, the paper situates ESRP within the context of Task and Motion Planning (TAMP), which integrates high-level task planning with low-level motion planning. While TAMP approaches (e.g., optimization-based, sampling-based, learning-based) enable robots to generate executable actions for complex tasks, ESRP introduces unique difficulties that challenge standard TAMP formulations. These include the long-horizon task complexity arising from sequences of interdependent actions and the demand for intricate 3D spatial understanding for precise object positioning and collision avoidance within the planning process. By confronting these challenges, ESRP pushes the boundaries of current TAMP and embodied AI capabilities, moving towards more practical and deployable intelligent agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce Embodied Scene Rearrangement Planning (ESRP), a novel task that requires an embodied agent to rearrange furniture in a 3D scene from an initial configuration to a specified target layout. This task is distinguished by its emphasis on realistic constraints pertinent to real-world robotic deployment.

The core motivation behind ESRP stems from the limitations observed in prior rearrangement research:
- **Lack of Realistic Environment Representation:** Previous work either confined rearrangement to tabletops or simplified 3D environments into 2D abstractions. These simplifications did not account for the complexities of navigating and manipulating large furniture items within realistic 3D indoor scenes.
- **Absence of Egocentric Observation Constraint:** Many existing scene-level rearrangement tasks assume access to a global state or top-down view of the entire scene. However, real-world embodied agents operate primarily through egocentric (first-person) observations, which introduce significant challenges in perceiving, localizing, and planning within dynamic environments.
- **Neglect of Inter-Object Dependencies and Occlusions:** Rearranging large furniture items involves long-horizon planning, where actions on one object can affect others, leading to physical deadlocks or mutual occlusions. Prior simplified tasks often did not fully account for these complex dependencies.

ESRP is specifically designed to incorporate three distinct challenges that reflect these real-world constraints:

- **Partial Observability:** The agent must rely solely on egocentric RGB images to understand its surroundings. This necessitates aligning partial, first-person views with a global top-down target layout and inferring comprehensive spatial information from limited local perception, without access to global state information or ground-truth localization.
- **Complex Inter-Object Dependencies:** The task involves manipulating multiple large furniture items. This transforms rearrangement into a long-horizon planning problem, requiring the agent to generate plausible action sequences that consider inter-object relationships, avoiding collisions, deadlocks, and dynamic occlusions caused by its own actions or the movement of other objects.
- **Dynamic Scene Evolution:** As the agent moves objects, the 3D scene itself transforms. This dynamic environment places high demands on the agent's ability to maintain accurate perception, localize itself relative to the changing scene, and navigate effectively within a non-stationary setting.

To facilitate research in this new domain, the authors establish several secondary objectives:
- **Develop a Comprehensive Benchmark:** Introduce ESRP-Bench, a large-scale dataset and simulation environment built on OmniGibson, to systematically evaluate agent performance.
- **Define Multi-Level Metrics:** Establish a suite of rigorous evaluation metrics to assess rearrangement quality across different aspects and difficulty levels.
- **Provide Baselines:** Implement four diverse baseline policies (Imitation Learning, Reinforcement Learning, Vision-Language Model-based, and Planning-based) to offer initial attempts at solving ESRP and highlight current method limitations.
- **Catalyze Future Research:** Position ESRP and ESRP-Bench as a foundation for future work, serving as a stepping stone towards deploying intelligent agents in practical, real-world rearrangement scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves defining the ESRP task, creating a comprehensive benchmark (ESRP-Bench), establishing evaluation metrics, setting up the simulation environment, and implementing several baseline models.

#### 3.1. Task Definition

ESRP requires an embodied agent to reconfigure an unseen 3D indoor scene from an initial layout to a target layout, using only egocentric observations and a top-down image of the target. The agent must comprehend the current scene, formulate a strategy, and execute actions to reposition furniture. Success is achieved when all rearrangeable objects are correctly placed.

- **Observations:** At each timestep, the agent receives:
 - An egocentric RGB image (128 × 128).
 - A top-down RGB image (128 × 128) of the target layout.
 - A binary proprioceptive indicator (grasping state).
- **Actions:** A discrete action space including {move\_forward, move\_backward, turn\_left, turn\_right, fetch, release}. Translations are 0.25m steps, and rotations are 22.5 degrees. Grasping is abstracted to instantaneous attachment of an object within a 1.0m radius, with continuous collision checking enforced.
- **Success Conditions:** An episode is successful if all rearrangeable objects are correctly positioned. An object is considered correctly placed if the 2D Intersection over Union (IoU) between its current and target bounding box on the horizontal plane exceeds 0.3.
- **Termination Conditions:** An episode ends if the maximum timestep horizon is reached (timeout failure) or if all objects are successfully rearranged (success).

#### 3.2. ESRP-Bench Dataset

ESRP-Bench is built upon OmniGibson and uses scenes from the 3D-FRONT dataset. It comprises 5,495 distinct indoor scenes, spanning 24 room types, with 1 to 6 rearrangeable furniture items per scene.

- **Difficulty Levels:** Scenes are categorized into Easy (1 object), Medium (2-3 objects), and Hard (4-6 objects) based on the number of rearrangeable objects.
- **Scene and Object Curation:** Filtering criteria were applied to ensure task feasibility: rooms with sufficient navigation space, furniture dimensions allowing effective agent manipulation (slightly smaller than the agent), and a maximum of 6 rearrangeable objects per scene.
- **Target Layout Acquisition:** Professionally designed layouts from the 3D-FRONT dataset are used as target configurations. 128 × 128 top-down orthographic RGB images are rendered to represent these target layouts.
- **Initial Layout Generation:** Initial layouts are generated by perturbing the target layouts through a rule-based simulation. A robot iteratively moves each rearrangeable object randomly until it reaches a non-overlapping, collision-free position. This backward construction aims to ensure the existence of a feasible solution.
- **Expert Trajectories:** The reverse trajectories generated during the initial layout process serve as expert demonstrations for imitation learning.
- **Dataset Statistics:** The dataset features diverse room types, with "MasterBedroom" and "LivingDiningRoom" being the most prevalent. A total of 8,213 movable objects are categorized, primarily "Cabinet/Shelf/Desk" and "Chair."

#### 3.3. Metrics

Three multi-level metrics are defined for quantitative assessment:
- **Success Rate (SR):** The proportion of episodes where *all* rearrangeable objects are successfully placed.
- **Object Success Rate (OSR):** The average proportion of *individual objects* correctly placed across all episodes.
- **Remaining Distance Ratio (RDR):** The ratio of the summed Euclidean (L2) distances between all objects and their target positions at the end of an episode, to the summed distances at the start. Lower RDR indicates better performance.

#### 3.4. Simulation Environment

ESRP is implemented within OmniGibson, a physics-based simulation framework utilizing NVIDIA Isaac Sim.
- **Robot Setup:** A Fetch robot embodiment, with its arm removed and a camera mounted overhead, is used.
- **Action Execution:** Physical fidelity is maintained through continuous collision detection against 3D meshes of the robot, furniture, and environment. Low-level grasping is abstracted as instantaneous object attachment to prioritize high-level planning. The agent can fetch objects within a 1.0m radius.

#### 3.5. Baselines

Four baselines representing different paradigms were developed:

- **ESRP-BC (Imitation Learning):** Employs a conditional diffusion policy to predict future action sequences. It is conditioned on egocentric observation history, the goal image, and gripper-state history, processed by ResNet-50 encoders and an MLP. Training involves diffusion denoising and a cross-entropy loss on denoised discrete action logits.
- **ESRP-PPO (Reinforcement Learning):** An RL policy using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). It processes egocentric and goal images with visual encoders, a learnable embedding for gripper state, and a single-layer LSTM for temporal memory, which then maps to actions via an MLP. A dense reward function is used, including success bonuses, arrival/leave signals, a potential term based on object-to-target distance, and a grasping bonus. Collisions result in action rejection by the simulator.
- **ESRP-VLM (Foundation-Model-based):** A multimodal ReAct agent built on the pretrained Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct Vision-Language Model (VLM), also evaluated with Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite. It receives egocentric and goal images, along with textual context encoding gripper state and action history. It generates reasoning traces and discrete actions through in-context prompting, without task-specific fine-tuning.
- **ESRP-PLAN (Planning-based):** This baseline utilizes privileged global information (complete occupancy map, exact object poses, agent pose, target locations) within a hierarchical Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) framework. A task planner determines object rearrangement order (via exhaustive search), and a motion planner executes a four-phase routine (BFS to object, fetch, A* to goal, release). Collision checking uses 2D polygonal obstacle representations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted with the ESRP-Bench and the four baseline models yielded several findings regarding the challenges and current capabilities in embodied scene rearrangement.

#### 4.1. Overall Rearrangement Results

Table I summarizes the overall performance across all three metrics.
- **ESRP-PLAN** demonstrated the highest performance across all metrics (SR: 30.20%, OSR: 36.59%, RDR: 0.6916). This is attributed to its access to privileged global environment state, which enables explicit and precise planning for navigation, object fetching, and collision avoidance.
- **ESRP-PPO** consistently outperformed ESRP-BC in all metrics (SR: 20.18% vs 19.09%; OSR: 20.35% vs 20.31%; RDR: 0.9845 vs 1.0170). While ESRP-BC showed comparable success rates, its higher RDR indicated greater accumulated errors over long horizons, suggesting that reinforcement learning's exploratory nature and direct reward feedback allowed for more stable and precise behavior compared to imitation learning.
- **ESRP-VLM** achieved performance comparable to ESRP-BC but did not surpass ESRP-PPO (SR: 17.64%, OSR: 18.97%, RDR: 0.9830). This suggests that generalist priors from off-the-shelf Vision-Language Models, when used solely via in-context prompting, are not sufficient for the complex long-horizon spatial reasoning required by ESRP.

The overall success rates highlight that even the strongest baseline, ESRP-PLAN, achieved a success rate of only 30.20%, indicating that ESRP remains a challenging task for current methods.

#### 4.2. Performance Across Varying Difficulty Levels

Table II presents the performance of the baselines across Easy, Medium, and Hard difficulty levels.
- A clear trend of performance degradation was observed as the number of rearrangeable objects increased.
- **ESRP-PLAN** experienced a significant drop in SR from 35.73% (Easy) to 22.40% (Medium), and further to 0.00% (Hard). This demonstrates that even with privileged global information, the combinatorial complexity of multi-object rearrangement, particularly with 4-6 objects, poses a substantial challenge for planning-based methods.
- Learning-based approaches (ESRP-PPO, ESRP-BC, ESRP-VLM) exhibited even more pronounced performance drops, frequently achieving success rates near 0% in Medium and Hard scenarios. ESRP-PPO, for instance, dropped from 33.33% SR (Easy) to 0.54% (Medium) and 0.00% (Hard).
- These results underscore the compounded difficulties introduced by long-horizon planning under egocentric partial observability, the increased complexity of object ordering decisions, and the dynamic evolution of the scene with more objects.

#### 4.3. Failure-Mode Decomposition

Table III provides a diagnostic breakdown of failure modes for the learning-based baselines.
- The dominant failure mode for ESRP-PPO, ESRP-BC, and ESRP-VLM was **Stage 3 (placement failure)**, accounting for 60.85%, 62.85%, and 66.55% of all rollouts, respectively. This indicates that agents successfully reach and grasp objects but fail to precisely place them at the target locations.
- Conversely, **Stage 1 (navigation failure)** and **Stage 2 (engagement failure)** were significantly less common, ranging from 11-13% and 4-6%, respectively.
- This decomposition suggests that the primary bottleneck for these methods is not navigation to objects or initial grasping, but rather the precise final placement under egocentric visual constraints, highlighting a specific area for future research focus.

#### 4.4. Effect of a Stronger VLM Backbone

An additional evaluation of ESRP-VLM using Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite as the VLM backbone, instead of Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, showed improved performance across all metrics (SR increased from 17.64% to 23.01%, OSR from 18.97% to 23.75%, and RDR decreased from 0.9830 to 0.9450). This indicates that stronger general-purpose VLM priors can partially mitigate the difficulty of the ESRP task, though still not surpassing the PPO baseline.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Results

Qualitative examples (Figures 6 and 7) illustrated that successful rearrangement occurred in simpler cases, with the agent effectively moving objects to target locations. However, failure cases highlighted that while agents might move objects closer to their goals (lower RDR), they often failed to achieve perfect placement or complete the task in complex scenarios within time limits, emphasizing the ongoing challenges in efficient planning and robust performance from egocentric views.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Embodied Scene Rearrangement Planning (ESRP), a task designed to more accurately reflect the challenges of real-world robotic deployment for furniture rearrangement. By focusing on egocentric observations and 3D scenes without global state access, the work establishes a new and more realistic benchmark for embodied AI.

The primary significance of ESRP lies in its direct relevance to practical applications in domestic and hospitality environments. Unlike prior rearrangement tasks that often rely on simplified 2D environments or global state access, ESRP demands agents to infer global scene states from partial, first-person views and reason about complex inter-object dependencies for long-horizon planning. This design makes the task a more representative testbed for developing agents capable of operating in human-centric spaces.

The introduction of **ESRP-Bench** provides a comprehensive and diverse dataset with over 5,400 scene pairs and 8,200 movable objects, along with multi-level evaluation metrics. This benchmark is a resource for the research community, enabling systematic evaluation and comparison of new methods. By offering expert demonstration trajectories, it also facilitates the development of imitation learning approaches.

The experimental results, derived from four distinct baseline models across different paradigms (Imitation Learning, Reinforcement Learning, Foundation-Model-based, and Planning-based), quantitatively demonstrate the difficulty of the ESRP task. Even the most capable baseline, ESRP-PLAN, which benefits from privileged global information, achieves limited success, particularly in complex scenarios. This highlights that current methods, irrespective of their underlying paradigm, face significant hurdles in 3D scene understanding from partial views and long-horizon task planning with combinatorial object complexities.

The **failure-mode decomposition** is particularly impactful, as it pinpoints precise object placement under egocentric observability as the dominant bottleneck for learning-based agents. This diagnostic insight can guide future research efforts towards targeted improvements in spatial reasoning, fine-grained motor control, and robust localization within dynamically changing environments.

The study also contributes to the understanding of foundation models in embodied AI. While a stronger VLM backbone showed partial improvements, the overall performance of VLM-based baselines suggests that off-the-shelf models, without task-specific fine-tuning or more sophisticated integration, are currently insufficient for the intricate spatial reasoning required by ESRP. This indicates a promising direction for future research in adapting and integrating large language and vision models more effectively for embodied tasks.

In conclusion, this work serves as a foundational step towards enabling intelligent agents to perform complex rearrangement tasks in real-world 3D environments. By defining a challenging task, providing a robust benchmark, and identifying key limitations of current approaches, it lays the groundwork for advancements in embodied AI, specifically in scene understanding and long-horizon planning for mobile manipulation.
