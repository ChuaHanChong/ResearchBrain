# Learning Object Manipulation from Scratch via Contrastive Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> Shen et al. introduce Interaction-Weighted Resampling (IWR) to enhance Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) for robot object manipulation tasks. The method improved average performance by 19.8% across simulation benchmarks and achieved a 140% higher success rate on a real-world robot air hockey task compared to previous CRL approaches, effectively learning robust interaction policies.

## Problem

- Standard Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) struggles with object manipulation due to its piecewise-smooth, mode-conditioned dynamics induced by robot-object interactions, which differ from the smoother dynamics CRL is typically designed for.
- The 'sampling challenge' in CRL means sparse and transient interaction events are underrepresented in training data, leading to a bias towards passive dynamics and inadequate learning of critical mode transitions.
- 'Error propagation' analysis reveals that local estimation errors in interaction-specific dynamics can amplify over subsequent passive steps, resulting in fragile energy estimates and imprecise policy gradients.

## Method

- **Interaction-Weighted Resampling (IWR)** reshapes the ==contrastive training distribution== by assigning higher sampling weights to future goals that are geometrically close to predicted interaction events.
- A ==soft interaction weighting== mechanism uses a ==Gaussian kernel== based on the Euclidean distance between the robot's actuated factor and the target object, ensuring critical interaction-relevant samples are prioritized.
- This focused resampling of positive future goals for the ==InfoNCE objective== helps the ==Contrastive RL (CRL) critic== better capture the dynamic mode changes at interaction boundaries, improving representation learning and policy performance.

## Results

- IWR achieved an average performance gain of **19.8%** across diverse simulation tasks (Box2D, Meta-World, Air Hockey) compared to prior Contrastive Reinforcement Learning methods.
- In a real-world **UR5e** robot air hockey task, IWR-trained policies transferred from simulation achieved a **60%** success rate (**12 out of 20** goals), representing a **140%** improvement over the next best method.
- The method improved both single-goal success rates and the robustness of continuous interaction control in tasks requiring repeated precise interactions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object manipulation dynamics can be conceptualized as a piecewise-smooth Markov process where interactions cause shifts in dynamic modes, leading to piecewise nonlinear reachability structures that challenge standard CRL representations.
> - Standard CRL's implicit linear-Gaussian temporal model for future occupancy struggles to represent these mode-conditioned dynamics, often smoothing over critical interaction boundaries.
> - Local errors in estimating interaction-specific dynamic shifts are formally shown to be amplified through subsequent passive dynamics, severely impacting the accuracy of learned energy functions for goal-reaching.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically addressing the challenge of object manipulation. Existing approaches in this domain typically rely on either expert demonstrations or meticulously engineered reward functions. While these paradigms have contributed to progress in robot manipulation, they often incur high supervision costs, as demonstrations can be expensive and cover a limited range of behaviors, and task-specific rewards require substantial engineering efforts that do not easily scale to more open-ended, multitask scenarios.

These limitations motivate the exploration of unsupervised approaches that can enable robots to acquire reusable manipulation skills and representations directly from interaction, without requiring demonstrations or manually specified rewards. Among such approaches, goal-conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) is a promising direction for developing policies capable of handling the inherent multitask nature of manipulation. Successful GCRL, however, depends on learning robust representations that can capture shared properties across diverse control behaviors.

Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) is identified as a relevant method within GCRL, having demonstrated capabilities in learning complex control from scratch and achieving some success in manipulation tasks. However, the paper identifies a limitation: despite its potential, CRL's performance in interaction-rich manipulation scenarios remains constrained. This work investigates the underlying reasons for CRL's struggles in such domains and proposes a method to address these challenges, thereby advancing the applicability of unsupervised, goal-conditioned learning to complex robot manipulation tasks. The work integrates concepts from CRL, unsupervised goal-conditioned control, and robot manipulation strategies that involve interactions.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate why Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) often underperforms in object manipulation tasks, particularly those involving intricate interactions, and to propose a method to enhance its efficacy in such settings.

The authors identify object-centric interaction as a core property of manipulation that complicates effective policy representation for CRL. They contend that events such as contact or grasping induce distinct changes in the underlying dynamic modes of the system. To formalize this, manipulation dynamics are conceptualized as a piecewise-smooth Markov process. In this framework, interactions cause shifts in the active dynamic mode, leading to piecewise nonlinear reachability structures. These structures are posited to be more challenging for standard CRL energy functions to represent and utilize for planning, compared to the smoother dynamics typically observed in locomotion tasks, which can often be approximated by linear-Gaussian models.

The paper argues that CRL representations, designed for smoother dynamics, may "smooth over critical interaction boundaries" and consequently fail to adequately capture the mode changes that dictate future reachability in manipulation tasks. This structural mismatch can lead to poorly shaped goal-reaching energy landscapes and diminished policy performance. For instance, in locomotion, a single action often consistently affects the same state variable, leading to smooth temporal structures. In contrast, manipulation involves a target factor (e.g., an object) whose dynamics are largely independent of the robot's actions until an interaction event occurs. This induces a "switched affine Gaussian model" where the representation dynamics change based on whether an interaction is active.

Based on this analysis, two specific challenges for CRL in interaction-rich manipulation are highlighted:
1. **Sampling Challenge:** Standard CRL typically samples future goals as discounted horizons from a trajectory. Interaction events, which trigger mode changes, can be sparse and latent. If a sampled future goal occurs before an interaction, the training signal only reflects passive dynamics, offering no direct supervision for interaction-induced shifts. Conversely, if the sampled future is far removed from an interaction, the signal can be attenuated or mixed. This can lead to critical "bridge transitions" (where dynamic modes change) being underrepresented in the training data, biasing the learning towards passive dynamics.
2. **Error Propagation:** Even when an interaction-crossing future is sampled, any local error in estimating the "interaction bridge shift" (the difference between actual interaction update and hypothetical passive update) can propagate through subsequent passive dynamics. This propagation, particularly over multiple post-interaction steps, can amplify initial estimation errors, resulting in fragile energy estimates and imprecise actor gradients in the vicinity of interactions.

The primary motivation is to overcome these identified limitations by designing a mechanism that explicitly encourages CRL's learned representations to better capture the dynamic complexity around interactions, thereby improving sample efficiency and overall policy performance in manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology proposed in this paper, Interaction-Weighted Resampling (IWR), is developed to address the identified challenges of Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) in manipulation tasks.

**Background on Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL):**
The paper first establishes the foundation of CRL within the Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) framework. In GCRL, the objective is to learn a policy π(a | s, g) that can achieve various goals g. CRL frames this problem as learning a representation of state-goal reachability. It achieves this by estimating a goal-reaching energy E*(s, a, g), which quantifies the likelihood of reaching goal g from a state-action pair (s, a). This energy is functionally related to the density ratio between the conditional future occupancy ρπ(g | s, a) (how likely g is reached from (s,a)) and a replay-marginal future distribution ρ̄πB(g).
In practice, CRL parameterizes this energy using an inner product between a state-action encoder ϕ(s, a) and a goal encoder ψ(g): Eϕ,ψ(s, a, g) = ϕ(s, a)ᵀψ(g). The encoders are trained using an InfoNCE-style objective, where for each (s, a) anchor, one "positive" future goal g(+) is sampled from ρπ(· | s, a) and N-1 "negative" goals g are sampled from ρ̄πB(·). The learned energy then implicitly serves as a reward signal for policy optimization.

**Analysis of CRL's Struggle with Interactions:**
The authors provide a theoretical analysis of why CRL struggles with interaction-rich manipulation, building on the Gaussian idealization of temporal contrastive representations.
1. **Linear-Gaussian Temporal Model in CRL:** Prior work suggests that CRL implicitly learns a linear-Gaussian model of discounted future occupancy in representation space. This model, where future representations are generated by powers of a single linear operator (e.g., ψt+n = Aⁿψt), is well-suited for smooth, single-mode dynamics common in locomotion tasks.
2. **Manipulation as Mode-Conditioned Dynamics:** Manipulation tasks are characterized by distinct entities (robot, object) where actions on the robot (actuated factor, ut) only affect the target object (target factor, yt) during specific interaction events. This leads to piecewise dynamics for the target factor. The paper models this as a "switched affine Gaussian model" in representation space: ψt+1 = Aωt ψt + ωtbt. Here, Aωt is a mode-dependent linear operator (A₀ for passive, A₁ for interaction), and bt is an interaction-dependent residual induced by the actuated factor and action, which is non-zero only during interaction (ωt = 1). This structure fundamentally differs from the single-operator model of standard CRL.
3. **Two Challenges Identified from Analysis:**
 - **Sampling Challenge:** Standard CRL samples future goals by discounting. Since interaction events can be sparse and transient, sampled future goals often fall into passive (pre- or post-interaction) phases. This means the critical mode-switching "bridge transitions" are underrepresented in the training data, hindering the representation's ability to capture the specific dynamics of interaction.
 - **Error Propagation (Proposition 1):** The analysis formally demonstrates that any local estimation error (e) in learning the interaction-specific "bridge shift" (δτ) is propagated and potentially amplified through subsequent passive dynamics. Specifically, the endpoint energy error scales as ∥Ak₀∥∥e∥ + ½∥Ak₀∥²∥e∥², where k is the number of passive steps after interaction and A₀ is the passive dynamics operator. This implies that small errors at interaction can have substantial, magnified effects on later energy estimates, making learned representations fragile around contact.

**Interaction-Weighted Resampling (IWR):**
To address these challenges, IWR is introduced as a method to reshape the effective contrastive training distribution without altering the core CRL critic objective.
- **Interaction Indicator:** For an anchor state-action pair (st, at), IWR first identifies the positions of the actuated factor (up,t+k) and the target factor (yp,t+k) at a candidate future time t+k. Their Euclidean distance, dt+k = ∥up,t+k - yp,t+k∥₂, is used as a proximity measure.
- **Soft Interaction Weighting:** Each candidate future goal is assigned a soft interaction weight: wk = ϵ + exp(−∥dt+k - c∥₂² / (2σ²)).
 - `c` is a user-defined "loose interaction threshold," an approximate distance at which interaction is likely to occur (e.g., contact distance between gripper and object).
 - `σ` controls the width of the interaction window, determining how sharply the weight peaks around `c`.
 - `ϵ > 0` is a small constant that ensures global coverage, meaning even very distant (or non-interacting) samples still have a non-zero probability of being selected, preventing complete exclusion of rare but important dynamics.
- **Resampling Process:** Instead of uniformly sampling future goals, IWR uses these computed weights to preferentially resample positive futures (g(+)) such that they are centered around interaction-relevant points. This increases the likelihood that sampled positives capture the actual mode transitions.
- **Integration with CRL:** The resampled positive futures, along with replay-marginal negative futures, are then fed into the standard InfoNCE objective (Eq. 3) to train the CRL critic. The learned energy subsequently guides the actor policy, which collects new trajectories, thus closing the self-improving loop.
- **Mechanism to Counter Challenges:** By increasing the probability of sampling futures near interactions, IWR aims to reduce the local estimation error (∥e∥) of the interaction bridge shift. It also implicitly favors endpoints closer to the interaction, effectively reducing the post-interaction propagation length (k) and thus mitigating the amplification factor (∥Ak₀∥) in the error propagation. This reduces the passive bias in standard sampling and improves the conditioning for learning interaction-relevant representations.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research evaluates Interaction-Weighted Resampling (IWR) across a range of simulation and real-world environments, comparing its performance against both reward-based Reinforcement Learning (RL) and other Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) methods.

**Simulation Experiment Settings:**
The evaluation encompasses three categories of interaction-centric environments:
1. **2D Box2D manipulation:** Five tasks (center, goal, hard, hard-velocity, maze) where a blue ball controls a green ball into a red goal region. Performance is measured by "success ticks" (time green ball stays in goal), normalized.
2. **Robotic manipulation (Meta-World):** Four tasks (PUSH, PICK-PLACE, SWEEP-INTO, PEG-INSERT) using success rate as the metric.
3. **Robot Air Hockey:** A simulator task to hit a falling puck into a goal. Performance is evaluated on single-goal success rate and "consecutive goal-reaching," which measures the number of goals reached and "survival rate" within one minute. A "real-transfer" setting introduces observation noise and history-based observations.

**Baseline Methods:**
- **Reward-based RL with sparse rewards:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), SAC with Hindsight Experience Replay (SAC+HER), and SAC with Interaction Replay Buffer (SAC+HINT). These methods receive a sparse reward (1 for success, 0 otherwise).
- **Contrastive RL methods:** SGCRL and CRTR. CRTR is notably considered an ablation of IWR, effectively representing uniform sampling across futures (interaction-agnostic).

**Simulation Results (Table 1):**
- **Overall Improvement:** IWR demonstrates an average performance gain of 19.8% across all simulation tasks compared to prior CRL methods.
- **Reward-based RL vs. CRL family:** The reward-free CRL family (CRL, CRTR, IWR) generally outperforms PPO and the SAC variants (even with HER and HINT), especially on MetaWorld and the Air Hockey real-transfer tasks, where reward-based methods had no success.
- **Box2D tasks:** IWR showed particularly substantial gains in tasks requiring precise control and dealing with reduced interaction signals:
 - Box2D (goal): +27.1% over the next best baseline (CRTR).
 - Box2D (hard): +54.8% over CRTR.
 - Box2D (hard velocity): +12.7% over CRL.
 These results support the hypothesis that IWR is effective when interaction events are critical but rare.
- **MetaWorld tasks:** IWR yielded significant improvements in tasks requiring precise, long-horizon interactions, such as PICK-PLACE (+86.9% over CRTR). This suggests that IWR's benefits extend beyond simplified one-step interactions to more complex, hybrid dynamics with repeated interaction propagation. Modest gains were observed in PUSH (+3.9% over CRL), PEG-INSERT (+1.9% over CRL), and SWEEP-INTO (+1.8% over CRTR).
- **Air Hockey Simulation:** IWR showed a modest single-goal success rate improvement (+2.1% over CRTR) in the default simulation. However, in the "real-transfer" setting, which incorporates noise and discontinuous observations to mimic real-world conditions, IWR's performance improved more significantly (+4.8% over CRL), indicating its potential for robustness in noisy environments and better generalizability through enhanced sample coverage near interactions.
- **Consecutive Goal Reaching (Air Hockey):** IWR achieved improved results in the consecutive goal-hitting evaluation (Fig. 3b), with a higher mean success number (2.83 vs. 2.44 for CRTR) and survival rate (0.362 vs. 0.350 for CRTR). This indicates that IWR enhances not only isolated successes but also the robustness and precision of continuous interaction control.

**Real-Robot Experiment (Air Hockey):**
- **Setup:** A UR5e robot with a Robotiq gripper, overhead camera, and a 20Hz control loop was used. Policies were transferred from simulation to the real robot using a sim-to-real pipeline that incorporated domain randomization (e.g., paddle density, puck damping, gravity) and observation noise/delays. No real-robot training was performed.
- **Results:** IWR demonstrated a notable improvement in real-world performance, achieving a 60% success rate (12 out of 20 goals), a 140% increase over the next best method (CRL, 5 out of 20 goals). Other baselines (PPO, SAC, SAC+HER, SAC+HINT, CRTR) performed substantially worse or failed completely (0/20 or 2/20).
- **Qualitative Observations:** The authors attribute this substantial real-world gain to IWR's ability to learn a more robust policy. Policies trained with IWR consistently made contact with the puck and applied greater upward force, resulting in more successful strikes compared to other methods, which often made contact only once, leading to unrecoverable failures.

**Ablation Studies (Appendix H.2):**
Ablation studies on the IWR hyperparameters (interaction threshold `c` and interaction window width `2σ²`) indicate that IWR's performance is robust across a range of these parameters, with further tuning potentially yielding even better results. This supports the utility of the interaction-focused sampling approach.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of robot learning, particularly in the context of object manipulation.

**Theoretical Significance:**
The paper provides a principled analysis of the fundamental challenges faced by Contrastive Reinforcement Learning (CRL) in interaction-rich manipulation tasks. By formulating manipulation as a piecewise-smooth Markov process and highlighting the mode-conditioned dynamics induced by object interactions, it elucidates why the linear-Gaussian temporal models often implicitly learned by standard CRL are insufficient. This analysis formalizes the concept of piecewise nonlinear reachability structures and explains how CRL's energy functions can "smooth over critical interaction boundaries," leading to performance limitations. The formalization of error propagation (Proposition 1) quantifies how local errors at interaction points can be amplified through passive dynamics, further substantiating the need for specialized treatment of interaction events.

**Algorithmic Significance:**
Based on this theoretical analysis, the paper introduces Interaction-Weighted Resampling (IWR), a practical and simple algorithmic modification to CRL. IWR directly addresses the identified "sampling challenge" and "error propagation" by strategically reshaping the contrastive training distribution. By assigning higher sampling weights to future states that are proximal to interaction events, IWR provides an inductive bias that directs the learning process towards critical mode-switching regions. This encourages the learned representations to better capture the complex dynamics surrounding interactions, leading to a more accurate and robust goal-reaching energy landscape. The method's ability to operate without changing the core CRL objective makes it readily integrable.

**Empirical Impact:**
The effectiveness of IWR is demonstrated through comprehensive empirical validation across a diverse set of environments:
- **Improved Simulation Performance:** IWR consistently achieves improved sample efficiency and overall performance in various interaction-centric tasks, including 2D dynamic control (Box2D) and robotic manipulation (Meta-World), yielding an average improvement of 19.8% over prior CRL methods. This is particularly pronounced in tasks requiring precise, long-horizon interactions or when interaction signals are sparse.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Precision:** In tasks like Air Hockey, IWR not only improves single-goal success rates but also enhances the robustness and precision of continuous interaction control in consecutive goal-reaching scenarios.
- **Groundbreaking Real-World Application:** A notable impact is the successful transfer of IWR-trained policies to a real UR5e robot for goal-conditioned air hockey. Achieving a 60% success rate on the real robot, a 140% increase over the next best simulated-to-real method, represents a significant step forward. This demonstrates that IWR learns policies that are not only effective in simulation but also more robust and generalizable to the complexities and uncertainties of real-world robotic systems, including perception noise and unmodeled dynamics. This marks the first reported goal-conditioned robot air hockey agent capable of hitting goals.

**Potential Future Impact:**
This work lays a foundation for future advancements in robot learning for manipulation:
- It motivates further research into developing more sophisticated multimodal models for manipulation dynamics, moving beyond IWR's current approach of modifying sampling for a unimodal model.
- The principles of interaction-weighted sampling could be applied to other unsupervised Reinforcement Learning methods, extending their applicability to interaction-rich domains.
- Integrating IWR with end-to-end perception systems, rather than relying on factored states and heuristic interaction indicators, is identified as a crucial next step for deploying these methods in more complex, unstructured environments.
- Addressing the inherent sample-efficiency challenges of CRL in general, and IWR in particular, is essential for reducing the sim-to-real gap and enabling direct training on physical robots, which remains a long-term goal for the field.

In conclusion, this research provides both a theoretical explanation for a significant challenge in robot manipulation and a practical, experimentally validated solution that substantially improves performance, particularly in real-world scenarios requiring precise and robust object interaction.
