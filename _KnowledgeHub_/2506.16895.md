# With Limited Data for Multimodal Alignment, Let the STRUCTURE Guide You

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at EPFL developed a framework for multimodal alignment that achieves high performance with limited paired data by using "STRUCTURE" regularization and similarity-based layer selection. The method yields average relative improvements of 51.6% in zero-shot classification and 91.8% in cross-modal retrieval, requiring less than 1% of the data typically used by state-of-the-art models.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art multimodal models rely on vast amounts of paired training data, often hundreds of millions of samples, which are prohibitive to collect in many specialized scientific and real-world domains.
- Existing methods for parameter-frozen modality alignment either lack coherence in shared embedding spaces (unsupervised) or still require millions of paired samples (supervised), limiting their use in low-data settings.
- Efficiently leveraging powerful, pre-trained unimodal foundation models for multimodal tasks is difficult when paired data for alignment is scarce.

## Method

- A modular alignment strategy is introduced, combining ==STRUCTURE regularization== with a ==similarity-based layer selection== mechanism.
- ==STRUCTURE regularization== prevents distortion of unimodal latent structures by preserving ==multi-scale neighborhood geometry== through a ==Jensen-Shannon divergence-based loss==.
- A data-driven method selects the optimal intermediate layers from pretrained unimodal encoders for alignment based on their representational similarity, rather than defaulting to final layers.

## Results

- The framework yields average relative performance gains of **51.6%** for zero-shot classification and **91.8%** for cross-modal retrieval tasks.
- It achieves high-quality multimodal alignment with limited paired data (tens of thousands of samples), requiring up to **23 times** fewer samples than baseline methods.
- The proposed solution generalizes across various unimodal foundation models (e.g., RoBERTa, DINOv2) and diverse modalities beyond vision-language, including audio-text and single-cell biology.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Preserving the intrinsic multi-scale neighborhood geometry of pretrained unimodal representations is crucial for effective multimodal alignment in low-data environments.
> - Selecting the most representationally similar intermediate layers from unimodal encoders, rather than just the final layers, leads to improved alignment performance.
> - The judicious inclusion of a very small number of in-domain paired samples can drastically enhance performance, sometimes outperforming models trained on orders of magnitude more general data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of unimodal foundation models (FMs) in recent years, exemplified by Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-4 and Llama, and vision FMs such as DINOv2 and Segment Anything, has revolutionized various fields by achieving unprecedented performance on tasks within their respective modalities. Simultaneously, specialized FMs like AlphaFold for protein structure prediction demonstrate the power of large-scale models in scientific domains.

However, many real-world applications inherently involve multimodal data, necessitating the integration of information across different modalities into a shared representation space. Pioneering multimodal models like CLIP and CLAP have successfully aligned visual, auditory, and language inputs, enabling powerful zero-shot capabilities in classification and retrieval. A critical limitation of these models, which the present work addresses, is their reliance on "vast amounts of paired training data," often hundreds of millions of samples (e.g., CLIP uses 400M pairs). Such data volumes are prohibitively expensive or simply infeasible to collect in many specialized domains, particularly in healthcare, biology, and other scientific fields where data annotation is laborious and expertise-intensive.

This paper positions itself within the emerging field of "parameter-frozen modality alignment," which seeks to leverage the rich representations learned by already-trained unimodal encoders without updating their internal weights. Existing approaches in this subfield include:
- **Unsupervised alignment methods**: These techniques, like Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA), aim to align representations without paired data, primarily focusing on sample-level matching but often failing to construct a coherent shared embedding space. They also cannot utilize paired data even when available.
- **Supervised alignment methods**: These methods, such as linear or non-linear projections, assume access to abundant paired data (tens of millions of samples) to learn lightweight alignment modules. While effective in data-rich scenarios, they share the same fundamental limitation as end-to-end multimodal FMs regarding data scarcity.

The work by Gröger et al. directly addresses the fundamental question of whether multimodal models can be built with a *limited* number of paired samples. By operating in a "low-data regime" (tens of thousands of samples – less than 1% of data typically used), it carves out a critical niche in the multimodal learning landscape, pushing the boundaries of what is possible with constrained resources. It also builds upon the "Platonic representation hypothesis," which suggests that independently trained unimodal models may converge to similar internal representations, making alignment feasible without joint end-to-end training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective and robust strategy for multimodal alignment using a significantly limited amount of paired training data. Specifically, the authors aim to demonstrate that high-quality alignment is achievable with as few as tens of thousands of paired samples, a dramatic reduction compared to the millions or hundreds of millions typically required by state-of-the-art multimodal models.

The motivation for this objective stems from several critical challenges in the broader application of multimodal AI:
- **Data Scarcity and Cost**: In many real-world domains, particularly in specialized fields like healthcare, bioinformatics, and robotics, collecting and accurately annotating vast amounts of paired multimodal data is either prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, or practically impossible due to privacy concerns, ethical considerations, or the sheer complexity of the data.
- **Leveraging Existing Foundation Models**: Billions of dollars and countless hours have been invested in pretraining powerful unimodal foundation models. The paper seeks to efficiently leverage these pre-existing, highly performant encoders for multimodal tasks, rather than requiring new, expensive end-to-end training efforts.
- **Bridging the Gap for Resource-Constrained Research**: By making multimodal alignment accessible with limited data, the research aims to democratize access to powerful multimodal AI capabilities for smaller research groups, startups, and applications where resources are constrained.
- **Domain Adaptation**: The paper also implicitly motivates better domain adaptation by showing that a small amount of in-domain data can significantly boost performance, highlighting the importance of data *relevance* over sheer *volume*.

In essence, the authors are motivated to move beyond the "big data" paradigm for multimodal learning, proposing a data-efficient alternative that preserves the intrinsic knowledge encoded in unimodal foundation models, thereby broadening the applicability and accessibility of multimodal AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a modular alignment strategy that integrates two core components into existing parameter-frozen alignment methods: (i) STRUCTURE regularization and (ii) a similarity-based layer selection mechanism. The overall approach centers on aligning representations from independently pretrained, frozen unimodal encoders into a shared embedding space using lightweight alignment functions (e.g., linear projections or MLPs) trained on limited paired data.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The problem is formalized as learning two alignment functions, $f_1: \mathbb{R}^{d_1} \rightarrow A$ and $f_2: \mathbb{R}^{d_2} \rightarrow A$, that map modality-specific latent spaces ($X_1, X_2$) into a shared space $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$. The goal is to ensure that paired samples $(x^i_1, x^i_2)$ are closer in $A$ than non-paired samples, typically optimized using contrastive losses. The key distinction is operating under a low-data condition, where $N$ (the number of paired samples) is small.

#### 3.2. Preserving Neighborhood via STRUCTURE Regularization

With limited paired data, there's a risk of the alignment functions distorting the valuable latent structure already learned by the pretrained unimodal encoders. STRUCTURE (Structured Regularization for Multimodal Embedding) is introduced to prevent this by preserving the multi-scale neighborhood geometry of each unimodal latent space during alignment.

The formulation for a given modality's latent space $X$ and its aligned shared space $A$ is as follows:
1. **Normalization and Centering**: Input vectors $x_i \in X$ and $a_i \in A$ (where $a_i = f(x_i)$) are first $\ell_2$-normalized and then centered to remove global translation bias.
2. **Similarity Matrices**: Scaled similarity matrices $S_X$ and $S_A$ are computed using cosine similarity with a temperature parameter $\tau > 0$. These matrices capture pairwise relationships within the normalized and centered spaces.
3. **Probability Distributions**: A row-wise softmax function is applied to $S_X$ and $S_A$ to interpret similarities as probability distributions, resulting in $P_X$ and $P_A$.
4. **Multi-Scale Relationships**: To capture hierarchical relationships, the authors define $l$-hop matrices, $P_X^{(l)} = (P_X)^l$ and $P_A^{(l)} = (P_A)^l$, for $l = 1, \dots, L$. These matrices represent transition probabilities in a random walk on the data manifold, capturing progressively more global structure with increasing $l$.
5. **Jensen-Shannon Divergence**: The Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence, chosen for its symmetric nature, measures the divergence between $P_X^{(l)}$ and $P_A^{(l)}$ at each hierarchical level $l$. This quantifies the consistency of neighborhood structures across modalities at different scales.
6. **Final Regularizer**: The STRUCTURE regularizer $R^{(L)}_S(X, A)$ is a weighted average of these level-specific JS divergences, with lower levels weighted more heavily to counteract the more concentrated distributions of higher levels.
7. **Combined Loss**: This regularizer is added to the standard alignment objective function $L_A$ (e.g., contrastive loss), forming the total loss: $L = L_A + \lambda (R^{(L)}_S(X_1, f_1(X_1)) + R^{(L)}_S(X_2, f_2(X_2)))$, where $\lambda$ is a regularization weight.

A generalization bound (Lemma 1) is provided, demonstrating that the empirical regularizer approximates its population expectation at a rate of $O(1/\sqrt{N})$, suggesting statistical reliability even with limited samples.

#### 3.3. Similarity-Based Layer Selection

Instead of the common practice of aligning only the last layers of unimodal encoders, the authors propose a data-driven approach to select the most suitable intermediate layers for alignment. This is motivated by the observation that representational similarity between layers of different modalities strongly correlates with downstream alignment performance (as shown in Figure 2).

The layer selection procedure involves:
1. **Representational Similarity Calculation**: Compute the representational similarity between all pairs of layers from the two unimodal encoders. The authors use mutual k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) with $k$ chosen according to Rice's criterion for this. This is performed on a small subset of paired samples (e.g., 5,000 pairs).
2. **Optimal Layer Pair Selection**: Choose the pair of layers (one from each modality) that exhibits the highest representational similarity as the input to the alignment functions.

This approach ensures that the alignment process leverages the most naturally compatible representations across modalities, rather than relying on an arbitrary choice like the final layer.

The proposed components are designed to be "plug-and-play," seamlessly integrating with various existing alignment methods (e.g., linear mapping, non-linear MLP mapping, or CSA) and objective functions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings, demonstrating the effectiveness and broad applicability of the proposed STRUCTURE framework for limited-sample multimodal learning:

- **Substantial Performance Gains**: Incorporating STRUCTURE regularization, especially when combined with similarity-based layer selection, leads to dramatic performance improvements across diverse tasks.
 - **Zero-shot Classification**: On average, a relative improvement of 51.6% across 22 datasets. For MLP-based alignment, the average relative improvement was 74.0%, and for Linear alignment, 68.4%.
 - **Cross-modal Retrieval**: Average relative improvement of 91.8% across Flickr30 and MS COCO benchmarks. For MLP, this was 137.0%, and for Linear alignment, 122.8%.
 - In some cases, such as CIFAR10, the proposed strategy even outperformed CLIP (a model trained on 400M pairs) by about 2% while using only 0.02% of the data.

- **Exceptional Data Efficiency**: The method achieves high-quality alignment with as few as tens of thousands of paired samples (e.g., 80,000 COCO pairs), which is less than 1% of the data typically used by existing methods.
 - Experiments scaling down training data to as little as 1,000 samples still showed significant improvements, highlighting robustness in extremely resource-constrained settings.
 - The "utility" metric indicated substantial label savings, with the method requiring up to 23 times fewer samples to achieve performance equivalent to baselines (e.g., 23.1x for CIFAR100 classification, 22.4x for Flickr30 T2I retrieval).

- **Effectiveness of Layer Selection**: Choosing layers based on representational similarity consistently improved performance across all alignment methods, even without STRUCTURE regularization. This strategy alone yielded average relative gains of 2.5-4.8% in classification and 2.7-18.3% in retrieval, affirming the hypothesis that optimal alignment layers are not necessarily the last ones.

- **Impact of In-Domain Samples**: A crucial finding is that mixing a very small number of *in-domain* examples (a few samples per class) into the training set effectively bridges distribution gaps and enables the model to match or even surpass the performance of large-scale multimodal models like CLIP. For example, with just three samples per class, accuracy on the Flowers dataset rose from ~24% to >95%, outperforming CLIP's 93%. This suggests that domain coverage and relevance are more critical than raw data volume for specialized tasks.

- **Robustness Across Models and Modalities**:
 - The framework consistently improved results across various combinations of language models (RoBERTa, Llama3-8B, Llama-13B) and vision models (DINOv2 ViT-G).
 - Extended experiments confirmed the method's efficacy in non-vision-language domains, including audio-text alignment (using BEATs-iter3 and RoBERTa on Clotho dataset) and single-cell image-transcriptome alignment (using UCE and MAE on biological data), demonstrating broad generalizability.

- **Neighborhood Preservation Verification**: Empirical monitoring of Trustworthiness and Continuity metrics showed that STRUCTURE regularization effectively preserves the multi-scale neighborhood geometry of the pretrained latent spaces throughout optimization, preventing undesirable distortion or "over-warping" that occurs with unregularized alignment.

- **Modality Gap Reduction**: The proposed method effectively reduces the "modality gap" (Euclidean distance between mean image and text embeddings), aligning with prior research suggesting that a smaller gap correlates with better downstream performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented by Gröger et al. offers a highly significant contribution to the field of multimodal machine learning, particularly addressing a major bottleneck in its real-world application: the prohibitive data requirements.

#### 5.1. Core Significance

- **Democratization of Multimodal AI**: By enabling high-quality multimodal alignment with limited paired data, this research significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing powerful multimodal systems. This is particularly impactful for resource-constrained domains such as healthcare, biology, specialized scientific research, and small businesses, where collecting millions of paired samples is often impossible or economically unfeasible.
- **Efficient Leverage of Pretrained Foundation Models**: The framework provides a principled way to build upon the immense investment in pretraining unimodal foundation models. Instead of training complex multimodal architectures from scratch with vast datasets, researchers can efficiently align existing, highly capable encoders, maximizing the utility of available resources.
- **Shift Towards Data Quality and Relevance**: The striking results demonstrating that a few in-domain samples can bridge the performance gap to models trained on orders of magnitude more general data suggest a paradigm shift. The emphasis moves from brute-force data volume to strategic data selection, focusing on data that is relevant to the target domain, implying that "smart data" is more valuable than "big data" in low-data regimes.
- **Modular and Generalizable Solution**: STRUCTURE regularization and similarity-based layer selection are designed as "plug-and-play" components that can be seamlessly integrated into various existing alignment pipelines (linear, MLP, CSA, etc.) and across different modalities. This modularity enhances the practical utility and widespread applicability of the method.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Accelerated Research in Specialized Domains**: Researchers in fields like medical imaging, genomics, and environmental science can develop multimodal AI tools (e.g., correlating medical images with patient records, or genetic data with phenotypic observations) without needing to undertake massive, costly data collection efforts.
- **Enhanced Zero-Shot Learning and Retrieval**: The substantial improvements in zero-shot classification and cross-modal retrieval make these capabilities more robust and practical in scenarios where explicit training data for new categories is scarce.
- **Reduced Computational Footprint**: Focusing on lightweight alignment functions and frozen encoders means the method is computationally efficient, requiring less GPU memory and training time compared to training large multimodal models from scratch, which contributes to more sustainable AI research.
- **Foundation for Future Work**: The findings pave the way for future research into optimal data curation strategies for multimodal alignment (what *types* of data are most effective?), as well as extensions to aligning more than two modalities, where data scarcity is even more pronounced. The paper explicitly mentions the straightforward extension of STRUCTURE to three or more modalities, outlining a clear path for future development.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors candidly acknowledge limitations. While performing competitively in low-data regimes, a performance gap remains compared to models like CLIP trained on hundreds of millions of samples for some challenging tasks. However, they demonstrate that this gap can be effectively reduced by incorporating a few in-domain samples, hinting at the potential of few-shot adaptation strategies. Future work will investigate how different types of data influence cross-modal alignment quality in low-data regimes. The paper also notes that while their current work focuses on two modalities, the STRUCTURE framework is designed to be easily extensible to three or more, an active area of research they defer to future efforts.

In conclusion, "With Limited Data for Multimodal Alignment, Let the STRUCTURE Guide You" represents a significant step towards making powerful multimodal AI more accessible and practical for a broader range of applications, especially in domains traditionally constrained by data availability. By emphasizing intrinsic structure preservation and strategic layer selection, it offers a compelling alternative to the data-hungry approaches that have dominated the field.
