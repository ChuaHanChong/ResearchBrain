# Don't Drop the BATON: Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation via Agentic Subtask Exploration and Transition-aware Memory

> [!summary] Summary
> BATON is an LLM coding agent that orchestrates a frozen VLA for thousand-step manipulation tasks by exploring subtasks individually and storing three kinds of transition contracts (invocation, handoff, lookahead) in language memory, with no parameter updates. On RoboMemArena it reaches 57.7% task success and 78.8% cumulative success, beating prior SoTA by 11.6 and 14.9 points.

## Problem

- Whole-task exploration for agent-plus-VLA recipes needs on the order of T^K episodes for K stages, and a failed rollout never reveals which stage broke.
- The VLA invocation primitive carries an exit condition but no entry condition, so control passes to the frozen policy without any check that the scene is ready.
- Skill libraries and memory-augmented VLAs collapse rival strategies to one canonical solution per subtask, discarding which realization was chosen even though the next subtask's feasibility depends on it.

## Method

- ==Hierarchical subtask exploration== decomposes a long-horizon instruction into subtasks and boundaries, explores each subtask in the cheap short-horizon regime, then composes verified pairs outward so exploration cost grows additively (KT) rather than multiplicatively.
- An **invocation transition** adds a wrist-camera verifier agent that gates ==VLA_ACT== calls on a stated readiness condition instead of a blindly discovered timing.
- A **handoff transition** and **lookahead transition**, enforced by verifier and scheduler agents, restore the entry state a predecessor disturbs and pick the rival execution strategy compatible with the remaining plan, all stored as edge-keyed language contracts in a ==skill-plus-transition memory==.

## Results

- BATON scores **57.7%** TSR and **78.8%** CSR averaged over RoboMemArena's four task categories, exceeding the strongest reported baseline FrameSamp+Modul (**46.1** TSR) by **11.6 points** and surpassing even the ground-truth-memory oracle (**46.1** TSR, **64.8** CSR).
- Whole-task Harness VLA under the same 20-episode exploration budget reaches only **26.9%** average TSR and never solves an occlusion task during exploration, versus BATON's **50.0%** TSR on occlusion and **68.8%** on counting.
- BATON converts **73%** of its completed stages into completed tasks, the highest stage-to-task ratio in the table, indicating fewer failures concentrated at subtask boundaries.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Long-horizon failure is dominated by transitions between stages, not stage-level competence: every method completes far more stages than full tasks, and even a perfect-memory oracle only converts 64.8% CSR into 46.1% TSR.
> - Pricing exploration per subtask instead of per whole task turns an exponential search into an additive one and makes failures locally attributable, a pattern applicable to any multi-stage test-time-search agent.
> - Retaining rival strategies keyed by transition type rather than collapsing to one canonical skill per task lets a solution transfer across unseen task pairings that share the same boundary type.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is progressing rapidly, particularly with the advent of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World-Action Models (WAMs). These models have demonstrated increasing competence in executing individual, contact-rich robotic skills, often leveraging web-scale pretraining to generalize across various tasks. Examples include models that can reliably open a drawer, pick up an object, or place it.

However, a significant challenge persists in chaining these individual skills into complex, long-horizon, multi-stage tasks. When individual skills are composed, errors tend to compound, and the execution of one subtask can inadvertently create conditions that make a subsequent subtask impossible or significantly more difficult. Current reactive VLA policies often overfit to the initial states present in their training demonstrations. Consequently, when deployed in chained tasks where each stage begins from a state left by its predecessor—rather than a curated initial state—they struggle to recognize or correct accumulating deviations from their training distribution. This leads to a substantial drop in overall task success rates, even when individual subtask success rates are high.

A promising paradigm for addressing task composition and long-horizon control is the use of coding agents, typically powered by Large Language Models (LLMs). These agents control robots by generating and revising programs based on perception and control primitives. They can plan in language, execute free-space movements using analytic, inverse-kinematics-solved primitives, and invoke learned policies (like VLAs) for contact-rich segments. Systems like Harness VLA have demonstrated that such agents can wrap a frozen VLA as a retryable primitive, enabling reliable skill execution under perturbations in short-horizon tasks. This approach avoids fine-tuning parameters, allows for symbolic reasoning, and stores learned adaptations in human-readable language memory.

Despite the advantages of coding agents, their direct application to long-horizon manipulation faces two primary limitations:
1. **Exploration Cost (Problem ❶):** Current agentic learning paradigms acquire competence through whole-task exploration at test time. For a multi-stage task with K stages, if a single stage requires T exploration episodes on average, a K-stage task can theoretically demand on the order of T^K episodes. This multiplicative scaling of exploration cost is prohibitive. Furthermore, failures in whole-task exploration are often uninformative, revealing only that the task failed somewhere, without pinpointing the exact problematic stage.
2. **Lack of Transition Representation (Problem ❷):** Existing VLA primitives and agent frameworks generally lack explicit representations of the interfaces or "transitions" between different operational phases. For instance, a VLA primitive might have a stop predicate (an exit condition) but no defined entry condition. This can lead to situations where a subtask succeeds locally, but the manner of its success leaves the environment in a state that the successor subtask cannot effectively utilize. Key transitions, such as the moment control passes to a contact-rich policy (invocation), how one subtask hands off the environment to the next (handoff), or how future subtasks might constrain current execution (lookahead), are not formalized or actively managed.

BATON positions itself within this landscape by building upon the foundation of LLM coding agents controlling frozen VLAs. Its core contribution is to directly address these two identified limitations. It reframes the problem by making "transitions" explicit first-class objects within the system's memory and control structure. By doing so, BATON aims to bridge the gap between individual skill competence and robust long-horizon task execution, a critical step towards generalist robotic policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the BATON research is to enable reliable, long-horizon robot manipulation by overcoming the systemic failures encountered when chaining individually competent robotic skills. This overarching goal is driven by two specific, identified challenges: the prohibitive cost of exploration in multi-stage tasks and the absence of explicit mechanisms to manage transitions between subtasks.

**Motivation for Addressing Problem ❶: Large Cost of Full Task Exploration**

The current paradigm for coding agents involves learning through test-time exploration. For short-horizon tasks, an agent might explore various staging orders, pre-contact poses, and VLA invocation timings until a successful solution is found and subsequently consolidated into memory. While this approach is feasible for single skills, extending it to long-horizon tasks (chains of K subtasks) incurs a significant cost, proportional to $T^K$ episodes, where $T$ is the average number of episodes to solve a single subtask. This multiplicative scaling makes exploration intractable for realistic long-horizon tasks, which can involve thousands of steps.

Furthermore, failures during whole-task exploration are inherently uninformative. When a K-stage task fails, the system only learns that the entire chain broke, without identifying which specific stage or transition was responsible. This lack of localized feedback hinders efficient diagnosis, iteration, and learning, leading to wasted exploration budget on repeatedly traversing already-solved initial stages.

The motivation for BATON here is to transform this exploration cost from multiplicative to additive ($T \cdot K$). This is to be achieved by enabling the agent to explore and learn individual subtasks and their boundaries efficiently, rather than attempting to solve the entire, complex chain at once.

**Motivation for Addressing Problem ❷: Unspecified Transitions Across the Chain**

The second major motivation stems from the observation that critical interfaces between different parts of a multi-stage task remain unrepresented in existing systems. These "transitions" are crucial points where errors propagate or where incompatible outcomes can derail subsequent actions. Three specific types of transitions are highlighted:

1. **Invocation Transition (within a subtask):** The moment control passes from the high-level LLM agent to the low-level contact-rich VLA. Current VLAs typically have an "exit condition" (when to stop acting) but lack an "entry condition" (when it is appropriate to start acting). Without such a condition, the VLA might be invoked prematurely or from a pre-contact state that falls outside its training distribution, leading to failures that the VLA's "harness" is designed to prevent. In a long-horizon task, the pre-contact state can vary significantly due to prior actions, making a fixed invocation timing unreliable.

2. **Handoff Transition (across subtasks):** The interface where one completed subtask hands off the environment to the next. A subtask might succeed according to its own criteria but leave residual effects (e.g., a rotated gripper after opening a door) that disturb the initial state required by the successor subtask. This leads to ambiguous failures, where it is unclear if the successor skill is inadequate or merely operating from an incorrect entry state. Current systems cannot distinguish these cases, leading to potentially fruitless re-exploration of a functional skill.

3. **Lookahead Transition (across subtasks):** The dependency where the requirements of a *future* subtask dictate how the *current* subtask must be executed. For instance, picking up a can with a top grasp might be locally successful but could make subsequent placement into a narrow microwave impossible due to height constraints. If the system only optimizes for local success, it may choose a strategy that makes global success unachievable. Existing approaches generally lack mechanisms for this backward propagation of constraints, leading to sub-optimal or incompatible local solutions.

The motivation for BATON in this area is to equip the agent with a "transition-aware memory" that explicitly defines, checks, and stores conditions for these critical interfaces. This aims to prevent errors at the "seams" of the task, ensuring that individually competent skills can compose reliably into a successful long-horizon operation.

In summary, BATON's core objectives are to:
- Develop a coding agent framework for long-horizon robot manipulation using a frozen VLA, learning via test-time exploration without parameter updates.
- Implement hierarchical subtask exploration to ensure exploration costs scale additively and failures are localized.
- Integrate a transition-aware memory to explicitly manage invocation, handoff, and lookahead transitions, thereby improving the robustness and composability of skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

BATON is designed as an LLM coding agent that orchestrates a frozen VLA for long-horizon robot manipulation tasks. The methodology directly targets the two identified problems: exploration cost and unspecified transitions, using hierarchical exploration and a novel transition-aware memory. No parameters are updated within the VLA or LLM during this process; all learning is through language-based memory.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Harnessing a Frozen VLA

BATON adopts the "Harness VLA" paradigm. This involves an LLM agent that controls the robot by writing programs over a set of primitives. These primitives include:
- **Analytic primitives:** For non-contact free-space motions (e.g., moving the end-effector along an inverse-kinematics-solved path, reorienting the wrist, actuating the gripper).
- **VLA ACT primitive:** A single, frozen VLA wrapped with a task-conditioned prompt and a stop predicate. This allows the LLM agent to invoke the VLA for contact-rich segments of a task, monitor its execution, and retry if necessary.
This division of labor ensures that the LLM handles high-level planning and composition, while the VLA handles low-level contact-rich interactions, typically from pre-contact poses staged by the agent, thus keeping the VLA within its training distribution.

#### 3.2. Addressing Problem ❶: Hierarchical Subtask Exploration

To manage the prohibitive $T^K$ exploration cost, BATON employs a hierarchical exploration strategy:

1. **Task Decomposition:** For any given long-horizon instruction, the LLM agent first decomposes it into an ordered chain of K individual subtasks ($u_1, \dots, u_K$) and $K-1$ explicit handoff boundaries ($\sigma_{1,2}, \dots, \sigma_{K-1,K}$). This decomposition leverages existing knowledge in memory for previously solved subtasks or boundaries. This step immediately transforms the multiplicative exploration problem into an additive one, as exploration can be scoped to individual pieces. Failures are now attributable to a specific subtask or boundary.

2. **Subtask Bootstrapping:** For each subtask ($u_k$) that does not have a usable entry in memory, the LLM agent (in its "Explorer" role) runs a localized exploration loop. This involves:
 - **Trialing strategies:** The agent explores different staging orders, pre-contact poses, and VLA ACT invocation timings, but crucially, these trials are constrained by the "invocation transition" readiness condition (described below).
 - **Failure Memory:** Each failed attempt is recorded in a "failure memory," noting what was attempted and how it failed. This prevents repeated mistakes and guides future exploration.
 - **Skill Memory Consolidation:** Upon the first successful execution of a subtask, its successful coarse trajectory is stored in "skill memory." This trajectory includes staging waypoints, the readiness condition for VLA invocation, the VLA ACT call, and a termination check, all represented as symbolic perception queries. This exploration is confined to the "short-horizon regime" of a single subtask.

3. **Hierarchical Composition:** Once individual subtasks are solved, BATON composes them outwards, level by level.
 - **Verified Units:** Related neighboring subtasks are chained first (e.g., opening a drawer then closing it). If they compose cleanly, they form a "verified unit."
 - **Boundary-focused Search:** The process then chains these verified units. Exploration efforts in this phase are concentrated solely on the new boundary being created between units.
 - **Additive Cost:** This ensures that each of the $K-1$ handoff boundaries is examined only once when its two sides first meet, keeping the overall composition cost additive.
 - **Localized Failures:** Any failure during composition is attributed to the newly formed boundary, not to the already-verified internal components.

#### 3.3. Addressing Problem ❷: Transition-Aware Memory

BATON introduces a memory structure that explicitly represents three types of transitions, both within and across subtasks, to overcome composition failures. All agent roles (Explorer, Verifier, Scheduler) are handled by the same LLM agent.

1. **Invocation Transition ($\sigma_{act_k}$) – Within a Subtask:**
 - **Explicit Factorization:** The skill is factored as $u_k = a_k \triangleright \sigma_{act_k} \triangleright v_k$, where $a_k$ is the LLM agent's non-contact staging, $v_k$ is the VLA ACT's contact-rich execution, and $\sigma_{act_k}$ is the invocation transition.
 - **Readiness Condition:** VLA ACT is only invoked when a specific "readiness condition" holds, verified by the LLM agent (as a "verifier") interpreting the live wrist-camera image. Examples include ensuring gripper fingers are on either side of a handle before pulling, or within a container before releasing.
 - **Error Handling:** If the condition is not met, the agent re-stages using analytic primitives and re-checks. Attempts that cannot be made ready are recorded in failure memory.
 - **Memory and Purpose:** This condition is stored with the skill's coarse trajectory. It ensures the VLA operates within its training distribution, preventing failures from out-of-distribution pre-contact states and guarding against accumulating drift during long runs.

2. **Handoff Transition ($\sigma_{k,k+1}$) – Across Subtasks (Forward Failure):**
 - **Entry Condition:** This transition addresses situations where a predecessor subtask leaves the environment in a state incompatible with the successor. The boundary records an "entry condition" defining the required initial state for $u_{k+1}$ (e.g., gripper vertical, open, and above the target object).
 - **Restoration:** The "verifier" agent enforces this condition. If the current state violates it, the agent uses analytic primitives to restore the scene to satisfy the entry condition before $u_{k+1}$ begins.
 - **Memory and Purpose:** This condition is stored on the edge (boundary) between $u_k$ and $u_{k+1}$ because it is a property of the pair. It distinguishes between a successor skill's inadequacy and an incorrect entry state, ensuring accurate problem diagnosis.

3. **Lookahead Transition ($\sigma_{k,k+1}$) – Across Subtasks (Backward Failure):**
 - **Execution Constraint:** This transition addresses cases where a subtask succeeds locally but in a form unusable by a *subsequent* subtask. The boundary records an "execution constraint" for $u_k$, specifying how it must end to enable later steps (e.g., use a side grasp for a can if it needs to fit into a narrow microwave later).
 - **Scheduling:** A "scheduler" agent enforces this. Before $u_k$ executes, it reviews the remaining plan ($P_{>k}$) and the constraints induced by future boundaries. It then retrieves the rival strategy from memory that satisfies these constraints.
 - **New Rival Discovery:** If no stored rival strategy fits, $u_k$ is returned to the Explorer with the added constraint for re-exploration.
 - **Memory and Purpose:** This mechanism allows future task requirements to influence current execution, ensuring global task success.

#### 3.4. Unified Memory Structure

BATON's memory (M) integrates these three types of entries, mirroring the task decomposition into nodes and edges:
- **Nodes (Subtasks):** Store failure entries and multiple rival coarse trajectories for a given subtask, each embedding its own invocation readiness condition.
- **Edges (Boundaries):** Store the entry conditions of the handoff transition and the execution constraints of the lookahead transition. Together, these form a "handoff contract" ($C_{k,k+1}$), drafted in language.
Rival strategies are not pre-enumerated; they are added to memory only when a new constraint dictates a different successful execution. Memory entries are keyed by transition type, promoting transferability across different tasks. When a contract is violated during execution, the violation and the offending strategy are recorded, so the lesson attaches to the transition and is inherited by future tasks, making the learning process additive. This contrasts with traditional skill libraries that typically store only one canonical strategy per skill.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of BATON focused on assessing its ability to recover composite-task performance from stage-level competence, specifically addressing how well it manages exploration costs and transitions.

**Evaluation Setup:**
- **Benchmark:** RoboMemArena, a long-horizon manipulation benchmark featuring 26 household tasks. These tasks involve over 1,000 steps on average and 3–9 verification stages in partially observable kitchen scenes, with 68.9% of subtasks being memory-dependent. Tasks are categorized into `transferring`, `occlusion`, `counting`, and `sequence`.
- **Protocol:** Exploration was performed on a single reference scene configuration (seed 50), while evaluation was conducted on two held-out configurations (seeds 51 and 52), with reported numbers averaging these two.
- **Metrics:** Task Success Rate (TSR), indicating completion of all verification stages, and Cumulative Success Rate (CSR), representing the fraction of stages completed.
- **Baselines:** Included various reactive VLAs ($\pi_{0.5}$), memory-augmented VLAs (HiF-VLA, MemoryVLA, MemER, PrediMem, FrameSamp+Modul), a VLM agent without manipulation harness (GPT-5.4), and a direct comparison against an unmodified Harness VLA (re-run by the authors with identical exploration budget).

**Key Findings:**

1. **Superior Average Performance:** BATON achieved the highest average performance on RoboMemArena:
 - **Task Success Rate (TSR): 57.7%**
 - **Cumulative Success Rate (CSR): 78.8%**
 This performance surpassed the strongest reported system (FrameSamp+Modul) by 11.6 percentage points in TSR and 14.9 percentage points in CSR.

2. **Outperformance of Oracle:** BATON also exceeded the "PrediMem + GT memory" oracle (46.1% TSR, 64.8% CSR), which has perfect retrospective memory. This result suggests that merely remembering the past is insufficient for long-horizon tasks; actively managing transitions is crucial.

3. **Effectiveness of Hierarchical Exploration and Transition Management:**
 - **Comparison to Harness VLA:** An unmodified Harness VLA (using whole-task exploration) achieved 26.9% TSR and 38.5% CSR. BATON's significant improvement over Harness VLA (57.7% TSR, 78.8% CSR) directly validates the benefits of its hierarchical subtask exploration and transition-aware memory.
 - **Category-Specific Gains:** While Harness VLA struggled severely with "occlusion" tasks (0.0% TSR), BATON achieved 50.0% TSR in this category, showing a substantial margin over the next best system (FrameSamp+Modul at 39.1%). Similar large gains were observed in "counting" tasks (BATON: 68.8% TSR vs. FrameSamp+Modul: 31.4%). These improvements indicate that BATON's methodology effectively addresses challenges where memory and inter-stage dependencies are critical.
 - **TSR/CSR Conversion Ratio:** BATON converted 73% of its completed stages into completed tasks, the highest ratio among all evaluated methods. This indicates that its transition management effectively minimizes failures at subtask boundaries, ensuring successful composition.

4. **Addressing the "Between Stages" Problem:** The baseline results consistently showed a large discrepancy between CSR (fraction of stages completed) and TSR (full tasks completed), indicating that skills often fail when chained, even if they succeed in isolation. BATON's strong TSR relative to CSR demonstrates its ability to bridge this gap, confirming that its explicit management of transitions significantly enhances composability.

5. **Limitations in "Transferring" Category:** "Transferring" was the only category where another reported system (FrameSamp+Modul) slightly outperformed BATON. The authors note that this deficit is partially an evaluation artifact due to a faulty BDDL scene definition for one of the four transferring tasks (task 19) on the held-out seeds, leading to zero scores for that specific task regardless of execution.

In conclusion, BATON's empirical results on RoboMemArena demonstrate that by making subtasks the unit of exploration and transitions the unit of memory, it can achieve a level of long-horizon manipulation robustness and success rate that surpasses current state-of-the-art methods, including those with perfect retrospective memory.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The BATON research presents a significant advancement in the field of long-horizon robot manipulation, particularly for systems leveraging LLM coding agents and frozen VLAs. Its implications extend across several dimensions:

1. **Paradigm Shift in Problem Formulation:** BATON proposes that long-horizon manipulation failures are primarily a problem of managing "transitions" rather than solely improving individual skills or planners. By making invocation, handoff, and lookahead transitions explicit, it introduces a structured way to diagnose, address, and remember solutions for inter-stage complexities. This conceptual shift can guide future research towards interface management between skills.

2. **Enabling Long-Horizon LLM-based Robotic Agents:** Prior LLM-based coding agents like Harness VLA demonstrated competence on short-horizon tasks but struggled with the multiplicative exploration costs and unaddressed transition issues of long-horizon scenarios. BATON directly addresses these limitations, demonstrating that these powerful, parameter-agnostic agents can robustly execute tasks spanning thousands of steps. This expands the practical applicability of LLM agents in real-world, multi-stage robotic operations.

3. **Enhanced Exploration Efficiency and Scalability:** The shift from multiplicative ($T^K$) to additive ($T \cdot K$) exploration costs for K-stage tasks is a fundamental improvement in efficiency. This makes learning for complex tasks tractable and scalable, enabling robots to acquire new long-horizon capabilities within reasonable exploration budgets. The localization of failure to specific subtasks or boundaries also renders the learning process more informative and faster.

4. **Improved Robustness and Reliability:** By implementing a transition-aware memory and explicit verification mechanisms (e.g., wrist-camera verifier for invocation and handoff transitions), BATON significantly enhances the robustness of robotic manipulation. This proactively prevents common failure modes such as invoking a VLA from an out-of-distribution state, misalignments between subtasks, or locally optimal actions leading to globally infeasible states. This leads to a higher conversion rate of completed stages into fully completed tasks.

5. **Transferability and Reusability of Knowledge:** The "handoff contracts" and transition-keyed memory entries are designed to be language-based and general. This means that a lesson learned about a specific transition (e.g., "release into a container with a narrow lip") can be transferred and reused across different tasks that involve similar boundaries or conditions, without requiring task-specific re-training. This fosters cumulative learning and reduces the need for re-exploration for every new task sequence.

6. **Interpretability and Auditability:** Since all learned adaptations and transition conditions are stored in language memory, the system's reasoning and problem-solving strategies become transparent, auditable, and interpretable by human operators. This is a significant advantage over opaque end-to-end neural policies, facilitating debugging, trust, and human-robot collaboration.

7. **Contribution to Generalist Robots:** The work contributes to the development of generalist robots by improving their ability to compose and adapt existing skills into complex behaviors without continuous re-training. The principles of explicit transition management and hierarchical exploration are foundational for agents expected to operate robustly in diverse, open-ended environments.

The quantitative results on RoboMemArena, with BATON surpassing previous state-of-the-art and even an oracle with perfect retrospective memory, underscore the practical impact of its methodology. This work establishes a new benchmark for long-horizon manipulation and lays groundwork for more capable, adaptable, and interpretable robotic systems.
