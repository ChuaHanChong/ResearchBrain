# Cross-Platform Control for Autonomous Surface Vehicles via Adaptive Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> An adaptive reinforcement learning framework from ETH Zurich enables autonomous surface vehicles (ASVs) to perform zero-shot cross-platform trajectory tracking. This single policy, trained on a lightweight 3-DoF model, learns to adapt to diverse vessel dynamics online, achieving comparable performance to platform-specific tuned controllers on physical ASVs and reducing position errors by up to 58% over non-adaptive generalist policies.

## Problem

- Traditional ASV control systems require extensive platform-specific system identification or controller tuning, creating a "per-platform bottleneck" hindering efficient deployment of diverse ASV fleets.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches for ASVs often rely on high-fidelity simulators tailored to a single vehicle, limiting generalization across different platforms.
- The challenge of deploying a generalist controller that can operate on multiple ASV platforms without prior knowledge of their specific dynamics.

## Method

- A ==teacher-student== adaptive ==reinforcement learning== architecture is proposed, where a student policy uses a ==GRU-based adapter== to infer unknown vessel dynamics from interaction history (a ==privileged-learning== distillation scheme).
- The policy is trained using a lightweight ==3-DoF analytical dynamics model== (Fossen formulation) with wide ==domain randomization==, avoiding the need for high-fidelity hydrodynamic simulators, and optimized via ==PPO==.
- ==Normalized body-frame forces== are used as policy outputs, which are then scaled by platform-specific actuator limits, decoupling the policy from absolute force magnitudes.

## Results

- The adaptive Student policy achieved the lowest position mean absolute error (**4.0 cm**) on physical Platform A, outperforming platform-specific MPC (**4.6 cm**) and non-adaptive PPO baselines.
- On physical Platform B, the Student policy matched platform-specific MPC for position MAE (**4.0 cm** vs. **4.1 cm**) and reduced position error by **58%** compared to the non-adaptive generalist PPO (**9.5 cm**).
- In simulation, the Student policy reduced position error by **44%** and heading error by **55%** compared to the non-adaptive PPO (general), with its performance closely approaching the oracle Teacher policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating cross-platform control as a partially observable Markov decision process allows the policy to infer unknown dynamics from real-time interaction history.
> - Training a teacher policy with privileged dynamics information and then distilling this knowledge into a student adapter provides an effective mechanism for online adaptation.
> - A lightweight 3-DoF model with broad dynamics randomization can effectively train a generalist policy, circumventing the need for complex, high-fidelity simulators and completing training in approximately 30 minutes on a single consumer GPU.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous Surface Vehicles (ASVs) are increasingly utilized across a variety of applications, including surveillance, oceanographic observation, environmental monitoring, disaster response, hydrographic surveying, autonomous shipping, and even robotic art. The diversity in ASV platforms, characterized by wide variations in their hydrodynamic and actuation properties, presents a significant engineering challenge. Traditionally, control systems for ASVs, whether model-based or reinforcement learning (RL) based, are designed and tuned for specific platforms. This necessitates extensive system identification and controller tuning for each new vessel, creating a "per-platform bottleneck" that impedes the efficient deployment of heterogeneous ASV fleets.

Model-based control methods, such as those relying on Fossen formulations, can achieve high tracking accuracy but require precise dynamics models and platform-specific tuning. Reinforcement learning offers an alternative, training policies in simulation. However, existing RL approaches for ASVs typically depend on a high-fidelity simulator that accurately replicates the target platform's dynamics and are often tailored for a single, fixed vehicle.

The concept of a generalist controller that can operate across multiple platforms without explicit knowledge of their dynamics or per-platform tuning is an emerging area of research. In the context of robotics, cross-platform deployment of a single policy has been demonstrated in domains such as legged locomotion, adapting to robots with diverse masses and morphologies. When the specific dynamics of a deployment platform are unknown, this problem can be framed as one of partial observability. Prior approaches to handle unknown dynamics in such settings include combining randomized training with online system identification or employing recurrent neural networks in policies. These methods often aim to predict exact dynamics parameters or use general recurrent architectures.

This work situates itself within this landscape by addressing the per-platform bottleneck in ASV control. It aims to develop a single, adaptive RL policy capable of zero-shot deployment across diverse ASV platforms, extending similar generalization objectives from other robotic domains to the marine context. The authors propose a structured adaptation mechanism using a teacher-student architecture to infer relevant dynamics information from interaction history, contrasting with purely recurrent or explicit parameter identification methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical challenges associated with deploying autonomous surface vehicles (ASVs) across a varied range of platforms. The authors identify a "per-platform bottleneck" inherent in conventional ASV control strategies. Given the wide spectrum of hydrodynamic and actuation characteristics among different ASVs, existing control methods typically require either:
1. **Platform-specific system identification:** Developing an accurate dynamics model for each individual vessel.
2. **Platform-specific controller tuning:** Calibrating controller parameters to match the identified dynamics of a particular ASV.
3. **High-fidelity simulation:** For reinforcement learning approaches, relying on a simulator that precisely captures the target platform's dynamics.

This dependency on specific platform knowledge or tuning leads to significant engineering effort and time investment whenever a new ASV type is introduced or when managing a fleet of heterogeneous vessels. This bottleneck limits the scalability and flexibility of ASV operations in emerging applications, which often involve diverse vehicle types operating in dynamic environments.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work are to develop and validate a control framework that:
1. **Enables zero-shot cross-platform deployment:** The goal is a single, universal policy that can be deployed on a new ASV platform without any prior knowledge of its specific dynamics, system identification, or fine-tuning. This directly addresses the per-platform bottleneck.
2. **Utilizes adaptive learning:** The policy must adapt to the unknown dynamics of the deployment platform by inferring relevant characteristics from its real-time interaction history. This is formulated as a partial observability problem.
3. **Achieves robust trajectory tracking:** The adaptive policy should maintain high accuracy in trajectory tracking across different platforms, approaching or exceeding the performance of non-adaptive baselines and ideally competing with platform-specific tuned controllers.
4. **Employs a lightweight training approach:** The authors aim to train the policy using a simple, analytical 3-degree-of-freedom (3-DoF) dynamics model, rather than complex, high-fidelity hydrodynamic simulators. This approach is motivated by its practical advantages, including faster training, easier dynamics randomization, and reduced computational resource requirements.
5. **Validates in real-world scenarios:** The ultimate objective is to demonstrate the practical viability and performance of the proposed adaptive control framework through real-world experiments on physical ASV platforms with varying characteristics.

In essence, the research seeks to create a more general, efficient, and adaptable control solution for ASVs that can scale across diverse hardware, thereby facilitating broader adoption and reducing operational overhead.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes an adaptive reinforcement learning approach utilizing a teacher–student architecture for cross-platform control of ASVs. The core idea is to train a single policy capable of trajectory tracking on diverse platforms whose specific dynamics are unknown at deployment.

**Problem Formulation:**
The problem of trajectory tracking is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP). The true Markov state comprises the observable system state (pose and body-frame velocities) and the unobservable vessel physical parameters (θ). Since θ is unknown at deployment, the policy must infer it from interaction history to restore approximate Markovianity.

**System Dynamics Model:**
A three-degree-of-freedom (3-DoF) horizontal-plane model, based on the standard Fossen formulation, is used for simulation and training.
- **Kinematics:** The vessel pose η=(x, y, ψ) and body-frame velocity ν=(u, v, r) are related by ˙η = J(ψ)ν.
- **Dynamics:** The equations of motion are M ˙ν + N(ν_r)ν_r = τ + τ_ext, where τ is the control forces/torque, τ_ext are disturbances, and ν_r is velocity relative to water current.
- **Vessel Parameters (θ):** Each vessel is characterized by seven parameters: mass (m), combined yaw inertia (I_comb), added-mass coefficients (X_u_dot, Y_v_dot), and linear damping coefficients (X_u, Y_v, N_r).

**Proposed Cross-Platform Control Recipe:**
1. **Normalized Body-Frame Forces:** The policy outputs normalized body-frame force and torque commands (¯τ ∈ [−1, 1]^3), which are then scaled by platform-specific actuator limits (F_max_x, F_max_y, M_max_z). This decouples the policy from absolute force magnitudes. These commands are subsequently mapped to individual actuators via a platform-specific allocation scheme.
2. **Randomized Training with Lightweight Model:** The policy is trained in simulation using the 3-DoF analytical dynamics model, with vessel parameters randomized over wide ranges. This lightweight model facilitates rapid training through massive parallelization and straightforward randomization of physically interpretable parameters, without requiring a high-fidelity hydrodynamic simulator.
3. **Dynamics Inference from Interaction History:** The policy is equipped with a mechanism to infer unknown dynamics from past observations and actions during deployment.

**Teacher–Student Architecture:**
A two-phase training approach, inspired by privileged learning, is employed:

- **Phase 1 (Teacher Policy Training):**
 - **Privileged Information (e):** A 9-dimensional vector `e` is constructed from the normalized dynamics coefficients (peak accelerations, damping rates, Coriolis coefficients) of the 3-DoF model. This vector explicitly encodes the platform dynamics.
 - **Encoder (μ):** An encoder network maps the privileged vector `e` to a latent representation `z` (d_z = 9, matching d_e to avoid bottlenecks).
 - **Policy (Actor-Critic):** The actor and critic networks receive the base observation `o_t` concatenated with the latent `z`.
 - **Training:** The teacher policy and encoder are jointly trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

- **Phase 2 (Student Policy Training - Adaptation Module):**
 - **Adapter (ϕ):** The encoder μ is replaced by an adapter ϕ, which is a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) network.
 - **History Feature (h_t):** The adapter estimates the latent `z` (denoted ˆz_t) from a per-step history feature `h_t`, which includes recent body-frame state differences, velocities, and previous normalized policy outputs.
 - **Policy (Frozen Actor):** The actor weights from Phase 1 are frozen.
 - **Training:** The adapter is trained via supervised learning to minimize the squared difference between its estimate ˆz_t and the true latent `z` provided by the teacher. The adapter is trained on rollouts where the actor conditions on its own predictions (ˆz_t) to ensure robustness to the induced state distribution.

**Observation and Action Spaces:**
- **Observation (o_t):** Concatenates base features (body-frame errors, velocities), N future reference waypoints (lookahead), and the previous action.
- **History Feature (h_t):** Includes body-frame state differences, velocities, and previous normalized policy outputs.
- **Action (¯τ):** Normalized body-frame force and torque commands.

**Reward Function:**
The reward function includes bounded terms for position, heading, and velocity tracking errors. Auxiliary rewards comprise a proximity bonus, forward velocity bonus, and quadratic penalties for lateral drift, excessive yaw rate, action magnitude, and action smoothness. A curriculum learning approach is used, progressively expanding dynamics randomization ranges, trajectory complexity, and initial state perturbations.

**Disturbance and Actuator Modeling:**
Environmental disturbances (water currents, wind, waves) are modeled as Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes. Actuator characteristics are approximated by a randomized pure time delay.

**Experimental Setup and Baselines:**
Performance is evaluated against several controllers:
- **MPC:** A platform-specific, tuned Model Predictive Controller.
- **PPO (specific):** A PPO policy trained and deployed on a single platform with domain randomization around its dynamics.
- **PPO (general):** A PPO policy trained on the full dynamics range for cross-platform deployment but without any adaptation mechanism.
- **Student:** The proposed adaptive teacher-student policy.
- **Teacher:** The oracle Phase 1 policy with direct access to privileged dynamics (used as an upper bound in simulation).
- **Recurrent:** An end-to-end recurrent baseline (GRU-based) trained with sequence-aware PPO, without explicit dynamics supervision.
- **Student (avg. z):** An ablation where the student's adapter output is replaced by the training-set mean latent, isolating the online adaptation component.

Real-world experiments are conducted on two physical ASV platforms (Platform A and Platform B) with distinct dynamics, while simulation studies encompass five diverse platforms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both real-world and simulation-based evidence to evaluate the performance of the proposed adaptive control framework.

**Real-World Experiments (Platform A and Platform B):**
Two physical ASV platforms with different dynamics and actuation were used for zero-shot deployment without any fine-tuning. The validation trajectory involved a rectangular path with rounded corners. Tracking accuracy was measured using RTK GPS and IMU data.
- **Platform A (22 kg):**
 - The Student policy achieved the lowest position mean absolute error (MAE) at 4.0 cm. This was an improvement over the platform-specific MPC (4.6 cm), PPO (specific) (5.5 cm), and PPO (general) (5.6 cm).
 - For heading MAE, the MPC achieved the lowest error (1.9°), followed by the Student (2.5°) and PPO (general) (2.6°). PPO (specific) had a higher heading error (4.0°).
 - Relative to the non-adaptive PPO (general) baseline, the Student reduced position error by 29%.
- **Platform B (5 kg):**
 - The Student policy matched the MPC in position MAE (4.0 cm vs. 4.1 cm) and exhibited a lower heading error (2.0° vs. 2.9°).
 - Compared to PPO (general), the Student reduced position error by 58% (4.0 cm vs. 9.5 cm), while heading error remained comparable (2.0° vs. 2.2°).
- **Overall Real-World Observations:** The Student policy consistently reduced position error compared to the non-adaptive PPO (general) across both platforms. It achieved similar position accuracy on both platforms despite their dynamic differences, suggesting effective adaptation. The larger performance gain on Platform B was attributed to its higher thrust-to-weight ratio, which amplifies the impact of suboptimal commands. Notably, PPO (specific), despite being trained with domain randomization around a specific platform, did not outperform the adaptive Student policy, indicating that adaptation is crucial for handling sim-to-real discrepancies.

**Simulation Experiments (Five Platforms):**
A simulation study was conducted across five different ASV platforms (including Roboat 1, 2, and 3) under ideal conditions (no delay, no disturbances) to characterize nominal tracking ability.
- **MPC (platform-specific):** Achieved the lowest errors (0.33 cm position, 0.07° heading), as expected with a perfect model of simulated dynamics.
- **PPO (specific):** Performed well (0.65 cm position, 0.97° heading) due to being trained and validated under matching conditions.
- **Student:** Outperformed non-adaptive baselines, achieving 0.84 cm position and 1.81° heading errors. This represented a 44% reduction in position error and a 55% reduction in heading error compared to PPO (general).
- **Teacher:** The oracle policy showed similar performance to the Student (0.85 cm position, 1.79° heading), indicating that the Student's adaptation module effectively approximated the privileged dynamics information.
- **Recurrent:** Achieved 0.96 cm position and 2.34° heading errors, demonstrating improvement over PPO (general) but lagging behind the Student.
- **PPO (general):** Showed the highest errors among generalist policies (1.49 cm position, 3.98° heading).
- **Student (avg. z) Ablation:** Replacing the adapter's output with the training-set mean latent significantly increased tracking errors (2.18 cm position, 4.06° heading), confirming the critical contribution of the online adaptation module.
- **Latent Space Analysis:** A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the learned latent representation (z) revealed that the first two principal components captured 91% of the variance. This indicated that the nine-dimensional latent effectively operated within a low-dimensional subspace, capturing the task-relevant dynamics variation.
- **Adaptation Illustration (Appendix):** An illustrative example with a one-dimensional dynamics sweep demonstrated that the teacher's latent varied smoothly and monotonically with dynamics parameters. The student's inferred latent (ˆz_0) was shown to converge within approximately 1.0 second following an abrupt change in dynamics, illustrating the rapid online adaptation mechanism.

In summary, the adaptive reinforcement learning approach using a teacher-student architecture demonstrated superior performance compared to non-adaptive learning-based baselines in both real-world and simulated environments. It approached the accuracy of platform-specific tuned controllers and effectively generalized across platforms without requiring specific dynamics knowledge or retraining.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an adaptive reinforcement learning framework for ASV trajectory tracking that significantly advances the capability for cross-platform control. The development of a single, generalist policy that can be deployed zero-shot across diverse ASV platforms represents a notable contribution to robotic autonomy.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
1. **Elimination of Per-Platform Bottleneck:** The core contribution is overcoming the need for explicit system identification and per-platform controller tuning. This dramatically reduces the engineering effort required to deploy and manage heterogeneous fleets of ASVs.
2. **Zero-Shot Cross-Platform Deployment:** The adaptive policy's ability to operate effectively on previously unseen platforms without any retraining or prior dynamics knowledge is a critical feature, enhancing the scalability and flexibility of ASV operations.
3. **Performance Equivalence to Tuned Controllers:** In real-world experiments, the adaptive policy approached or matched the tracking accuracy of a platform-specific tuned Model Predictive Controller (MPC). This demonstrates its practical viability and competitive performance.
4. **Efficient Training Paradigm:** The reliance on a lightweight 3-DoF analytical dynamics model for training, rather than high-fidelity hydrodynamic simulators, enables rapid policy development. The entire teacher-student pipeline completed in approximately 30 minutes on a single consumer GPU, suggesting a cost-effective and time-efficient training process.
5. **Robustness to Sim-to-Real Gap:** The strong performance of the adaptive policy on real-world platforms, despite training solely in simulation, indicates its robustness to the inherent discrepancies between simulated and physical environments.
6. **Structured Adaptation Mechanism:** The teacher-student architecture provides an interpretable latent representation of dynamics, offering insights into what information the policy learns to adapt to. The ablation study confirms that online adaptation based on interaction history is crucial for its performance.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated ASV Deployment:** By streamlining the control system development process, this work could accelerate the deployment of ASVs in various applications, from environmental monitoring and infrastructure inspection to logistics and autonomous urban transport.
- **Enhanced Fleet Management:** It enables the operation of more diverse and flexible ASV fleets, where different vessels can perform specialized tasks without requiring individual control solutions.
- **Broader Accessibility of ASV Technology:** Reduced engineering complexity could make ASV technology more accessible to a wider range of researchers and industries.
- **Advancements in Adaptive Control:** The successful application of a teacher-student architecture for zero-shot adaptation in the marine domain could inspire similar approaches in other robotic systems facing high dynamics variability.

**Limitations:**
The study acknowledges certain limitations:
- The experiments were primarily conducted within a low-speed displacement regime. In this regime, the 3-DoF linear-damping model employed for training adequately captures dominant dynamics.
- The current adaptation mechanism has limited capacity to accommodate highly velocity-dependent effects like quadratic drag or more complex hydrodynamic phenomena. While some velocity-dependent effects might be approximated as effective damping within the randomized training range, more pronounced non-linearities or regimes such as planing, or strong heave, roll, and pitch coupling, are not explicitly addressed by the current model.

**Future Work:**
The authors propose several directions for future research:
- **Expanding Operating Envelope:** Broadening the control framework to accommodate more complex dynamics, including velocity-dependent effects, planing regimes, and coupled heave, roll, and pitch motions.
- **Online and Continual Adaptation:** Integrating online or continual adaptation mechanisms would allow the policy to progressively specialize into a platform-specific expert controller over extended operation, leveraging increasing amounts of operational data from deployed systems.
- **Integration with Model-Based Control:** Exploring tighter integration with model-based control methods. This could involve using learned modules to condition parameters and objectives online, thereby combining the benefits of constraint handling and interpretability from model-based approaches with the flexibility and adaptation capabilities of learning-based methods.
