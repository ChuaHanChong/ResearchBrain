# Beyond Fixed Goal Delivery: Online POMDP Planning for Target Interception in Crowds

> [!summary] Summary
> The research investigates the role of action-space structure in online tree search for dynamic target interception in crowded environments, formulating the problem as a POMDP. Experiments demonstrate that jointly planning steering and speed (ESP) significantly improves safe interception rates by up to 53% and reduces interception time by 26% compared to decoupled path and speed planning (LSP) in dense crowds.

## Problem

- Robotic target interception in crowded environments combines the challenges of reaching a moving objective and navigating safely among multiple uncertain human agents.
- Existing methods for interception assume static or deterministic environments, while crowd navigation research typically assumes fixed goals, failing to address dynamic interception in dense, uncertain crowds.
- It is unclear whether limiting action space to speed modulation along a pre-planned path (LSP) or allowing joint steering and speed optimization (ESP) is more effective for online planning in interception tasks under high crowd densities.

## Method

- The **Target Interception in Crowds (TIC)** problem is formulated as a ==Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)== to model human intent uncertainty.
- Online sampling-based tree search, specifically the ==ARDESPOT== algorithm, is employed for approximate POMDP solving using a ==receding horizon== approach.
- Two action-space structures are compared: ==Limited Space Planning (LSP)==, which decouples path generation from speed optimization, and **Extended Space Planning (ESP)**, which jointly optimizes steering and speed.

## Results

- At a density of 200 humans, ESP achieved a **97%** safe-success rate, substantially outperforming P-LSP (**66%**) and R-LSP (**44%**).
- ESP consistently required less time to intercept the target (e.g., **98** seconds vs. **114s** for P-LSP and **132s** for R-LSP at N_h=200 in a secondary study) and exhibited fewer sudden braking events.
- The benefits of ESP in success, safety, and motion efficiency persisted in scenarios where the target state was partially observable and estimated by a particle filter, demonstrating its robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action-space expressiveness, allowing joint control over both heading and speed, is crucial for effective online tree-search planning in dense, uncertain human environments.
> - Spatially constrained planning approaches (LSP) suffer pronounced performance degradation in safe interception rates and motion efficiency as crowd density increases due to limited maneuverability.
> - Greater spatial flexibility (ESP) enables the exploration of a richer set of trajectories, leading to improved success and safety under high crowd densities, even when operating on imperfect target estimates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the problem of robotic target interception in crowded environments, a scenario that combines two distinct challenges: reaching a moving objective and navigating safely among multiple uncertain human agents. Existing research has predominantly focused on these components in isolation.

Prior studies on target interception have largely assumed static environments or environments with known, deterministic dynamic obstacles. These approaches often involve predicting target trajectories, selecting an interception point, and then planning a feasible path to that point, followed by speed optimization. While effective in simplified settings, they do not account for the complexities introduced by dense, intention-driven crowd interactions.

Conversely, research in crowd navigation has explored various methods for reasoning over latent human intent uncertainty, often employing Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs). These methods have demonstrated improved performance through online planning or learned value approximations. However, this body of work typically assumes a known, fixed goal for the robot. The dynamic nature of a moving target, where the interception point is not static and evolves during execution, represents a key unaddressed complexity in this context.

Specifically, while some crowd navigation studies have investigated the impact of action-space structure in tree search for navigation to fixed goals, none have examined how the structural assumptions of decoupled path-and-speed planning (Limited Space Planning - LSP) compare against joint steering and speed optimization (Extended Space Planning - ESP) in the more challenging context of dynamic target interception in scaling crowd densities. This paper aims to bridge this gap by conducting a controlled comparison to understand the influence of action-space expressiveness on online tree-search planning for target interception in dense multi-agent settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the role of action-space structure in online tree search for dynamic target interception in crowded environments. The authors formulate the Target Interception in Crowds (TIC) problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) to account for the uncertainty arising from unobservable human navigation intent.

The motivation stems from the increasing deployment of autonomous robots in human-populated environments, such as delivery robots in hospitals or airports. Traditional robotic delivery systems typically require recipients to wait at fixed drop-off locations. A more efficient and user-centric approach involves the robot actively navigating to intercept a moving recipient. This "Target Interception in Crowds" presents a significant challenge because the robot must:
1. **Intercept a moving target:** Unlike fixed-goal navigation, the target's position is dynamic, requiring continuous adaptation of the robot's trajectory and timing.
2. **Navigate among uncertain human agents:** Human intent is not directly observable, necessitating probabilistic reasoning over their future movements and potential interactions.

Solving POMDPs exactly is computationally intractable, leading to reliance on approximate online planning methods, such as sampling-based tree search. For these methods, the structure of the action space directly influences the branching factor of the search tree and the set of reachable future states that can be explored under a fixed computational budget. This raises a critical question: how does the action-space structure impact the effectiveness of online tree-search planning for interception tasks in dense crowds?

Specifically, the paper investigates a "structural tension" within dynamic interception:
- **Path-first decomposition (LSP):** Common in navigation, this approach first plans a spatial path and then optimizes speed along it. This reduces the search space, potentially allowing deeper exploration of timing along a predicted interception path.
- **Joint steering and speed control (ESP):** This approach allows the robot to modify its spatial trajectory and speed simultaneously during search, expanding the reachable state space and potentially preserving more maneuvering options.

It is unclear whether deeper search along a restricted path or greater spatial flexibility is more advantageous when crowd-induced delays tightly couple timing and spatial decisions in interception tasks. The research aims to clarify this by conducting a controlled comparison between LSP and ESP under identical sensing, belief estimation, reward structures, and computational budgets, thereby isolating the impact of spatial restriction versus flexibility. An additional objective is to assess whether the identified structural advantages persist when the target state itself is partially observable and estimated using a particle filter.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates the Target Interception in Crowds (TIC) problem as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) and solves it using online sampling-based tree search.

**A. POMDP Formulation**
- **State Space (s):** Comprises the robot state (s_R), target state (s_T), and the states of N nearby humans ({s_i_H}). Each human state includes position, motion variables, and an unobservable intent variable (g_i), which induces partial observability.
- **Action Space (A):** Consists of control inputs (steering angle and speed update) admissible under the robot dynamics, plus a sudden braking action.
- **Observation Space (Z):** The robot observes its own state, the target state, and positions of nearby humans without noise. This idealization isolates the impact of latent intent uncertainty.
- **Reward Function (R):** Designed to balance interception success, safety, and efficiency. It includes a positive terminal reward (R_I) for successful interception, negative penalties for collisions with environment boundaries (R_obs) or human proximity violations (R_h) while moving, a penalty for sudden braking (R_SB), and a constant per-step penalty (R_step) to encourage faster interception.
- **Generative Model (G(s, a)):** A simulator-based model that provides sampled next states, observations, and rewards. Robot and target dynamics are deterministic during planning, while human motion is stochastic with bounded process noise and intent-conditioned.

**B. Belief Representation**
The robot maintains a categorical distribution (b_i(g)) over discrete goal hypotheses (G) for each human, representing their intent. Beliefs are initialized uniformly and updated via Bayes' rule, with likelihoods derived from human motion models predicting movement towards each goal. Robot and target states are assumed directly observable (in the primary study) and do not require belief representation.

**C. Online Tree Search**
The ARDESPOT algorithm, implemented in POMDPs.jl, is used for online replanning.
- **Receding Horizon:** Operates in a loop with a decision period of ∆t = 0.5 s, allowing planning to overlap with execution.
- **Scenario Sampling:** At each planning cycle, K = 50 scenarios are sampled from the current belief. For each of the N = 6 nearest humans, intent is sampled from its categorical belief. The target state for all scenarios is initialized to the current observed or estimated target state.
- **State Propagation:** Robot and target dynamics are propagated deterministically during tree search, while human motion is stochastic.
- **Observation Discretization:** Continuous positions are discretized at 2m spatial resolution for ARDESPOT.
- **Bounds:** Lower bounds are obtained from rollout policies. Upper bounds are optimistic, assuming maximum speed travel to the target without human interference, or set to the collision penalty if a human safety distance is violated.

**D. Action-Space Formulations**
The core of the methodology lies in comparing two distinct ways of structuring the robot's control authority within tree search, impacting the branching factor and explored trajectories:

1. **Limited Space Planning (LSP):** Adopts a decoupled approach.
 - **Path Generation:** At each planning cycle, a Hybrid A* algorithm computes a dynamically feasible path from the robot's current state to a target state (current or predicted). Human uncertainty is incorporated conservatively by inflating safety margins during path generation.
 - **Tree Search:** ARDESPOT then performs tree search *only* to select speed updates (δ_v ∈ {−0.5, 0, 0.5} m/s) along this precomputed path. Heading adjustments are fixed by the path.
 - **Variants:**
 - **Reactive LSP (R-LSP):** Plans paths to the *current* target state.
 - **Predictive LSP (P-LSP):** Accounts for target motion by propagating the target state forward during path generation, planning to a *predicted future* target position.

2. **Extended Space Planning (ESP):** Eliminates the fixed reference path constraint.
 - **Joint Optimization:** ARDESPOT directly selects dynamically feasible controls that determine both heading (discretized steering angles like {−ϕ_m, −2ϕ_m/3..., ϕ_m}) and speed updates (δ_v ∈ {−0.5, 0, 0.5} m/s). To manage branching, velocity updates are only permitted when steering angle ϕ = 0.

**E. Rollout Policies**
Effective rollout policies are crucial for evaluating leaf nodes and guiding tree expansion in the sparse reward environment of TIC.
- **LSP Rollout:** Employs a conservative reactive controller to modulate speed along the precomputed path: increases speed if no humans are within D_far, decreases if any human is within D_near, else maintains speed.
- **ESP Rollout:** Uses the same reactive speed controller as LSP. For steering, at each rollout step, it selects the steering command from its action set that brings the robot closest to the propagated target state, actively reducing the distance to the target. This ensures an interception-oriented behavior without a reference path.

**F. Experimental Protocol**
- **Simulation Environment:** 50m x 50m workspace, no static obstacles.
- **Robot Model:** Kinematic bicycle model (max speed 2 m/s, max steering 0.475 rad).
- **Target Model:** Constant speed and turn-rate model, smoothly redirected at boundaries.
- **Human Model:** Goal-directed, constant speed motion with bounded additive displacement noise.
- **Trial Setup:** 500 trials per planner and crowd size (N_h ∈ {50, 100, 150, 200} humans). All planners are evaluated under identical initializations and motion realizations.
- **Crowd Dynamics:** Humans are initialized randomly with goals at workspace corners; upon reaching a goal, a human is replaced to maintain constant density.
- **Termination:** Successful interception (robot within 1m of target), timeout (300 s), or collision with boundary. Proximity violations (robot within 1m of human while moving) are recorded but do not terminate trials.

**G. Secondary Study (Estimator-Planner Setting)**
To evaluate robustness to imperfect information, a secondary study introduces partial observability of the target.
- **Target Sensing:** A forward-facing detector (15m range, 180° FOV) provides intermittent target position measurements with a 0.1 probability of missed detection.
- **Target Estimation:** A Sequential Importance Resampling (SIR) particle filter (1000 particles) maintains a belief over the target state.
- **Planning Input:** The planner receives the maximum a posteriori (MAP) particle as the single target-state hypothesis for planning. Uncertainty in the target state is handled externally, not within the POMDP tree search.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the performance of the three planners (R-LSP, P-LSP, ESP) across increasing crowd densities in both fully observable and partially observable target settings.

**A. Interception Performance (Success and Safety)**
- **Success Rate:** In the primary study (fully observable target), all planners performed comparably at low crowd density (N_h = 50). However, the performance gap widened significantly with increasing density. At N_h = 200, ESP achieved a 98% success rate, P-LSP 96%, and R-LSP dropped to 73%.
- **Safe-Success Rate:** This metric, defined as successful interception without any human proximity violations, showed a more pronounced divergence. At N_h = 200, ESP maintained a 97% safe-success rate, while P-LSP achieved 66%, and R-LSP only 44%. This indicates a substantial decrease in safety for LSP variants in dense crowds.
- **Secondary Study Impact:** In the secondary study (estimator-planner with intermittent target observations), all planners exhibited decreased success rates due to estimation uncertainty. Nevertheless, the relative performance trend persisted: ESP maintained approximately 90% success, while P-LSP dropped to 83% and R-LSP to 69% at N_h = 200. Similar relative degradation in safe-success rates was observed, with ESP outperforming LSP variants.
- **Interpretation:** The results suggest that the limited action space of LSP, which primarily relies on speed modulation, constrains the robot's ability to identify collision-free paths in dense interactions. The spatial flexibility offered by ESP, allowing joint optimization of steering and speed, enables the exploration of a richer set of trajectories, improving both success and safety under high crowd densities. Predictive path planning (P-LSP) offered some improvement over reactive (R-LSP) but did not close the gap with ESP.

**B. Motion Characteristics**
Analysis of motion statistics for successful trials further elucidated the performance differences:
- **Time to Interception:** ESP consistently required less time to intercept the target than both LSP variants. In the secondary study at N_h = 200, ESP averaged 98 s, compared to 114 s for P-LSP and 132 s for R-LSP.
- **Idle Time & Average Speed:** ESP exhibited lower idle time and higher average speed compared to LSP variants. This indicates that steering-restricted planners experienced increased hesitation and slower progress in dense environments.
- **Sudden Braking Events:** ESP recorded fewer sudden braking events than LSP variants, suggesting smoother and less reactive navigation.
- **Distance Covered:** ESP traveled a greater distance than P-LSP, yet achieved shorter interception times. This implies that ESP engaged in more active maneuvering and maintained consistent forward progress, avoiding the stalling or repeated corrections observed in LSP variants.

**C. Summary of Findings:**
The findings consistently demonstrate that performing online tree search over both heading and speed (ESP) significantly improves interception reliability, safety, and motion efficiency in dense human interactions. Restricting the robot's heading choices during search (LSP) leads to a pronounced performance degradation as human density increases. These benefits of ESP extend to scenarios where planning must operate on imperfect target estimates, demonstrating its robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous robot navigation and human-robot interaction by providing an in-depth analysis of the impact of action-space structure on online POMDP planning for dynamic target interception in crowded environments. The work highlights a structural limitation of spatially constrained planning approaches in complex, multi-agent scenarios.

The primary significance lies in the demonstration that **action-space expressiveness** (i.e., the ability to jointly plan for both steering and speed) is crucial for effective online tree-search planning in dense, uncertain environments. The controlled comparison between Limited Space Planning (LSP) and Extended Space Planning (ESP) under identical computational budgets and other conditions allows for a clear isolation of this factor. The results indicate that LSP, which first plans a spatial path and then modulates speed, suffers pronounced degradation in safe interception rates and motion efficiency as crowd density increases. In contrast, ESP, which explores both steering and speed concurrently within the tree search, maintains higher success rates, significantly improves safety (fewer proximity violations), reduces reactive braking, and achieves more efficient interception times.

These findings have several potential impacts:
1. **Advancement in Robot Navigation:** The study provides design principles for planning algorithms in dynamic and uncertain human-populated environments. It suggests that allowing robots more flexibility in their action choices during decision-making, rather than restricting them to precomputed paths, can lead to more robust and safer behavior, particularly in high-density crowd scenarios.
2. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** By enabling safer and more efficient interception in crowds, this research can contribute to the development of more socially intelligent robots capable of seamless interaction with humans. This could enhance the utility and acceptance of robots in public and service settings.
3. **Applications in Service Robotics:** The "Target Interception in Crowds" problem is directly relevant to emerging applications such as delivery robots in hospitals, airports, or other dynamic public spaces, where robots need to deliver items directly to moving individuals. The insights gained can inform the design of more effective navigation systems for these applications.
4. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a clear benchmark and methodology for studying action-space effects in online POMDP planning. It also points to future research directions, such as extending the framework to environments with static obstacles (requiring collision-aware and interception-driven rollout policies for ESP) and exploring efficient parallelized motion-planning methods to generate these policies within online planning budgets. The secondary study also highlights the importance of robust target estimation in integrated estimator-planner architectures.

In summary, this research underscores the importance of considering the coupling between spatial and temporal decisions in dynamic interception tasks and provides evidence for the benefits of flexible, joint action-space planning when navigating dense, uncertain human crowds.
