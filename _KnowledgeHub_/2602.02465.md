# MentisOculi: Revealing the Limits of Reasoning with Mental Imagery

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce MENTISOCULI, a benchmark with five procedurally generated visual reasoning tasks to evaluate how frontier multimodal models leverage self-generated visual information. The study reveals that current models struggle with consistent visual generation and interpretation of these images, often failing to effectively use visual aids for multi-step reasoning despite possessing underlying textual reasoning capabilities.

## Problem

- The effectiveness of "machine mental imagery" for reasoning in multimodal models remained ambiguous, with inconsistent results in existing literature.
- It was unclear if failures in using visual aids stemmed from fundamental reasoning deficits, poor image generation quality, or an inability to interpret self-generated visual cues.
- A rigorous, standardized evaluation framework was lacking to comprehensively assess and disentangle these potential failure modes across different model architectures.

## Method

- Developed ==MENTISOCULI==, a novel benchmark comprising ==five multi-step visual reasoning tasks== designed with high information density, sequential manipulation, and procedural generation.
- The benchmark includes tasks like FORM BOARD, HINGE FOLDING, PAPER FOLD, RUSH HOUR, and SLIDING PUZZLE, each with five stratified difficulty levels.
- Evaluated a spectrum of models including MLLMs, latent visual reasoning models, Unified Multimodal Models (UMMs), and video models, using automated scoring, human reference data, and ablation studies to isolate failure sources.

## Results

- MENTISOCULI tasks consistently challenge state-of-the-art models, with performance degrading to or below chance levels by Level **5** for most models across all tasks.
- Providing UMMs with ground-truth intermediate images did not consistently enable them to reach human performance or fully leverage the correct visual information for decision-making on planning tasks.
- Common reasoning enhancements (in-context learning, prompt optimization, increased reasoning budget, tool use) failed to provide systematic or reliable performance gains on MENTISOCULI tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit visual intervention strategies, such as interleaved generated images from UMMs or latent visual tokens, do not consistently improve or even match text-only MLLM performance in visual reasoning.
> - Frontier MLLMs demonstrate strong underlying reasoning competence when provided with verbose symbolic textual descriptions of visual tasks, indicating that core reasoning capacity exists.
> - UMMs exhibit pervasive issues with visual generation consistency (e.g., object identity, geometry maintenance, hallucinations) and struggle to effectively interpret even oracle (ground-truth) visual aids for planning-intensive tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a developing area within artificial intelligence, specifically the transition of frontier models from multimodal large language models (MLLMs) that primarily process visual information as input to unified multimodal models (UMMs) capable of native, interleaved generation across modalities such as text, images, video, and audio. This evolution has prompted an investigation into the potential of intermediate visualizations as a reasoning aid, drawing an analogy to human mental imagery.

Prior research explores various manifestations of machine mental imagery. Some approaches suggest that large language models (LLMs) can solve pictorial tasks using only internal representations, though the robustness of these "mental visualizations" has been questioned. Other methods involve more explicit visual aids, ranging from latent visual tokens to generated images within UMMs, which have demonstrated inconsistent performance gains, particularly in multi-step reasoning scenarios. On the natively visual end of the spectrum, certain image editing and video models have shown an ability to solve reasoning tasks by directly manipulating pixel data.

The current research landscape is characterized by ambiguity regarding the effectiveness of machine mental imagery for reasoning. A key challenge is distinguishing whether observed failures arise from fundamental reasoning deficits, inaccuracies in image generation, or a model's inability to interpret its self-generated visual cues. The field previously lacked a comprehensive and rigorous framework to systematically evaluate and isolate these factors across diverse model architectures. This paper introduces MENTISOCULI as such a framework, designed to scrutinize models' capacities for forming, maintaining, and manipulating visual representations in a goal-oriented manner. The benchmark differentiates itself from existing evaluations that often focus on reasoning *about* visual information rather than actively reasoning *with* it, by adhering to a strict set of desiderata for tasks amenable to visual solution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to thoroughly investigate the capabilities of frontier models in performing multi-step reasoning tasks that are intrinsically solvable through mental imagery. This involves evaluating their ability to generate, maintain, and interact with internal visual representations in a goal-directed fashion.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations within the evolving landscape of artificial intelligence:
1. **Advancement of Multimodal Models:** The emergence of unified multimodal models (UMMs) capable of generating both text and visual content has opened new avenues for multimodal reasoning. These models can actively produce interleaved visual outputs, which contrasts with earlier MLLMs that primarily consumed visual inputs.
2. **Analogy to Human Cognition:** Human problem-solving frequently involves mental imagery—the ability to visualize and manipulate quasi-sensory experiences internally. Researchers hypothesize that providing models with an analogous capacity for visual reasoning might enhance their ability to tackle complex tasks, especially those involving spatial relationships, geometric constraints, physical interactions, or object dynamics, which are often difficult to fully capture in textual descriptions alone.
3. **Inconsistent Prior Results:** Despite the intuitive appeal of visual reasoning, previous attempts to integrate generated visuals into model reasoning pipelines have yielded ambiguous and inconsistent performance improvements. It has remained unclear whether limitations stemmed from fundamental reasoning deficiencies, errors in the visual generation process, or an inability of models to effectively interpret their own generated visual cues.
4. **Absence of a Rigorous Evaluation Framework:** A significant gap existed in the research landscape for a benchmark specifically designed to rigorously probe multi-step visual reasoning. Existing benchmarks often failed to meet criteria such as ensuring the inherently visual nature of intermediate steps, requiring high information density to prevent textual shortcuts, demanding sequential manipulation, offering procedural generation with ground-truth visual solutions, supporting stratified difficulty levels, and respecting generative feasibility constraints.

To address these motivations, the study set out to achieve specific objectives:
1. **Develop MENTISOCULI:** Construct a procedural and stratified benchmark suite of five multi-step visual reasoning problems specifically crafted to challenge frontier models. This benchmark is designed to necessitate the formation, maintenance, and manipulation of visual representations for successful solution.
2. **Analyze Diverse Model Families:** Conduct a comprehensive evaluation of various model families—including MLLMs, latent visual reasoning models, UMMs, and video models—to assess their performance across the spectrum of machine mental imagery explicitness.
3. **Identify Failure Mechanisms:** Investigate the specific reasons behind the observed limitations of UMM visual reasoning, particularly focusing on distinguishing between errors in generating coherent visual states and failures in interpreting or leveraging even correct visual aids.
4. **Provide Human Reference Data:** Collect human performance data on a subset of tasks to establish an upper bound for reasoning capabilities and to highlight differences in reasoning strategies and effort allocation between humans and artificial models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers developed MENTISOCULI, a benchmark consisting of five procedurally generated, multi-step visual reasoning tasks. Each task is designed to be difficult to textualize but intuitive for humans to solve visually, with five stratified difficulty levels (1-5) based on the minimum number of steps required. Thirty samples were generated per level for the initial benchmark release, and the code for generating more instances is provided.

The design of MENTISOCULI adhered to six desiderata:
1. **Visual Nature:** Tasks emphasize spatial relations, geometric constraints, and object transformations, minimizing reliance on common knowledge or symbolic logic.
2. **High Information Density:** Problems avoid grid-based or easily text-transcribed layouts, instead featuring complex shapes, continuous transformations, and fine-grained visual details.
3. **Sequential Manipulation:** Tasks require successive updates to a visual state, where subsequent actions depend on prior manipulations, allowing for discrete solution sequences.
4. **Procedural Generation:** Tasks are easily generated, include ground-truth visual chain-of-thought solutions for granular analysis, and mitigate data contamination risks.
5. **Stratified Difficulty:** A clear mechanism exists to control complexity, enabling the identification of model breaking points and future benchmark extensions.
6. **Generative Feasibility:** Visual states are representable in 2D projections, avoiding ambiguous depth cues or occlusions, and details remain legible at standard resolutions.

The five tasks are:
- **Form Board:** Requires comparing shapes, understanding spatial constraints, and maintaining geometry under translation to identify a subset of shapes that fill a target silhouette without gaps or overlaps.
- **Hinge Folding:** Involves comparing shapes, mental rotation, and object dependencies to predict discrete rotation angles for hinges in a polygon chain to match a target silhouette.
- **Paper Fold:** Demands maintaining spatial locations under reflection symmetry to identify the unfolded pattern of holes after a sequence of folds and a punch.
- **Rush Hour:** A multi-step planning task under dynamic geometric constraints, where models navigate a red car out of a crowded lot. Vehicles are non-axis-aligned with continuous positions, but actions are discrete.
- **Sliding Puzzle:** Evaluates multi-step planning and visual coherence by requiring a sequence of moves to restore a scrambled natural image on a grid.

**Model Families Evaluated:**
The study benchmarked models across a spectrum of visual reasoning explicitnes:
- **Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs):** Gemini 2.5 (Flash), Gemini 3 (Pro), GPT-5.1, and Qwen3-VL. These models produce text-only outputs, with visual information handled implicitly or through textual proxies like ASCII art.
- **Latent Visual Reasoning Models:** Qwen2.5-VL-32B, fine-tuned on RUSH HOUR using the Mirage and LatentSketchpad frameworks. These models interleave text reasoning chains with visually grounded latent representations.
- **Unified Multimodal Models (UMMs):** Gemini 2.5-I (Flash Image), Gemini 3-I (Pro Image), and Emu 3.5. These models are designed to explicitly generate and interleave images within their reasoning chains. Evaluation focused only on samples where visual generation occurred.
- **Video Models:** Veo 3.1 and Wan 2.6. These models operate purely in pixel space, producing visual rollouts conditioned on a prompt and an initial frame.

**Evaluation Procedures:**
- **Automated Scoring (Text Outputs):** For MLLMs, UMMs, and latent models, text outputs were scored. FORM BOARD and PAPER FOLD required exact matches. HINGE FOLDING and SLIDING PUZZLE involved parsing action sequences and simulating them; correctness was based on the simulated terminal state. RUSH HOUR allowed any trace containing one correct final state, with invalid identifiers or moves scored as incorrect.
- **Automated Scoring (Visual Outputs):** For RUSH HOUR, a video auto-rater processed video model outputs frame-by-frame to recover object identities and trajectories. Implied action sequences were extracted via a lenient heuristic, and parsed actions were verified by simulation. Large scene changes or spurious objects invalidated samples.
- **Human Reference Data:** A psychophysical experiment was conducted on RUSH HOUR with five PhD students. Participants were instructed for speed, with response time serving as a proxy for perceived difficulty, to establish an upper bound for human performance.
- **Chance Performance:** Calculated based on a uniform distribution over possible answers for some tasks, and the probability of a random six-step action sequence reaching the goal for planning tasks.

**Additional Analyses:**
- **Symbolic vs. Sensory Reasoning:** RUSH HOUR was presented to MLLMs in a verbose, lossless text transcription of the simulator state to assess reasoning competence independent of visual understanding.
- **Oracle Visual Chain of Thought:** UMMs were tested with ground-truth visual aids replacing their self-generated imagery to isolate performance limitations due to generation errors versus interpretation errors.
- **Coupling of Image and Text Actions:** For UMMs on RUSH HOUR, the consistency between actions proposed in text and actions depicted in generated images was analyzed using the video auto-rater.
- **Reasoning Enhancements:** The impact of in-context learning, prompt optimization, increased reasoning budget (token usage), and tool use on RUSH HOUR performance was investigated.
- **Human-Machine Comparison:** Model performance was mapped to human response times, and the adaptive reasoning effort (token usage vs. human response time) was compared.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of models on MENTISOCULI yielded several findings regarding the current state of visual reasoning in frontier models:

**Benchmark Challenge and Model Performance:**
- **Unsaturated Benchmark:** MENTISOCULI proved challenging, with model performance degrading significantly as difficulty increased. By Level 5, all evaluated models typically performed at or below chance levels, indicating that the benchmark is far from saturated.
- **UMMs Underperform MLLMs:** Contrary to the expectation that explicit visual generation would aid reasoning, Unified Multimodal Models (UMMs) with interleaved images often performed below their text-only Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) counterparts. This suggests that self-generated imagery did not provide a consistent benefit.
- **Latent Reasoning Limitations:** While fine-tuned latent visual reasoning models (Mirage, LatentSketchpad) showed some improvement over base MLLMs on easier levels of RUSH HOUR, this advantage diminished rapidly at higher difficulties, eventually performing similarly to or worse than MLLMs. Text-only fine-tuning of these models often matched or exceeded the performance of their latent-visual counterparts, suggesting limited gains from latent visual tokens at the evaluated scales.
- **Video Model Ineffectiveness:** Video models (Veo 3.1, Wan 2.6) consistently performed at or below chance across all difficulty levels, despite operating purely in pixel space.

**Analysis of UMM Failure Modes:**
- **Competence vs. Bottleneck:** MLLMs (Gemini 3, GPT-5.1) demonstrated the underlying textual reasoning capacity to solve RUSH HOUR when provided with a detailed, verbose text transcription of the problem state. This performance was comparable to humans, suggesting that the primary bottleneck for visual tasks lies in visual understanding and manipulation rather than fundamental reasoning capacity.
- **Dual Problem: Generation and Interpretation Errors:**
 - **Generation Errors:** When UMMs were provided with *oracle visualizations* (ground-truth intermediate images), their performance on all tasks increased significantly. This indicates that pervasive errors in generating accurate and consistent visual states contribute substantially to performance degradation.
 - **Interpretation Errors:** Even with oracle visuals, UMMs often failed to fully leverage these correct visual aids to improve their reasoning. On some tasks (e.g., Hinge Folding, Paper Fold), oracle visuals only helped models reach the performance of their underlying MLLM, not surpass it, highlighting a deeper inability to interpret visual states as actionable evidence for decision-making.
- **Weak Coupling of Image and Text Actions:** For UMMs, the actions inferred from generated images and those derived from textual reasoning were often inconsistent. Even on the easiest RUSH HOUR puzzles, approximately half of the successfully solved instances were attributed to only one modality. As difficulty increased, the text channel typically carried the multi-step planning, while the image channel struggled. Stronger base models did not inherently resolve this coupling failure.

**Limits of Common Reasoning Enhancements:**
- Standard techniques commonly used to enhance language-based reasoning, such as in-context learning, prompt optimization, increased reasoning budget (token usage), and tool use, did not yield consistent or systematic performance gains for visual reasoning tasks, particularly at higher difficulty levels. For instance, significantly increasing the token budget for GPT-5.1 and Gemini 3 did not translate to improved accuracy.

**Human-Machine Comparisons:**
- **Performance Comparison:** Gemini 3's performance aligned with human performance when humans were constrained to a response time of 5 to 10 seconds. However, models generally lagged far behind unconstrained human performance.
- **Adaptive Reasoning Effort:** Humans adaptively allocate more time (and implicitly, effort) to solve harder puzzles. In contrast, models like Gemini 3 did not demonstrate a corresponding increase in token usage from Level 3 to Level 5, suggesting a lack of adaptive reasoning depth in response to increasing visual-spatial complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of advanced artificial intelligence systems by revealing the current limitations of frontier models in multi-step visual reasoning with mental imagery. The primary significance of the work lies in challenging the prevailing intuition that explicit visual thought, through self-generated images or videos, inherently benefits machine reasoning. The findings suggest that, at present, multimodal models and video models do not consistently outperform text-only reasoning approaches on the types of complex visual-spatial problems introduced by MENTISOCULI.

The study's detailed analysis provides a critical distinction between different failure modes:
- **Generation Errors:** The ubiquitous presence of errors in self-generated visual outputs significantly hinders model performance. This indicates a need for advancements in image and video generation fidelity and consistency across sequential steps.
- **Interpretation Errors:** Even when provided with perfectly accurate, ground-truth visual aids, models frequently fail to effectively interpret and integrate this information into their reasoning process. This points to a more fundamental issue in how models utilize visual evidence for decision-making and planning, suggesting a lack of "representational alignment" between visual data and reasoning mechanisms.

The introduction of **MENTISOCULI** itself is a significant contribution. As a procedural, stratified benchmark with ground-truth visual chain-of-thought solutions, it offers a controlled and rigorous testbed. This design facilitates the isolation of specific failure modes, allowing researchers to disentangle reasoning capacity from representational alignment issues. Its procedural nature also ensures longevity and guards against data contamination, providing a sustainable resource for future research.

The findings have several potential impacts on future research and development in multimodal AI:
- **Guiding Model Development:** The results suggest that simply enabling multimodal generation is insufficient for robust visual reasoning. Future model development may need to focus on dedicated training methodologies and architectural designs that explicitly foster the ability to maintain consistency across generated visual states, interpret visual cues accurately, and effectively integrate visual information into multi-step planning.
- **Rethinking Training Data:** The observed limitations imply a need for more specialized training data that emphasizes multi-step visual reasoning and the grounding of decisions in visual imagery, potentially moving beyond current large-scale pre-training paradigms.
- **Adaptive Reasoning:** The contrast between human and machine adaptive reasoning effort highlights a crucial area for improvement. Developing models that can dynamically adjust their computational resources and reasoning depth based on the complexity of visual-spatial problems could lead to more efficient and capable AI.
- **Economic Viability:** The study raises concerns about the economic viability of machine mental imagery, given the high computational costs of generating visual traces without clear performance benefits. This may prompt a more pragmatic approach to when and how visual generation is employed for reasoning tasks.

In conclusion, this work establishes a foundation for a deeper understanding of the interplay between visual representation and reasoning in advanced AI. It emphasizes that progress in machine mental imagery requires not only improvements in visual generation but also significant advancements in the capacity of models to meaningfully integrate and leverage visual information for complex, goal-oriented problem-solving.
