# Integrating Learning-Based Manipulation and Physics-Based Locomotion for Whole-Body Badminton Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Shandong University and collaborators developed Hamlet, a hybrid control system for an agile badminton robot that integrates a physics-based strategy for locomotion with a learning-based policy for arm manipulation. This system achieved a 94.5% success rate against a serving machine and a 90.7% return success rate against human players, demonstrating robust whole-body control in a dynamic sports environment.

## Problem

- Controlling agile robots in complex, dynamic sports environments like badminton demands high-speed reactions, precise synchronization, and handling flexible object dynamics within tight temporal constraints.
- Existing purely model-based methods struggle with accurate environmental modeling and extensive tuning, while learning-based approaches face challenges with policy convergence, long training times, and safety concerns.
- A lack of integrated hybrid control systems for agile badminton robots that synergistically combine the strengths of both model-based stability and learning-based adaptability for whole-body control.

## Method

- Developed Hamlet, a hybrid control system that decouples the robot's chassis and arm control, using a model-based strategy for chassis locomotion and a learning-based policy for agile arm manipulation.
- Introduced a physics-informed "IL+RL" (Imitation Learning + ==Reinforcement Learning==) training pipeline for the arm policy, which leverages a model-based teacher with privileged information to guide warm-up and supervise policy exploration.
- Designed the training scheme to enable zero-shot sim2real transfer and generalization, allowing the arm policy to function effectively with different chassis types without requiring retraining.

## Results

- The learning-based arm policy achieved a **97.0%** hit rate and **94.5%** success rate against a serving machine in real-world experiments, outperforming a model-based arm strategy which yielded **91.0%** hit rate and **77.5%** success rate.
- Hamlet demonstrated robust real-world performance against human players, achieving an average return success rate of **90.7%** over 300 rounds, including rallies extending up to 40 consecutive hits.
- Ablation studies confirmed the "IL+RL" training recipe's components (IL warm-up, pre-trained critic, model-based supervision) were crucial for accelerated learning, optimized policy performance, and reduced training volatility, while also showing zero-shot generalization of the arm policy to multiple chassis types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling whole-body control into distinct components (locomotion and manipulation) and applying tailored control strategies (model-based for chassis, learning-based for arm) effectively leverages their respective strengths in dynamic tasks.
> - A physics-informed "IL+RL" training framework, guided by a model-based teacher with privileged information and pre-trained critic, can effectively accelerate learning, improve performance, and maintain stability in complex RL tasks with sparse rewards.
> - Achieving zero-shot sim2real transfer and generalization to varying hardware configurations is possible by incorporating noise into simulated chassis control and training the critic during the imitation learning phase.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of agile robots capable of operating in complex, dynamic environments, such as those found in competitive sports, presents significant challenges in robotics. Badminton, specifically, demands high-speed reactions, precise synchronization of perception and motor responses, and the ability to model the dynamics of flexible objects like shuttlecocks, all within approximate one-second temporal constraints.

Existing research in sports robotics includes efforts like the Kengoro humanoid robot, which demonstrates racket swinging but lacks full badminton play capabilities, and Robomintoner, which achieves badminton play but with a simplistic arm mechanism limiting complex strokes. Other works have focused on table tennis robots, utilizing both model-free reinforcement learning and model-based control methods, or on general agile catching tasks. However, these systems often face limitations regarding complex motion diversity, the ability to swiftly navigate in a court environment, or the integration of sophisticated perception and control for whole-body agile tasks.

Control strategies in robotics typically fall into two categories: model-based and learning-based. Model-based strategies offer stability and safety but depend on accurate environmental models, which can be difficult to maintain in dynamic environments, and often require complex manual adjustments. Learning-based strategies, conversely, demonstrate higher success rates within their training data distribution and adapt more effectively to environmental changes. However, they encounter difficulties in policy network convergence, particularly with sparse rewards or lengthy training times (e.g., in reinforcement learning, RL), and raise concerns regarding safety due to the unexplainability of neural networks. Challenges also arise when transitioning from offline learning to online performance, often involving performance drops.

While some work has explored integrating model-based and learning-based approaches, often with learning-based policies assisting physical strategies, a dedicated hybrid control system for agile badminton robots that synergistically combines the strengths of both paradigms for whole-body control has not been previously established. This gap highlights the need for a system that can leverage the stability and safety of model-based methods for critical components while exploiting the adaptability and high performance of learning-based methods for complex, agile manipulations. The paper positions its work within this context by addressing the challenge of harmonizing these different control philosophies to achieve robust and high-performance control for a badminton robot.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate Hamlet, a novel hybrid control system designed for agile badminton robots. This system aims to integrate learning-based manipulation with physics-based locomotion to achieve effective whole-body control in a complex, high-speed sports environment.

The motivation for developing Hamlet stems from several challenges inherent in controlling agile sports robots and limitations of existing control paradigms:

- **Complexity of Agile Badminton:** Badminton requires rapid reactions, precise timing, and coordination to intercept high-velocity shuttlecocks (up to 426 km/h) within tight temporal windows. This necessitates a control system capable of prompt unification of perception, decision-making, and actuation.
- **Limitations of Pure Control Strategies:**
 - **Model-based methods:** While offering stability and safety, they struggle with accurately modeling complex, dynamic environments (e.g., shuttlecock dynamics) and require extensive manual tuning, which compromises robustness and flexibility.
 - **Learning-based methods:** While demonstrating adaptability and high success rates within trained distributions, they face challenges with policy network convergence, long training times (especially with sparse rewards in RL), and safety concerns due to neural network opacity.
- **Lack of Integrated Solutions:** Existing work on badminton or similar sports robots either employs simplified arm mechanisms, lacks true whole-body agile play, or focuses on one control paradigm. There is a recognized absence of a hybrid control system specifically designed for agile badminton robots that effectively merges model-based stability with learning-based adaptability.
- **Challenges in Learning-based Training:** Reinforcement learning (RL) is often time-consuming, especially with sparse rewards. Methods involving offline data for warm-up (e.g., imitation learning, IL) can suffer from confined data distribution and performance drops during the transition to online RL. For agile tasks like badminton, collecting human demonstrative offline data is challenging, necessitating alternative warming-up strategies.
- **Simplifying Deployment and Adaptability:** A modular control approach could simplify training and enable easier adaptation to different hardware configurations, such as various chassis types, without requiring complete retraining of the arm policy.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of Hamlet are:

- **Harmonize Whole-Body Control:** To decouple the control of the robot's chassis and arm, using a model-based strategy for the chassis to ensure stability and safety, and a learning-based strategy for the arm to facilitate agile, complex manipulations.
- **Develop a Physics-Informed Training Framework:** To introduce a "IL+RL" training recipe for the learning-based arm policy that is informed by physics and guided by a model-based strategy with privileged information.
- **Mitigate IL-to-RL Performance Drop:** To train the critic model during the imitation learning (IL) phase to alleviate the common issue of performance degradation when transitioning from IL to reinforcement learning (RL).
- **Enable Zero-Shot Sim2Real Transfer:** To design a training scheme that allows the learned policies to be transferred directly from simulation to real-world hardware without additional fine-tuning.
- **Achieve Generalization to Hardware:** To enable the arm policy to function effectively with different chassis types without requiring retraining, aiding future hardware adaptations.
- **Demonstrate High Performance:** To achieve high success rates in real-world badminton play against both serving machines and human opponents, showcasing the system's robustness and effectiveness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Hamlet, a hybrid control system for an agile badminton robot, which integrates model-based and learning-based strategies. The robot consists of an omnidirectional chassis and a 5-degrees-of-freedom (DOF) arm, equipped with high-frame-rate binocular cameras for visual perception.

**A. Whole-Body Control Strategy Architecture**

The control system decouples the chassis and arm control, assigning a model-based approach to the chassis and a learning-based approach to the arm. This decision is based on empirical observations of a larger sim2real gap and higher volatility in chassis control compared to the arm.

1. **Chassis Control Strategy (Model-Based):**
 - The chassis control aims to position the robot's base (`p_base`) effectively for the arm to strike the shuttlecock.
 - The system first detects the ball's current trajectory (`F_o`) and predicts its future trajectory (`ˆF_o`).
 - Based on the predicted trajectory, an appropriate hitting position (`p_h`) and hit time (`t_h`) are determined. Inverse Kinematics (IK) are then used to compute the desired chassis planar position (`p_tar`).
 - For stability, especially when the remaining action execution time (`t_avail`) is greater than a threshold (`T_b`), `p_h` is selected at a fixed hitting height (`H_b`). This mitigates the impact of `ˆF_o` inaccuracies for short trajectories.
 - A trust factor (`σ`) is introduced to smooth the initial movement, gradually transitioning the chassis target from its initial position (`p_init`) to `p_tar`.
 - When `t_avail` is less than or equal to `T_b`, `p_tar` is no longer recalculated to prevent erratic movements, maintaining platform stability.

2. **Arm Control Policy (Learning-Based):**
 - The arm policy operates under the assumption that the chassis has reached its `p_base`.
 - Ball trajectory data (`F_o`) from the world coordinate system is rigidly transformed into the robot's coordinate system, originating at `p_base` and rotated according to the robot's orientation (`α`). This transformed trajectory (`ep_o`) is then used as input to the arm policy.
 - The arm policy is parameterized as a four-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). Its input consists of stacked past observations (8 frames) of the arm's 5 joint angles and the 3D position of `ep_o`, resulting in an input tensor of shape (8, 8). The output is a 5-element tensor representing velocity control commands for each joint.
 - This formulation treats the badminton task as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

**B. Physics-Informed "IL+RL" Policy Training Method**

A two-phase "Imitation Learning (IL) + Reinforcement Learning (RL)" recipe is used to train the arm policy (`π_θ`).

1. **Preparations:**
 - **Teacher with Privileged Information (`ˆπ_PM`):** A model-based control strategy is developed in simulation, possessing privileged access to the complete ball trajectory (information unavailable in the real world). This `ˆπ_PM` serves as a teacher, minimizing control errors and guiding `π_θ` training in both IL and RL phases.
 - **Augmented Real-world Ball Trajectories:** 10,000 real-world badminton trajectories are collected. 9,000 of these are augmented through translation and flipping and then mapped into the simulation environment for training. This addresses data sparsity and ensures an even distribution of ball landing points.
 - **Chassis Control Strategy with Noise:** The same chassis control strategy used in the real robot is applied in simulation. Noise is injected into the simulated chassis control to reduce the sim2real gap and enable zero-shot policy transfer.

2. **IL Phase (Warm-up):**
 - The Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) algorithm is employed for warming up `π_θ`. The objective is to maximize `J_SUP(θ)`, which is the expected log-likelihood of the teacher's actions given the policy's state.
 - Crucially, during this phase, a critic model (`V_ϕ`) is also trained simultaneously by minimizing the Bellman loss (`L_BL(ϕ)`) using the collected data, including both correct and incorrect actions. This provides a balanced dataset for critic training and helps mitigate performance drops when transitioning to RL.

3. **RL Phase (Fine-tuning and Exploration):**
 - Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to further enhance `π_θ`'s performance.
 - Advantages (`ˆA_t`) are estimated using the pre-trained critic (`V_ϕ`).
 - The PPO objective function (`J_PPO(θ)`) is augmented with a weighted supervision term (`λJ_SUP(θ)`) from `ˆπ_PM`. This `ˆπ_PM` supervision helps guide `π_θ`'s exploration, preventing excessive bias and promoting safer and more efficient learning by establishing "soft boundaries."
 - **Reward Function:** A comprehensive reward function is designed for RL, incorporating:
 - Sparse rewards for hitting/returning the ball and crossing the net.
 - Penalties for exceeding dynamic constraints (angle, velocity, acceleration, jerk limits) and unsafe actions (base joint angle too backward, self-collision, object collision, racket below height).
 - Dense rewards based on ball's closest distance to paddle center, ball height above net, and landing position in the opponent's court.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted evaluations in both a PyBullet-based simulation environment and with a self-engineered real badminton robot. Performance was measured using hit rate (racket touching the ball), success rate (hitting the ball to the designated area on the opponent's court), and maximum rally length.

**A. Gap Analysis from Simulation to Reality:**
- A comparison of 194 control trajectories between simulation and reality showed that the manipulator (arm) exhibited a smaller and less varied control gap in terms of final racket position error compared to the chassis's final position error.
- The chassis error showed more variation, indicating higher sensitivity to real-world conditions and more complex physical interactions (e.g., ground contact). This finding supported the decision to use a model-based approach for the chassis and a learning-based approach for the arm, due to the arm's better sim2real transferability.

**B. Model-based Strategy vs. Learning-based Policy for Arm Control:**
- In real-world experiments, the model-based strategy for arm control achieved a hit rate of 91% and a success rate of 77.5%.
- The proposed learning-based arm policy (Hamlet) achieved a hit rate of 97% and a success rate of 94.5%.
- These results indicate that the learning-based policy significantly outperformed the model-based strategy, particularly in terms of success rate.

**C. Ablation Study:**
- An ablation study on the arm policy training recipe demonstrated the contributions of different components:
 - The inclusion of the Imitation Learning (IL) phase significantly accelerated initial performance enhancement compared to training Reinforcement Learning (RL) from scratch.
 - The complete "IL+RL" recipe further optimized the policy network's performance during the RL phase.
 - Omitting the pre-trained critic warm-up resulted in a noticeable initial drop in reward during RL, indicating extended exploration phase requirements.
 - The absence of model-based strategy (`ˆπ_PM`) supervision during RL led to a more pronounced reward degradation and greater volatility in policy training, highlighting the importance of the physics-informed guidance.

**D. Robot vs. Serving Machine:**
- Experiments against a badminton serving machine involved 20 diverse scenarios (combinations of three positions, three angles, and two strengths), with 10 test outcomes per strategy (200 outcomes per strategy).
- The learning-based policy deployed with the robot achieved a 97.0% hit rate and a 94.5% success rate.
- The model-based strategy achieved a 91.0% hit rate and a 77.5% success rate.
- These results further confirmed the superior performance of the learning-based policy.

**E. Human-Robot Interaction:**
- Three sets of human-robot combat data were collected, each comprising 100 rounds.
- The robot, utilizing the Hamlet system, achieved an average return success rate of 90.7% against human players.
- The system demonstrated robustness by achieving a maximum rally length of 40 consecutive hits.

**F. Zero-Shot Generalization:**
- The system successfully demonstrated zero-shot generalization of the arm policy to multiple chassis configurations in simulation, without requiring re-training of the arm policy. This indicates the decoupling of arm and chassis control was effective.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents Hamlet, a whole-body control system for agile badminton robots, which represents a contribution to the field of agile robotics and mobile manipulation.

**A. Novelty and Methodological Contribution:**
- Hamlet is introduced as a novel hybrid control system that effectively integrates model-based and learning-based control strategies for the distinct components of an agile badminton robot. This is noted as the first such system specifically designed for agile badminton robots.
- The development of a physics-informed "IL+RL" training pipeline is a methodological contribution. This pipeline leverages a model-based strategy with privileged information to guide actor and critic warm-up during IL and supervise policy exploration during RL. This approach addresses common challenges in reinforcement learning, such as slow training convergence, sparse rewards, and the performance degradation often observed when transitioning from IL to RL.

**B. Performance and Robustness:**
- The system achieves high performance in a complex, high-speed, and dynamic environment, demonstrated by a 94.5% success rate against a serving machine and a 90.7% success rate against human players, including rallies up to 40 consecutive hits. This level of performance indicates the robustness and effectiveness of the hybrid control and training framework in handling real-world variability.

**C. Practical Implications and Generalizability:**
- The achieved zero-shot transfer from simulation to reality simplifies deployment, reducing the need for extensive real-world fine-tuning.
- The arm policy's ability to generalize to multiple chassis types without retraining offers flexibility in hardware adaptation and future system development. This modularity could streamline the design and upgrade process for such robots.
- The authors suggest that the proposed system and training framework are generalizable to other agile mobile manipulation tasks, such as high-speed object catching or table tennis. This indicates potential broader applicability beyond badminton, suggesting a foundational approach for controlling robots in other dynamic, high-dexterity scenarios.

In summary, Hamlet advances the capabilities of sports robots by providing a robust and adaptable control solution for a challenging task, contributing both a practical system and a principled training methodology to the robotics community.
