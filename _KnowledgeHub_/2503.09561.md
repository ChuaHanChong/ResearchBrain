# Strategyproof Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zurich and the University of Oxford introduce a formal framework for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) that accounts for strategic human labelers who might misreport preferences. They propose the Pessimistic Median of MLEs (Pessimistic MoMLEs) algorithm, which achieves approximate strategyproofness and converges to optimal social welfare, while also identifying a fundamental trade-off between incentive alignment and policy alignment in multi-labeler settings.

## Problem

- Existing RLHF methods assume truthful human feedback, making them vulnerable to strategic misreporting when labelers aim to influence the learned policy.
- Strategic behavior in pluralistic alignment settings can introduce self-serving biases, degrading the fairness, robustness, and overall effectiveness of AI systems.
- There is a lack of formal analysis and robust algorithms to ensure strategyproofness in RLHF without relying on external payment mechanisms.

## Method

- The work formalizes offline RLHF with multiple ==strategic human labelers== in episodic Markov Decision Processes, assuming linearly realizable reward functions and using the Bradley-Terry model for preferences.
- The Pessimistic Median of MLEs (Pessimistic MoMLEs) algorithm is proposed, which computes Maximum Likelihood Estimates (MLEs) and confidence sets for individual labeler reward parameters.
- It then optimizes for a 'pessimistic median return' within a constructed 'median confidence set' to find a policy robust to strategic manipulation, applicable to contextual bandits and general MDPs.

## Results

- Existing methods showed substantial degradation in social welfare suboptimality under strategic labeling in synthetic contextual bandit experiments, with the difference increasing with sample size.
- Pessimistic MoMLEs was proven to be approximately strategyproof, with the incentive to misreport bounded by ~O(κ_i * √(d/n)), diminishing as the number of samples (n) increases.
- Under truthful reporting, Pessimistic MoMLEs' suboptimality is bounded by ~O(√(d log(k/δ)/k) + max_{i∈[k]} κ*_i * k√(d + log(k/δ)/n)), demonstrating convergence to the optimal policy as both n and k increase.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Existing pluralistic RLHF methods (e.g., Pessimistic Social Welfare) are not strategyproof, allowing a single strategic labeler to cause arbitrary degradation in social welfare.
> - There is an inherent trade-off in strategyproof RLHF: any such algorithm incurs a worst-case constant suboptimality of at least (k-1)/k and an approximation ratio no better than 1/k, where k is the number of labelers.
> - Approximate strategyproofness and convergence to optimal social welfare can be achieved by optimizing a pessimistic median return, balancing robustness against strategic behavior with policy alignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a prominent methodology for aligning artificial intelligence systems with human preferences. This approach, which utilizes human-labeled comparisons to guide policy optimization, has been applied in various domains including robotics, recommendation systems, and Large Language Models (LLMs). While effective in enhancing usability and alignment with intended objectives, RLHF introduces complexities, particularly in scenarios involving diverse, subjective, and potentially conflicting human preferences.

A growing area of research, termed pluralistic alignment, specifically addresses the challenge of aligning AI with the varied preferences of multiple individuals or groups. Traditional reinforcement learning typically optimizes policies based on a single, well-defined reward function. In contrast, pluralistic settings necessitate methods for reconciling and aggregating multiple perspectives. Existing approaches in pluralistic alignment often assume that human labelers provide feedback truthfully. However, this assumption overlooks the potential for strategic behavior when labelers understand that their feedback directly influences the learned policy.

Prior work has explored the implications of social choice theory for preference aggregation in RLHF, focusing on properties such as fairness, but largely under the assumption of truthful reporting. Other research has examined robustness against adversarial corruptions, where a fraction of data is maliciously manipulated, differing in perspective (adversarial vs. strategic) and techniques (robust offline RL vs. mechanism design). Recent efforts have also incorporated mechanism design principles into RLHF, primarily through the use of payment rules, to incentivize truthful feedback.

This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on strategyproofness in RLHF without relying on external payment mechanisms, which may be impractical. It also adopts a perspective where labelers aim to influence the final policy rather than just an estimate of aggregated preferences, a distinction from some prior work. The research therefore bridges the gap between the established success of RLHF in aligning AI with human preferences and the unaddressed challenge of strategic misreporting in multi-labeler, pluralistic environments, drawing on principles from mechanism design to propose robust solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate and address the problem of strategic preference labeling within the context of offline Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), particularly in pluralistic alignment settings where multiple labelers contribute feedback.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in current RLHF methodologies:

1. **Vulnerability to Strategic Misreporting**: Existing RLHF algorithms, including those designed for pluralistic alignment, implicitly assume that human labelers provide truthful feedback. However, when labelers' preferences influence the final policy, individuals or groups may have incentives to manipulate their feedback to steer the learned policy towards outcomes that maximize their personal objectives, potentially at the expense of broader social welfare.
2. **Potential for Misalignment and Degradation**: Such strategic behavior can distort the alignment process, introducing self-serving biases into the feedback data. This threatens to undermine the fairness, robustness, and overall effectiveness of the AI system. For instance, in LLM fine-tuning, labelers might systematically misreport preferences to amplify specific biases or reinforce narratives aligned with their views. The paper highlights that this issue is not confined to human labelers but can also be exacerbated when learning from synthetic feedback sources designed for influence.
3. **Lack of Formal Analysis of Incentive Alignment**: Despite its importance, the issue of strategic labeler behavior has largely remained unaddressed in existing RLHF methodologies. There is a need for a formal framework to analyze the conditions under which strategyproofness can be achieved and to quantify the costs associated with it.
4. **Trade-off between Incentive and Policy Alignment**: A central motivation is to explore a potential fundamental trade-off between incentivizing truthful reporting (incentive alignment) and optimizing for maximal social welfare (policy alignment). The paper seeks to understand if ensuring strategyproofness inherently compromises the ability to achieve optimal policy performance.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

1. **Formal Problem Introduction**: To formally introduce the problem of offline RLHF with strategic human labelers, where each labeler might misreport preference labels to maximize their personal reward function. The focus is on linear reward functions and social welfare maximization, analyzing the tensions between individual incentive alignment and policy alignment.
2. **Analysis of Existing Methods**: To demonstrate that current RLHF methods are not strategyproof and quantify the potential for a single strategic labeler to cause significant misalignment with social welfare.
3. **Identification of Inherent Limitations**: To prove that, in the worst case, any strategyproof RLHF algorithm faces fundamental limitations, specifically performing worse than the optimal policy by a factor dependent on the number of labelers. This aims to formally establish the trade-off between incentive alignment and policy alignment.
4. **Proposal of a Robust Algorithm**: To propose a novel RLHF method that mitigates incentive misalignment while maintaining policy performance, aiming for approximate strategyproofness and convergence to the optimal policy under specific conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formalizes Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) within the framework of episodic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), including contextual bandits as a special case (where the horizon H=1).

**Problem Formulation:**
- **MDP Structure**: An MDP is defined by (S, A, P, H, ρ) (state space, action space, transition function, horizon, initial state distribution), but without a reward function. Trajectories are sequences of actions and states.
- **Multiple Labelers with Diverse Preferences**: The setting involves `k` labelers, each possessing a distinct, unknown reward function `r_i : S × A → R`. The expected return of a policy `π` for labeler `i` is `J_i(ρ, π)`.
- **Linear Realizability**: A core assumption is that each labeler's reward function `r_i` is linearly realizable, meaning `r_i(s, a) = ⟨θ*_i, ϕ(s, a)⟩`, where `ϕ` is a known feature embedding and `θ*_i` is the true reward parameter.
- **Offline RLHF with Bradley-Terry Model**: The study focuses on offline RLHF. Each labeler `i` is given `n` examples, each consisting of an initial state `s` and two trajectories `(τ_0, τ_1)`. Labelers provide binary preference labels (`o_i,j ∈ {0, 1}`). The Bradley-Terry (BT) model is used to define the probability of preferring one trajectory over another, dependent on the labeler's reward parameter.
- **Strategic Preference Labeling**: A key aspect is the assumption that labelers may strategically manipulate their feedback. Instead of sampling labels `o_i,j` truthfully according to `P_θ*_i`, a strategic labeler `i` might sample labels `õ_i,j` according to a manipulated reward function `r_θ̃_i`. The RLHF algorithm receives these reported datasets `D = (D_1..., D_k)` and computes a policy `π̂_RLHF(D)` without knowing if feedback was truthful.
- **Strategyproofness Definitions**:
 - **Strategyproofness**: An algorithm is strategyproof if reporting truthfully is an optimal strategy for every labeler, regardless of what other labelers report (dominant strategy incentive compatibility).
 - **ε-Strategyproofness**: A relaxed version where the gain from misreporting is bounded by `ε > 0`.
- **Learning Objective**: The objective is to compute a policy that maximizes the average social welfare `W(ρ, π) = (1/k) ∑_i J_i(ρ, π)`. The suboptimality of a policy `π` is defined as `SubOpt(ρ, π) = W(ρ, π*) - W(ρ, π)`, where `π*` is the optimal social welfare policy.

**Limitations of Existing RLHF Methods:**
The paper evaluates two existing pluralistic RLHF methods: Pessimistic Social Welfare and MaxMin-RLHF. It demonstrates that these methods are not strategyproof. Furthermore, a single strategic labeler can cause an arbitrarily large degradation in policy performance for methods like Pessimistic Social Welfare.

**Inherent Limitations of Strategyproof RLHF:**
The authors prove that any algorithm satisfying strategyproofness must suffer from a fundamental trade-off. Specifically, it implies a constant suboptimality in the worst case, and its approximation ratio (ratio of achieved social welfare to optimal) is at most `1/k`, where `k` is the number of labelers. This suggests that achieving perfect strategyproofness fundamentally limits policy alignment with social welfare.

**Pessimistic Median of MLEs (Pessimistic MoMLEs) Algorithm:**
To address the identified trade-off, the paper proposes Pessimistic MoMLEs. The methodology proceeds as follows:
- **Hyperrectangle Spanning Assumption**: For contextual bandits (and an analogous assumption for MDPs), it assumes that the set of expected feature embeddings `E_{s∼ρ}[ϕ(s, π(s))]` for all policies `π` spans a hyperrectangle in `R^d`. This assumption simplifies the analysis of policy optimization based on feature vectors.
- **Maximum Likelihood Estimates (MLEs) and Confidence Sets**: For each labeler `i`, an MLE `θ̂_MLE_i` of their reward parameter is computed from their reported preference data `D_i`. Around each `θ̂_MLE_i`, a confidence ellipsoid `C_i` is constructed. These confidence sets quantify the uncertainty in the reward parameter estimation.
- **Pessimistic Median Return**: The algorithm defines a "median confidence set" `C` as the set of coordinate-wise medians derived from all possible `θ_i ∈ C_i`. The objective then becomes to find a policy `π` that maximizes the "pessimistic median return," defined as `W(π) = min_{θ∈C} E_{s∼ρ}[⟨θ, ϕ(s, π(s))⟩]`. This involves optimizing for the worst-case policy performance with respect to any reward parameter within the median confidence set.
- **Policy Computation**: The algorithm returns `π̂(D) = argmax_{π∈Π} W(π)`.
- **Extension to MDPs**: For MDPs, the methodology adapts by considering trajectory-wise preferences and adjusting the reward function sum over trajectories. The confidence ellipsoid for `θ̂_MLE_i` is constructed similarly. The expected return is then expressed using state occupancy measures `q_π(s|ρ)`, and the pessimistic median return is defined as `W(π) = min_{θ∈C} E_{s∼q_π}[⟨θ, ϕ(s, π(s))⟩]`. An analogous hyperrectangle spanning assumption is made for MDPs.

The computational complexity analysis suggests that computing the pessimistic median return function `W(π)` (or `W(ψ)` for parameterized policies) can be formulated as a convex optimization problem for contextual bandits, allowing for efficient gradient-based optimization. For MDPs, it can be framed as a concave problem in state-action occupancy measures, or as a non-concave but optimizable problem for parameterized policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings across both theoretical analyses and the proposed algorithmic solution.

**Limitations of Existing RLHF Algorithms:**
- **Non-Strategyproofness**: The authors formally demonstrate that common existing RLHF methods, specifically Pessimistic Social Welfare and MaxMin-RLHF, are not strategyproof (Proposition 3.3). This means that labelers can strategically misreport their preferences to manipulate the outcome.
- **Arbitrary Degradation**: It is shown that even a single strategic labeler can lead to an arbitrarily large degradation in the social welfare achieved by policies learned by existing methods like Pessimistic Social Welfare (Proposition 3.4). In the worst-case, the suboptimality can approach `BL - 3ε`, where `B` and `L` are bounds on reward parameters and feature embeddings, respectively.

**Inherent Limitations of Strategyproof RLHF:**
- **Fundamental Trade-off**: The research identifies a fundamental trade-off between incentive alignment (strategyproofness) and policy alignment (maximizing social welfare).
- **Constant Suboptimality**: Any strategyproof RLHF algorithm is proven to incur a worst-case expected suboptimality of at least `(k-1)/k`, where `k` is the number of labelers (Theorem 3.5). This implies that even with infinite data, perfect strategyproofness prevents convergence to the optimal policy in the worst case.
- **Limited Approximation Ratio**: Consequentially, the approximation ratio (achieved social welfare relative to optimal) of any strategyproof RLHF method is bounded above by `1/k` in the worst case (Corollary 3.6). This means a strategyproof algorithm could perform `k`-times worse than the optimal policy.

**Pessimistic Median of MLEs (Pessimistic MoMLEs) Performance:**
The proposed Pessimistic MoMLEs algorithm offers guarantees for both approximate strategyproofness and social welfare maximization under specific assumptions, applicable to contextual bandits and MDPs.

- **Approximate Strategyproofness**: The algorithm is shown to be `~O(κ_i * √(d/n))`-strategyproof for labeler `i` (Theorem 4.1 for contextual bandits, Theorem 5.1 for MDPs). Here, `κ_i` represents the "uniform policy coverage" of labeler `i`'s data, which quantifies how well their data constrains *any* possible policy. The bound indicates that the incentive to misreport diminishes as the number of samples `n` increases. The reliance on uniform policy coverage (rather than just optimal policy coverage) is noted as a distinction from standard offline RL guarantees, as strategic behavior might induce policies far from the true optimum.
- **Social Welfare Maximization**:
 - Under truthful reporting (which is shown to be an approximately dominant strategy), the suboptimality of the learned policy `π̂` is bounded by `~O(√(d log(k/δ)/k) + max_{i∈[k]} κ*_i * k√(d + log(k/δ)/n))` (Theorem 4.2 for contextual bandits, Theorem 5.2 for MDPs). Here, `κ*_i` is the optimal policy coverage for labeler `i`.
 - This bound also holds when labelers report according to any weakly dominant strategy induced by the algorithm (Proposition 4.3).
 - The bound indicates two sources of suboptimality: `√(d log(k/δ)/k)` from approximating social welfare with a median (which improves with `k`), and `max κ*_i * k√(d + log(k/δ)/n)` from reward parameter estimation (which improves with `n`). Overall, `π̂` converges to the optimal policy as `n` and `k` increase.
 - For a single labeler (`k=1`), the suboptimality simplifies to `~O(κ*_1 * √(d + log(k/δ)/n))`, matching existing offline RLHF bounds (Corollary 4.5).
 - If all `k` labelers have identical reward functions, the `√(d log(k/δ)/k)` term disappears, but an `O(k)` factor remains in the estimation error term (Corollary 4.5), suggesting that the algorithm anticipates strategic manipulation even when it's not present.
- **Approximation Ratio**: When the optimal social welfare `W(π*)` is constant and data coverage is sufficient, the approximation ratio of Pessimistic MoMLEs is bounded below by `1 - O(√(d log(k/δ)/k))` (Corollary 4.6).

**Preliminary Experimental Evaluation (Appendix C):**
Synthetic experiments in a contextual bandit setting (`d=16, k=5, n ∈ {20, 50, 100, 200}`) confirm the theoretical findings.
- Naive MLEs and Pessimistic Social Welfare show substantial degradation in social welfare suboptimality under strategic labeling compared to truthful labeling, with the difference increasing with `n`.
- Pessimistic MoMLEs exhibits minimal degradation under strategic labeling, demonstrating robustness consistent with its approximate strategyproofness guarantees. Median of MLEs also shows improved robustness compared to naive methods, but less effectively than Pessimistic MoMLEs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a significant contribution to the field of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) by formally addressing the understudied problem of strategic preference labeling in pluralistic alignment settings.

The key significance and potential impact are:

1. **Identification of a Fundamental Trade-off**: The paper formally identifies and quantifies an inherent trade-off between incentive alignment (ensuring truthful feedback) and policy alignment (maximizing social welfare). By proving that any strategyproof RLHF algorithm must incur constant suboptimality and perform at least `k`-times worse than the optimal policy in the worst case, the authors establish a foundational limitation. This insight is critical for understanding the intrinsic challenges of designing AI systems that are both robust to manipulation and optimally aligned with collective preferences.
2. **Increased Robustness in RLHF Systems**: The demonstration that existing RLHF methods are vulnerable to strategic manipulation, with potentially arbitrary degradation in policy performance, highlights a critical vulnerability in current AI alignment efforts. The proposed Pessimistic Median of MLEs algorithm offers a principled approach to mitigate this vulnerability without relying on complex payment mechanisms, which are often impractical in real-world applications. This can lead to more robust and trustworthy AI systems, particularly in sensitive applications where diverse stakeholders provide feedback.
3. **Guidance for Pluralistic Alignment**: The findings provide a theoretical foundation for developing RLHF systems that can operate effectively in pluralistic environments. By showing how to achieve approximate strategyproofness while converging to the optimal policy, the research offers concrete algorithmic design principles. The role of "uniform policy coverage" in strategyproofness is a novel insight that could inform future data collection strategies in multi-agent RLHF.
4. **Advancement of Mechanism Design in AI**: The work integrates concepts from mechanism design and social choice theory into the RLHF framework, enriching the theoretical understanding of incentive structures in AI alignment. This interdisciplinary approach expands the toolkit for designing AI systems that can fairly and effectively aggregate diverse human preferences.
5. **Practical Implications for LLM Fine-tuning**: Given the widespread use of RLHF in fine-tuning Large Language Models, the risk of strategic manipulation is considerable. If labelers can steer LLMs towards specific biases or narratives, the fairness and trustworthiness of these models can be compromised. This research offers a pathway to develop LLMs that are more resistant to such manipulations, thereby fostering more equitable and reliable AI outputs.
6. **Directions for Future Research**: The paper opens several avenues for future work, including extending strategyproofness analysis to non-linear reward functions, parameterized policy classes, and more general preference models beyond the Bradley-Terry model. Empirical evaluation at scale, particularly in LLM fine-tuning, is also suggested, recognizing potential trade-offs between theoretical guarantees and scalability in practical deployments.

In summary, this research advances the theoretical understanding of incentive structures in RLHF and proposes a concrete algorithm to address a critical vulnerability, paving the way for more resilient, fair, and aligned AI systems in the presence of strategic human feedback.
