# Scalable Multi-Task Data Generation via Reinforcement Learning for Language-Conditioned Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> RLDC (Reinforcement Learning as Data Collector) is a teacher-student pipeline for bimanual dexterous manipulation: per-task state-based RL teachers trained with one reusable four-term reward are rolled out under heavy physical and visual randomization with auto-generated, LLM-paraphrased language labels, then distilled into a single language-conditioned point-cloud diffusion policy that transfers zero-shot to two 44-DoF real arms.

## Problem

- Human teleoperation and video demonstrations scale poorly and suffer embodiment mismatch, partial observability, and retargeting error, leaving no large multimodal bimanual dexterous dataset.
- Demonstration-augmentation generators assume object-centric trajectory equivariance, so transformed trajectories ignore robot kinematic feasibility and collision-free execution in cluttered randomized scenes.
- RL data generation scales in principle but needs handcrafted per-task rewards and often depends on explicit state estimation, which is brittle and slow to deploy in the real world.

## Method

- A ==generalizable four-term reward==, hand-pose alignment, fingertip-to-center contact, goal pose gated by a lifting height threshold, and joint reset, shared with identical scaling factors across all three tasks; teachers trained with independent ==PPO== per arm.
- Data collection rolls converged teachers out under randomized object geometry, friction, mass, color, lighting, camera intrinsics and viewpoint, with template language labels over object, color, and arm assignment paraphrased by GPT.
- Distillation into a transformer diffusion policy with a ==PointNet++== encoder without max pooling, SigLIP language tokens, separate left and right action heads, and an auxiliary head regressing ground-truth object 6D state.

## Results

- The shared reward matches task-specific rewards on Insert and beats them on Lift, while object-reference-trajectory rewards fail across all tasks; reward setup drops from about a day to roughly **one hour** per task.
- In simulation the distilled policy reaches **0.80** Multi-Object, **0.74** Insert, **0.96** Lift, and **0.67** multi-task, versus 0.42/0.38/0.703/0.06 for DP3 and 0.41/0.47/0.65/0.14 for iDP3.
- Scaling trajectories from 500 to 5,000 raises real-world multi-task success from **3/20 to 8/20** and Insert from **5/20 to 16/20**; removing the state-prediction loss cuts every task's success rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reward flexible goal poses plus an initial hand-pose anchor rather than full object trajectories; letting the agent discover its own path avoids joint-limit and collision failures under randomization.
> - Simulation's ground-truth state is free supervision: an auxiliary state-prediction head anchors the visual encoder and reliably improves robustness whenever policies are distilled from simulated data.
> - Simulation success can invert in reality: shared action heads fit training data better, yet decoupled per-arm heads recover from one hand's failure and win most real-world tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robotic policies, particularly for complex bimanual dexterous manipulation, is significantly hindered by the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality, and diverse datasets. Traditional approaches to data generation each present distinct limitations.

Human teleoperation, while providing natural demonstrations, is constrained by its high cost and time demands, making it impractical for scaling across a wide variety of tasks and objects. Human videos, though abundant, introduce challenges such as embodiment mismatch between humans and robots, partial observability, and difficulties in retargeting human actions to robot kinematics, which impede robust policy learning.

Simulation-based data generation offers scalability in terms of visual appearance, object diversity, scene complexity, and physical parameters. However, existing simulation methods often rely on a limited set of expert demonstrations or predefined skills, which restricts the diversity and optimality of the generated data. Approaches that augment human demonstrations by transforming object-centric trajectories frequently lead to robot-infeasible actions, as they may neglect collision avoidance or kinematic limits in complex scenes.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents an alternative by enabling agents to autonomously generate diverse and high-quality trajectories. However, the practical application of RL for data generation faces two primary obstacles:
1. **Reward Engineering:** The design of reward functions typically requires extensive, task-specific manual effort, which limits scalability across multiple tasks.
2. **State Estimation Dependence:** Many RL methods rely on explicit state estimation (e.g., object tracking), which can be challenging to deploy smoothly in real-world scenarios due to inference latency and compounded errors.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by proposing a systematic RL-based data generation pipeline, named Reinforcement Learning as Data Collector (RLDC). It aims to overcome the identified challenges by integrating a generalizable reward design, extensive domain randomization, and automated language conditioning. By doing so, the work seeks to provide a scalable solution for synthesizing diverse, robot-feasible data for bimanual dexterous manipulation, thereby contributing to the training of language-conditioned multi-task policies and addressing a critical data bottleneck in robotic foundation models. It differentiates itself from prior RL-based data generation methods by focusing on a multi-task setting and enriching data with language labels, as well as by emphasizing a generalizable reward design to reduce engineering overhead.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a scalable Reinforcement Learning-based data generation pipeline, termed Reinforcement Learning as Data Collector (RLDC), for synthesizing high-quality, diverse, and multimodal datasets specifically for language-conditioned bimanual dexterous manipulation. This pipeline aims to facilitate the training of generalist policies capable of robustly executing multiple tasks in both simulated and real-world environments.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and limitations in the field of robotic manipulation:

1. **Data Scarcity for Complex Manipulation:** There is a significant lack of large-scale, high-quality, and diverse datasets necessary for training generalist policies for bimanual dexterous manipulation. Existing datasets are often confined to single-arm setups or bimanual systems with simpler grippers, failing to address the complexities of dexterous hands.
2. **Limitations of Current Data Generation Methods:**
 - **Human Teleoperation:** This method is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming to scale across a broad spectrum of tasks and objects.
 - **Human Videos:** While abundant, human videos suffer from embodiment mismatches, partial observability, and imperfections in retargeting actions to robot kinematics, which impede robust policy learning.
 - **Simulation-based Augmentation:** Current simulation approaches, often based on augmenting human demonstrations, suffer from limited task diversity and can lead to robot-infeasible trajectories when object-centric transformations neglect physical constraints (e.g., collisions).
3. **Challenges in Applying Reinforcement Learning for Data Generation:**
 - **Reward Engineering Burden:** Traditional RL requires extensive, task-specific reward engineering, which significantly limits its scalability to multiple diverse tasks. A more generalizable approach is needed to reduce this overhead.
 - **Dependency on State Estimation:** Many RL methods rely on explicit state estimation (e.g., object tracking), which can introduce latency and errors in real-world deployment.
4. **Need for Multi-Task and Language-Conditioned Policies:** To move towards truly generalist robots, policies need to be capable of performing diverse tasks and responding to natural language instructions. This requires datasets that are not only diverse in trajectories but also rich in multimodal annotations, particularly language.
5. **Achieving Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Policies trained purely in simulation often struggle with robust transfer to the real world due to the sim-to-real gap. Strategies are needed to enhance the robustness of vision-based policies trained on synthetic data.

To address these motivations, the RLDC pipeline incorporates several specific innovations aimed at improving scalability and performance:
- Designing a generalizable reward function to reduce engineering effort across tasks.
- Employing extensive domain randomization to generate diverse data in simulation.
- Automatically generating and augmenting language task descriptions to enrich multimodal datasets.
- Developing a robust language-conditioned multi-task student policy using diffusion models, with an auxiliary loss to anchor visual representations to ground-truth state information available in simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Reinforcement Learning as Data Collector (RLDC) method is a four-stage pipeline designed to generate diverse, high-quality synthetic datasets for bimanual dexterous manipulation and subsequently distill them into a multi-task, language-conditioned policy.

**A. Tasks**
The method is evaluated on three bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks:
1. **Box Lifting:** A single-stage, single-object task requiring precise two-arm coordination for stable lifting.
2. **Multi-Object Pick-and-Place:** Involves perception, scene understanding, grasping in cluttered environments, and sequential placement while avoiding inter-arm collisions.
3. **Drawer Insertion:** Requires interaction with articulated objects, precise timing, and bimanual cooperation for successful object insertion.
These tasks collectively cover high inter-arm coordination, long-horizon planning, cluttered scene reasoning, and articulated object interaction.

**B. Generalizable Reward Design (Teacher Training Stage 1)**
To address the challenge of task-specific reward engineering, the authors designed a set of four generalizable reward terms applicable across various bimanual dexterous tasks with the same scaling factors. These terms encourage functional manipulation while maintaining generalization:
1. **Initial Manipulation Hand Pose (`r_align`):** This term minimizes the distance and quaternion difference between the current robot hand pose (`p_palm`, `q_palm`) and a predefined task-specific initial hand pose (`p_def`, `q_def`) relative to the object. It ensures that the robot's hands are correctly positioned for effective manipulation.
2. **Object Contact (`r_contact`):** After hand alignment, this term encourages the robot's fingertips to establish contact with the object by minimizing the distance between the fingertip positions (`p_i_tip`) and the object's center of mass (`p_com`).
3. **Object Goal Poses with Lifting Gate (`r_goal`):** Instead of enforcing full object pose trajectories, this term rewards progress towards a flexible goal pose (`p_g`, `q_g`). To prevent undesirable behaviors like pushing, the reward is activated only after the object is lifted above a specified height threshold (`τ`) relative to its initial height (`p_o,z`). This allows the RL agent to explore diverse strategies for reaching the target.
4. **Robot Reset (`r_reset`):** This term encourages the robot arms to return to a predefined reset joint configuration (`θ_reset`) upon sub-task completion. This promotes consistent starting states for chained tasks and prevents unsafe behaviors.

For policy training, the bimanual problem is formulated as a multi-agent setting using Independent PPO (IPPO). Each arm is controlled by a dedicated policy, conditioned on local observations and optimizing local rewards, which helps in reducing the exploration space and simplifying credit assignment. During this teacher training, domain randomization is applied to factors directly affecting state-based learning, such as object initialization and physical parameters.

**C. Policy Training and Multimodal Data Collection (Data Generation Stage 2)**
After the RL teacher policies converge, they are used to collect a diverse multimodal dataset in simulation. To maximize diversity and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, extensive domain randomization is applied:
- **Objects:** Objects are drawn from a dataset that includes complex geometries. Their pose, physical parameters (static/dynamic friction, mass, restitution), and visual attributes (color, reflectivity, roughness) are randomized.
- **Perception-related factors:** Camera viewpoints, intrinsic parameters, and lighting conditions are also randomized.
This broad range of perturbations exposes the policy to varied scenarios, enhancing robustness.

**D. Automated Language Labeling (Labeling Stage 3)**
Language labels are automatically generated for each trajectory. Predefined templates, which specify objects, colors, and goals (e.g., "use the right arm to place the red car into the bin"), are instantiated at each environment reset. To increase linguistic diversity and improve generalization to unseen instructions, a Large Language Model (GPT) is employed to paraphrase these templates (e.g., "pick the nearest objects with both arms and place them respectively"). These language inputs primarily disambiguate task goals, object choices, and arm-object assignments.

**E. Multi-task Policy Distillation (Policy Learning Stage 4)**
The collected large-scale multimodal dataset is then used to train a multi-task policy, modeled as a transformer-based diffusion model.
- **Denoising Network:** The denoising network `ϵ_θ` takes a 6-channel point cloud `O`, robot state `S`, and language tokens `L` as input to predict the noise `ϵ_k` required for the diffusion process. The action loss is defined as `ℓ_action = ||ϵ_k - ϵ_θ(¯α_k a_0 + ¯β_k ϵ_k, k, O, S, L)||^2`. The model is trained with 20 denoising steps, and DDIM sampler is used for inference with 5 steps.
- **Network Architecture (Fig. 3):**
 - **Input Preprocessing:** Raw point clouds are cropped to the region of interest and downsampled to 2,048 points.
 - **Feature Encoding:** A PointNet++ encoder (without max pooling) processes point cloud features. Low-level robot states (joint positions/velocities) are embedded using Fourier positional encodings. Language instructions are encoded using SigLip.
 - **Transformer:** Point cloud features, robot state features, and language embeddings are combined and processed by a transformer, using cross-attention for interaction between action queries and conditioning tokens.
 - **Decoupled Action Heads:** To reflect the partially independent yet coordinated nature of bimanual manipulation, separate output heads decode refined action tokens to predict diffusion noise for each arm.
- **Auxiliary Loss for State Prediction:** Leveraging the availability of ground-truth object states in simulation, an auxiliary prediction head is introduced. It maps point cloud features to object states (`x_obj`, including position and orientation, represented in 6D) with a loss `ℓ_aux = ||ˆx_obj - x_obj||^2`. This loss regularizes the visual encoder, encouraging it to extract task-relevant geometric information and improving visual grounding.

**F. Real-World and Simulation Setup**
The robotic platform consists of two 6-DOF xArm UF850 arms, each with a 16-DOF Allegro Hand V4, operating over a 1.2x0.8m tabletop workspace. A low-level PD controller runs at 120Hz, while policy inference is at 20Hz. Perception is provided by a single egocentric ZED2i RGB-D camera. NVIDIA IsaacLab is used as the simulator, with a digital twin calibrated to replicate real-world physics parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from evaluations in both simulation and real-world settings, covering the effectiveness of the generalizable reward design, the performance of the distilled multi-task policy, and ablation studies on architectural choices.

**A. Evaluation of Generalizable Reward Design**
- **Performance Comparison:** The proposed generalizable reward design was compared against two baselines: (i) task-specific rewards manually tuned for each task, and (ii) an object reference trajectory reward.
 - In simulation (Fig. 4), the proposed reward design achieved success rates comparable to (and in the case of "Lift", superior to) task-specific rewards. For the "Insert" task, performance was comparable, and for "Multi-object," it was slightly lower. This suggests that while comprehensive, the defined reward terms can be selectively applied to optimize for specific task constraints.
 - The object reference trajectory reward baseline performed poorly across all tasks, often encountering joint limits due to its reliance on predefined trajectories that do not adapt to heavily randomized object poses. This finding supports the utility of flexible goal conditions over strict trajectory adherence.
- **Implementation Efficiency:** The generalizable reward setup required approximately one hour per task for defining initial hand alignment poses. In contrast, setting up object reference trajectory rewards could take a full day due to video recording, object tracking, and coordinate alignment. This highlights the practical scalability benefits of the proposed reward design.

**B. Evaluation of Policy Distillation**
- **Comparison with Baselines:** The distilled vision-based policy was compared against two point-cloud-based policies, DP3 and iDP3, in both single-task and multi-task settings (Table I).
 - **Simulation:** The proposed policy consistently outperformed both DP3 and iDP3 across "Multi-Object," "Insert," "Lift," and the aggregated "Multi-Task" success rates.
 - **Real-World:** Similar trends were observed in real-world evaluations, where the proposed policy achieved a higher number of successful trials (e.g., 15/20 for "Multi-Object," 16/20 for "Insert," 14/20 for "Lift," and 8/20 for "Multi-Task" out of 20 trials) compared to DP3 and iDP3.
 - The authors suggest that the large-scale and consistent data generated by converged RL policies (on the order of 10^3 trajectories) in their dataset might reduce the policy's sensitivity to architectural differences compared to smaller human-teleoperated datasets.
- **Real-World Generalization:** The multi-task policy successfully followed diverse language instructions in real-world rollouts (Fig. 5).
- **Out-of-Distribution Generalization:** For the "Insert" task, the policy successfully manipulated an out-of-distribution object (a toy dog) that was not part of the "Insert" training set but was included in the "Multi-Object" task. This demonstrated the policy's ability to transfer knowledge across tasks due to multi-task training.

**C. Ablations on Design Choices**
- **Separate Action Heads (Table I):**
 - In simulation, a single shared action head achieved slightly higher success rates than separate heads for some tasks, possibly due to easier fit to the training distribution.
 - However, in real-world experiments, separate action heads yielded significantly higher success rates for "Multi-Object" and "Insert" tasks, suggesting improved robustness to sim-to-real gaps. The hypothesis is that errors in one hand's action are localized, allowing the other hand to remain unaffected and potentially recover. For "Lift," a single-object task where both hands are interdependent, a shared space was slightly preferred.
- **State-Prediction Auxiliary Loss (Table I):** Incorporating this loss consistently improved performance across all tasks in both simulation and real-world. This confirms that leveraging ground-truth object states available in simulation helps in regularizing the visual encoder and anchoring it to task-relevant geometric features, enhancing policy robustness.

**D. Impact of Dataset Size**
- Experiments with varying dataset sizes (500, 2,000, 5,000 trajectories) showed that the transformer-based architecture scaled effectively with larger datasets (Table II).
- Larger datasets improved performance in both simulation and real-world, particularly for more complex tasks like "Insert," which exhibited greater performance degradation with smaller datasets. This is attributed to the inclusion of more robust strategies and better coverage of rare initializations in larger datasets of successful trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents RLDC, a scalable pipeline for generating high-quality, diverse synthetic data for language-conditioned bimanual dexterous manipulation. This approach directly addresses a critical bottleneck in robotics: the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality datasets necessary for training generalist and foundation models.

The significance and potential impact of this research can be delineated as follows:

1. **Enabling Scalable Data Generation for Complex Tasks:** RLDC provides a systematic method for autonomously generating diverse and robot-feasible manipulation trajectories in simulation. This overcomes the limitations of manual teleoperation (cost, time) and human video demonstrations (embodiment mismatch, retargeting errors), offering a scalable alternative for creating datasets for challenging bimanual dexterous manipulation, which is essential for generalist robot development.

2. **Advancing Generalizable Reward Design:** The introduction of a set of generalizable reward terms significantly reduces the engineering overhead associated with handcrafting task-specific rewards. This innovation promotes the rapid prototyping and scaling of RL-based data collection across a wider array of manipulation tasks, accelerating the development cycle for new robotic skills. The empirical results demonstrate that these generalizable rewards can achieve performance comparable to or sometimes exceeding finely tuned task-specific rewards, while drastically improving implementation efficiency.

3. **Enhancing Data Diversity and Multimodality:** By coupling extensive domain randomization across physical and visual parameters with automated, LLM-augmented language labeling, RLDC generates multimodal datasets that are rich in variability. This diversity is crucial for training robust policies that generalize to unseen scenarios and respond to natural language instructions, moving beyond single-task, fixed-environment capabilities.

4. **Improving Sim-to-Real Transfer and Policy Robustness:** The distillation of RL-generated data into a diffusion-based multi-task policy, particularly with a decoupled transformer architecture and an auxiliary state-prediction loss, contributes to robust sim-to-real transfer. The auxiliary loss leverages ground-truth information available only in simulation to anchor the visual encoder, leading to more reliable visual perception. The decoupled action heads enhance real-world robustness by compartmentalizing potential errors, allowing the policy to recover more effectively from unexpected behaviors.

5. **Paving the Way for Robotic Foundation Models:** The ability to efficiently generate large-scale, high-quality, and diverse datasets with language conditioning is a fundamental requirement for training robotic foundation models. RLDC's contributions directly support this direction, offering a methodology for supplying the extensive data volumes these models necessitate for learning broad, transferable skills.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge two key limitations. First, setting up simulation environments, including tuning physics parameters and preparing assets, remains time-consuming, even with reduced reward engineering. Future work could explore automating real-to-sim reconstruction or leveraging community resources for reusable assets. Second, RLDC inherits behavioral biases from its RL teachers, meaning the generated trajectories, while robot-feasible, may not always be human-like and could exhibit unnecessary finger motions or abrupt releases. Future research could investigate combining RL-generated trajectories with human demonstrations, or integrating smoothness and contact regularization to produce more natural and refined manipulation behaviors, especially for delicate objects or tasks requiring precise in-hand manipulation.
