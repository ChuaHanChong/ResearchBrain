# Optimizing Instructions and Demonstrations for Multi-Stage Language Model Programs

> [!summary] Summary
> MIPRO optimizes the free-form instructions and few-shot demonstrations of every module in a multi-stage language model program, using only a final task metric and no module-level labels or gradients. It bootstraps demonstrations, proposes instructions grounded in data and program summaries, and picks combinations with a Bayesian surrogate on mini-batches. It beats baselines on five of seven tasks with Llama-3-8B, by up to 13%.

## Problem

- Existing prompt optimizers such as APE, OPRO and EvoPrompt assume a single prompt with a direct metric, so they do not apply to multi-stage programs that lack per-module labels.
- The proposal space of free-form instructions is intractably large, and it grows with every added module, so proposing a few high-quality candidates is hard.
- Credit assignment is unsolved: only a final task metric is available, so the effect of each module's prompt on it is latent, and greedy per-stage search is inefficient.

## Method

- **MIPRO** ==bootstraps few-shot demonstration sets== by running training inputs through the program and keeping traces that pass the metric. It also proposes instructions with a ==grounded proposer LM==, given dataset and program summaries, demos and tips.
- A ==Tree-structured Parzen Estimator== ==Bayesian surrogate== learns how task score depends on each module's chosen instruction and demonstration set. It is scored on ==stochastic mini-batches==, with periodic full-trainset evaluation of the best candidate.
- **MIPRO++** ==meta-optimizes proposal hyperparameters== (whether to use dataset or program summaries, proposer temperature, tip, demo choice) with the same Bayesian model. Ablation variants include **0-Shot MIPRO**, **Bayesian Bootstrap** and **Module-Level OPRO**.

## Results

- MIPRO outperforms baselines on five of seven diverse tasks, with gains of up to **13%** accuracy on Llama-3-8B. Examples: HotPotQA Conditional test **23.3** versus 10.4 for Bootstrap Random Search, and Iris-Typo test **68.7** versus 58.7.
- Demonstration-only optimization usually beats instruction-only: Bootstrap Random Search beats the best instruction-only optimizer in all but one task. The exception is HotPotQA Conditional, where 0-shot instruction optimization wins on test (**14.6** versus 10.4).
- Grounding helps HotPotQA and HoVer but hurts ScoNe. 0-Shot MIPRO++ recovers ScoNe performance (test **75.7** versus 71.5 for 0-Shot MIPRO). Results use Wilcoxon tests over **5** runs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Bootstrapped few-shot demonstrations carry most of the gain in multi-stage programs, more than instruction rewriting, because they show successful reasoning behavior and not only the task format.
> - Instruction optimization matters most for conditional rules that are non-obvious and cannot be captured by a few examples. Optimizers still need a good seed prompt to start from.
> - Separating proposal from credit assignment, with a surrogate model for credit, lets the proposer focus on candidates. The best proposal strategy varies by task, so tune it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Natural Language Processing (NLP) has seen a paradigm shift with the advent of large language models (LLMs). While LLMs excel at many tasks, complex problem-solving often requires chaining multiple LM calls into sophisticated multi-stage pipelines, known as Language Model Programs. These programs leverage techniques like Chain-of-Thought (Wei et al., 2022) and tool use (Qin et al., 2023) to enhance reasoning and capabilities.

A significant bottleneck in developing these LM programs is "prompt engineering"—the manual, iterative process of crafting effective prompts for each modular LM call. This manual effort is laborious, error-prone, and often sub-optimal, especially when prompts need to be jointly effective across multiple stages of a pipeline.

Recent research has attempted to automate prompt optimization. Early works include gradient-guided search (Shin et al., 2020), reranking brute-force search (Gao et al., 2021), and evolutionary algorithms (Fernando et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024). More pertinent to this paper are methods that use other LMs as "optimizers," such as APE (Zhou et al., 2023) and OPRO (Yang et al., 2024). However, a significant limitation of most existing prompt optimizers is their inability to directly apply to *multi-stage LM programs* where module-level labels or evaluation metrics are unavailable. These optimizers typically assume a single prompt for a single task, or that intermediate steps have explicit ground truth for evaluation.

This paper directly addresses this crucial gap. While the DSPy framework (Khattab et al., 2024) has already provided a programming model for arbitrary LM pipelines and optimizers for demonstrations and LM weights, it lacked robust methods for tuning free-form *instructions* in multi-prompt pipelines. This research builds upon DSPy by introducing novel strategies and optimizers specifically designed for this complex problem. Unlike methods requiring access to LM weights or log-probabilities (e.g., Sordoni et al., 2023), this work operates under more general and practical assumptions: it only requires the LM program itself, an end-to-end metric, and a training set of inputs and final outputs, making it widely applicable to black-box LM APIs.

By tackling the joint optimization of instructions and demonstrations across multiple stages without intermediate supervision, this paper pushes the boundaries of automated LLM pipeline construction, moving beyond manual prompt engineering towards more systematic and scalable development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **develop efficient algorithms for optimizing prompts, specifically free-form instructions and few-shot demonstrations, within arbitrary multi-stage Language Model Programs**. This optimization aims to maximize a downstream, end-to-end metric, crucially without requiring access to module-level labels, gradients, or internal LM states (like log-probabilities).

The primary motivations for this work stem from the inherent challenges of building complex LM programs:
1. **Inefficiency of Manual Prompt Engineering:** Current practices heavily rely on manual trial-and-error to craft prompts for individual modules. This process is time-consuming, requires significant human expertise, and often leads to sub-optimal performance for the entire pipeline.
2. **Lack of Intermediate Supervision:** In multi-stage LM programs, intermediate steps often do not have readily available ground truth labels or evaluation metrics. This makes it difficult to apply standard optimization techniques that require fine-grained feedback for each module.
3. **Intractably Large Search Space:** The space of possible free-form instructions is combinatorially vast. When combined with the need to optimize prompts across multiple modules in a pipeline, the search space becomes prohibitively large for brute-force or simple heuristic approaches.
4. **Credit Assignment Problem:** When an LM program fails or succeeds, it is challenging to attribute the performance to specific instructions or demonstrations within individual modules, especially without intermediate supervision. Effectively allocating search effort across these interdependent variables is a major hurdle.

To address these, the paper formally defines the prompt optimization problem for LM programs and identifies two core challenges:
- **The Proposal Challenge:** How to efficiently sample high-quality candidate prompts from the immense space of possibilities.
- **The Credit Assignment Challenge:** How to infer the impact of individual module configurations on the overall program performance to guide the optimization process.

The authors aim to provide practical, robust solutions that operate under weak assumptions, making them broadly applicable to real-world LM program development scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes a general optimization framework (Algorithm 1) for LM programs, which serves as a blueprint for various instantiations. This framework involves an optimizer (`M`) that iteratively proposes modifications to the program's parameters (prompts), evaluates the modified program on a mini-batch of training data, and updates its internal state to inform future proposals.

The methodology is built upon novel strategies to tackle the identified "Proposal" and "Credit Assignment" challenges:

#### 3.1. Strategies for the Proposal Problem

The core idea is to leverage another LM (a 'proposer' LM, typically a stronger model like GPT-3.5 or GPT-4) to generate effective prompt candidates.
- **Bootstrapping Demonstrations:** This strategy, adapted from previous DSPy work (Khattab et al., 2022, 2024), involves running training inputs through the LM program. If the end-to-end output is successful (scores above a threshold `λ`), the intermediate input/output traces for each module are collected as potential few-shot demonstrations. The optimization then focuses on selecting effective combinations of these bootstrapped demonstrations.
- **Grounding:** To guide the proposer LM in crafting performant *instructions*, relevant context is provided. This context includes:
 - Characterizations of patterns in the raw dataset.
 - Summaries of the program's control flow (its modular structure).
 - Bootstrapped program demonstrations.
 - Histories of previously evaluated per-stage prompts along with their scores. This "grounding" aims to make instruction proposals more task- and program-aware.
- **Learning To Propose (Meta-optimization):** This advanced strategy involves optimizing the hyperparameters of the *proposal process itself*. A Bayesian model is used to learn which proposal settings (e.g., proposer LM temperature, inclusion of data/program summaries, specific "tips" on prompt engineering) lead to the best overall program performance.

#### 3.2. Strategies for the Credit Assignment Problem

To effectively allocate search effort across multiple interdependent modules, the paper explores three main approaches:
- **Greedy:** (Explored but not adopted due to inefficiency and limited effectiveness in preliminary experiments). This would involve optimizing one module at a time.
- **Surrogate (Bayesian Learning):** This is a key strategy for MIPRO. A Bayesian surrogate model (specifically, Optuna's Tree Structured Parzen Optimizer) learns to predict the quality of different combinations of instructions and demonstrations across modules from past evaluations. This allows for efficient joint optimization over a discrete set of proposed parameters by focusing exploration on promising regions of the search space, incorporating uncertainty, and being robust to noise (e.g., from mini-batch evaluation).
- **History-Based:** As seen in OPRO, the proposer LM is directly provided with a history of proposed instructions and their associated program-level scores. The assumption is that a sufficiently powerful LM can infer credit assignment and propose better instructions based on this history.

#### 3.3. Specific Optimizer Instantiations

The paper evaluates several concrete optimizers:
- **Bootstrap Random Search (Baseline):** Primarily relies on Bootstrapping Demonstrations combined with random search to select the best few-shot examples.
- **Module-Level OPRO:** An extension of OPRO where each module's prompt is optimized independently, using the program's overall score as a proxy for individual module performance (history-based credit assignment). Includes a variant `−G` to ablate grounding.
- **MIPRO (Multi-prompt Instruction PRoposal Optimizer):** The flagship optimizer. It combines **Bootstrapping Demonstrations** and **Grounded Instruction Proposal** with a **Bayesian Surrogate Model** for joint optimization of instructions and demonstrations across all modules. It also employs stochastic mini-batch evaluation for efficiency.
- **MIPRO Variants:**
 - **0-Shot MIPRO:** A variant of MIPRO that optimizes *instructions only*.
 - **Bayesian Bootstrap:** A variant of MIPRO that optimizes *demonstrations only*.
 - **0-Shot MIPRO++:** Leverages the "Learning To Propose" strategy, where a Bayesian model optimizes the *hyperparameters* of the instruction proposal process itself, rather than the instructions directly.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmark:** A new benchmark suite of seven diverse tasks is introduced, ranging from multi-hop question answering (HotPotQA, HoVer) and natural language inference (ScoNe) to classical classification tasks (Iris, Heart Disease). These include both single and multi-stage LM programs, and specific variants (e.g., HotPotQA Conditional, Iris-Typo) designed to test the robustness of instruction optimization.
- **Models:** Llama3-8B is used as the *task model* (the LM within the program being optimized). GPT-3.5 (and GPT-4o for challenging bootstrapping) serves as the *proposer LM*. This setup reflects a common real-world scenario where a smaller, open-source model is deployed, but a more powerful (and potentially more expensive) model is used for development/optimization.
- **Evaluation:** Optimizers are run for a fixed budget of "full evaluation trials" (20-50, translating to more mini-batch trials for MIPRO). Performance is averaged across 5 runs, and statistical significance is assessed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yield several key insights and practical lessons, summarized in Table 2 and discussed in Section 6 of the paper. MIPRO, the proposed joint optimizer, demonstrates superior performance across a majority of the benchmark tasks.

1. **Optimizing Bootstrapped Demonstrations is Crucial (Lesson 1):** For most tasks, optimizing few-shot demonstrations alone (e.g., using Bootstrap Random Search or Bayesian Bootstrap) yields significantly better performance than optimizing instructions alone. The authors confirm this with statistical tests, noting that Bootstrap Random Search beats the best instruction-only optimizer in all but one case (HotPotQA Conditional). This suggests that high-quality examples convey successful reasoning behavior more effectively than just task format.

2. **Joint Optimization (MIPRO) Generally Yields the Best Performance (Lesson 2):** MIPRO, which jointly optimizes both instructions and few-shot examples, typically achieves the best overall performance. It outperforms other optimizers on five of the seven diverse LM programs, by as much as 13% accuracy in some cases. Exceptions include HotPotQA (where the final Q&A module might be in-distribution for many models, making instructions less impactful) and Heart Disease (where the initial seed instruction was too simple, and instruction optimizers struggled to infer complex classification criteria).

3. **Instruction Optimization is Key for Conditional Rules (Lesson 3):** The study highlights scenarios where instruction optimization becomes particularly vital. For tasks with subtle or conditional rules that are not easily inferred from a few examples, such as "HotPotQA Conditional" (which requires specific answer formatting based on answer type) or "Iris-Typo" (where the seed prompt has a misspelling), instruction optimization significantly boosts performance. In these cases, optimizing a seed prompt that conveys the rules is crucial, as optimizers are not yet able to fully infer all task rules themselves. The Iris-Typo experiment notably shows instruction optimization correcting mistakes in the initial prompt.

4. **Grounding Helps Instruction Proposal, but the Optimal Strategy Varies (Lesson 4):** Providing grounding context (data summaries, program flow, tips) to the proposer LM generally improves instruction quality. However, the best grounding strategy is task-dependent. For instance, grounding was essential for HotPotQA and HoVer but surprisingly detrimental for ScoNe with Module-Level OPRO. This variability motivates meta-optimization approaches like 0-Shot MIPRO++, which can learn task-specific proposal strategies. Analysis of learned importance scores (Appendix D) reveals that the choice of bootstrapped demonstrations in the meta-prompt and "tips" are often highly important for proposal quality, while the importance of other parameters (like dataset summary) varies significantly across tasks.

5. **More to Learn About Optimizer Dynamics (Lesson 5):** While MIPRO shows strong results, the comparative performance among other optimizers (Module-Level OPRO, 0-Shot MIPRO, 0-Shot MIPRO++, Bayesian Bootstrap) is mixed and often not statistically significant. This suggests that the optimal optimizer might depend on specific constraints, such as the available budget. For example, 0-Shot MIPRO's mini-batching might excel in very low-budget settings, allowing more exploration, while 0-Shot MIPRO++ might shine with larger budgets that allow for extensive learning of optimal proposal dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of AI and NLP, with substantial potential impact:

1. **Formalization and Framework:** The paper provides a clear formalization of the multi-stage LM program prompt optimization problem, which was previously ill-defined in the context of lacking intermediate supervision. It introduces a systematic design space for optimizers, offering a conceptual framework for future research.

2. **Advancing Automated Prompt Engineering:** By effectively tackling the "proposal" and "credit assignment" challenges without requiring log-probabilities or intermediate labels, the work significantly advances the automation of prompt engineering. This capability is critical for scaling the development and deployment of complex LLM applications. It moves beyond manual trial-and-error, making the process more systematic, efficient, and robust.

3. **Practical Tooling (DSPy Integration):** The direct integration of the developed optimizers and benchmark into the open-source DSPy framework (dspy.ai) is a major practical contribution. This ensures reproducibility, accessibility, and immediate utility for practitioners and researchers. It democratizes the ability to build and optimize state-of-the-art LM pipelines without deep expertise in prompt engineering.

4. **Empirical Insights for Practitioners:** The five derived lessons offer actionable guidance for developers working with LM programs. Understanding when to prioritize demonstration optimization, when instructions are critical (especially for conditional logic), and the nuanced role of grounding strategies can save significant development time and improve performance.

5. **Robustness and Efficiency:** MIPRO's ability to jointly optimize instructions and demonstrations, coupled with mini-batch evaluation and Bayesian optimization, contributes to more robust and efficient LM program development. This means better performance with fewer LM calls, which is crucial given the computational cost of LLMs.

**Potential Impact:**
The implications of this work are far-reaching. It has the potential to:
- **Lower the Barrier to Entry:** Simplify the creation of complex LLM applications for a wider range of developers, reducing the need for highly specialized prompt engineering skills.
- **Improve LLM Application Quality:** Enable the development of more accurate, robust, and reliable multi-stage LLM systems across diverse tasks.
- **Accelerate Research:** Provide a solid benchmark and open-source tools for further research into automated LLM optimization, including exploring different budget constraints, task/proposer LM combinations, and more sophisticated learning of task dynamics.
- **Enable Adaptive LLM Systems:** Lay groundwork for LLM programs that can automatically adapt and refine their internal components (prompts) based on performance feedback, leading to more autonomous and self-improving AI systems.

While acknowledging limitations such as fixed budget analysis and reliance on seed prompts for complex rules, this paper represents a significant step towards compiler-like automation for LLM programs, moving the field closer to truly programmable and optimizable language model pipelines.
