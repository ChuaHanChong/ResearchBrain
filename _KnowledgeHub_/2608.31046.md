# Does On-Policy Distillation Really Distill? From Noisy Teacher to Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> On-Policy Self-Adaptation (OPSA) is a supervision-free token-level RL method for LLM reasoning. Motivated by evidence that on-policy distillation gains come from suppressing low-probability tokens rather than teacher knowledge, OPSA assigns entropy-adaptive negative advantages to the lowest-20% log-probability tokens, sharpening confident positions while redistributing mass at high-entropy forks. On Qwen3-1.7B it lifts AIME24 Avg@32 by 35.41 points, beating on-policy distillation by 16.77.

## Problem

- RLVR gives sparse response-level rewards whose group-normalized advantages vanish when all sampled responses share the same correctness outcome, weakening signal and destabilizing long-horizon reasoning training.
- On-policy distillation requires white-box teacher logits and a shared vocabulary, while self-distillation variants need hints built from extra sampling or annotation, risking answer leakage.
- Both paradigms make the teacher score student-sampled prefixes that are inherently off-policy for it, leaving the reliability of that token-level supervision unverified.

## Method

- Noise audit defines ==supervision noise== as advantage sign disagreeing with the verifier on boxed answer tokens, then partitions trajectories into noisy-only and clean-only ==on-policy distillation== runs.
- ==Token selection== restricts updates to the 20% of student-sampled tokens with lowest log-probability, justified by logit-level gradients that vanish for high-log-probability, near-zero-advantage tokens.
- The **OPSA** objective assigns ==entropy-adaptive negative advantages==, fixed base minus a per-rollout normalized entropy term, so advantage magnitude grows with token entropy; no teacher, reward, or hint needed.

## Results

- Teacher noise rate rises with scale: **30.6%** for the 4B teacher, **34.7%** for 30B-A3B, **50.6%** for 235B-A22B, yet noisy-only, clean-only and standard runs converge comparably.
- On Qwen3-1.7B OPSA reaches **48.85** Avg@32 on AIME24 versus **33.96** GRPO, **32.08** OPD, **33.33** OPSD, averaging **11.04** points over the best baseline at **46.3s**/step vs **61.2** OPD and **186.2** GRPO.
- Ablations confirm the mechanism: entropy-positive coupling reaches **50.0** Avg@4 against **35.13** for distillation, masking reflective fork tokens erases gains, and **20-40%** token ratios all exceed **45**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense token-level gains credited to knowledge transfer may come from distribution reshaping instead; always test whether a teacher-free negative-advantage control reproduces your distillation result.
> - Aggregate entropy is a poor exploration proxy: OPSA trains at lower entropy yet higher Pass@32, because how uncertainty is allocated across token positions beats its total.
> - Redistributing probability mass needs mass to exist, so expect limited benefit on heavily post-trained sharp policies; inference-time temperature or min-p tuning cannot substitute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Training a model to solve multi-step math problems usually relies on a correct/incorrect score. That score gives little guidance about where a long solution went wrong. On-policy distillation (OPD) offers feedback on individual tokens by having a teacher score a response the student generated. However, OPD requires access to a teacher's logits and extra teacher computation.

This paper changes who supplies that signal. The student's own token probabilities and uncertainty decide which sampled tokens to discourage, rather than a teacher, verifier, or hint judging them. The method is called OPSA. On AIME24, non-thinking Qwen3-1.7B OPSA scores 48.85 Avg@32, versus 13.44 for the base model and 32.08 for OPD.

Left: RLVR, OPD, OPSD (a hint-conditioned student acts as teacher), and OPSA, which uses a self-adapted negative advantage with no external teacher, hint, or verifiable reward. Right: AIME24 Avg@4 against training steps for OPD and OPSA, and Avg@32 and Pass@32 for Qwen3-1.7B versus OPSA on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25.

### 2. What the teacher signal shows

In OPD, the student generates a trajectory and a teacher supplies token log-probabilities. The difference between the two produces token-level positive or negative advantages.

The paper's noise test is narrow. It counts a negative teacher advantage on a correct boxed answer, or a positive one on an incorrect boxed answer. It does not judge every intermediate reasoning token. In the Qwen3-1.7B analysis, the measured noise rate rises from 30.6% for a Qwen3-4B teacher to 50.6% for Qwen3-235B-A22B. The authors attribute the trend to growing student–teacher distribution mismatch. The scale comparison does not establish that as the only cause.

The authors then filtered the training data. Standard OPD, training only on trajectories with noisy signals, and training only on trajectories without them all reach comparable AIME24 Avg@4 after similar numbers of gradient steps. This is evidence of insensitivity to the filtering. It is not proof that teacher information never contributes.

### 3. The self-adaptation recipe

Many high-log-probability sampled tokens have negligible gradients. In this analysis, 29.2% of OPD tokens have exactly zero advantage and 51.7% have magnitude below $10^{-4}$. Restricting OPD to the lowest-log-probability 20% of tokens gives performance comparable to full-token OPD. On that selected set, a fixed negative advantage improves AIME24 Avg@4, while a fixed positive advantage collapses response length and destabilizes training.

OPSA builds on these controls in five steps:

1. Generate a response from the policy.
1. Record each sampled token's log-probability and entropy.
1. Select the lowest-log-probability 20% of positions.
1. Assign those tokens negative advantages whose magnitude increases with their entropy relative to the other selected positions in that rollout.
1. Leave the remaining tokens out of the policy loss.

In short, log-probability picks the tokens and entropy sets the strength of the negative update. Training uses prompts but no teacher logits, answer labels, verifier reward, or reference hints.

An ablation tested the direction of the entropy weighting. The high-entropy-weighted variant reaches 50.0% Avg@4, versus 35.13% for OPD. Reversing the direction is less stable and ends slightly below the fixed-negative variant. This supports the value of the entropy-adaptive assignment in that run, not a universal causal account of reasoning.

Entropy ablation over training steps: AIME24 Avg@4 (left), response length (middle), and gradient norm (right) for standard OPD and δ=1, δ=0, δ=−1. δ=1 finishes at 50.0% and OPD at 35.13%.

### 4. Payoff and limits

Avg@32 is mean accuracy across 32 sampled answers per problem. Pass@32 is the proportion of problems with at least one correct answer among those 32. On AIME24, OPSA raises Pass@32 from 40.00 to 80.00, compared with 73.33 for OPD. Across AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25, mean Avg@32 is 35.83 for OPSA versus 24.79 for the best baseline, GRPO. In these comparisons, OPD used Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the teacher.

The efficiency picture is mixed:

- **Training:** OPSA removes the teacher model and its forward passes. It reports a Qwen3-1.7B training step time of 46.3 seconds, versus 61.2 for OPD and 186.2 for GRPO. That is per-step time, not total training time.
- **Inference:** OPSA does not use fewer tokens. It averages 23,205 tokens versus 15,286 for OPD, and 6.58 seconds of inference time versus 5.73.

Experiments reach models up to 9B. The authors leave larger models, mixture-of-experts models, and already very sharp, low-entropy policies uncertain.

The contribution is a teacher-free alternative that improves the policy by acting on its own token-level uncertainty. The experiments motivate reexamining whether OPD's gains come solely from teacher imitation. They do not settle that question for every distillation method or model.
