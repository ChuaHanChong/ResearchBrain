# Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems

> [!summary] Summary
> OpenAI researchers developed GSM8K, a new dataset of 8,500 grade school math word problems, and introduced a verification method to improve large language models' multi-step reasoning. The verification approach, which involves training a separate model to select the best among multiple generated solutions, enabled a 6B parameter model to surpass a finetuned 175B parameter model's performance on the dataset.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) consistently struggle with multi-step logical and mathematical reasoning due to error propagation in their autoregressive generation process.
- Existing math word problem (MWP) datasets were often small, lacked linguistic diversity, or provided only final answers/equations, hindering robust training and evaluation of LLMs' informal reasoning abilities.
- Achieving high accuracy on complex reasoning tasks through brute-force scaling of LLM parameters alone appeared computationally prohibitive, with models over-confidently making errors.

## Method

- Created **GSM8K**, a new dataset of 8,500 high-quality, linguistically diverse grade school math word problems with natural language solutions and ==calculator annotations==.
- Developed a ==verification== method where a finetuned ==generator== model produces multiple candidate solutions, and a separate ==verifier== model evaluates their correctness to select the best one.
- Trained the verifier model with a ==joint objective==, combining standard language modeling with a ==correctness prediction== task, and utilized a separate generator to prevent overfitting.

## Results

- GSM8K proved challenging for state-of-the-art LLMs, with initial finetuning achieving low accuracy and showing poor scaling for high solve rates (e.g., **80%** estimated to require **10^16 parameters** for finetuning alone).
- The verification method consistently provided a significant performance boost, with a **6B** parameter model using verification outperforming a finetuned **175B** parameter model.
- Residual dropout (**20%**) acted as a strong regularizer, notably improving performance and mitigating overfitting for both finetuning and verification.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training verifiers using a "generate-and-rank" approach offers a substantial performance improvement over direct finetuning for multi-step reasoning tasks.
> - Verification exhibits more favorable data scaling laws, allowing smaller models with verification to achieve performance comparable to much larger finetuned models (e.g., 6B with verification > 175B finetuned).
> - Token-level verification and a joint language modeling and correctness prediction objective for the verifier lead to better performance, suggesting value in evaluating intermediate reasoning steps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of natural language processing (NLP) has seen revolutionary advancements with the advent of large language models, which have achieved near-human performance on a wide array of tasks. However, despite their impressive linguistic fluency and general knowledge, these models notoriously struggle with tasks requiring robust, multi-step logical or mathematical reasoning. This paper directly addresses this critical weakness, placing it firmly at the forefront of current LLM research.

**Challenges in Mathematical Reasoning for LLMs:**
Existing LLMs, particularly those based on autoregressive generation, exhibit a fundamental limitation in mathematical reasoning: their sequential generation process is highly susceptible to error propagation. A single arithmetic mistake early in a multi-step problem can render the entire solution incorrect, with no inherent mechanism for self-correction. Prior research (e.g., Hendrycks et al., 2021, who introduced the challenging MATH dataset) has highlighted this deficiency, suggesting that achieving high performance through brute-force scaling of model parameters alone might be prohibitively expensive, requiring astronomically large models. This paper frames the problem as a search for "methods with more favorable scaling laws."

**Addressing Dataset Limitations:**
Before this work, many math word problem (MWP) datasets suffered from critical flaws. Earlier datasets were often small, limiting their utility for training large neural models. Larger datasets sometimes sacrificed quality, containing templatized problems that lacked linguistic diversity, or offered solutions only as equations or final answers, not natural language explanations (e.g., Dolphin18K, AQuA-RAT, Ape210K). While some recent datasets like ASDiv (Miao et al., 2021) and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) have improved quality and diversity, GSM8K carves out a niche by offering high-quality, linguistically diverse grade-school level problems with natural language solutions, a difficulty sweet spot that is challenging for current LLMs but tractable for measurable progress. It directly addresses the need for a dataset that reveals the "informal reasoning ability" of LLMs.

**Advancing Solution Methodologies:**
The paper positions its proposed "verification" method against a backdrop of existing approaches. Traditional methods for MWPs often involved recurrent seq2seq models or specialized encoder-decoder architectures, sometimes augmented with pretrained encoders like BERT. More recently, efforts have focused on additional pretraining tasks (e.g., AMPS corpus, pre-K to college curricula) to imbue models with better mathematical skills.

Crucially, this work is positioned in relation to concurrent research exploring "sample-and-rank" or "generate & rank" paradigms. Shen et al. (2021a) applied a similar approach to math word problems, jointly training a model to generate and rank solutions. This paper builds upon and distinguishes itself from such concurrent work by:
1. **Focusing on natural language solutions:** Emphasizing richer, human-interpretable solutions over pure mathematical expressions, enabling models to develop "verbal analytical skills."
2. **Demonstrating superior data scaling:** Providing strong empirical evidence that verification scales more effectively with additional data.
3. **Using separate generator and verifier networks:** A design choice aimed at preventing the generator from overfitting, which is a key technical distinction.

In essence, this work contributes to the broader research landscape by providing a new high-quality benchmark dataset and a robust method that pushes the boundaries of LLM capabilities in multi-step reasoning, addressing both data and algorithmic limitations within the current paradigm of large neural networks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research paper are threefold:

1. **Diagnose and Characterize LLM Failures in Mathematical Reasoning:** The authors observe that despite the impressive general capabilities of state-of-the-art language models, they consistently falter when confronted with multi-step mathematical reasoning problems. A key motivation is to pinpoint the exact nature of these failures, such as the catastrophic mistakes stemming from the autoregressive generation process and the inability of models to self-correct.

2. **Introduce a High-Quality Dataset to Facilitate Research:** Recognizing the shortcomings of existing mathematical reasoning datasets—which often suffer from small size, low quality, problem templatization, or lack of natural language solutions—the authors aimed to create a new, superior benchmark. The GSM8K dataset was developed to be high-quality, linguistically diverse, and of moderate difficulty, providing a challenging yet tractable testbed for language models. This objective is driven by the need for a robust tool to "diagnose the failures of current models and support research."

3. **Propose and Evaluate a Novel Method (Verification) to Enhance Mathematical Reasoning Performance:** Given the limitations of purely generative approaches and the unfavorable scaling laws predicted for achieving high accuracy through increased model size alone, the central motivation was to develop a more effective method. The "verification" approach seeks to overcome the inherent fragility of autoregressive generation by allowing models to generate multiple candidate solutions and then select the most plausible one using a separate judgment mechanism. This aims to achieve "more favorable scaling laws" for performance improvement.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the desire to push past the current limitations of large language models in complex reasoning, providing both a new diagnostic tool (GSM8K) and a promising algorithmic solution (verification) to advance the field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach, combining dataset creation with a systematic comparison of two primary methods for solving math word problems: finetuning (as a baseline) and a novel verification-based approach.

#### 3.1. GSM8K Dataset Creation

The authors first introduce **GSM8K**, a dataset of 8,500 high-quality grade school math word problems.
- **Scale and Split:** 7,500 problems for training and 1,000 for testing.
- **Problem Characteristics:** Each problem requires 2 to 8 steps to solve, primarily using basic arithmetic operations. They are designed to be solvable by a middle school student.
- **Design Principles:**
 - **High Quality:** Human-created problems (by freelance contractors and Surge AI), with extensive quality control (answer agreement checks) to minimize errors.
 - **High Diversity:** Active avoidance of linguistic templates, ensuring each problem is relatively unique to make test performance a more relevant metric.
 - **Moderate Difficulty:** Challenging for state-of-the-art LLMs but not intractable, allowing for observation of data scaling trends. No concepts beyond early Algebra, mostly solvable without explicit variables.
 - **Natural Language Solutions:** Solutions are collected in natural language, encouraging models to develop verbal analytical skills and produce interpretable solutions.
- **Calculator Annotations:** To mitigate models' arithmetic calculation errors, the training set is injected with "calculator annotations" (e.g., `<<20+10=30>>`). At test time, a simulated calculator evaluates these expressions, overriding model sampling. These annotations were auto-generated, not human-provided.

#### 3.2. Finetuning (Baseline Method)

This serves as the primary baseline against which the verification method is compared.
- **Model Initialization:** Models from the GPT-3 family (primarily 6B and 175B parameters) are used as initializations.
- **Training Objective:** Standard language modeling objective, minimizing cross-entropy loss over training tokens.
- **Test Time:** A single low-temperature (T=0, i.e., greedy) solution is autoregressively sampled for each test problem, and its final answer is checked for correctness.
- **Observations:** Performance improves with model size and training data. However, extrapolation suggests extremely large models (10^16 parameters) or vast amounts of data would be needed for high accuracy. Overfitting occurs with more epochs, leading to overconfidence and degraded performance when sampling multiple solutions (test@N performance).

#### 3.3. Verification (Proposed Method)

This method aims to overcome the limitations of finetuning by introducing a separate mechanism to evaluate solution correctness.
- **Core Idea:** Generate multiple (e.g., 100) candidate solutions for a problem, then use a trained "verifier" model to score each solution's correctness probability, and finally select the highest-ranked solution.
- **Verifier Training Pipeline (Figure 4):**
 1. **Generator Finetuning:** A language model (the "generator") is finetuned for 2 epochs on the GSM8K training set. This limited training duration is chosen because solution diversity (essential for verifier training) starts to collapse after this point due to overfitting.
 2. **Sample Generation & Labeling:** For each training problem, 100 completions are sampled from the finetuned generator. Each sampled solution is labeled as "correct" or "incorrect" solely based on whether it yields the correct final numerical answer.
 3. **Verifier Training:** A separate model (the "verifier") is trained for a single epoch on this dataset of problem-solution pairs and their correctness labels.
- **Verifier Architecture & Objective:** The verifier is also a GPT-3 family model. It is trained with a *joint objective*: a language modeling objective (like the generator) combined with a correctness prediction objective. For correctness prediction, a small scalar head outputs a prediction for each token, and the loss is based on whether the solution leads to the correct final answer. The language modeling objective is included as an auxiliary task, found to be beneficial.
- **Test Time Verification:** 100 completions are sampled (typically at T=0.7) from the generator, ranked by the verifier, and the one with the highest verifier score is outputted.
- **Key Design Choices:** Separate generator and verifier models are used to prevent generator overfitting. The verifier can be initialized from the same pretrained LM as the generator or from the finetuned generator itself (the latter performed slightly better).

#### 3.4. Verification Ablations

The authors conduct several ablation studies to understand the optimal configuration for the verifier:
- **Token-level vs. Solution-level Verification:** Comparing training a verifier to predict correctness after every token ("token-level") versus only after the final token ("solution-level"). Token-level is found to perform better in the long run, hypothesized to encourage judging reasoning throughout the solution.
- **Joint Objective vs. Verification-Only:** Comparing training the verifier with both language modeling and correctness prediction objectives versus only the latter. The joint objective is a "strict improvement."
- **Generator vs. Verifier Model Size:** Investigating the impact of varying the size of the generator and verifier independently. A large generator with a small verifier performs better than a small generator with a large verifier, suggesting the generator's quality is paramount.

#### 3.5. Additional Experiments

- **Test Time Compute:** Exploring the impact of generating more candidate solutions (up to 3200) and using majority voting among the top-ranked solutions to determine the final answer. Performance generally improves up to a point before degrading.
- **Regularization (Dropout):** Investigating the effect of residual dropout (20%) on both finetuning and verification. Dropout significantly improves performance by acting as a strong regularizer, particularly for solution-level verifiers and mitigating overfitting. Models are additionally pretrained with dropout for these experiments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings regarding the capabilities of large language models in mathematical reasoning and the efficacy of the proposed verification approach:

1. **GSM8K's Challenge for LLMs:** Despite consisting of conceptually simple grade school math, the linguistic diversity of GSM8K makes it a challenging dataset for state-of-the-art transformer models. Initial finetuning performance on this dataset is low, demonstrating that even the largest models struggle with robust multi-step reasoning.

2. **Limitations of Finetuning:**
 - **Poor Scaling for High Accuracy:** While larger models and more training data generally improve finetuning performance, extrapolations suggest that achieving high solve rates (e.g., 80%) would require impractically large models (estimated 10^16 parameters for 80% on full GSM8K) or immense amounts of data.
 - **Overfitting and Overconfidence:** Prolonged finetuning leads to models becoming overconfident and less calibrated, which degrades performance when multiple samples are considered (test@100 performance drops significantly after a few epochs), even as single-sample performance (test@1) continues to improve. This highlights a critical weakness for generative models in complex reasoning.

3. **Verification Significantly Improves Performance:**
 - **Substantial Boost:** The verification method provides a significant performance boost compared to the finetuning baseline, especially with larger datasets. For instance, on the full GSM8K training set, a 6B model with verification slightly outperforms a finetuned 175B model, demonstrating an approximate "30x model size increase" equivalent in performance gain.
 - **Favorable Data Scaling:** Verification scales more effectively with increased data than the finetuning baseline. While verification may not be beneficial at very low dataset sizes (due to overfitting to the correct answer), its advantage becomes pronounced with sufficiently large datasets. The 175B verifier "takes off" earlier, requiring less training data to surpass the finetuning baseline.

4. **Optimal Verifier Design:**
 - **Token-level Verifiers:** Training verifiers to make predictions after each token ("token-level") is more effective and less prone to overfitting than predicting only the final correctness ("solution-level"). This suggests the intermediate reasoning steps provide valuable signal.
 - **Joint Objective:** Including the language modeling objective alongside the correctness prediction objective significantly improves verifier performance, indicating that better language understanding aids in discrimination.
 - **Generator Quality is Key:** Using a large generator with a smaller verifier is more effective than the reverse, suggesting that the quality and diversity of generated candidate solutions are more critical than the verifier's absolute size. Verifiers may rely on relatively coarse heuristics, even if smaller.

5. **Test-Time Optimization and Regularization Benefits:**
 - **Sample Count and Voting:** Increasing the number of candidate solutions for verification improves performance up to a certain point (e.g., 400 completions for 6B), beyond which the benefits are outweighed by the risk of "adversarial solutions" fooling the verifier. Majority voting among the top-ranked solutions can further enhance accuracy.
 - **Dropout Effectiveness:** Residual dropout (20%) acts as a strong regularizer, significantly improving performance for both finetuning and verification, particularly in mitigating overfitting for solution-level verifiers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the development of more capable and robust artificial intelligence systems, particularly in areas requiring multi-step reasoning.

1. **A Benchmark for Reasoning:** The introduction of **GSM8K** is a major contribution. It addresses critical limitations of prior datasets by providing a high-quality, linguistically diverse, and moderately difficult benchmark for math word problems. This dataset will serve as a crucial tool for diagnosing model failures, guiding future research, and accurately measuring progress in the informal reasoning abilities of language models. Its natural language solutions also push the field towards more interpretable AI outputs.

2. **Validation of the Verification Paradigm:** The paper provides compelling evidence that **training verifiers offers a substantial performance boost** over traditional finetuning for complex reasoning tasks. This "generate-and-rank" or "search-and-verify" paradigm is demonstrated to be a powerful approach for overcoming the inherent error-propagation issues of autoregressive generative models. It suggests a promising direction for building more reliable AI systems that can self-evaluate and refine their outputs.

3. **Improved Scaling Laws for Reasoning:** Perhaps the most impactful finding is that **verification scales more favorably with increased data** than simply finetuning. The observation that 6B verification can outperform a finetuned 175B model is a critical insight. It suggests that instead of relying solely on building astronomically larger models—which comes with immense computational costs—researchers can achieve significant performance gains by intelligently leveraging existing model capacities through verification. This opens up more practical pathways to tackling increasingly complex reasoning problems.

4. **Path to More Robust and Interpretable AI:** By demonstrating the effectiveness of token-level verifiers and the benefits of a joint objective, the work also provides insights into how to build verifiers that not only judge final answers but also understand the intermediate reasoning steps. This capability has the potential to lead to AI systems that are not only more accurate but also more transparent and interpretable, as their decision-making process can be scrutinized at a granular level (as shown in Appendix F visualizations).

5. **Broader Applicability:** While demonstrated in the context of math word problems, the verification principle is generalizable. It suggests a meta-learning approach that could be applied to a wide range of complex, multi-step tasks where an AI system needs to generate a solution and then critically evaluate its correctness or quality. This could include tasks in code generation, scientific discovery, logical deduction, or even creative writing where coherence and consistency are paramount.

In conclusion, this research marks a significant step forward in enabling large language models to perform robust multi-step reasoning. It provides both a valuable resource for the community and a powerful methodological approach that promises to unlock new levels of performance and reliability in AI systems beyond mere generative fluency.
