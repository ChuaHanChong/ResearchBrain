# Quagmires in SFT-RL Post-Training: When High SFT Scores Mislead and What to Use Instead

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from FAIR at Meta and Virginia Tech found that high Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) scores often poorly predict subsequent Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) performance in Large Language Models. Their work introduces generalization loss on validation examples and Pass@large k accuracy as effective predictors for RLVR outcomes, enhancing predictability and optimizing resource use in LLM post-training workflows.

## Problem

- The common assumption that higher Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) performance reliably translates to superior final outcomes after Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is often incorrect.
- This 'SFT metric trap' leads to substantial wasted computational resources and time, as expensive RL stages are often initiated from suboptimal SFT-trained models.
- Current SFT optimization methods, frequently carried out in isolation, lack the ability to consistently predict or improve downstream RL performance, creating inefficiencies in LLM development.

## Method

- The study empirically investigated the ==SFT-then-RLVR== (GRPO) paradigm across hundreds of Llama3-8B, Mistral-Nemo-12B, and Qwen3-4B-base models, conducting extensive evaluations on 7 math benchmarks using over 1M GPU hours.
- They systematically identified specific failure modes where SFT performance metrics diverge from actual RL outcomes through both 'dataset-level' and 'instance-level' experimental scenarios.
- Two new metrics were proposed and validated to reliably predict post-RL performance: generalization loss on validation examples during SFT and Pass@large k accuracy of the post-SFT model.

## Results

- Existing SFT `Pass@1` accuracy metrics demonstrated low predictive power for final RL outcomes (e.g., R² of **0.43** for Llama3-**8B**), failing to reliably rank which SFT models would achieve the best post-RL performance.
- The proposed combination of generalization loss and `Pass@large k` significantly improved RL outcome prediction for dataset-level scenarios, achieving a Spearman correlation of **0.97** and an R² of **0.94** for Llama3-**8B**.
- `Pass@large k` alone proved to be a robust predictor for instance-level scenarios (selecting optimal SFT datasets), showing Spearman correlation improvements of up to **0.28** (from **0.70** to **0.98** for Mistral-NeMo-**12B**) and R² improvements of up to **0.34** (from **0.58** to **0.92** for Llama3-**8B**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing SFT solely for high `Pass@1` accuracy can be misleading, as models appearing strong after SFT (especially from simpler or over-trained data) often underperform after the subsequent RLVR stage.
> - The 'SFT metric trap' stems from a misalignment: SFT focuses on fitting a specific data distribution, while RLVR aims to explore the solution space to distill a broad capability into a robust `Pass@1` solution.
> - Generalization loss during SFT signals overfitting, which limits a model's capacity for later RL improvement, whereas a high `Pass@large k` (the ability to generate multiple correct solutions) indicates strong underlying reasoning potential that RLVR can effectively compress.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical and often overlooked challenge in the post-training phase of reasoning-capable Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically within the prevalent Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) followed by Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR, or "RL") paradigm.

The broader research landscape for LLMs has seen an intense focus on enhancing their reasoning abilities, leading to the development of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs). A significant recent advancement, DeepSeek R1, introduced Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as a highly effective post-training method. Following this success, the current state of practice for post-training reasoning LLMs typically involves two sequential stages: SFT to provide an initial policy by exposing the model to high-quality reasoning chains, and then RLVR to further improve reasoning and problem-solving capabilities by allowing broader exploration of the solution space. An optional Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) stage may also be included between SFT and RL.

Crucially, many current works and industrial practices operate under the assumption that models exhibiting better performance after SFT will consistently yield superior final outcomes after subsequent RLVR. This assumption has led to SFT and RL stages often being optimized in isolation, sometimes by separate teams, each focusing on maximizing their respective performance metrics.

This paper directly challenges this foundational assumption, identifying it as a "critical gap" and "SFT metric trap." It highlights inconsistencies and even contradictions in existing viewpoints regarding the role and impact of SFT on subsequent RL, noting debates on whether SFT helps or hurts. For instance, while some emphasize SFT's necessity for RL "cold-start," others caution against "over-SFT" constraining RL's exploration or argue that SFT can generalize poorly.

While alternative post-training paradigms are being explored (e.g., interleaving SFT and RL, gradually shifting objectives, or unifying SFT/RL), these methods often introduce their own complexities and have not yet universally replaced the robust and widely adopted sequential SFT-then-RL pipeline. Therefore, Kang et al.'s work is situated squarely within the context of improving the predictability and efficiency of the *prevailing industry standard*. By focusing on the SFT-then-RL pipeline, the paper offers immediately applicable tools and insights for current state-of-the-art workflows, addressing a practical "quagmire" that causes friction, overhead, and sub-optimal resource allocation in real-world LLM development. The findings are highly relevant for informing better SFT data curation, optimizing training paradigms, and potentially extending to RL for non-verifiable tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to resolve a critical **predictability problem** within the common SFT-then-RLVR post-training paradigm for reasoning LLMs. The authors directly challenge the prevailing assumption that high SFT performance reliably translates to strong final performance after subsequent Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).

The paper articulates two primary research questions (RQs) that define its objectives:

- **RQ1: Do models with better pre-RL performance always lead to better outcomes after RLVR? If not, what are the failure modes?**
 - This question aims to rigorously test the industry-standard assumption. The motivation here stems from observed inconsistencies where SFT improvements do not linearly lead to RL success, or worse, can even hinder it. Identifying "failure modes" is crucial for understanding *why* the assumption breaks down.
- **RQ2: What are effective SFT paradigms and data recipes when considering subsequent RLVR training? Can we determine the suitability of an SFT model before committing to the expensive RL stage?**
 - This question is highly practical, seeking to develop actionable solutions to the identified problem. The motivation is driven by the enormous computational cost and time investment associated with RL training, particularly in large-scale industrial settings. If the quality of an SFT-trained model (as a starting point for RL) cannot be reliably predicted, significant resources can be wasted on suboptimal SFT models that ultimately lead to poor RL outcomes.

Beyond these specific RQs, the broader motivations are deeply rooted in practical challenges faced in LLM development:

- **High Computational Cost of RL:** RL training is prohibitively expensive, consuming vast amounts of GPU hours. Unreliable SFT-stage predictions mean that precious compute is often spent on models that offer diminishing returns or even negative outcomes after RL.
- **Long and Complex Pipelines:** The end-to-end SFT-RL pipeline can be lengthy, especially for agentic use cases. A lack of early predictability makes it difficult to make timely adjustments or optimizations.
- **Organizational Friction:** In large organizations, SFT and RL stages are often handled by different teams. A disconnect in performance metrics and predictability can lead to "friction and overhead," hindering productivity and creating ambiguity when diagnosing failures.
- **Ineffectiveness of Early Stopping in RL:** The paper notes that early stopping during RL is generally ineffective, as models with fast initial improvement may not achieve the highest final performance. This reinforces the need for better *pre-RL* indicators.

In essence, the authors are motivated to move beyond heuristic-based SFT optimization by providing reliable, quantifiable metrics that can de-risk the expensive RL stage, streamline development workflows, and enable more intelligent allocation of computational resources, ultimately accelerating the advancement of reasoning LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a rigorous and extensive empirical methodology to investigate the relationship between SFT and RL performance, test the reliability of current metrics, and validate their proposed alternatives. The sheer scale of experimentation is a hallmark of this work, leveraging substantial computational resources.

**Experimental Scale and Resources:**
The research involved training "hundreds of models up to 12B-parameter" (Llama3-8B, Mistral-Nemo-12B, Qwen3-4B-base). This was coupled with "extensive evaluations on 7 math benchmarks with up to 256 repetitions," collectively consuming ">1M GPU hours" on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. This massive undertaking ensures the robustness and generalizability of the findings across different model architectures and training conditions.

**Post-Training Paradigm:**
The study focuses on the standard sequential **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** followed by **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)** paradigm, specifically using **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** for the RL stage. This choice is deliberate, as this paradigm remains a dominant industry standard despite ongoing research into alternatives.

**Models and Datasets:**
- **Base Models:** Llama3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-Nemo-12B-Instruct, and Qwen3-4B-base were used, representing state-of-the-art open-source LLMs known for their reasoning capabilities.
- **SFT Datasets:** Llama-Nemotron-SFT (math samples generated by QwQ-32B or DeepSeek R1) and AceReasoner1.1-SFT were utilized for supervised fine-tuning.
- **RL Datasets:** The MATH dataset (train-split) and DeepScaleR dataset were used for the RLVR training phase.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:** Model performance was assessed on a comprehensive suite of 7 math benchmarks: MATH-500, AIME 1983-2024, GSM8k, AIME 2025, AMC, Olympiad, and Minerva. Pass@1 accuracy, averaged over 64 repetitions across these 7 tasks, was the primary performance metric.

**Experimental Scenarios (Addressing RQ1):**
To systematically investigate the "SFT metric trap," the authors designed two representative scenarios:
1. **Dataset-Level Scenarios:** SFT examples were drawn from the *same underlying data distribution*, but various training configurations were altered, such as the number of unique samples, training epochs, and learning rates. This scenario mimics industrial practices where teams optimize SFT paradigms given a fixed dataset.
2. **Instance-Level Scenarios:** The model and training configurations were *fixed*, but the SFT datasets themselves were varied. This involved creating curated SFT datasets by selecting subsets (e.g., shortest/longest solutions, random samples, mixtures). This scenario focuses on SFT data selection and curation strategies.

**Proposed Metrics (Addressing RQ2):**
Based on their initial observations of failure modes, the authors propose and validate two alternative metrics to reliably predict RLVR outcomes:
1. **Generalization Loss on Validation Examples:** This metric tracks the validation loss during SFT training. The insight is that while SFT performance on training data might continuously improve with more epochs, an eventual increase in generalization loss on held-out validation data indicates overfitting, which strongly correlates with decreased potential for subsequent RL gains.
2. **Pass@large k Accuracy:** This metric evaluates the post-SFT model's Pass@k accuracy at a large `k` (e.g., Pass@64). The rationale is that the RLVR objective, particularly GRPO, aims to "compress" Pass@k accuracy into Pass@1. Therefore, a higher inherent capability to generate correct solutions (even if not as the top-ranked one), as captured by Pass@large k, should be a strong predictor of eventual Pass@1 success after RL. The authors leverage an unbiased estimation formula for Pass@k accuracy for efficient computation.

**Evaluation of Predictor Performance:**
The effectiveness of the proposed metrics in predicting post-RL performance was quantitatively assessed using two widely accepted statistical measures:
- **Coefficient of Determination (R²):** Measures the proportion of variance in the final RL performance that can be predicted from the proposed metrics, indicating prediction accuracy.
- **Spearman’s Rank Correlation Coefficient:** Assesses the strength and direction of the monotonic relationship between the predicted ranks (based on proposed metrics) and the actual ranks of final RL performance, indicating how effectively the metrics can identify the best-performing models.

The thorough experimental design, extensive compute, use of state-of-the-art models and datasets, and rigorous statistical evaluation lend high credibility to the paper's findings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core findings of this paper fundamentally challenge existing assumptions in LLM post-training and offer robust, practical solutions.

**Failure of Current SFT Metrics (Addressing RQ1):**
The research provides extensive counter-examples demonstrating that high SFT scores do *not* reliably translate to improved performance after RLVR. This "SFT metric trap" manifests in several ways:

1. **Bias Towards Simpler/Homogeneous Data:** High SFT scores can be biased towards models trained on simpler or more homogeneous data. For instance, training on shorter reasoning examples often led to faster and higher SFT performance but resulted in substantially worse outcomes after RL (Figure 1, Figure 7). These simpler examples, while easy to learn, do not adequately prepare the model for the more complex reasoning demanded by RL.
2. **Over-training During SFT:** Over-training during SFT, even if it continually boosts post-SFT performance, can significantly diminish a model's potential for subsequent RL. In some cases, RL training on models with improved SFT performance led to substantially *worse* final outcomes compared to RL on the base model without SFT (Figure 4). The linear correlation between post-SFT and post-RL performance was found to be low (R² = 0.43 for Llama3-8B), indicating that SFT performance explains less than half the variation in final RL outcomes.
3. **Divergent Trends:** The paper clearly illustrates scenarios where post-SFT performance and final post-RL performance trends visibly diverge. Optimizing solely for post-SFT performance often proved suboptimal or even ineffective for improving the final model after RL.

**Effectiveness of Proposed Metrics (Addressing RQ2):**
The authors successfully identify and validate two alternative metrics that serve as strong proxies for RL outcomes, offering significant improvements over relying on SFT Pass@1 accuracy:

1. **Generalization Loss on Validation Examples:**
 - **Finding:** As SFT training progresses, an eventual increase in validation loss is strongly correlated with decreased potential for performance improvements in the later RL stage (Figure 5). This indicates overfitting during SFT.
 - **Utility:** For "dataset-level prediction" (optimizing SFT training paradigms on a single dataset), generalization loss is a viable indicator of a model's RL potential. It allows practitioners to rule out suboptimal SFT models (those with low performance and high generalization loss) early, guiding decisions and saving compute.
 - **Quantitative Improvement (Dataset-level):** This metric significantly improved prediction accuracy (R²) and rank correlation (Spearman) compared to SFT Pass@1. For Llama3-8B, combining generalization loss with Pass@large k achieved a Spearman correlation of 0.97 (a 0.22 improvement over the baseline 0.75) and an R² of 0.94 (a 0.37 improvement over the baseline 0.57).

2. **Pass@large k Accuracy:**
 - **Finding:** Pass@large k accuracy of the post-SFT model (e.g., Pass@64) is a highly robust predictor for the final outcome after RL. This aligns with the understanding that GRPO in RLVR compresses Pass@k accuracy into Pass@1, implying that a model's inherent capability to produce multiple correct solutions is a strong foundation for RL success.
 - **Utility:** Pass@large k is effective for both "dataset-level" and "instance-level prediction" (selecting optimal SFT datasets). Crucially, for instance-level selection, generalization loss is not applicable due to distributional gaps between different datasets, making Pass@large k the primary reliable predictor. It enables effective ranking of SFT datasets and selection of the most promising one without requiring actual RL runs for calibration.
 - **Quantitative Improvement (Instance-level):** This metric demonstrated exceptional robustness and accuracy. For Mistral-NeMo-12B, Pass@large k improved Spearman correlation by 0.28 (from 0.70 to 0.98) and R² by 0.25 (from 0.73 to 0.98). For Llama3-8B, it improved Spearman by 0.25 (from 0.69 to 0.94) and R² by 0.34 (from 0.58 to 0.92).

**Practical Implications from Examples:**
The paper provides concrete examples of how their findings contradict common intuitions:
- With the same SFT budget, training on unique examples for one epoch often underperforms training on half examples for two epochs, both after SFT and SFT-then-RL.
- Training solely on short examples might yield better SFT performance but often leads to worse outcomes after RL compared to training on examples with varying lengths. These subtle but significant differences are captured by the proposed predictors but missed by traditional SFT performance metrics.

Finally, the authors announce the **open-sourcing of an enhanced evaluation tool**, a practical contribution stemming from this research to facilitate broader adoption of their findings and further community research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant theoretical and practical implications, challenging a fundamental assumption in the training of reasoning-capable Large Language Models (LLMs) and offering tangible solutions to improve efficiency and effectiveness.

**1. Debunking a Critical Assumption:**
The most immediate impact is the comprehensive refutation of the widely held belief that high Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) scores reliably predict strong performance after Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). By extensively demonstrating the "SFT metric trap" across multiple state-of-the-art LLMs and datasets, the paper establishes that blindly optimizing SFT performance can be misleading, or even detrimental, to the final RL outcome. This is a critical wake-up call for researchers and practitioners, forcing a re-evaluation of established post-training workflows.

**2. De-risking the Expensive RL Stage:**
Reinforcement Learning (RL) training for LLMs is notoriously computationally expensive and time-consuming. The lack of reliable pre-RL indicators means that substantial compute resources are often wasted on suboptimal SFT starting points. The proposed metrics—generalization loss on validation examples and Pass@large k accuracy—provide robust and early predictors for RL success. This capability allows practitioners to:
 - **Filter suboptimal SFT models:** Quickly identify and discard SFT-trained models unlikely to benefit from RL, saving millions of GPU hours.
 - **Optimize SFT paradigms:** Guide the selection of optimal SFT training configurations (e.g., number of epochs, data volume) that truly prepare models for RL, rather than just maximizing immediate SFT scores.
 - **Streamline data curation:** Effectively select SFT datasets with the highest potential for downstream RL success, ensuring that high-quality, diverse reasoning examples are prioritized over simpler ones that might offer superficial SFT gains.

**3. Improving Collaboration and Productivity in Industrial Settings:**
In large AI labs and companies like Meta, SFT and RL development are often handled by different teams. The "SFT metric trap" can lead to friction and misaligned incentives when SFT-optimized models yield disappointing RL results. By providing common, reliable predictive metrics, this work fosters better communication, clearer success targets, and more efficient collaboration between these specialized teams, ultimately enhancing overall productivity in LLM development.

**4. Advancing the Science of LLM Post-Training:**
Beyond immediate practical benefits, the paper contributes to a deeper understanding of the complex dynamics between SFT and RL. It highlights the importance of "RL potential" and generalization, rather than mere performance on SFT training distributions. This theoretical insight can inform the design of future post-training paradigms, encouraging methods that jointly optimize SFT and RL objectives or explicitly consider how SFT prepares a model for exploration and reward maximization in RL.

**5. Broader Applicability and Future Research:**
While the current work focuses on mathematical reasoning, the principles and proposed metrics could have broader applicability to other complex reasoning tasks (e.g., code generation, scientific problem-solving) and even agentic use cases where RL is critical. The open-sourcing of an enhanced evaluation tool further facilitates community adoption and encourages extensions of this research.

The paper also outlines natural next steps, such as extending the study to a wider range of reasoning tasks, investigating different RL algorithms (e.g., offline RL, DPO), and exploring more efficient ways to estimate Pass@k accuracy. This proactive stance for future work underscores the foundational nature of their contributions.

In conclusion, "Quagmires in SFT-RL Post-Training" is a highly impactful paper that provides crucial insights and practical tools for the efficient and effective post-training of reasoning LLMs. By providing reliable predictors for RL success, it promises to de-risk development, save vast computational resources, and foster more productive research and application of advanced language models.
