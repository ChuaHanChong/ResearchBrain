# Decoupled Early Exits for Task-Dependent Compute Allocation in Flow-Matching VLAs

> [!summary] Summary
> Decoupled early exits expose three compute axes in flow-matching VLAs: VLM backbone depth, action expert depth, and denoising steps. Lightweight Exit Transformers distilled from a frozen policy make intermediate depths valid exits, while a KV cache synthesis mechanism refills prefix keys and values of skipped backbone layers so the expert runs deeper than the backbone. Joint configurations cut latency 79.2% and FLOPs 31.8% while raising mean success 5.6%.

## Problem

- Prior VLA efficiency methods target the vision-language backbone with layer pruning, early exits or caching, leaving the action expert's depth untouched as a compute axis.
- The action expert dominates latency because it reruns every denoising step, yet no prior work treats its depth as an independent reducible axis.
- Methods that do truncate depth couple backbone and expert at the same layer, and none measures how backbone depth, expert depth and step count interact.

## Method

- **Exit Transformers**: a single Transformer block copied from each candidate depth in both backbone and expert, ==distilled== against the frozen policy's last layer so the pretrained action head is reused unchanged.
- ==KV cache synthesis== re-projects the exit output through each skipped backbone layer's own key and value matrices plus key ==RoPE==, filling empty cache slots so the expert exits deeper than the backbone.
- Two ==distillation== losses: backbone exits combine ==velocity MSE== with ==cosine feature distillation== to the full-depth prefix; expert exits combine ==flow-matching loss== with ==velocity distillation==. Denoising step count needs no training.

## Results

- Joint configurations beat full-compute baselines across SmolVLA and pi-0.5 on LIBERO and Meta-World: latency down **79.2%**, FLOPs down **31.8%**, mean success up **5.6%**; SmolVLA Meta-World rose **60.7%** to **74.9%**.
- The full policy was optimal on only **11/50** Meta-World tasks for SmolVLA and **22/50** for pi-0.5; per-task axis selection would lift mean success to **79.5%** and **75.9%**.
- Exit Transformers beat plain layer truncation by up to **86%** success at shallow expert depths; KV cache synthesis gave **4x** prefix speedup, removing **54.6%** of pi-0.5's total FLOPs with near-unchanged success.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compute axes are not interchangeable: backbone depth mainly buys FLOPs, expert depth buys latency, and fewer denoising steps buys both, so measure each separately.
> - Default denoising step counts are often wrong: cutting steps can raise success and cut cost at once, especially on dexterous tasks the full policy fails.
> - Optimal compute is a per-task control variable, not a model property: perception-limited tasks need backbone depth, contact-precision tasks need expert depth.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot policy of this kind repeatedly turns camera images, language, and robot state into action chunks. Its practical bottleneck is not just a large vision-language model (VLM). A flow-matching action expert also reruns during each denoising step. The authors therefore let compute vary at three distinct points instead of treating the policy as one indivisible model: VLM depth **V**, action-expert depth **A**, and denoising-step count **D**.

The authors argue that task requirements differ. In their framing, stick-push calls for finer action dexterity, while box-close depends more on perceiving the lid relative to the box. These are the authors' interpretations of the tasks, not isolated causal findings.

SmolVLA on stick-push and π0.5 on box-close. The figure shows the three compute dials and two 3D Pareto plots of success rate (%), latency (seconds/action), and GFLOPs/action. In each example, the joint-configuration star appears at higher success and lower latency/FLOPs than the full-model star.

### 2. One policy, three dials

The VLM encodes visual and language context. The action expert generates continuous action chunks. Flow matching starts from a noisy chunk and refines it by repeatedly evaluating a velocity field. **D** is the number of these denoising updates, and **V** and **A** select the respective layer depths.

Exit Transformers (ETs) make the shallower exits usable. Each ET is one transformer block initialized from the block at its depth. It is trained to distill the frozen policy's final representation or output. The original backbone, action expert, and action head stay frozen. Each exit adds 2.1% parameters for SmolVLA and 4.1% for π0.5. **D** needs no learned parameters and can change at inference time.

Exiting the VLM early creates a cache problem. Deeper action-expert layers expect their own prefix keys and values, but a VLM exit did not compute those layers. The method projects the ET output with each skipped layer's own key/value matrices and key rotation, and it skips that layer's attention and MLP. This key/value (KV) cache synthesis supplies the missing cache and permits $A > V$. It adds projections rather than a full skipped block.

Illustrated SmolVLA setting (V,A,D)=(16,24,D). VLM layers 17–32 and expert layers 25–32 are skipped, but the expert runs deeper than the VLM. KV entries for layers 17–24 are synthesized from the VLM16 ET output: key/value projections and key rotation are retained while attention and MLP are skipped.

### 3. What the trade-off buys

The evaluation uses LIBERO and Meta-World, both simulation benchmarks, with tests run on one NVIDIA A100. Success rate is the fraction of successful episodes across a benchmark suite. FLOPs are measured for one action-generation call, including the backbone and full denoising loop. Latency is average wall-clock time for that call at batch size one.

**SmolVLA on Meta-World.** The baseline **(V,A,D)=(16,16,10)** achieves 60.7% success, 0.397 seconds per call, and 318.0 GFLOPs per call. Joint **(8,8,3)** achieves 74.9%, 0.081 seconds, and 173.0 GFLOPs.

**All four settings.** Across SmolVLA and π0.5, each on LIBERO and Meta-World, selected joint configurations averaged 79.2% lower measured latency, 31.8% fewer FLOPs, and a 5.6-percentage-point higher mean success rate than their full-compute baselines. The average hides an exception: π0.5 on LIBERO drops from 87.6% to 86.7% success while reducing latency and FLOPs.

**Effect of each dial.** **V** is most effective for reducing FLOPs, and **A** for latency. **D** can improve both.

**Trained exits versus truncation.** On LIBERO, the authors compare ET exits with direct layer truncation. ETs improve the action-expert results at evaluated depths, with a reported maximum gap of 86 percentage points. For VLM exits, π0.5 is an important exception: at deeper depths, truncation recovers and outperforms ETs.

Success rate (%) against exit layer. VLM exits are at left and action-expert exits at right; SmolVLA is above and π0.5 below. Blue shows ET exits and orange shows layer truncation. The gray dashed line marks full depth (69.0% for SmolVLA; 96.3% for π0.5).

On Meta-World's 50 tasks, the full policy is the best tested configuration for only 11/50 SmolVLA tasks and 22/50 π0.5 tasks. Selecting the best tested configuration separately for each task raises reported mean success from 60.7% to 79.5% for SmolVLA and from 64.9% to 75.9% for π0.5. This selection is retrospective over evaluated settings.

The contribution is a way to reassign compute among perception, action generation, and denoising, rather than reduce it uniformly. The main boundary is that the results come from simulation benchmarks. The authors also leave automatic selection of compute by task or timestep to future work.
