# CORE: Common Outcome Regularities from Action-Free Visual Demonstrations for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Peking University developed CORE (Common Outcome Regularities), a framework that extracts outcome-level visual goal conditions from action-free visual demonstrations for robot manipulation. It improved robot task success rates across simulation benchmarks and real-world scenarios compared to policies relying on language or vanilla approaches.

## Problem

- Robot imitation learning is limited by the high cost and scarcity of robot-specific demonstration data.
- Abundant human visual demonstrations are challenging to leverage due to the absence of explicit action labels and a significant embodiment gap between humans and robots.
- Natural language instructions for task goals often lack the precise physical details required for robust, contact-rich robot manipulation.

## Method

- **CORE** employs a three-stage framework, first training a ==terminal outcome encoder== using ==contrastive==, ==time-to-goal regression==, and ==terminal classification== objectives to represent successful final states.
- It constructs a robust, task-level ==visual goal prototype== by ==clustering (K-Means)== and averaging Top-K embeddings of successful terminal states from action-free visual demonstrations.
- This static goal prototype is then integrated, alongside a dynamic current outcome embedding, into various robot policy backbones in a ==backbone-agnostic== manner to guide action generation.

## Results

- CORE-integrated policies improved average success rates on Meta-World by **2.5-3.9 percentage points** and on RoboTwin 2.0 by **4.8-11.1 percentage points** over vanilla or text-conditioned policies.
- Ablation studies confirmed that auxiliary objectives in the encoder training and the Top-K prototype averaging strategy are essential for constructing robust goal conditions.
- In real-world experiments, MP1+CORE achieved an **85.0%** average success rate (**16.0 percentage point** improvement) and DP3+CORE achieved **82.0%** (**17.0 percentage point** improvement) across five contact-rich tasks on an ARX R5 robotic arm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Successful terminal states of manipulation tasks often exhibit stable and consistent 'Common Outcome Regularities' regardless of diverse motion trajectories.
> - Extracting and utilizing these outcome-level visual regularities provides more concrete physical constraints for robot policies than abstract language instructions.
> - Focusing on desired task outcomes offers a more transferable form of supervision across different embodiments, helping to bridge the human-to-robot embodiment gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot imitation learning has demonstrated success in robot manipulation, typically by training end-to-end policies on large datasets of robot-specific demonstrations. These demonstrations, often collected through teleoperation or kinesthetic teaching, are expensive and time-consuming to acquire, thus limiting the scalability of robot learning across a wide range of tasks, objects, and environments.

In contrast, action-free visual demonstrations, such as human videos found online or recorded daily activities, are abundant and contain rich information regarding task priors, object interaction patterns, and physical common sense. However, leveraging these visual demonstrations for direct robot policy learning presents significant challenges. Firstly, they typically lack robot-executable action labels. Secondly, a substantial embodiment gap exists between human demonstrators and robots, manifesting in differences in morphology, action spaces, and control interfaces. Current methods attempting to utilize human videos often focus on learning latent actions, pretraining world models, recovering 3D hand/object trajectories, or performing data editing to make the data compatible with robot embodiments. Many of these approaches still require some form of correspondence at the level of actions, trajectories, or embodiments.

Concurrently, a common approach in robot policy learning involves using natural language instructions to specify task goals. While language offers a readily available and semantically rich way to express high-level task intent (e.g., "stack the bowls"), it frequently underspecifies the precise physical details crucial for contact-rich manipulation. These details include exact object geometry, spatial poses, contact relations, and the terminal state structure required for successful task completion. Policies relying solely on language may therefore fail to achieve the specific physical constraints defining success, leading to suboptimal or failed executions.

This work positions itself at the intersection of these two challenges. It aims to bridge the embodiment gap and address the imprecision of language instructions by extracting supervision from action-free visual demonstrations at an "outcome-level" rather than an "action-level." By focusing on the shared structural regularities of successful terminal states, CORE proposes a method that is less sensitive to varying motion trajectories across embodiments and provides more concrete physical constraints than abstract language descriptions, thereby offering a complementary approach to existing visuomotor and vision-language-action policy learning paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to overcome the limitations associated with conventional robot imitation learning and goal specification for robot manipulation.

**Motivation stems from two main challenges:**

1. **Cost and Scarcity of Robot Demonstrations:** Robot imitation learning policies typically require extensive robot-specific demonstration data. The collection of such data is resource-intensive, which restricts the application of these policies to a limited set of tasks and scenarios. Simultaneously, action-free visual demonstrations, such as human videos, are plentiful but difficult to directly utilize due to the absence of robot-executable actions and a significant embodiment gap between humans and robots (differences in physical form, action capabilities, and control mechanisms). The central problem is thus to devise a method to extract actionable supervision from these abundant visual data sources that can be effectively leveraged by robots despite these discrepancies.

2. **Ambiguity of Language Instructions for Precise Manipulation:** Many robotic systems employ natural language to define task goals. While language excels at conveying high-level semantic intent (e.g., "place the object"), it often lacks the specificity required for contact-rich manipulation tasks. Language instructions may not precisely specify geometric configurations, spatial relationships, contact constraints, or the exact terminal physical structure necessary for successful completion. This lack of concrete physical detail can lead policies conditioned solely on language to misinterpret or overlook critical outcome constraints, resulting in execution failures or suboptimal performance.

**Against this backdrop, the authors propose a core observation:** While the motion trajectories employed by different demonstrators to achieve a task can be highly diverse, their successful terminal states often converge to stable and consistent physical configurations. For instance, in a stacking task, different methods of grasping and moving objects might be used, but successful completion consistently results in a specific nested or stacked arrangement. These shared, stable structures across successful terminal states are termed "Common Outcome Regularities." The motivation is that these outcome regularities are inherently more transferable across different embodiments (e.g., human to robot) than explicit action sequences, and they provide a more precise specification of the desired physical end-state than general language instructions.

**Therefore, the key objectives are:**

- To develop a framework capable of extracting these Common Outcome Regularities from action-free visual demonstrations.
- To convert these regularities into robust, outcome-level visual goal conditions that can guide robot manipulation policies.
- To demonstrate that such visual goal prototypes offer more concrete geometric and physical constraints for task completion compared to language instructions, thereby improving the success rate and robustness of robot policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CORE (Common Outcome Regularities) is a three-stage framework designed to convert action-free visual demonstrations into effective visual goal conditions for robot policies. The framework operates on a visual demonstration dataset $D_V = \{\tau_V^i\}_{i=1}^{N_V}$, where each trajectory $\tau_V^i = (O_1^{(i)}, \dots, O_{T_i}^{(i)})$ consists of observations relevant to the goal but lacks explicit action labels. The method assumes demonstration-level success labels, where the last $w$ observations of a successful trajectory are considered terminal positives. Negative samples are drawn from non-terminal observations or terminal observations of other tasks or failed trajectories.

**Stage I: Terminal Outcome Representation Learning**
The initial stage focuses on training a terminal outcome encoder, denoted $f_z^\phi$, which maps an observation $O$ to a normalized outcome embedding $z = \frac{q_\phi(f_\phi(O))}{\|q_\phi(f_\phi(O))\|_2}$. This encoder is designed to capture the shared structural properties of successful terminal states, rather than specific motion patterns within a trajectory.

The encoder training incorporates three objectives:
- **Terminal Contrastive Objective ($L_{NCE}$):** This loss function is designed to pull embeddings of terminal observations from successful demonstrations of the same task closer together in the embedding space. Simultaneously, it pushes apart embeddings of non-terminal observations or terminal observations from unrelated tasks or failed trajectories. This encourages the representation to discriminate between successful final states and other states.
- **Time-to-Goal Regression Loss ($L_{ttg}$):** This auxiliary task trains the encoder to predict the remaining time steps until task completion. It encourages the representation to capture temporal progress information, allowing the policy to be aware of how far it is from the goal.
- **Terminal Classification Loss ($L_{term}$):** This objective trains a binary classifier to distinguish between terminal and non-terminal states. It further refines the encoder's ability to identify and differentiate the characteristics of a successful end-state.

The total loss for Stage I is a weighted sum: $L_{aux} = \lambda_{nce} L_{NCE} + \lambda_{ttg} L_{ttg} + \lambda_{term} L_{term}$.

**Stage II: Goal Prototype Construction**
Following the training of the terminal outcome encoder, this stage generates a task-level visual goal prototype, $z_{goal}$. For a given task, all successful terminal observations $\{O_i^*\}$ are encoded into their respective embeddings $z_i^* = f_z^\phi(O_i^*)$. To create a robust goal prototype that suppresses noise and captures dominant successful outcome modes, these embeddings are clustered (e.g., using K-Means). The final visual goal prototype $z_{goal}$ is derived by averaging the selected Top-K representative cluster centers. This aggregation process produces a static and stable task-level goal condition, abstracting away individual demonstration variations.

**Stage III: Backbone-Agnostic Goal Injection**
In the final stage, the learned visual goal prototype is integrated into a robot policy backbone. At each policy step, the current observation $O_t$ is encoded by $f_z^\phi$ to produce a current outcome embedding $z_t = f_z^\phi(O_t)$. This $z_t$ is then concatenated with the static task-level goal prototype $z_{goal}$ to form a comprehensive goal feature $g_t = [z_t; z_{goal}]$.

For a given policy backbone $B_\theta$ (which includes its own observation encoder $\phi_\theta$), the historical observation features $e_t = \phi_\theta(I_{t-T_o+1:t})$ are fused with the CORE-derived goal feature $g_t$ using a backbone-specific conditioning operator $\Gamma_B$, resulting in the final policy condition $c_t = \Gamma_B(e_t, g_t)$. This condition $c_t$ is then fed into the policy backbone $B_\theta$ to predict the action chunk $A_{t:t+H-1}$. The action loss ($L_{ACT}$) remains the original training objective of the chosen policy backbone (e.g., denoising loss for diffusion policies or velocity matching loss for flow-based policies). This design renders CORE backbone-agnostic, allowing its seamless integration with various action-chunking policy architectures without altering their core action-learning mechanisms.

**Inference:** During execution, the current observation $O_t$ is continuously encoded to $z_t$, and the static goal prototype $z_{goal}$ is used (retrieved or aggregated via Top-K). These are combined to form $c_t$, which guides the policy. The receding-horizon control strategy allows the policy to continuously receive outcome-level guidance, enabling it to correct actions and converge reliably towards the desired terminal state.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of CORE was evaluated through experiments on two simulation benchmarks, Meta-World and RoboTwin 2.0, and subsequently validated in real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

**Simulation Evaluation:**

- **Meta-World Benchmark:** CORE demonstrated improvements across various task difficulties when integrated with Diffusion Policy (DP3) and MP1 policy backbones.
 - MP1+CORE improved the average success rate from 81.7% to 85.6% (an increase of 3.9 percentage points) compared to vanilla MP1.
 - DP3+CORE improved the average success rate from 81.5% to 84.0% (an increase of 2.5 percentage points) compared to vanilla DP3.
 - In contrast, text-conditioned variants (DP3+TEXT and MP1+TEXT), which use task instructions instead of visual prototypes, achieved only marginal gains (81.9% and 81.4%, respectively).
 - The performance gains were more substantial on tasks classified as "Hard" and "Very Hard," with MP1+CORE improving success rates from 55.1% to 64.0% and 75.5% to 81.4% on these categories, respectively. This suggests that visual outcome prototypes provide more precise and actionable constraints, particularly for tasks demanding accurate terminal-state alignment.

- **RoboTwin 2.0 Benchmark:** Similar trends were observed on this benchmark, which features 10 contact-rich manipulation tasks.
 - DP3+CORE achieved an average success rate of 82.3%, an improvement of 4.8 percentage points over DP3 and 4.9 percentage points over DP3+TEXT.
 - MP1+CORE reached 79.4%, outperforming MP1 by 11.1 percentage points and MP1+TEXT by 9.7 percentage points.
 - At least one CORE-integrated policy achieved the best result on each evaluated task. Training curves indicated that CORE variants generally yielded higher and more stable success rates over the course of training.

**Ablation Study:**

- **Component Ablation:** On Meta-World Hard and Very Hard tasks (using MP1 backbone), removing auxiliary objectives from Stage I (time-to-goal regression $L_{ttg}$ and terminal classification $L_{term}$) led to a decrease in average success rate. Removing $L_{ttg}$ alone resulted in a drop from 71.7% (full CORE) to 65.9%, and removing $L_{term}$ alone to 66.2%. When both were removed, the performance further declined to 63.4%, which was below the baseline MP1 performance. This underscores the importance of these auxiliary objectives in shaping a useful terminal outcome representation. Removing the current outcome embedding $z_t$ from the policy condition also reduced success to 68.0%, indicating that the dynamic context of the current state relative to the goal is important, not just the static goal prototype.

- **Goal Prototype Construction:** The "Top-K prototype averaging" strategy, where successful terminal embeddings are clustered and averaged, yielded the best performance (71.7% average success on Meta-World Hard/Very Hard tasks). This approach surpassed simply averaging all successful terminal embeddings (71.4%) and randomly selecting a single terminal embedding (70.5%), suggesting that filtering representative modes before averaging contributes to a more robust goal condition.

- **Number of Visual Demonstrations:** Experiments showed that CORE could extract useful outcome information from a limited number of action-free visual demonstrations (e.g., 20 demonstrations for some tasks), with performance generally improving and stabilizing as more demonstrations were added (up to 100). This indicates that increased data coverage of successful outcomes enhances the robustness of the visual goal prototypes.

- **Visual Encoder in Stage I:** A comparison between the specifically trained CORE terminal encoder and a frozen, general-purpose pretrained Uni3D encoder revealed that the learned CORE encoder consistently delivered superior performance. Across four representative tasks, CORE+DP3 achieved 79.8% success compared to 59.2% for Uni3D+DP3, and CORE+MP1 achieved 85.7% versus 67.4% for Uni3D+MP1. This demonstrates that learning outcome representations specifically tuned for successful terminal structures, spatial relations, and contact patterns is more effective than directly utilizing generic visual features.

**Real-World Experiments:**
CORE was deployed on an ARX R5 robotic arm for five contact-rich manipulation tasks (Adjust Bottle, Cook, Close Drawer, Open Drawer, Stack 3 Bowls).
- MP1+CORE achieved an average success rate of 85.0%, representing a 16.0 percentage point improvement over vanilla MP1.
- DP3+CORE achieved 82.0% average success, an improvement of 17.0 percentage points over vanilla DP3.
- Text-conditioned variants showed negligible improvements (DP3+TEXT at 68.0%, MP1+TEXT at 67.0%).
- Qualitative observations confirmed that CORE actively guided the robot to complete tasks that vanilla policies might stop slightly short of, such as fully opening or closing a drawer. These results indicate that visual goal prototypes provide precise physical constraints essential for successful real-world execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CORE framework presents a significant contribution to robot manipulation by addressing fundamental challenges in imitation learning and task goal specification.

**Enhanced Utilization of Abundant Data:** CORE provides a viable methodology for leveraging abundant action-free visual demonstrations, such as human videos, for robot policy learning. This capability has the potential to substantially reduce the reliance on costly and scarce robot-specific demonstrations, thus lowering the barrier to entry for developing new robotic skills and scaling robot learning across a wider array of tasks and environments.

**Improved Task Specification and Robustness:** By extracting Common Outcome Regularities from successful terminal states, CORE offers a more concrete and physically grounded representation of task goals than traditional natural language instructions. For contact-rich manipulation tasks, where precise geometric alignment, spatial relationships, and contact constraints are critical, this outcome-level visual guidance enables policies to achieve higher success rates and exhibit greater robustness. The consistent performance improvements observed across diverse simulation benchmarks and real-world experiments, particularly on challenging tasks, underscore this benefit.

**Bridging the Embodiment Gap at an Outcome Level:** The framework introduces a novel perspective by focusing on the "what" (desired outcome) rather than the "how" (specific actions) of task completion. This outcome-level abstraction offers a more transferable form of supervision across different embodiments, as the physical configuration of a successful end-state is often more consistent and less dependent on the specific morphology or action space of the demonstrator compared to the detailed action trajectories.

**Generalizability and Modularity:** The backbone-agnostic design of CORE allows it to be integrated seamlessly with various existing and future robot policy architectures (e.g., diffusion-based, flow-based). This modularity increases its applicability and potential for widespread adoption within the robotic learning community.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Scalable Robot Learning:** By effectively utilizing readily available human video data, CORE could enable more scalable and accessible methods for teaching robots new skills, making robot manipulation more practical for diverse applications beyond controlled industrial settings.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** Decreased reliance on expensive robot demonstrations could translate to lower development costs and faster iteration cycles for robotic systems.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Collaboration:** A more robust understanding of task outcomes could facilitate better human-robot collaboration by allowing humans to demonstrate tasks naturally, with the robot interpreting the desired end-state rather than requiring precise action labels.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The concept of Common Outcome Regularities and the methodology for extracting them could serve as a foundation for further research into more complex task planning, hierarchical skill learning, and the discovery of latent task structures from diverse observational data. The focus on outcome representations could also be extended to other domains where precise goal specification is challenging.
