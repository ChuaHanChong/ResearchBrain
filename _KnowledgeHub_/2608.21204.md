# Beyond Imitation: Self-Improving Robot Policies via Off-Policy Q-Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Q-Planning makes a frozen behaviour-cloning policy self-improving by pairing it with a small off-policy Q-function over action chunks. At inference the policy draws N candidate chunks and executes a Q-weighted average; deployment rollouts, successful and failed, refine only the Q-function. Mean simulated success rises 92.1 to 97.6%, and real bimanual stack-cups goes 40 to 90%.

## Problem

- Behaviour cloning is bounded by its demonstration data: a policy that fails cannot learn from that failure without fresh human teleoperation, so deployment rollouts are wasted.
- RL fine-tuning of multi-billion-parameter vision-language-action models on sparse-reward on-policy data is expensive, brittle, and can silently degrade the competent behaviour-cloning prior.
- Value-based planners re-rank candidate actions but were never shown at VLA scale, and prior value-guidance work never iterates online to actively self-improve.

## Method

- ==Off-policy Q-function== over length-H action chunks with parameter-disjoint ==DinoV2== and ==T5== encoders, ==Q-chunking== to shrink the bootstrap horizon, and ==HL-Gauss categorical regression== for stability.
- ==Value-guided action selection==: draw N chunks from a truncated 3-step ==flow-matching== pass, score them in one batched decoder pass, and execute a single-step ==softmax Q-weighted average==.
- ==Self-improvement loop== appends successful and failed deployment rollouts to a ==replay buffer== and runs Q-only gradient steps each iteration, leaving the behaviour-cloning weights frozen throughout.

## Results

- Ten self-improvement iterations lift every benchmark: LIBERO-10 **93 to 99%**, LIBERO-Spatial **91.5 to 98.5%**, RoboTwin **83.8 to 91.4%**, mean **92.1 to 97.6%** (plus **5.5 points**).
- Real bimanual tasks improve without human intervention in five iterations: stack-cups **40 to 90%**, insert-wallet **25 to 80%**, while supervised fine-tuning on successes alone stalls at **55%** and **30%**.
- Against five online baselines at equal budget, Best-of-N plateaus at **95%** and filtered SFT at **93.5%**; a planning step costs **400 ms** on RoboTwin, inside the **960 ms** budget.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Critics and actors can be trained on different data: value estimation absorbs failures that imitation must discard, so decoupling them turns wasted rollouts into learning signal.
> - Scaling self-improvement with the critic's size rather than the policy's grows more attractive as backbones enlarge, and frozen weights make the update loop inherently safe.
> - Proposal quality bounds everything: candidates must stay on the policy's manifold, since jagged perturbations land outside the value function's support and get spuriously high scores.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation has experienced substantial advancements, largely driven by large-scale Behavior Cloning (BC) policies. Models such as RT-2, Octo, $\pi^{*}0.6$, Diffusion Policy, and FastWAM have demonstrated capabilities in zero-shot generalization on real-world tasks. However, this success is accompanied by a structural limitation inherent to BC: these policies are bounded by the data they were trained on. A BC policy that fails during deployment cannot inherently learn from that failure without additional human demonstrations. This "demo ceiling" becomes a bottleneck as models scale to billions of parameters trained on millions of demonstrations.

A predominant response to this limitation has been Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning of the policy parameters. While this approach offers a path to self-improvement, it faces significant challenges when applied to multi-billion-parameter Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Updating such large models with sparse-reward, on-policy data is computationally expensive, often brittle, and carries the risk of degrading the pre-existing BC prior that endows the policy with its initial competence.

An alternative line of research involves value-based planning, where action candidates are re-ranked at inference time based on estimated values. However, scaling these methods to VLA architectures, which involve high-dimensional action spaces and long planning horizons, has proven difficult due to the computational intractability of online search over random action proposals.

This research positions itself by addressing these limitations through a novel integration of BC and value functions. It proposes to decouple the actor (the large BC policy) and the critic (a smaller Q-function). The core insight is that a BC policy can only be trained on successful demonstrations, whereas an off-policy Q-function can be trained on any trajectory, including failures, because it estimates value rather than imitating actions. This asymmetry allows for a self-improvement loop where the expensive, large BC policy can remain frozen, while all deployment signals, including failures, are absorbed into a much smaller, off-policy Q-function.

The paper distinguishes its approach, Q-Planning, from several related methods:
- **V-GPS:** Steers a frozen policy with an offline-trained value function but does not perform online iteration for self-improvement.
- **DSRL:** Adapts online but uses a latent-noise auxiliary actor, which Q-Planning avoids.
- **IBRL:** Keeps the imitation learning policy frozen but trains a fresh RL actor from scratch, which at VLA scale means relearning behaviors already encoded in BC.
- **DAWR (advantage-weighted regression):** Directly fine-tunes policy weights, which is resource-intensive and risks degrading the BC prior.
- **Concurrent BC-to-Q work:** Shares the frozen-BC and off-policy-Q components but typically trains an auxiliary RL actor and has not been demonstrated at multi-billion-parameter VLA scale.

Q-Planning is presented as the only method that combines five specific characteristics: a frozen BC policy, plug-and-play compatibility with a large VLA, the ability to learn from failures, offline-to-online Q-function training, and operation without an auxiliary actor.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to enable self-improvement for large visuomotor Behavior Cloning (BC) policies in robot manipulation, addressing the fundamental limitations of BC while mitigating the challenges associated with direct Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning of multi-billion-parameter models.

The motivation for this research stems from several identified issues:

- **Bounded Performance of BC:** Behavior Cloning policies, despite their success in imitating demonstrations, are inherently limited by the data they are trained on. They cannot autonomously learn from failed execution attempts during deployment, meaning that any observed failure requires additional human intervention or demonstrations to correct. This "demo ceiling" becomes increasingly restrictive as robot policies scale in complexity and parameter count.
- **Challenges of RL Fine-Tuning at Scale:** While RL offers a pathway to self-improvement, applying it to modern multi-billion-parameter Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models is problematic. Fine-tuning such large models with sparse-reward, on-policy data is often computationally prohibitive, can be brittle, and may inadvertently compromise the robust BC prior that initially confers the policy's competence.
- **Intractability of Pure Value-Based Planning for VLAs:** Traditional value-based planning methods, which re-rank action candidates, have not been successfully demonstrated at VLA scale due to the high-dimensional action spaces and long task horizons, making online search over action proposals computationally intractable.

To address these motivations, the central insight of Q-Planning is the strategic decoupling of the actor (the BC policy) and the critic (a Q-function). This approach is motivated by a critical asymmetry:
- A BC policy mandates training exclusively on successful demonstrations, as failures do not represent the desired behavior to imitate.
- An off-policy Q-function, however, is not restricted to successful trajectories. It can be trained on any data, including both successful and failed deployment rollouts, because its role is to estimate the value of states and actions, not to imitate specific behaviors.

Exploiting this asymmetry forms the core of Q-Planning's objectives:

1. **Develop an Off-Policy Q-function for Large BC Policies:** Design a Q-function architecture that can estimate values for action chunks proposed by a large BC policy. This Q-function is intended to be significantly smaller and parameter-disjoint from the BC policy, facilitating efficient training and updates. It must also be capable of learning from diverse data, including failures.
2. **Create a Real-Time Value-Guided Action Selector:** Implement an efficient inference mechanism that leverages the Q-function to select actions from the BC policy's proposals. This mechanism must be fast enough for real-time robotic control and capable of effectively utilizing the multi-modal nature of modern BC action distributions.
3. **Establish a Self-Improvement Loop for Q-function-Only Updates:** Devise an online learning process where only the Q-function is updated using newly collected deployment data (both successes and failures), while the parameters of the large BC policy remain frozen. This loop aims to provide continuous self-improvement, allowing the robot to learn from its own experiences without human intervention or the risks associated with fine-tuning the foundational policy.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to provide a scalable, robust, and efficient method for robots to autonomously improve their manipulation capabilities, moving "beyond imitation" towards true self-learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Q-Planning integrates three main components to achieve self-improvement for robot policies based on Behaviour Cloning (BC): an off-policy Q-function designed for action chunks, a single-step Q-weighted average for action selection during inference, and an iterative self-improvement loop that exclusively updates the Q-function while keeping the base BC policy frozen.

The problem setting involves language-conditioned manipulation tasks within a partially-observed Markov Decision Process (MDP). The robot's actions ($a_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a}$) are generated as action chunks of length $H$ ($a_{t:t+H}$) by the BC policy ($\pi_{BC}$). Observations ($o_t$) are RGB images, accompanied by natural-language instructions ($\ell$). Rewards ($r_t$) are sparse and binary, indicating task success. The core constraint is the ability to improve policy performance using online rollouts without collecting new human demonstrations or modifying the pre-trained $\pi_{BC}$, which was initially trained on a dataset of successful demonstrations ($D_{BC}$).

#### 3.1. Off-Policy Q-function over Action Chunks

To address the instability of standard scalar Q-regression in environments with sparse rewards and long horizons typical of manipulation tasks, two key design choices were made for the Q-function:
- **Q-Chunking:** The entire length-$H$ action chunk is treated as a single "super-action." This reduces the effective bootstrapping horizon by a factor of $H$, which helps mitigate variance in value estimation.
- **HL-Gauss Categorical Regression:** Instead of regressing a scalar Q-value directly, the target value is projected onto a fixed grid of bins using a Gaussian kernel. The Q-network then predicts a categorical distribution over these bins, trained with a cross-entropy objective. This approach stabilizes learning under sparse and potentially bimodal return distributions.

**Module Design:** The Q-function, denoted $Q_{\phi}(o_t, \ell, a_{t:t+H})$, has its own dedicated visual (DinoV2) and language (T5) encoders. These encoders, $E^{vis}_{\phi}(o_t)$ and $E^{txt}_{\phi}(\ell)$, are parameter-disjoint from the BC policy. The encoded visual ($z^{vis}_t$) and text ($z^{txt}$) tokens serve as keys and values for a transformer decoder. The candidate action chunk $a_{t:t+H}$ is input as a sequence of query tokens. The decoder's output is projected to $B$ HL-Gauss logits, and the predicted scalar Q-value is the expectation of the softmaxed categorical distribution over a predefined grid of bin centers. This architecture ensures that updates to the Q-function do not interfere with the BC policy's encoders.

**Training:** $Q_{\phi}$ is trained on chunked transitions $(o_t, a_{t:t+H}, r_{t:t+H}, o_{t+H}, a_{t+H:t+2H})$ sampled from a replay buffer $D$. Initially, $D$ contains only $D_{BC}$ (successful demonstrations), but it is subsequently augmented with online rollouts. The Bellman target for the Q-chunking loss uses the *next chunk from the buffer* ($a_{t+H:t+2H}$) for bootstrapping, rather than an action selected by the current planner. This design decision simplifies training by avoiding a full planner call per Bellman update and maintains an unbiased estimate of $Q_{\pi_{BC}}$ since buffer chunks are drawn from the distribution the BC policy was trained on or the distribution generated by the evolving Q-guided planner. A critical aspect is that the loss function applies uniformly to both successful and failed rollouts. Failed rollouts, having zero terminal rewards, will have their Q-values driven towards zero, allowing the Q-function to learn from undesired outcomes—an asymmetry that BC policies cannot exploit. The Q-function parameters ($\phi$) are updated using AdamW, and exponential moving average (EMA) target parameters ($\bar{\phi}$) are used for stability.

#### 3.2. Q-Weighted Action Selection

At inference time, the Q-function is used to select an action chunk from multiple proposals generated by the frozen BC policy.
**Motivation:** Modern flow-matching BC heads can produce multi-modal action draws, meaning the candidate actions are diverse and cover distinct behavioral modes, rather than being clustered around a single mean. These candidates are also within the BC policy's manifold, ensuring they are plausible actions for the Q-function to evaluate. This multi-modality is crucial for effective exploration and self-improvement. The method avoids computationally intensive iterative sample-based search (e.g., MPPI) in favor of a simpler, faster selection mechanism.

**Module Design:** At each planning step $t$:
1. $N$ candidate action chunks $a^{(n)}_{t:t+H}$ are drawn from the frozen $\pi_{BC}(o_t, \ell)$. A truncated 3-step flow-matching pass (instead of the default 10 steps) is used for lower latency and to preserve sample diversity.
2. Each candidate $a^{(n)}_{t:t+H}$ is scored by $Q_{\phi}(o_t, \ell, a^{(n)}_{t:t+H})$.
3. A softmax Q-weighted average of these candidates is computed to determine the executed action chunk $\bar{a}_{t:t+H}$, with temperature $\lambda$ (Equation 3).
The Q-network evaluation is efficient: visual and text encoders are amortized (run once per planning step), and the $N$ candidates are processed in a single batched forward pass by the Q-decoder. This results in a planning step latency of 400 ms (for $N=32$) on RoboTwin, which is faster than a single 10-step BC inference and within typical replanning budgets.

#### 3.3. Self-Improvement Loop

The self-improvement loop (Algorithm 1) continuously refines the Q-function based on online deployment data, including failures, without altering the BC policy.
1. **Rollout Collection:** The Q-Planning agent is deployed for $M$ episodes per task. Both successful and failed rollouts are collected and appended to the replay buffer $D$.
2. **Q-function Refinement:** For $S$ gradient steps, the Q-function parameters $\phi$ are updated using minibatches sampled from the expanded replay buffer $D$ via the HL-Gauss Bellman loss (Eq. 2). The target network parameters $\bar{\phi}$ are updated via EMA.
This process iterates. Since only the Q-function (approximately 1 billion parameters, compared to multi-billion-parameter BC policies) is updated, each iteration is significantly less expensive than a full policy gradient step. Typical parameters include $M=100$ episodes per task in simulation (20 on real robots) and $S=200$ Q-only gradient steps per iteration.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on two simulated benchmarks: the four LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10, comprising 10 hard long-horizon tasks) and 47 RoboTwin tasks. Performance was measured by success rates and successful episode lengths, aggregated over 20 episodes per task. The base BC policy used was the publicly available FastWAM model, which remained frozen. The Q-function was trained exclusively on the same successful demonstration data as FastWAM, with specific gradient steps (12k for LIBERO, 45k for RoboTwin). Evaluation protocols adhered to standard settings, including episode step limits.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings across four primary experimental questions, evaluating the efficacy and efficiency of Q-Planning in simulated and real-robot environments.

#### 4.1. Q1: Action Source for Planning (Simulated)

An initial set of experiments compared different action proposal sources for the Q-function on LIBERO-10, with $N=64$ candidate chunks and a fixed Q-function:
- **FastWAM (reference, no Q):** Achieved 90.0% success.
- **MPPI with zero-mean Gaussian noise:** Resulted in 89.5% success, underperforming the unguided BC baseline. This was attributed to jagged noise producing action chunks outside the Q-function's training support, leading to unreliable Q-value estimations.
- **MPPI with temporal smoothing:** Improved success to 93.0%. The 1-D Gaussian kernel applied to noise ensured temporally coherent perturbed chunks, keeping them closer to the BC's trajectory manifold.
- **Q-Planning's 3-step flow-matching draws + Q-weighted average:** Also achieved 93.0% success. This method matched the performance of temporally-smoothed MPPI while running 1.7 times faster on LIBERO (640 ms vs. 1114 ms latency) and 3.2 times faster on RoboTwin. It also eliminated the need for several handcrafted hyperparameters. Given its comparable performance and superior efficiency, this method was adopted for subsequent experiments.

#### 4.2. Q2: Online Self-Improvement with Q-Planning (Simulated)

Experiments on the four LIBERO suites and RoboTwin evaluated Q-Planning's performance after 10 iterations of online self-improvement (Q-only updates, frozen BC).
- **Offline Q-Planning:** Even without self-improvement, Q-weighted selection over BC draws (the "offline" column) demonstrated a mean success lift of +1.3 percentage points (pp) over the frozen FastWAM BC baseline, winning 4 out of 5 benchmarks initially.
- **10 Iterations of Self-Improvement:** The self-improvement loop consistently elevated performance across all tested benchmarks:
 - **LIBERO-Spatial:** 91.5% (offline) $\to$ 98.5% success.
 - **LIBERO-Object:** 99.5% (offline) $\to$ 100.0% success. For this near-ceiling task, the loop also shortened successful episode length from 139 to 120 steps.
 - **LIBERO-Goal:** 99.0% (offline) $\to$ 99.0% success. Episode length shortened from 110 to 99 steps.
 - **LIBERO-10:** 93.0% (offline) $\to$ 99.0% success. Episode length shortened from 261 to 224 steps.
 - **RoboTwin (47 tasks):** 83.8% (offline) $\to$ 91.4% success.
- **Overall:** Mean success across all benchmarks improved from 92.1% (frozen BC) to 93.4% (offline Q-Planning) and further to 97.6% (self-improved Q-Planning), representing a +5.5 pp gain over the frozen BC.

#### 4.3. Q3: Q-Planning's Role in Self-Improvement (Simulated)

The study compared Q-Planning on LIBERO-10 against five other online baselines under an identical rollout budget to ascertain its unique contribution to stable self-improvement:
- **Q-Planning:** Achieved stable improvement, lifting success from an initial 93% to 99%.
- **Best-of-N (argmax selection):** Plateaued at 95%, indicating that while value-guided selection contributes, the Q-weighted average combined with self-improvement is necessary for higher gains.
- **Filtered SFT (re-imitating only successful rollouts):** Plateaued at 93.5%, demonstrating that re-imitating only successes cannot leverage the critical failure signal that $Q_{\phi}$ can absorb.
- **Other baselines (IBRL, DSRL, DAWR):** IBRL collapsed, DSRL exhibited wide oscillations (between 69% and 91%), and DAWR performed below the initial frozen BC.
- **Conclusion:** Under the same online budget, Q-Planning was the only method that demonstrated stable improvement from deployment failures.
- **Q-value Visualization:** Analysis of Q-values over time showed that on successful episodes, $Q_{\phi}$ monotonically increased (from $\sim 0.26$ to $\sim 0.63$), while on failed episodes, it oscillated in a lower range. The scatter of candidate Q-values indicated that the Q-function effectively separated high-value from low-value BC draws at each step, providing the signal for both weighted averaging and the self-improvement loop.

#### 4.4. Q4: Real-Robot Self-Improvement

Q-Planning was deployed on a real bimanual robot for two contact-rich tasks: "stack-cups" and "insert-wallet."
- **Experimental Protocol:** Starting with 100 base demonstrations, 20 online episodes were collected per task per iteration for 5 iterations. The BC policy remained frozen, and no human intervention (beyond episode resets and success labels) was involved.
- **Stack-Cups:** Initial BC baseline of 40% success improved to 90% after five iterations.
- **Insert-Wallet:** Initial BC baseline of 25% success improved to 80% after five iterations.
- **Comparison with SFT:** A baseline of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on successful rollouts alone stalled at 55% for stack-cups and 30% for insert-wallet. This result reinforces that the significant gains of Q-Planning on real-robot tasks stem from its ability to absorb and learn from failure signals, which SFT discards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Q-Planning framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robot manipulation, particularly in addressing the limitations of Behavior Cloning (BC) for large-scale robot policies.

- **Enabling Self-Improvement from Failure:** The most direct significance is Q-Planning's ability to allow robot policies to learn from their own deployment failures without requiring additional human demonstrations. This overcomes a fundamental constraint of BC, moving beyond mere imitation to autonomous adaptation. This capability is critical for scaling robotic agents to more complex, less-structured real-world environments where exhaustive demonstration coverage is impractical.

- **Scalability for Large Foundational Models:** Q-Planning offers a scalable solution for fine-tuning multi-billion-parameter Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. By keeping the large BC policy frozen and updating only a comparatively smaller Q-function (approximately 1 billion parameters), the computational cost and risks associated with direct Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning of the entire VLA backbone are substantially reduced. This "critic-only" update mechanism makes online self-improvement dramatically cheaper and safer, preserving the hard-learned BC prior while still allowing for adaptation. As VLA backbones continue to grow, this property becomes increasingly vital.

- **Real-Time and Efficient Inference:** The development of a single-step Q-weighted average for action selection enables real-time operation. This efficiency, achieved by amortizing encoder costs and leveraging the multi-modality of BC draws, is crucial for practical robot deployment where swift decision-making is necessary. The measured latency profiles demonstrate that Q-Planning can operate within typical replanning budgets.

- **Improved Robustness and Performance:** The empirical results in both simulation (lifting mean success from 92.1% to 97.6% across LIBERO and RoboTwin suites) and on real-robot contact-rich tasks (stack-cups 40% $\to$ 90%, insert-wallet 25% $\to$ 80%) demonstrate substantial performance improvements. The ability to incorporate failure signals is particularly impactful for tasks where initial BC performance might be low or where robust recovery from errors is essential.

- **Generalizability of the Framework:** The core framework is not intrinsically tied to imitation learning. The BC policy serves as a proposal distribution for plausible action chunks. This implies that other diverse action generation methods, such as offline RL policies, reward-weighted imitation, or world-model rollouts, could potentially be substituted for the BC head. This flexibility opens avenues for integrating Q-Planning with future advancements in action generation.

- **Path Towards Autonomous Learning Systems:** By providing a robust and efficient mechanism for self-improvement from deployment experiences, Q-Planning contributes to the long-term vision of truly autonomous robotic systems that can continuously refine their skills and adapt to novel situations with minimal human oversight. Future work could extend this to open-ended tasks by developing more sophisticated success criteria beyond simple terminal rewards, further enhancing the system's autonomy.
