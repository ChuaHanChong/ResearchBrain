# Formal Verification of Learned Multi-Agent Communication Policies via Decision Tree Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework developed by researchers at the University of Arkansas at Little Rock enables the formal safety verification of learned multi-agent communication policies by distilling neural network behaviors into interpretable decision trees, achieving 97.9% fidelity for 5-agent systems and scaling verification to 7 agents in under 4 hours. The approach successfully verified 16 out of 18 safety, liveness, and cooperation properties, demonstrating improved fidelity and faster verification for policies using discrete communication.

## Problem

- Deploying multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) in safety-critical domains requires formal safety guarantees, which are challenging due to the black-box nature of neural network policies.
- Formally verifying deep neural networks is computationally intensive (NP-hard), making direct verification of complex MARL policies impractical.
- The exponential growth of the state space in multi-agent systems (curse of dimensionality) renders traditional model checking computationally prohibitive.

## Method

- A four-stage pipeline is proposed: domain-specific feature extraction, decision tree distillation of neural policies, automatic translation to PRISM modules with complete feature mapping, and compositional verification.
- Neural network policies are distilled into classification decision trees using the CART algorithm, trained on state-action pairs with 50 engineered features (spatial, communication, task-related).
- Compositional verification mitigates state space explosion by decomposing the problem based on property category, modeling non-focal agents as empirically estimated stochastic processes.

## Results

- Decision tree distillation achieved **97.9%** ± **1.2%** fidelity for a 5-agent system, with fidelity decreasing slightly to **96.8%** for 7 agents.
- The compositional verification scaled to 7 agents in 3 hours and 46 minutes, significantly reducing the effective state space (e.g., from 8.8x10^15 to ~160,000 states for an agent pair).
- 16 out of 18 PCTL properties (**88.9%**) were satisfied; safety properties transferred closely to neural policies (≤**0.6** pp deviation), while discrete communication policies showed 11-13 pp higher fidelity and 3-4 times faster verification than continuous alternatives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Domain-specific feature engineering is crucial for achieving high distillation fidelity, improving accuracy by up to 19.7 percentage points over raw observations.
> - Compositional verification enables scaling to practical team sizes by effectively reducing the verifiable state space, resulting in sub-exponential scaling of verification time (T ≈ 3.8 N^2.9 minutes).
> - Discrete communication policies (e.g., VQ-VIB) yield significantly higher distillation fidelity and faster verification compared to continuous communication methods, offering advantages for verifiable autonomy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) in safety-critical domains, such as drone swarms or autonomous vehicle fleets, necessitates formal safety guarantees that extend beyond empirical performance metrics. Traditional neural network policies, while effective in achieving complex coordination and emergent communication, lack these guarantees due to their inherent black-box nature. The formal verification of neural networks is a known NP-hard problem, and this complexity is compounded in multi-agent systems by the exponential growth of the state space. Previous research, such as that by Garg et al., has specifically identified the verification of learned communication policies as a significant open challenge.

Existing neural network verification techniques, exemplified by Reluplex and DeepPoly, typically focus on single neural networks and do not scale effectively to multi-agent systems, particularly those involving communication. Similarly, policy distillation methods like VIPER and MAVIPER extract decision trees from single-agent or multi-agent policies, respectively, but often do not integrate these abstractions into a comprehensive, end-to-end formal verification pipeline that addresses communication semantics. Approaches such as COOL-MC and VERINTER construct policy-induced Discrete-Time Markov Chains (DTMCs) by querying neural networks, thereby avoiding approximation, but they are also generally limited to single-agent contexts.

For multi-agent systems, compositional model checking and assume-guarantee reasoning aim to mitigate state space explosion by decomposing verification problems. PRISM-games supports the verification of stochastic multi-player games, but these require explicit game-theoretic models rather than learned policies. While these methods offer ways to handle complex systems, their direct application to learned multi-agent neural policies, especially those with emergent communication, remains a challenge.

In the realm of multi-agent communication, various architectures like CommNet and TarMAC facilitate continuous message passing, while more recent work, including Vector-Quantized Variational Information Bottleneck (VQ-VIB), explores discrete communication to enhance bandwidth efficiency. The choice between continuous and discrete communication has implications for interpretability and, consequently, verifiability.

This work contributes to this landscape by introducing an end-to-end framework specifically designed for the formal safety verification of *learned multi-agent communication policies*. It bridges the gap between deep MARL and formal methods by abstracting complex neural policies into interpretable, verifiable decision trees, thereby addressing both the intractability of neural network verification and the state space explosion in multi-agent settings, with a particular focus on the semantics of inter-agent communication.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the critical need for formal safety guarantees when deploying multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) systems in real-world, safety-critical applications. These applications, such as coordinated drone swarms or fleets of autonomous vehicles, require assurance that agents will operate reliably and safely, avoiding undesirable outcomes like collisions or deadlocks. While MARL policies, often implemented as neural networks, have demonstrated advanced coordination capabilities through emergent communication, their black-box nature prevents formal certification. This lack of verifiable safety is a significant barrier to their widespread adoption in regulated industries.

The researchers identify two principal challenges that necessitate their proposed approach:
1. **Intractability of Neural Network Verification:** Formally verifying deep neural networks is computationally intensive, often classified as NP-hard. This makes direct verification of complex MARL policies impractical.
2. **Exponential State Space Growth in Multi-Agent Systems:** As the number of agents in a system increases, the global state space grows exponentially. This phenomenon, known as the "curse of dimensionality," renders traditional model checking approaches computationally prohibitive for even moderately sized multi-agent teams.

Furthermore, existing verification efforts have largely focused on single-agent systems or multi-agent systems without sophisticated learned communication. The paper highlights an open challenge identified by Garg et al. regarding the verification of *learned communication policies*, which are central to complex multi-agent coordination.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:
- **To develop an end-to-end framework** that enables the formal safety verification of learned multi-agent communication policies. This framework is designed to transform opaque neural network policies into a symbolic, interpretable representation suitable for formal analysis.
- **To achieve high-fidelity abstraction** of neural policies into interpretable decision trees. This abstraction must faithfully capture the behavior of the original neural networks, ensuring that properties verified on the abstracted model are representative of the neural policy's behavior.
- **To facilitate efficient formal verification** of these abstracted policies using established probabilistic model checking tools (specifically PRISM). This requires a methodology for translating decision trees, including their communication semantics, into formal specifications.
- **To ensure the transferability of verified properties** from the abstracted decision trees back to the original neural network policies. This empirical validation step is crucial to demonstrate that the safety guarantees obtained from the formal model apply to the actual learned system.
- **To demonstrate scalability** of the verification process to practical team sizes, primarily through compositional decomposition techniques that mitigate the state space explosion problem.
- **To investigate the impact of discrete communication** on verification, specifically assessing whether discrete message spaces offer advantages in terms of distillation fidelity and verification efficiency compared to continuous communication methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The framework proposed by Farooq and Iqbal is a four-stage pipeline designed for the formal verification of learned multi-agent communication policies.

#### 3.1. A. Stage 1: Domain-Specific Feature Extraction

The initial stage involves converting raw agent observations into a more abstract and informative feature space. The raw observations, consisting of 14-dimensional vectors of position, velocity, and local sensing data, were found to be insufficient for achieving high-fidelity policy distillation (only 78.2% accuracy). To address this, 50 candidate features were engineered and organized into three categories:
- **Spatial Features (15):** These include normalized position, velocity, distances to nearest targets and neighbors, boundary distances, quadrant indicators, and angular features. All these are directly computable from agent and target positions.
- **Communication Features (20):** This set encompasses received message codes (one-hot encoded for up to 4 nearest neighbors), the count of unique messages, a measure of message consistency, and communication degree. Attention weights from the communication module are discretized into four ordinal bins (low, medium, high, dominant) and recomputed dynamically at each PRISM timestep.
- **Task Features (15):** These relate to the mission objective, including target capture status, normalized time remaining, local target density, indicators for closeness to targets, episode progress ratio, and role indicators. History-dependent features, such as recent reward sums and movement entropy, are also included, necessitating auxiliary state variables in the PRISM model.

Post-distillation analysis indicated that 32-38 of these 50 features were utilized in the decision trees, with spatial and communication features accounting for the majority (85%) of tree splits.

#### 3.2. B. Stage 2: Decision Tree Distillation

In the second stage, the neural network policy for each agent is distilled into a classification decision tree (ˆπ_i). This process uses the CART (Classification and Regression Trees) algorithm.
- **Training Data:** Each decision tree is trained on 100,000 state-action pairs sampled from 1,000 multi-agent episodes. The input to the tree is the 50-dimensional feature vector (x), and the output is the predicted action (a).
- **Objective:** The objective is to minimize the misclassification rate between the decision tree's predicted action and the neural network's action for a given feature vector.
- **Tree Structure:** For a maximum depth of 15, the decision trees typically contain approximately 1,800 internal nodes and 1,801 leaves per agent. Increasing the depth to 20 results in around 4,200 nodes. Key features for splits included nearest-target distance, communication degree, and boundary distance.
- **Fidelity Measurement:** The quality of distillation is assessed by "fidelity," defined as the percentage of agreement between the decision tree's action and the neural network's action on a held-out validation set of 20,000 state-action pairs.

#### 3.3. C. Stage 3: PRISM Translation with Complete Feature Mapping

The third stage involves automatically translating each distilled decision tree into a PRISM module, which models a Discrete-Time Markov Chain (DTMC). A critical design principle here is ensuring *complete feature-to-state correspondence*, meaning every feature used in a decision tree split must be computable from the PRISM model's state variables.
- **State Variables:** PRISM models are defined with base state variables for each agent, including position (x, y coordinates on a 20x20 grid), velocity (discrete changes), current message, and target capture flags. Auxiliary state variables are introduced for history-dependent features (e.g., recent reward accumulator, action history buffer for movement entropy) and discretized attention bins. Global variables track target positions and the overall timestep.
- **Feature Computation:** The automated translator ensures that all decision tree predicates are expressed using integer arithmetic over these PRISM state variables. For example, continuous distance thresholds are converted to equivalent squared integer distances. Attention weights, which are dynamic, are handled by pre-computing a lookup table from the trained neural network, mapping current neighbor distances and message codes to one of four attention bins. This table covers all reachable inputs, ensuring no out-of-table lookups during verification.
- **Probabilistic Transitions:** The DTMC accounts for two sources of stochasticity:
 1. **Stochastic Initial States:** Agent starting positions are uniformly sampled across the 20x20 grid, and property probabilities reflect expectations over this initial distribution.
 2. **Leaf Purity:** At each leaf node of a decision tree, the action predicted by the leaf is executed with a probability equal to the leaf's "purity" (the fraction of training samples at that leaf that resulted in the predicted action). With probability (1 - purity), a uniform random alternative action is chosen. Leaf purities are computed on a held-out validation set to prevent overfitting.
- **Environment Dynamics:** The environment dynamics (e.g., grid movement, collision detection, target capture) are modeled as deterministic rules within PRISM.

#### 3.4. D. Stage 4: Compositional Verification

To address the exponential state space growth for N-agent systems, the verification process employs compositional decomposition, tailored by property category.
- **Safety Properties (e.g., pairwise collision):** Collision is an inherently pairwise event. The system-level collision probability is bounded by the union bound: P(any collision) ≤ Σ P(collide(i, j)) for all pairs (i, j). To compute each pairwise collision probability, the non-focal agents (i.e., agents other than the pair being verified) are modeled as stochastic processes. Their transition kernels are empirically estimated from 1,000 dedicated calibration rollouts, projecting their state onto contextual features (grid region, neighbor count, distance bin). Laplace smoothing is applied to sparse bins. This empirical modeling aims for realistic rather than provably conservative estimates, which are then validated via Monte Carlo simulation.
- **Liveness Properties (e.g., per-agent reachability):** These properties are verified for individual agents, with the behavior of other agents modeled using the same empirical transition distributions calibrated in the safety analysis.
- **Cooperation Properties (e.g., communication-dependent behaviors):** These are verified on communication subgraphs, which limit the scope of the problem to agents within communication range (r_comm). This approach partially addresses multi-hop dependencies but acknowledges limitations for complex, longer-range information relay chains.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding distillation fidelity, verification scalability, property satisfaction, property transferability, and the advantages of discrete communication.

#### 4.1. A. RQ1: Decision Tree Fidelity

- **Overall Fidelity:** For a 5-agent system, the decision tree distillation achieved a fidelity of 97.9% ± 1.2% to the original neural network policies.
- **Impact of Feature Engineering:** Domain-specific feature extraction significantly improved fidelity. Raw 14-dimensional observations yielded only 78.2% fidelity. Adding spatial features increased this by +11.2 percentage points (pp), communication features by an additional +5.3 pp, and task features by +3.2 pp, cumulatively reaching 97.9%. All these gains were statistically significant (p < 0.001).
- **Effect of Team Size:** Fidelity decreased slightly with increasing team size: 98.2% for 3 agents, 97.9% for 5 agents, and 96.8% for 7 agents.

#### 4.2. B. RQ2: Verification Scalability

- **Runtime:** The compositional verification approach demonstrated scalability to practical team sizes. For N=3, verification took 18.3 minutes; for N=5, 52.2 minutes; and for N=7, 3 hours and 46 minutes.
- **Scaling Law:** Verification time scaled sub-exponentially with team size, approximated by T ≈ 3.8 · N^2.9 minutes, which is more efficient than the exponential state space growth that would occur without decomposition.
- **State Space Reduction:** The compositional decomposition significantly reduced the effective state space. For a pair of agents (part of the N=7 system), the reachable state space was pruned from a potential 8.8x10^15 states to approximately 160,000 states.

#### 4.3. C. RQ3: Property Satisfaction

Out of 18 defined PCTL properties across safety, liveness, and cooperation categories, 16 were satisfied (88.9%).
- **Safety Properties (S1–S5):** All five safety properties met their specified thresholds.
 - Collision probability (S1) was 0.3%, well below the ≤1% threshold.
 - Boundary violation (S2) was 2.1% (threshold ≤5%).
 - Minimum separation (S3) was 97.2% (threshold ≥95%).
 - Deadlock probability (S4) was 0.8% (threshold ≤2%).
 - Connectivity (S5) was 94.3% (threshold ≥90%).
 These favorable margins were attributed to the collision penalty in the cooperative training environment.
- **Liveness Properties (L1–L6):** Five out of six liveness properties were satisfied.
 - L4 (all targets captured within 200 steps) failed, achieving 43.8% probability against a 50% threshold, indicating a performance limitation rather than a safety issue.
- **Cooperation Properties (C1–C7):** Six out of seven cooperation properties were satisfied.
 - C6 (role differentiation) failed, achieving 68.8% probability against a 75% threshold. This was attributed to the decision trees' reliance on opportunistic specialization based on features like nearest-target distance rather than explicit role-assignment features.

#### 4.4. D. RQ4: Property Transfer via Monte Carlo Validation

To confirm that the properties verified on the decision trees transferred to the original neural policies, Monte Carlo simulations were conducted over 10,000 episodes (5 seeds).
- **Safety Property Transfer:** Safety properties demonstrated close transfer, with deviations of ≤0.6 percentage points (pp) between the tree-verified results and the neural policy Monte Carlo estimates. All deviations were within the 95% confidence intervals.
- **Liveness and Cooperation Property Transfer:** These categories showed larger deviations, up to 4.4 pp (e.g., L4).
- **Analysis of Discrepancies:** The 2.1% total tree-NN action disagreements were found to occur predominantly (68%) in high-density regions with complex communication (≥3 neighbors). Only 7% of disagreements occurred in collision-adjacent states. This spatial distribution of disagreement largely explained why safety property transfer was tight, while liveness and cooperation properties exhibited larger discrepancies.
- **Worst-Case Bound:** A theoretical worst-case transfer bound (Proposition 1) was noted, p_θ ≤ ˆp + H · ε, where H is the episode horizon and ε is the fidelity gap. However, this bound proved vacuous for practical parameters, reinforcing the need for empirical validation.

#### 4.5. E. RQ5: Discrete Communication Advantage

Comparison of VQ-VIB (discrete messages) with continuous communication methods (TarMAC and CommNet), where continuous messages were discretized via k-means for PRISM encoding, revealed distinct advantages for discrete communication:
- **Higher Fidelity:** VQ-VIB achieved +11.6 to +13.2 pp higher fidelity (97.9%) compared to TarMAC (86.3%) and CommNet (84.7%).
- **Faster Verification:** The higher fidelity of VQ-VIB policies resulted in 3–4 times faster verification times (52 minutes for VQ-VIB vs. 3h 12m for TarMAC and 2h 48m for CommNet), despite comparable task success rates across all methods.
- **Reason:** Discrete messages provided more natural categorical partitions for decision tree distillation, reducing information loss and improving fidelity compared to discretizing continuous vectors at cluster boundaries.

#### 4.6. F. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies confirmed the robustness of VQ-VIB hyperparameters. Different values for the Information Bottleneck parameter β (0.0–0.1) and vocabulary size K (64–256) generally maintained similar levels of property satisfaction (16/18 satisfied), with K=64 resulting in one fewer satisfied cooperation property. A tree depth of 15 was identified as a good balance between fidelity (97.9%) and verification time, with a marginal fidelity gain (+0.4 pp) at depth 20 incurring a 71% increase in runtime.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the first end-to-end abstraction-based framework specifically designed for the formal verification of learned multi-agent *communication* policies. This work is significant because it provides a practical method to bridge the gap between the empirical performance of deep multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) and the formal safety guarantees required for real-world deployment of multi-robot systems.

The potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Trust and Deployment in Safety-Critical Domains:** By offering empirically validated safety guarantees, the framework can increase trust in MARL systems, paving the way for their deployment in high-stakes applications such as drone swarms for infrastructure inspection, autonomous vehicle fleets, or collaborative robotics in hazardous environments, where formal certification is essential.
- **Addressing Fundamental Verification Challenges:** The methodology effectively tackles two core difficulties in MARL verification: the intractability of directly verifying neural networks and the exponential state space explosion inherent in multi-agent systems. It does so through policy abstraction via decision tree distillation and compositional probabilistic model checking.
- **Insights into Policy Behavior:** The use of interpretable decision trees provides an "audit trail" into controller behavior, revealing which features (e.g., nearest-target distance, communication degree) drive decisions. This interpretability is valuable for debugging, understanding emergent behaviors, and identifying areas for policy improvement.
- **Advantage of Discrete Communication:** The finding that discrete communication (VQ-VIB) leads to significantly higher distillation fidelity and faster verification times offers a design principle for future MARL communication architectures aimed at verifiable autonomy.
- **Practical Scalability:** The demonstrated scalability of the compositional verification to practical team sizes (up to 7 agents with runtimes suitable for nightly builds or continuous integration pipelines) indicates its readiness for realistic system sizes.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The research identifies clear pathways for strengthening its guarantees, such as integrating Interval DTMCs for provably conservative bounds and employing assume-guarantee contracts for tighter multi-agent composition. These directions suggest that the framework is robust and extensible, contributing to the broader field of verifiable AI.

In summary, this work represents a step towards making learned multi-agent behaviors formally certifiable, which is a crucial requirement for their safe and reliable integration into complex robotic systems.
