# SEED-UMI: Sharing the Exoskeleton between human and robot for onE-to-one Dexterous demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> SEED-UMI is a dexterous demonstration framework where the human operator and the robot hand wear the same kinematically isomorphic 20-DoF exoskeleton, making joint encoders and wrist cameras a shared physical measurement. Robot motor babbling plus paired replay fine-tuning learns a contact-robust encoder-to-command mapping, and policies train on raw wrist images without segmentation or inpainting, reaching 70.0% mean success while collecting data 3.0x faster than teleoperation.

## Problem

- Vision-based hand tracking degrades under occlusion and jitter during contact, while glove systems suffer skin-motion artifacts and need complex human-to-robot joint correspondence mapping.
- Prior exoskeletons mount only on the human side and calibrate retargeting once in free space, so the open-loop mapping drifts under contact friction, load, and joint coupling.
- Appearance gaps between human and robot observations are patched by generative inpainting or segmentation, adding model dependence and destroying contact pixels needed for fine manipulation.

## Method

- Kinematically isomorphic 20-DoF ==exoskeleton== co-designed with the target hand, worn by both human and robot; a parallel four-bar linkage routes joint rotation to a dorsal contactless magnetic encoder.
- Two-stage ==encoder-to-command mapping==: robot motor babbling fits an initial history-conditioned model, then paired replay fine-tunes it against a learned forward model using encoder-consistency, command-regularization, and smoothness losses.
- Exoskeleton-mounted sensing, a dorsal T265 for 6-DoF wrist pose and a ventral 150-degree fisheye, feeds **ACT**, **Diffusion Policy**, and **pi-0.5** raw wrist images plus the 20-dim encoder vector.

## Results

- Across five contact-rich tasks and three backbones, paired fine-tuning reaches **70.0%** mean success versus **71.7%** for robot-side teleoperation and **57.3%** without fine-tuning.
- Paired fine-tuning adds **12.7** points on average, concentrated on contact-loaded tasks: Screw Driving **+16.7**, Table Cleaning **+15.0**, Air Freshener Spray **+15.0** points.
- In a 30-minute window one operator collected **52** successful AirPods demonstrations versus **18** by teleoperation, a **3.0x** throughput gain; it also beat teleoperation on throwing, **68.3%** vs **60.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sharing one physical device across embodiments converts retargeting into paired supervised learning, turning the human-robot correspondence gap into a measurable, correctable signal.
> - Free-space calibration is the failure point: mapping error appears only under contact load, so calibration should be collected in the regime where the policy operates.
> - Hardware alignment can substitute for generative visual bridging; removing the appearance gap by design preserves contact pixels, but costs cross-hand generality and operator wearability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous hand manipulation by robots presents a significant challenge in robotics due to the high number of degrees of freedom (DoF) involved and the complex, contact-rich interactions required for many tasks. Imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a promising approach for robots to acquire complex skills, but its application to dexterous manipulation has been hindered by difficulties in collecting high-quality demonstration data and reliably transferring human motions to robotic hands, a process known as retargeting. These challenges stem primarily from inherent kinematic, sensing, and contact behavior differences between human and robot embodiments.

Current demonstration collection methods each involve trade-offs:
- **Vision-based hand tracking** offers natural human motion but suffers from data degradation due to occlusion and temporal jitter during object contact. Such systems often simplify dexterous tasks to end-effector control due to the difficulty of reliably recovering fine-grained finger motions.
- **Glove-based systems** provide more consistent motion data for interactive tasks but are susceptible to skin-motion artifacts and require complex, often post-processed, correspondence mapping to robot hand kinematics.
- **Exoskeleton-based devices** yield the most consistent hand gesture measurements, mechanically aligning with robot motions. However, existing exoskeletons can be bulky and restrictive, impacting operator comfort and demonstration throughput. Prior exoskeleton systems typically operate as one-way mappings from human to robot, often calibrated in free space. This free-space calibration degrades under the external loads, friction, and joint coupling effects encountered during contact-rich tasks. Furthermore, these systems (e.g., DexUMI) frequently rely on generative image translation to bridge visual disparities between human and robot observations, and do not typically leverage robot-side measurements to improve correspondence learning.

SEED-UMI positions itself within this landscape by proposing a fundamental shift in the human-robot interface design. Instead of developing increasingly complex retargeting models to bridge the gap between separate human and robot embodiments, SEED-UMI introduces a physically shared measurement interface. This approach transforms the retargeting problem into one of paired cross-embodiment supervision and aims to eliminate the need for advanced visual processing techniques like hand segmentation or generative inpainting by design. It extends the concept of exoskeleton-based systems by enabling the *same* exoskeleton to be worn by both the human and the robot, thus creating a truly shared physical and sensory framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to overcome the limitations in demonstration data collection and retargeting that bottleneck progress in imitation learning for dexterous hand manipulation. The authors identify several specific problems that motivate the SEED-UMI framework:

1. **Difficulty in Collecting Contact-Rich and Faithful Demonstrations:** Accurately capturing the intricate, natural human hand motions during contact-rich tasks and reliably transferring them to a robot is a persistent challenge. Existing methods either sacrifice naturalness, data quality under contact, or impose significant processing overheads. The motivation is to create an interface that allows human operators to perform demonstrations naturally, even in contact, while ensuring the data is directly applicable to the robot.

2. **Degradation of Retargeting under Contact Loads:** Traditional exoskeleton-based systems often establish human-to-robot mappings through free-space calibration. This approach is inadequate for tasks involving physical interaction, where factors like friction, external loads, and joint coupling effects introduce significant discrepancies between desired and actual robot motion. A key motivation is to develop a retargeting mechanism that remains robust and accurate under these real-world contact conditions.

3. **Visual Discrepancies and Complex Image Processing:** Differences in appearance between human hands (wearing the exoskeleton) and robot hands necessitate complex visual alignment techniques, such as generative image translation or inpainting. These methods can be computationally expensive, may not generalize well, and can potentially obscure critical contact information. The motivation is to design a system where the visual observations from the human demonstration phase are inherently aligned with those during robot execution, thereby simplifying the visuomotor policy learning.

4. **Inefficiency of Current Data Collection Methods:** Many high-quality data collection methods, especially teleoperation, are time-consuming and require the robot to be actively controlled in real-time by the human. This process is often slow, requires high concentration, and ties up the robotic hardware. The research is motivated by the need for a more scalable and efficient data collection paradigm that decouples human demonstration from real-time robot operation.

To address these motivations, SEED-UMI proposes a "shared physical measurement" framework. This involves co-designing a kinematically isomorphic exoskeleton that can be worn by both the human demonstrator and the target robot hand. By sharing the same physical device, the system aims to:
- Make joint encoder measurements physically identical across both embodiments, turning discrepancies into natural paired supervisory signals.
- Ensure wrist-mounted cameras observe the same outer mechanism during both human data collection and robot policy rollouts, enabling direct policy training on raw, aligned visual observations.
- Develop a two-stage software adaptation process that uses both robot motor babbling and paired replay under contact to learn a robust encoder-to-command mapping.

This approach is motivated by the hypothesis that rethinking the interface between human and robot embodiments, rather than solely improving retargeting algorithms between disparate systems, can fundamentally resolve the bottlenecks in dexterous manipulation learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SEED-UMI framework is built upon the principle of shared physical measurement, achieved by enabling both the human operator and the robotic hand to wear the same specially designed exoskeleton. This approach integrates hardware design with a two-stage software adaptation process.

#### 3.1. Hardware: A Shared Exoskeleton

The exoskeleton is co-designed with a specific fully-actuated dexterous hand (Wuji hand) to ensure kinematic isomorphism and identical contact geometry across human and robot embodiments.

**Exoskeleton Mechanism Design:**
- **Isomorphic Kinematic Skeleton:** The exoskeleton's link lengths, joint axes, and range-of-motion limits are mechanically replicated from the target robot hand's kinematic tree. This design ensures a one-to-one joint correspondence for all 20 active degrees of freedom (DoF) across the five fingers and guarantees that configurations reachable by the human operator are precisely those reachable by the robot.
- **Parallel Four-Bar Linkage for Encoder Routing:** To maintain an unobstructed palmar contact surface and lateral clearance (crucial for shared contact geometry and full abduction/adduction range), joint rotations are routed through parallel four-bar linkages. These linkages connect the joint axis to dorsally mounted contactless magnetic encoders, preserving a one-to-one angular correspondence. The linkage design minimizes dorsal height and ensures stable encoder alignment.
- **Identical Contact Geometry:** The human-worn and robot-worn exoskeletons share the same external physical structure, including link lengths, joint axes, dorsal linkages, and palmar fingertip pads. Custom cuffs and mounts adapt the skeleton to each embodiment without altering the fundamental joint frames. This ensures that both embodiments present the same external contact geometry to objects, allowing any observed human-robot encoder discrepancies to primarily reflect load-dependent effects such such as contact force, friction, and hysteresis.

**Sensor Integration:**
All sensors are rigidly mounted on the exoskeleton frame, ensuring their physical frame and intrinsic properties remain consistent regardless of whether the human or robot wears the device.
- **Joint Encoders:** Contactless magnetic rotary encoders are integrated at every actuated joint (20 in total) on both the human and robot instances. These encoders provide a shared vector of joint readings, $e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{20}$. Due to the four-bar linkage and manufacturing tolerances, the mapping from exoskeleton encoder readings $e_t$ to robot joint commands $q_t$ is non-linear and is learned data-drivenly.
- **Wrist-Mounted Cameras:** An Intel RealSense T265 camera is dorsally mounted to provide 6-DoF wrist pose, and a ventral fisheye camera (150° FoV) is mounted to observe the workspace. Their rigid attachment to the exoskeleton fixes their intrinsics and extrinsics relative to the encoder frame. This alignment allows policies to be trained directly on raw wrist images ($I_t$) with standard augmentations, eliminating the need for hand segmentation or generative image translation, which could obscure contact-relevant pixels.

#### 3.2. Software Adaptation: Two-Stage Cross-Embodiment Mapping

The shared exoskeleton provides a common encoder signal, but a precise encoder-to-command mapping ($e_t \to q_t$) is still required for robot execution. SEED-UMI develops a two-stage process for this mapping:

1. **Motor Babbling on the Robot (Initial Mapping):**
 - **Procedure:** The robot, wearing the exoskeleton, executes a structured exploration protocol. This includes single-joint sweeps, coupled motions at varying speeds, and some self-contact poses.
 - **Data Collection:** Robot joint positions ($q_t$) and the robot-side exoskeleton encoder readings ($e_t^{(r)}$) are recorded. A history vector ($h_t$) concatenates previous encoder readings, finite-difference velocities, and joint opening/closing indicators.
 - **Model Training:** An initial mapping function, $f_{\theta_0}: (e_t, h_t) \to q_t$, is trained. This free-space model captures basic kinematics and history effects but is acknowledged to drift under contact loads.

2. **Paired Replay and Fine-Tuning (Contact-Aware Refinement):**
 - **Procedure:** The human operator performs contact-rich object manipulation tasks while wearing the exoskeleton, recording human-side encoder trajectories $\{e_t^{(h)}, h_t^{(h)}\}$. Each human demonstration is then replayed on the robot using the initial babbling-only mapping $f_{\theta_0}$. During this robot replay, robot-side data $\{e_t^{(r)}, h_t^{(r)}, q_t^{(r)}\}$ are recorded.
 - **Dataset:** This process generates a paired mapping dataset, $D_{map} = \{(e_t^{(h)}, h_t^{(h)}, q_t^{(r)}, e_t^{(r)}, h_t^{(r)}) \}$.
 - **Mapping Refinement:** The initial mapping $f_{\theta_0}$ is fine-tuned to $f_\theta$ using a loss function $L_{map}$ (Equation 1). This loss is designed to:
 - Match predicted robot-side encoders (derived from $f_\theta$ through a forward model $g_\phi$) to the actual human-side encoder trace ($e_t^{(h)}$).
 - Regularize the predicted robot commands toward the commands recorded during the paired robot replay ($q_t^{(r)}$).
 - Penalize temporal jumps in the mapping to ensure smoothness.
 - **Significance:** This stage leverages the discrepancy between the human's intended motion (captured by $e_t^{(h)}$) and the robot's actual response (captured by $e_t^{(r)}$ when commanded by $f_{\theta_0}$) as a supervisory signal to correct and improve the encoder-to-command mapping under realistic contact conditions, without manual recalibration.

3. **Policy Training and Rollout:**
 - **Learning Frameworks:** Standard visuomotor policy learning frameworks, such as ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), and $\pi_{0.5}$, are employed.
 - **Inputs:** Policies receive raw exoskeleton-centric wrist observations ($I_t$) and the shared encoder vector ($e_t$).
 - **Outputs:** The policies predict an action chunk comprising a 6-DoF wrist action and a 20-DoF hand command.
 - **Training Data:** Policy training relies exclusively on human-collected real data. This pipeline explicitly avoids simulation, reinforcement learning, hand segmentation, or generative inpainting, owing to the inherent alignment provided by the shared exoskeleton.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The SEED-UMI framework was evaluated on a RealMan RX75 robot arm equipped with a Wuji dexterous hand (20 DoF). Both human and robot used the shared exoskeleton. Five real-world, contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks were chosen for evaluation: Screw Driving, AirPods Case Insertion, Ball Basket Throwing, Table Cleaning, and Air Freshener Spray. These tasks were selected to stress various aspects of dexterous manipulation, including tool stabilization, fine finger coordination, dynamic release, and sustained contact.

Three conditions were compared:
- **Teleoperation (T):** Policies trained on demonstrations collected directly by an operator teleoperating the robot in real-time.
- **Ours without paired fine-tuning (P-):** Policies trained on human-side demonstrations, using the initial babbling-only encoder-to-command mapping ($f_{\theta_0}$).
- **Ours with paired fine-tuning (P+):** Policies trained on human-side demonstrations, using the contact-robust mapping ($f_\theta$) refined through the full SEED-UMI pipeline.

Each policy was trained with 100 demonstration episodes per task. Paired fine-tuning utilized an additional 250 calibration episodes across five diverse objects. Evaluation involved 20 autonomous robot rollouts for each task, policy backbone (ACT, DP, $\pi_{0.5}$), and condition, with randomized initial object positions. Success rates were reported.

The key findings are summarized as follows:

**F1: SEED-UMI improves fine and dynamic tasks.**
- Across all tasks and policy backbones, Teleoperation (T) achieved an average success rate of 71.7%. SEED-UMI with paired fine-tuning (P+) achieved 70.0%, demonstrating that it can nearly match the quality of robot-side teleoperation data while decoupling human collection from robot execution. SEED-UMI without paired fine-tuning (P-) achieved a lower average success rate of 57.3%, highlighting the importance of the refinement stage.
- Specifically, P+ outperformed T on tasks requiring fine manipulation and precise timing:
 - **AirPods Case Insertion:** P+ achieved 73.3% success compared to T's 65.0%.
 - **Ball Basket Throwing:** P+ achieved 68.3% success compared to T's 60.0%.
- The authors suggest that these improvements are due to the natural, direct feedback experienced by the human operator during SEED-UMI data collection, which allows for more delicate contact regulation and precise timing that is challenging to achieve through a delayed real-time teleoperation loop.

**F2: Paired fine-tuning matters most under contact load.**
- Comparing P- to P+, the paired fine-tuning stage yielded an average improvement of +12.7 percentage points in success rate.
- The benefits were particularly pronounced in tasks dominated by contact loads, where the babbling-only mapping ($f_{\theta_0}$) would typically degrade:
 - **Screw Driving:** Improved by +16.7 percentage points.
 - **Table Cleaning:** Improved by +15.0 percentage points.
 - **Air Freshener Spray:** Improved by +15.0 percentage points.
- This result supports the hypothesis that the paired-replay loss (Equation 1) effectively corrects the mapping's drift that occurs due to finger compliance and external loads during contact interactions.

**F3: SEED-UMI is significantly more efficient than teleoperation.**
- A direct comparison of data collection throughput on the AirPods Case Insertion task within a 30-minute window showed that the same operator collected 52 successful demonstrations using SEED-UMI, compared to 18 using teleoperation. This represents an approximately 3.0x gain in throughput.
- Given that the overall rollout success rates for P+ and Teleoperation were comparable (70.0% vs. 71.7%), the success-normalized gain in useful training data per minute remained approximately 2.9x.
- This finding indicates that SEED-UMI provides a practical advantage by decoupling human demonstration from real-time robot execution, allowing for faster data acquisition without a significant compromise in the quality of the learned policies.

A qualitative study of the AirPods task (Figure 6) further illustrated that SEED-UMI collection enables operators to regulate delicate contacts more effectively through immediate feedback, whereas teleoperation can lead to over-driving motions and excessive force due to the separation of the operator from direct object contact.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SEED-UMI framework presents several significant contributions to the field of robot learning for dexterous manipulation, with potential broad impacts:

1. **Addressing a Core Bottleneck in Dexterous Manipulation:** The research directly tackles the long-standing challenge of collecting high-quality, contact-rich demonstrations and ensuring their faithful transfer to robotic hands. This bottleneck has significantly limited the scalability and effectiveness of imitation learning for complex dexterous tasks.

2. **Novel Paradigm of Shared Physical Measurement:** SEED-UMI introduces a new conceptual approach by designing a human-robot interface where both human and robot share the *same* physical exoskeleton mechanism. This fundamentally shifts the problem from bridging discrepancies between two separate embodiments to leveraging a unified physical and sensory measurement system. This paradigm could inspire future designs for human-robot collaboration and data collection systems.

3. **Enhanced Data Collection Efficiency and Scalability:** By decoupling human demonstration collection from real-time robot execution, SEED-UMI achieves a 3.0x increase in data collection throughput compared to traditional teleoperation. This efficiency is critical for scaling up robot learning, as large, diverse datasets are often necessary for robust policy training. The ability to collect data faster and with less reliance on the robot's real-time availability has the potential to accelerate research and development in this area.

4. **Improved Performance in Complex, Contact-Rich Tasks:** The framework demonstrates superior performance on precise and dynamic contact-rich tasks (e.g., AirPods insertion, ball throwing) compared to teleoperation. This is attributed to the more natural and direct feedback experienced by the human operator during data collection, which enables the generation of higher-quality demonstrations for such challenging interactions.

5. **Simplified Visuomotor Policy Learning:** By design, the shared exoskeleton ensures visual alignment between human demonstration and robot execution. This eliminates the need for complex and resource-intensive visual processing techniques like hand segmentation or generative image translation, allowing policies to be trained directly on raw, aligned wrist camera observations. This simplification can make policy development more straightforward and robust.

6. **Robust Retargeting under Contact:** The two-stage software adaptation, particularly the paired replay and fine-tuning, effectively transforms an initial free-space mapping into a contact-robust encoder-to-command function. This capability is essential for deploying dexterous manipulation policies in real-world scenarios where contact forces and compliance are inevitable.

7. **Future Directions for Robot Learning:** SEED-UMI lays groundwork for future advancements, including the automation of cross-hand design and mapping, improvements in wearability for extended use, and the integration of tactile sensing for even finer contact estimation. These directions could lead to more generalized, user-friendly, and capable dexterous robot manipulation systems.

In summary, SEED-UMI offers a practical and effective solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics. By fundamentally rethinking the human-robot interface, it provides a more scalable, efficient, and robust method for collecting and leveraging dexterous manipulation demonstrations, thereby holding the potential to significantly advance the capabilities of robotic hands in real-world applications.
