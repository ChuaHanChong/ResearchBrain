# Motion Blender Gaussian Splatting for Dynamic Scene Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> Motion-Blender Gaussian Splatting (MBGS) is a framework that reconstructs dynamic 3D scenes by combining 3D Gaussians with explicit motion graphs, enabling direct manipulation of scene dynamics. The approach achieves an LPIPS score of 0.37 on the iPhone dataset, outperforming Shape-of-Motion, and supports novel pose animation, robot demonstration synthesis, and visual planning at rendering speeds up to 46 FPS.

## Problem

- Existing dynamic 3D Gaussian Splatting methods use implicit and dense motion representations, hindering direct manipulation or alteration of reconstructed scene dynamics.
- The incompatibility of implicit motion representations with traditional robot planning pipelines limits their utility for tasks requiring explicit kinematic models.
- The lack of explicit controllability confines dynamic 3DGS applications to passive replay, restricting interactive scenarios, especially in robotics.

## Method

- **MBGS** decouples dense visual representation (==3D Gaussians==) from sparse, explicit motion representations (==motion graphs==), allowing for direct control.
- It employs parametric motion graphs, including ==kinematic trees== for articulated objects and ==deformable graphs== for non-rigid objects, whose motions are propagated to Gaussians via a learnable ==weight painting function==.
- The entire framework, including Gaussians, motion graph parameters, and weight painting parameters, is optimized end-to-end from input videos using ==differentiable rendering== and various loss functions.

## Results

- **MBGS** achieved an LPIPS score of **0.37** on the iPhone dataset, surpassing Shape-of-Motion, and competitive LPIPS scores of **0.35** on the HyperNeRF dataset, demonstrating state-of-the-art or comparable rendering quality.
- The explicit motion graph representation enables novel pose animation by direct manipulation and facilitates robot demonstration synthesis from human videos for imitation learning.
- A visual planning prototype was demonstrated for goal-conditioned manipulation by rapidly reconstructing objects and simulating motion graph trajectories, achieving rendering speeds of up to **46 FPS**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling object motion with sparse motion graphs offers enhanced controllability and manipulability compared to dense or implicit neural fields.
> - A learnable motion blending mechanism effectively transfers sparse graph motions to dense Gaussian representations while maintaining high-fidelity rendering.
> - Instance-level reconstruction, augmented with 2D track loss and regularization for canonical frame and 2D keypoints, improves the robustness and accuracy of dynamic scene reconstructions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Dynamic 3D Scene Reconstruction

The field of dynamic 3D scene reconstruction has seen rapid advancements, driven by the need to create digital models of the real world that can evolve over time. This capability is crucial for applications ranging from augmented reality and virtual production to autonomous navigation and robotic manipulation. Early methods relied on explicit geometric representations like Truncated Signed Distance Functions (TSDF) or surfels, often combined with deformation graphs to model motion.

More recently, neural radiance fields (NeRFs) and 3D Gaussian Splatting have emerged as dominant paradigms. NeRFs, with their implicit neural representations, excel at photorealistic static scene rendering and have been extended to dynamic scenes (e.g., HyperNeRF, D-NeRF, NeRFies). However, their slow training and rendering times can be a bottleneck for real-time applications.

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has rapidly gained prominence due to its ability to achieve comparable or even superior visual quality to NeRFs while offering significantly faster training and real-time rendering capabilities. Dynamic 3DGS methods (e.g., 4D-Gaussians, Deformable-GS, Shape-of-Motion, STG, GaussianFlow) extend the static representation by introducing time-dependent parameters for Gaussians, often implicitly encoding motion either through neural networks (deformation fields) or dense per-Gaussian motion parameters (e.g., motion coefficients or polynomials). These methods have pushed the boundaries of visual fidelity and rendering speed for dynamic scenes.

#### 1.2. The Gap Addressed by MBGS

Despite the impressive progress in high-fidelity reconstruction, a critical limitation of most existing dynamic 3DGS methods, particularly for robotics, lies in their **implicit and dense motion representations**. When motion is implicitly encoded in a neural network or spread across hundreds of thousands of individual Gaussian parameters, it becomes extremely difficult to:
1. **Manipulate the reconstructed motions directly:** Users cannot easily edit or alter the scene's dynamics in a controllable manner. This restricts applications to merely replaying recorded motions.
2. **Integrate with robot planning:** Robot systems often rely on explicit kinematic models or deformable meshes for planning and control. Implicit motion representations are not readily compatible with these traditional robotics pipelines, making it challenging to use reconstructed scenes for tasks like inverse kinematics, motion planning, or physics-based simulation.

This lack of explicit controllability and manipulability severely hinders the wider application of dynamic 3DGS in interactive scenarios, especially in robotics where precise control and prediction of object behavior are paramount. The paper highlights this gap, posing the key question: "can we develop an explicit and sparse motion representation without compromising the ability to reconstruct complex dynamic scenes?"

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop a novel framework for dynamic scene reconstruction using an explicit and sparse motion representation**, specifically motion graphs, within the Gaussian splatting paradigm. The overarching goal is to enable direct and intuitive manipulation of reconstructed dynamic scenes, thereby extending their utility beyond mere replay to practical applications in robotics and animation.

The motivation for this work stems directly from the limitations of existing dynamic Gaussian splatting methods:
- **Lack of Controllability:** Current methods primarily offer implicit motion encoding, which, while capable of high-fidelity reconstruction, restricts users to replaying recorded motions without the ability to easily alter or animate new ones. This is a significant drawback for interactive applications.
- **Hindrance to Robotics Applications:** The inability to explicitly control and manipulate motion in a reconstructed scene prevents its effective integration into robot manipulation planning, simulation, and data synthesis pipelines, which often require explicit kinematic or deformation models.
- **Desire for Interpretability:** Implicit representations can be black boxes. An explicit, sparse representation offers greater interpretability, allowing researchers and users to understand and visualize the underlying motion dynamics.
- **Inspiration from Classical Animation:** The authors draw inspiration from classical animation techniques that use explicit models like deformation graphs and kinematic trees for animating objects, aiming to adapt these concepts for modern differentiable rendering pipelines.

By addressing these points, the authors aim to unlock new possibilities for dynamic scene reconstruction, particularly in enabling robots to interact with and learn from reconstructed real-world environments more effectively.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Motion-Blender Gaussian Splatting (MBGS)**, introduces a novel framework that bridges the gap between high-fidelity Gaussian splatting and explicit, manipulable motion representations.

#### 3.1. Core Concept: Motion Blender Gaussian Splatting (MBGS)

MBGS's core idea is to decouple the dense visual representation (3D Gaussians) from a sparse, explicit motion representation (motion graphs). Instead of each Gaussian having its own implicit motion parameters, the motion of a small number of graph links is propagated to the much larger set of individual Gaussians through a **motion blending** mechanism. This ensures both high-fidelity rendering and explicit control.

The motion of a Gaussian $p_t$ at time $t$ is defined as $p_t = B(R(P_{L,0}, P_{L,t}), W(x_0, X_{L,0})) \cdot p_0$, where $p_0$ is the Gaussian's initial pose, $P_{L,t}$ are the poses of all links in the motion graph at time $t$, $R$ computes relative rigid transforms of links, $W$ is the weight painting function, and $B$ is the motion blending function.

#### 3.2. Motion Graph Representation

MBGS employs two types of parametric motion graphs to accommodate different object types:

##### 3.2.1. Kinematic Tree

- **Description:** A hierarchical, acyclic graph with a root joint, ideal for articulated objects like human bodies or robots.
- **Parameterization:** Defined by time-independent link lengths ($\ell_i$) and time-varying joint rotations ($r_t \in SO(3)$) and a root joint pose ($X \in SE(3)$).
- **Motion Prediction:** Link poses in world coordinates are computed via a differentiable forward kinematics algorithm, propagating transformations from the root.

##### 3.2.2. Deformable Graph

- **Description:** A free-form graph without topological constraints, allowing joints to move freely in 3D space, suitable for non-rigid objects like cloth or soft bodies.
- **Parameterization:** Defined by time-varying 3D positions of joints ($n_{j,t} \in R^3$).
- **Motion Prediction:** To handle non-rigid link deformations, the method tracks projection points. For each Gaussian $x_0$ and link $l$ at $t=0$, a projection point $n_{x_0,l}$ on the link is found. At time $t$, this point $n_{x_t,l}$ moves proportionally to the stretching of link $l$. Link poses are then derived from these projection points using a differentiable look-at transformation, incorporating the link's direction and an inferred "up-direction" from adjacent triangular faces.

##### 3.2.3. Graph Connectivity Initialization

- **Kinematic Trees:** Connectivity (links between joints) is derived from domain-specific priors (e.g., lifting 2D human skeletons using SAPIENS or extracting from robot models).
- **Deformable Graphs:** Connectivity is initialized by sampling points from an instance point cloud (using farthest point sampling for uniform coverage) and connecting adjacent points. The connectivity is fixed during optimization.

#### 3.3. Weight Painting Function

The influence of each graph link on a Gaussian's motion is determined by a learnable **weight painting function** $W$. This function computes a `softmax` over Gaussian kernel affinities:
$W(x_0, X_{L,0}) = \text{softmax}(\{K(x_0, X_{L,0,i}) \mid \forall i \in [1, |L|]\})$, where $K(x_0, X_{L,0,i}) = \exp(-\gamma \cdot \text{dist}(x_0, X_{L,0,i}))$.
Here, $x_0$ is the Gaussian's initial position, $X_{L,0,i}$ are the initial start/end joint positions of link $i$, `dist` measures the distance from point to line segment, and $\gamma$ is a **learnable parameter** for each link that controls the kernel radius. This allows the influence regions to collectively span the object's surface. The weights are computed at the canonical frame ($t=0$) and then used to propagate motion for all subsequent frames.

#### 3.4. Optimization Details

The entire framework, including the 3D Gaussians, motion graph parameters ($\theta_t$, $\phi$), and weight painting parameters ($\gamma$), is optimized **end-to-end jointly from input videos** via differentiable rendering.
- **Canonical Frame:** A frame with the largest object area or most valid 2D tracks is selected as the canonical frame ($t=0$).
- **Initialization:** 3D Gaussians are initialized as in prior work. Motion graphs are initialized at the canonical frame using instance segmentation masks (Grounding SAM2) and 2D human skeletons (SAPIENS), lifted to 3D using depth.
- **Loss Functions:** The primary loss is L1 RGB loss against video frames, augmented with a 2D track loss for improved motion guidance.
- **Instance-Level Reconstruction:** A binary matrix groups Gaussians by instance, and rendering loss is applied to instance masks (from Grounding SAM2) to ensure accurate instance geometry. This is a novel contribution in dynamic Gaussian splatting.
- **Regularization:**
 - **Canonical Frame Regularization:** Minimizes the distance between learned motion graph joint positions and their initial positions at $t=0$, preventing graph drift.
 - **2D Keypoints Regularization:** For kinematic trees (e.g., human bodies), the projected 3D joints are regularized against 2D human keypoints from SAPIENS.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

MBGS demonstrates strong performance in dynamic scene reconstruction and offers significant advantages in controllability, particularly for robotics applications.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Performance

- **iPhone Dataset:** MBGS achieved state-of-the-art performance, outperforming Shape-of-Motion with an LPIPS score of 0.37 (lower is better), compared to Shape-of-Motion's 0.39. It also achieved competitive PSNR and SSIM scores (16.79 and 0.65, respectively).
- **HyperNeRF Dataset:** MBGS performed competitively, closely matching the state-of-the-art 4DGaussians in the key LPIPS metric (0.35 vs. 0.36), although 4DGaussians showed higher PSNR and SSIM overall.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Performance and Efficiency

- **Visual Quality:** Qualitative comparisons on the iPhone dataset showed MBGS rendering sharper, more complete, and perceptually higher-quality novel views compared to 4DGaussians and Shape-of-Motion. On HyperNeRF, MBGS produced clearer results for scenes like the 3D printer (accurately rendering text) and the chicken, though it struggled with the "peel-banana" scene (attributed to strong camera and object motions).
- **Rendering Speed:** MBGS achieved efficient rendering speeds: 18 FPS (25 FPS with caching) for complex scenes with 2 million Gaussians (e.g., teddy bear), and 32 FPS (46 FPS with caching) for smaller scenes with 300K Gaussians (e.g., chicken toy). Training typically converges within 10-30 hours on an A100 GPU.

#### 4.3. Demonstrated Applications

The explicit motion graph representation enables several impactful applications:

##### 4.3.1. Novel Pose Animation

MBGS allows users to manipulate the learned motion graphs directly (e.g., adjusting joint angles in a kinematic tree or joint positions in a deformable graph) to generate and render objects in **novel, unseen poses**. This capability operates directly in 3D space, offering fast and precise control without relying on large diffusion models, thus creating "imagined" object motions while maintaining high rendering quality.

##### 4.3.2. Robot Demonstration Synthesis

The framework was prototyped to **synthesize robot demonstrations from human videos**. By reconstructing scenes from human and robot videos independently, extracting human motion graphs, mapping human hand poses to robot gripper poses via inverse kinematics, and then using the robot's kinematic chain as the motion graph, MBGS can generate videos where a robot arm mimics human actions. This could significantly reduce the labor-intensive process of collecting robot demonstration data for imitation learning.

##### 4.3.3. Robot Visual Planning

MBGS enables a simple **visual planning prototype for goal-conditioned manipulation**. For deformable (cloth, rope) and articulated (microwave door) objects, the method reconstructs object-specific Gaussians and their motion graphs on the fly (within a minute). It then simulates various graph trajectories, renders the resulting object images, and selects the optimal trajectory by maximizing PSNR with a provided target goal image. This demonstrates potential for data-efficient, model-based robot learning solutions, where the reward function is simply a goal image.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (from Appendix)

- **Motion Graph Size:** Experiments revealed an optimal motion graph size for rendering quality (e.g., 200 joints for a teddy bear scene), with performance degrading if too many joints are used, suggesting the potential for adaptive graph structures.
- **2D Keypoints Regularization:** Enforcing consistency between projected 3D kinematic tree joints and 2D human keypoints improved LPIPS by 0.01-0.02 and resulted in cleaner, more meaningful motion graphs that better aligned with human 3D structure.
- **Canonical Frame Regularization:** This regularization was crucial for preventing the motion graph from drifting away from the object surface during optimization, ensuring accurate alignment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Key Contributions and Advantages

The "Motion-Blender Gaussian Splatting" paper makes several significant contributions to dynamic 3D scene reconstruction and its applications:
- **Explicit and Sparse Motion Representation:** This is the most critical innovation. By introducing motion graphs (kinematic trees and deformable graphs) as an explicit and sparse representation, MBGS overcomes the limitations of implicit and dense motion models, offering a new paradigm for dynamic Gaussian splatting.
- **Enhanced Controllability and Manipulability:** The explicit nature of motion graphs allows for direct manipulation of reconstructed scenes, moving beyond passive replay to active animation and editing of object poses. This significantly broadens the utility of dynamic reconstructions.
- **Bridging High-Fidelity Reconstruction with Actionable Models:** MBGS effectively combines the photorealistic rendering capabilities and speed of Gaussian splatting with the structural interpretability and manipulability of traditional animation models, a crucial step for real-world interactive applications.
- **Per-Instance Reconstruction:** The work introduces instance-level reconstruction in dynamic Gaussian splatting, maintaining accurate geometry for individual objects within a dynamic scene, which is valuable for targeted manipulation tasks.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** MBGS achieves competitive or superior rendering quality on challenging benchmarks like the iPhone and HyperNeRF datasets, demonstrating that explicit motion representation does not compromise reconstruction fidelity.

#### 5.2. Broader Impact, Especially in Robotics

The potential impact of MBGS is particularly profound in robotics:
- **Robot Manipulation Planning:** The ability to represent and manipulate object motions explicitly makes it much easier to integrate reconstructed scenes into robot planning frameworks, allowing robots to plan interactions with dynamic objects in digital twins of real environments.
- **Accelerated Robot Learning:** By enabling the synthesis of robot demonstrations from human videos and facilitating model-based visual planning, MBGS can significantly reduce the need for labor-intensive real-world robot data collection, thereby accelerating the development of more capable robot learning policies.
- **Digital Twin Creation:** The framework lays groundwork for creating more interactive and controllable digital twins of dynamic real-world environments, where objects are not just passively observed but can be actively manipulated.
- **Interactive Simulation:** The explicit motion graphs are a natural fit for physics-based simulations, potentially enabling the generation of physically plausible behaviors and interactions within reconstructed scenes.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The paper is transparent about its limitations, which also suggest fertile ground for future research:
- **Visual Artifacts on Novel Poses:** Gaussian splatting's lack of explicit surface representation can lead to artifacts when extreme novel motions are applied, as Gaussians may deviate from object surfaces. Future work could explore methods like 2D-GS that formulate Gaussians directly on 3D surfaces.
- **Ungrounded Motion Graphs:** The learned motion graphs may not always align perfectly with object geometry, especially for intricate structures, small objects, or when lacking strong structural priors. Incorporating semantic or physical priors from foundation models could improve geometric consistency and robustness.
- **Fast Camera and Object Motions:** The method struggles with scenes involving rapid camera or object movements, which can lead to inconsistent reconstructions. Leveraging temporal continuity more effectively, perhaps through neural networks that predict graph deformations over time, could be a solution.
- **Integration with Physics Simulation:** The current motion graphs lack physical awareness. Future work could integrate the sparse motion graphs with differentiable physics engines (e.g., MuJoCo) to learn physical parameters and enable more realistic interaction and planning for deformable and articulated objects.
- **Modeling Light Sources and Reflective Surfaces:** Current Gaussian splatting methods, including MBGS, struggle with reflective surfaces and varying lighting conditions, leading to blurry reconstructions. Reflection-aware Gaussian splatting techniques are needed for robust robotic applications in diverse lighting environments.

In conclusion, MBGS presents a significant step forward in making dynamic 3D scene reconstructions more controllable and usable for practical applications, especially in the burgeoning field of robot learning and manipulation. Its explicit motion representation paradigm offers a powerful new tool for generating and interacting with dynamic digital twins of the real world.
