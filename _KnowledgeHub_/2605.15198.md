# ATLAS: Agentic or Latent Visual Reasoning? One Word is Enough for Both

> [!summary] Summary
> ATLAS is a visual reasoning framework that integrates discrete 'functional tokens' into autoregressive Vision-Language Models, enabling them to represent and execute visual operations within a standard generation loop. This method allows models to perform complex interleaved visual reasoning more efficiently and interpretably than prior approaches, leading to improved performance on benchmarks like WeMath and BLINK.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) face challenges in performing multi-step, interleaved visual reasoning that necessitates dynamic manipulation of visual inputs.
- Current methods for interleaved visual reasoning are often computationally expensive (e.g., generating pixel-level images), introduce high latency due to verbose operations (e.g., tool calls), or lack compatibility with scalable training architectures.
- Reinforcement learning for visual reasoning in VLMs suffers from 'gradient dilution,' where sparse but critical functional tokens receive insufficient optimization.

## Method

- The **ATLAS** framework extends autoregressive Vision-Language Models by incorporating ==discrete functional tokens== (e.g., `<|Shape|>`, `<|Line|>`) directly into the vocabulary, allowing visual operations to be represented within the standard sequence generation.
- A two-stage training process combines ==Supervised Fine-Tuning== on the ==ATLAS-178K dataset== (curated from visual-code data and LLM-polished reasoning trajectories) with ==Reinforcement Learning== using a composite reward function.
- ==Latent-Anchored GRPO (LA-GRPO)== is introduced, which adds a ==token-level auxiliary loss== specifically for functional tokens during RL, mitigating ==gradient dilution== and stabilizing their optimization.

## Results

- ATLAS-LA-GRPO achieved an average score of **51.3%** on the BLINK benchmark and **45.0%** on WeMath, demonstrating substantial improvements over the baseline VLM and competitive performance against other state-of-the-art models.
- The framework significantly improved efficiency, reducing inference latency by approximately **80%** (from **18.83s to 3.80s**) and peak memory usage by **44%** (from **2.55GB to 1.43GB**) on the BLINK-Jigsaw benchmark compared to agentic methods.
- Qualitative analysis confirmed that ATLAS intelligently invokes functional tokens at relevant reasoning steps, with visual attention patterns aligning with the intended visual operations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex visual manipulation and reasoning steps can be compactly represented by a small set of discrete 'functional tokens' within the language model's vocabulary, avoiding computationally expensive image generation or verbose external tool calls.
> - Integrating functional tokens directly into the autoregressive generation loop allows for seamless compatibility with standard, scalable VLM training and inference pipelines without requiring architectural changes or recurrent dependencies.
> - A specialized reinforcement learning approach, Latent-Anchored GRPO, is essential for providing stable and sufficient learning signals to sparse functional tokens, overcoming the 'gradient dilution' issue and enhancing their effective invocation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has evolved to address tasks requiring complex visual reasoning beyond basic perception. Such reasoning often necessitates the generation, perception, and utilization of intermediate visual states to guide subsequent inference. This capability is referred to as interleaved visual reasoning.

Current VLMs demonstrate limitations in handling dynamic visual reasoning processes. Existing approaches to interleaved visual reasoning typically fall into three categories, each with associated challenges:

1. **Unified Models:** These models aim to directly generate pixel-level images as intermediate visual representations. While intuitive, this approach is computationally intensive due to the cost of image decoding and re-encoding. It also presents significant training difficulties and often requires specialized architectural designs and extensive pre-training.

2. **Agentic Visual Reasoning:** This paradigm positions the VLM as a high-level controller that generates code or calls external tools to manipulate visual inputs. This approach offers lower computational overhead compared to generating full images. However, it can necessitate verbose code or tool-call formulations even for simple operations, leading to increased output length, inference latency, and context-switching overhead from external execution.

3. **Latent Reasoning:** This method performs intermediate reasoning within hidden representations rather than generating explicit images or lengthy operational descriptions. Latent methods aim for efficiency by avoiding pixel-level generation. Nevertheless, they often rely on supervision signals derived from a restricted set of tasks, which may limit their generalization across broader domains. Furthermore, some latent methods introduce recurrent dependencies, disrupting compatibility with standard parallel training techniques and consequently increasing training costs.

The ATLAS framework positions itself within this landscape by seeking to integrate attributes of both agentic and latent reasoning approaches. It aims to achieve the explicitness and interpretability often associated with agentic methods, while maintaining the efficiency and compatibility with scalable training pipelines typically sought by latent methods, thereby addressing the limitations observed in the three established paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel visual reasoning framework, termed ATLAS, that bridges the gap between agentic and latent visual reasoning paradigms. The motivation stems from the desire to combine the strengths of these existing approaches while mitigating their respective limitations.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Compact Representation of Visual Operations:** The authors observe that both unified and agentic methods suffer from verbosity. Unified models generate entire pixel-level images, incurring high computational costs. Agentic methods, while avoiding image generation, often require verbose code or tool-call sequences to describe visual operations, increasing output length and inference latency. ATLAS aims to represent visual operations using a single, discrete "functional token" within the standard vocabulary, thereby providing a compact and efficient interface for visual manipulation.

- **Compatibility with Scalable Training Architectures:** Traditional latent reasoning methods, while efficient in principle, sometimes introduce recurrent dependencies or non-standard computation patterns. These architectural modifications can break compatibility with highly optimized autoregressive parallel training and inference pipelines. A core motivation for ATLAS is to ensure that visual reasoning operations remain strictly within the standard autoregressive generation loop. This design goal allows the framework to leverage existing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks without architectural or methodological modifications, promoting scalability and efficient training.

- **Interpretability without External Execution:** Agentic methods offer a degree of interpretability by explicitly outlining the steps of visual manipulation (e.g., "draw a line," "zoom in"). Latent methods, conversely, operate in hidden spaces, potentially reducing interpretability. ATLAS seeks to retain the explicitness and interpretability of agentic reasoning by using distinct, semantically meaningful functional tokens, while avoiding the latency and overhead associated with external tool execution.

- **Avoidance of Visual Supervision for Operations:** Many latent visual reasoning approaches require auxiliary supervision, reconstruction, or distillation targets for their latent states. This can restrict flexibility and generalization. ATLAS is motivated to demonstrate that functional tokens can be optimized with standard cross-entropy loss over token sequences, learning when and how to invoke visual operations effectively from the reasoning context itself, without explicit image-level supervision.

- **Addressing Gradient Dilution in Reinforcement Learning:** During the reinforcement learning (RL) phase, the authors identified a "gradient dilution" issue where sparse functional tokens, critical for visual reasoning, are overshadowed by the significantly more numerous ordinary text tokens. This imbalance can lead to insufficient optimization of functional tokens. A specific objective is to develop a mechanism, Latent-Anchored GRPO (LA-GRPO), to provide a stronger and more persistent learning signal for functional tokens, thereby stabilizing their invocation and improving reasoning performance.

In summary, the motivation behind ATLAS is to create a visual reasoning framework that is efficient, scalable, interpretable, and effective, by carefully integrating and refining concepts from existing paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ATLAS framework conceptualizes visual operations as discrete functional tokens embedded within a standard autoregressive sequence. The methodology comprises the model architecture, a two-stage training recipe, and a specialized reinforcement learning objective.

#### 3.1. Model Architecture

ATLAS builds upon existing autoregressive VLM architectures, specifically instantiating with Qwen2.5-VL-7B. The core design involves expanding the tokenizer vocabulary to include a compact set of five functional tokens:
- `<|Manip|>`: General image enhancement (denoising, sharpening, filtering, cropping, zoom-in).
- `<|Shape|>`: Marking regions for grounding, highlighting, or area reasoning.
- `<|Line|>`: Adding lines for geometric construction, separation, or structural cues.
- `<|Arrow|>`: Indicating direction, motion, force, flow, or highlighting elements.
- `<|Text|>`: Adding symbolic labels, numerical values, or textual notes.

These tokens are treated as ordinary tokens within the vocabulary ($V = V_{text} \cup V_{spec} \cup V_{func}$) and are generated via standard next-token prediction. When a functional token is predicted, it serves as an internal reasoning unit specifying the type of visual operation needed. This unified sequence modeling approach avoids external tool execution, pixel-level intermediate supervision, and recurrent latent dependencies, thereby maintaining compatibility with standard VLM pipelines and enabling efficient parallel training. The functional tokens do not require image-level supervision; instead, they are optimized with the same cross-entropy (CE) objective as natural language tokens, learning their usage from the surrounding reasoning context.

#### 3.2. Two-stage Training Recipe

ATLAS employs a two-stage training process:

**Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with ATLAS-178K**
To provide an initial capability for invoking functional tokens, a new dataset, ATLAS-178K, was curated.
1. **Source Data and Token Extraction:** The dataset originates from the V-Interaction-400K dataset, which provides image-construction code paired with visual reasoning problems. Visual operations within this code (e.g., line drawing, text annotation, cropping) are parsed and mapped to the five functional tokens. This process yielded 138K high-quality examples.
2. **Trajectory Construction and Polishing:** The mapped operations are converted into reasoning trajectories, where functional tokens are explicitly inserted within a predefined transition template. These templated trajectories are then polished using Gemini-2.5-Pro to generate more natural reasoning text while preserving the original semantics and functional token order.
3. **Perception Preservation:** To prevent catastrophic forgetting of low-level perceptual abilities, V-Perception-40K is included in the SFT stage. This data does not contain functional tokens but provides supervision for fine-grained visual understanding.
The model is then trained using the vanilla CE loss across all tokens, enabling it to learn valid functional token invocation based on context.

**Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning (RL) with GRPO**
Following SFT, reinforcement learning is applied to further enhance multi-step visual reasoning by optimizing for outcome-driven performance. Standard Group-based Reinforcement Policy Optimization (GRPO) is adopted without custom modifications.
1. **Composite Reward Function:** A comprehensive reward function `r(o)` is defined for each generated output `o`:
 - `r_acc`: Binary reward (1 or 0) for answer correctness, assessed via exact string matching and mathematical equivalence.
 - `r_func`: Conditional reward (1 or 0) for meaningful functional token usage, granted only if at least one functional token is invoked *and* the final answer is correct. This prevents arbitrary token generation.
 - `r_fmt`: Binary reward (1 or 0) for adherence to required output formats.
 - `p_len`: Linear penalty for responses exceeding a predefined maximum length `L_max`.
 - `p_spam`: Bounded linear penalty for excessive invocation of functional tokens (exceeding a threshold `τ_spam`), designed to prevent "reward hacking."
 The total reward is `r(o) = λ_acc * r_acc + λ_func * r_func + λ_fmt * r_fmt - λ_len * p_len - λ_spam * p_spam`, where `λ` are weighting coefficients.
2. **GRPO Objective:** The policy `π_θ` is optimized using the GRPO objective, which considers the relative advantage `A_i` of sampled outputs `o_i` within a group `G`.

#### 3.3. Latent-Anchored GRPO (LA-GRPO)

To address the "gradient dilution" issue, where sparse functional tokens receive insufficient updates during standard GRPO, the authors propose Latent-Anchored GRPO (LA-GRPO).
The problem arises because GRPO assigns a sequence-level advantage to all tokens, and functional tokens constitute a small fraction (e.g., 2.3%) of a typical response. LA-GRPO augments the standard GRPO objective with a token-level auxiliary loss.
For each rollout `o_i`, the positions `M_func(o_i)` where functional tokens appear are identified. An additional clipped surrogate loss `L_token(t)` is applied *only* to these functional token positions, reusing the group advantage `A_i` but concentrating the update.
The final LA-GRPO objective is:
`L_LA-GRPO = L_GRPO + α * (1/|M_func|) * Σ(t∈M_func) L_token(t)`
Here, `α` controls the anchor strength, and normalization stabilizes the auxiliary loss scale. This design provides a stronger and more persistent learning signal for functional tokens, aiming to stabilize their invocation and improve visual reasoning without altering the autoregressive training pipeline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ATLAS was conducted across multiple benchmarks, focusing on quantitative performance, qualitative analysis of reasoning, efficiency, and ablation studies of reward components.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Performance

The performance comparison (Table 3) across V*, WeMath, and BLINK benchmarks indicates that ATLAS consistently improves upon the base VLM, Qwen2.5-VL.
- **Overall Improvement:** ATLAS demonstrates clear performance gains. For instance, on the BLINK benchmark, Qwen2.5-VL achieved an average accuracy of 22.8%, whereas ATLAS$_{LA-GRPO}$ reached 51.3%. This suggests that the integration of discrete functional tokens enhances structured visual reasoning.
- **Impact of SFT:** The ATLAS$_{SFT}$ variant, trained solely with supervised fine-tuning, already improved the BLINK average to 46.0%, indicating that the curated ATLAS-178K dataset effectively teaches the model to utilize functional tokens.
- **Impact of GRPO:** ATLAS$_{GRPO}$ further improved the overall performance, reaching 50.5% on BLINK and 40.3% on WeMath. This variant showed significant gains on subsets like Jigsaw and Spatial Relation within BLINK. However, improvements were not uniform, with some subsets like IQ and multi-view reasoning experiencing a slight decrease compared to ATLAS$_{SFT}$, suggesting potential instability in functional token behavior with sequence-level preference optimization alone.
- **Impact of LA-GRPO:** ATLAS$_{LA-GRPO}$ achieved the highest and most balanced performance among the proposed variants on WeMath (45.0%) and the BLINK average (51.3%). It also improved over standard GRPO on several subsets, including Art Style, Counting, Forensic Detection, and Multi-view Reasoning (e.g., multi-view reasoning increased from 43.6% to 53.4%). This indicates that anchoring optimization on functional tokens stabilizes training and enhances the overall effectiveness of ATLAS on complex visual reasoning tasks.
- **Comparison with Other Models:** ATLAS$_{LA-GRPO}$ achieved competitive results against various closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini-2.5-Pro) and existing open-source standard VLMs, unified models, agentic visual models, and latent visual models.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative examples (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7) illustrate the model's reasoning trajectories.
- **Meaningful Token Invocation:** ATLAS invokes functional tokens at appropriate steps in the reasoning process. For instance, `<|Shape|>` is used for localizing relevant regions, `<|Arrow|>` for guiding attention to specific evidence, and `<|Text|>` for supporting counting or labeling tasks. These examples suggest that functional tokens are integrated into the reasoning flow rather than being used as isolated markers.
- **Attention Scores:** Image attention maps generated around functional tokens (Figures 6, 7) show that these tokens tend to focus on task-relevant visual regions. For example, `<|Line|>` focuses on geometric segments in math problems, and `<|Shape|>` highlights target objects for spatial comparisons. This indicates a learned association between functional tokens and meaningful visual evidence.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Analysis

Efficiency comparisons (Table 4) between ATLAS and V-Thinker (an agentic method) on BLINK-Jigsaw demonstrated performance benefits.
- **Reduced Overhead:** ATLAS significantly reduced output length and the overhead associated with formulating operations. This is attributed to representing visual operations with single functional tokens instead of verbose textual descriptions or tool-call sequences.
- **Improved Speed and Memory:** ATLAS reduced average inference latency from 18.83s to 3.80s and lowered peak memory usage from 2.55GB to 1.43GB, while maintaining or improving accuracy. These findings indicate that the compact functional token representation can reduce inference cost while preserving effective visual reasoning.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study of Negative Reward Penalties

An ablation study on the negative reward penalties (Table 5) in LA-GRPO on BLINK revealed their importance for stable functional token alignment.
- **Necessity of Penalties:** Removing any of the format reward (`r_fmt`), length penalty (`p_len`), or token spam penalty (`p_spam`) led to a performance drop.
- **Token Spam Penalty Impact:** Removing the token spam penalty caused the largest degradation (from 51.3% to 47.0%). In this condition, the model exhibited reward hacking by generating an excessive number of functional tokens (up to 18.7 per sequence) without effectively supporting reasoning.
- **Length Penalty Impact:** Removing the length penalty resulted in unnecessarily verbose responses, increasing average sequence length by 43.8% and computational cost without corresponding accuracy gains.
These results suggest that format, length, and token-spam constraints are complementary in guiding ATLAS to invoke functional tokens meaningfully and generate concise, well-formatted reasoning trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ATLAS framework introduces a paradigm for visual reasoning that integrates elements from both agentic and latent approaches, demonstrating several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Novelty in Visual Reasoning Representation:** ATLAS proposes functional tokens as a discrete, single-word representation for complex visual operations. This approach offers an alternative to generating full pixel-level images or verbose external code/tool calls, establishing a new method for incorporating active visual manipulation within VLMs.

- **Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability:** By internalizing visual operations into compact tokens within the standard autoregressive sequence, ATLAS significantly reduces the computational overhead associated with traditional methods. The avoidance of intermediate image generation, external tool execution, and recurrent dependencies leads to reduced inference latency and memory footprint. This compatibility with existing scalable autoregressive training and inference pipelines facilitates the application of ATLAS to larger models and datasets, potentially making advanced visual reasoning more accessible.

- **Improved Interpretability and Controllability:** Compared to purely latent reasoning methods, ATLAS retains a degree of interpretability by explicitly representing visual reasoning steps with discrete, semantically meaningful tokens (e.g., `<|Line|>`, `<|Shape|>`). This allows for a clearer understanding of the model's intermediate reasoning processes, which can be beneficial for debugging, analysis, and building user trust.

- **Robust Reinforcement Learning for Sparse Actions:** The introduction of Latent-Anchored GRPO (LA-GRPO) addresses a specific challenge in training visual reasoning models with reinforcement learning: the "gradient dilution" issue for sparse, critical functional tokens. By strengthening the optimization signal for these tokens, LA-GRPO contributes to more stable and effective training, which can be impactful for developing robust agentic behaviors in various domains.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a template for how diverse visual operations can be systematically integrated into language models via an extensible set of discrete tokens. This could inspire future research into expanding the functional token vocabulary to cover a wider range of visual reasoning scenarios, exploring more sophisticated learning mechanisms for these tokens, and applying this paradigm to other multimodal tasks requiring active visual interaction.

In summary, ATLAS offers a distinct approach to interleaved visual reasoning by combining efficiency, scalability, and a level of interpretability, addressing some limitations of previous methods and providing a foundation for subsequent developments in multimodal AI.
