# DSO: Direct Steering Optimization for Bias Mitigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Apple and Carnegie Mellon University developed Direct Steering Optimization (DSO), an approach leveraging reinforcement learning to directly optimize activation steering for mitigating biases in generative models. This method reduces occupation-gender bias by up to 10 percentage points on VLMs like Qwen-2.5-7B while preserving model capabilities, offering controllable bias reduction at inference time.

## Problem

- Generative models, including VLMs and LLMs, often perpetuate stereotypical biases (e.g., occupation-gender) leading to unfair and potentially harmful outcomes in critical applications.
- Existing bias mitigation strategies (fine-tuning, prompt engineering, heuristic-based activation steering) are computationally expensive, lack inference-time adaptability, or do not offer principled control over the bias-capability trade-off.
- Current activation steering methods struggle to achieve specific bias reduction goals, often relying on predefined directions that are not directly optimized for nuanced fairness objectives.

## Method

- **DSO** introduces an inference-time ==activation steering== framework that applies sparse, linear transformations to internal model activations to modify behavior.
- The approach formulates bias mitigation as a reinforcement learning (RL) problem, optimizing steering parameters to maximize a per-generation fairness reward that incentivizes equiprobable outcomes across demographic stereotypes.
- It utilizes an intervention strength parameter (lambda) at inference time to provide controllable trade-offs between bias reduction strength and the preservation of original model capabilities, guided by theoretical guarantees for capability preservation.

## Results

- DSO demonstrated superior fairness-accuracy trade-offs, Pareto-dominating other methods on both Gemma-3-**4B** and Qwen-VL-**3B** for VLM tasks and on LLMs for coreference resolution.
- On the Qwen-2.5-**7B** VL model, a moderate DSO setting (lambda=**0.2**) reduced Per-Occupation Bias by 10 percentage points and Stereotype Gap by 12 percentage points, with less than a 1.1 percentage point drop in unambiguous accuracy.
- The method provided consistent and monotonic control over bias reduction with increasing intervention strength, outperforming baselines which often showed non-monotonic or inconsistent behavior, and generalized robustly across VLM and LLM modalities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The RL objective of maximizing a per-generation fairness reward is theoretically equivalent to minimizing the aggregate Per-Occupation Bias, providing a direct and principled path to bias mitigation.
> - A hard KL divergence constraint effectively guarantees capability preservation, while the intervention strength parameter (lambda) empirically provides a monotonic and controllable trade-off between fairness and model performance.
> - Effective bias mitigation can be achieved by intervening on a remarkably small fraction of model parameters (less than 0.005% of total), highlighting the efficiency and targeted nature of learned steering transformations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Generative models, including Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs), are increasingly deployed in applications that involve decision-making on behalf of users. Such applications span critical domains like aiding visually impaired individuals, supporting hiring decisions, assisting medical diagnostics, and performing content moderation. However, these models have demonstrated a susceptibility to biases, particularly those linked to perceived demographic attributes such as gender and ethnicity. For instance, VLMs may exhibit gender stereotypes by failing to identify women as doctors, leading to potentially harmful and systematically biased outcomes.

The broader research landscape for addressing these biases includes several approaches:
1. **Training-based methods:** These involve fine-tuning models on counterfactuals or incorporating fairness penalties, but they are computationally intensive and lack inference-time adaptability.
2. **Prompt-based techniques:** Methods like soft prompting or prompt engineering aim to guide model behavior without retraining, but they can incur additional memory costs and decoding latency, and may not offer principled control over the bias-capability trade-off.
3. **Model steering (Activation Steering):** This approach modifies model behavior by directly altering internal activations (representations) at inference time. It offers advantages in efficiency and dynamic control, allowing practitioners to adjust intervention strength. While activation steering has shown promise in LLMs for controlling aspects like truthfulness and toxicity, its application to mitigate biases, especially in VLMs, remains an underexplored area. Prior steering methods often rely on predefined heuristics or proxy objectives to define linear transformations, which may not be optimal for directly achieving specific behavioral changes, such as ensuring equiprobable outcomes across demographic groups to mitigate bias.

This work addresses the limitations of existing bias mitigation strategies by focusing on activation steering and introducing a novel optimization framework. It positions itself as an advancement in inference-time bias mitigation, particularly for VLMs, by moving beyond heuristic-based steering to directly optimize interventions for fairness and controllability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research outlines several key objectives and motivations.

### 3. Key Objectives

1. **Mitigate Occupation-Gender Bias at Inference Time:** The primary objective is to develop an activation steering method that effectively reduces stereotypical biases in generative models, specifically focusing on occupation-gender biases in VLMs. The goal is to ensure that model decisions are independent of perceived demographic attributes (e.g., gender) when identifying occupations or making hiring decisions.
2. **Preserve Overall Model Capabilities:** A critical objective is to reduce bias without significantly degrading the model's performance on its intended tasks and general capabilities (e.g., accuracy on non-biased tasks or broad understanding benchmarks).
3. **Enable Controllable Bias Reduction:** The method aims to provide practitioners with inference-time control over the trade-off between bias mitigation strength and the preservation of model capabilities. This allows for dynamic adjustment of interventions based on varying user needs or contextual fairness requirements.
4. **Develop an Optimized Steering Strategy:** The research seeks to move beyond heuristic-driven steering by proposing a method that directly learns optimal linear transformations for activations to induce desired unbiased behaviors.

**Motivation:**

1. **Prevalence and Harm of Stereotypical Biases:** Generative models often perpetuate societal stereotypes, such as associating men with "doctors" more frequently than women. This behavior is problematic in consequential applications (e.g., hiring, medical assistance) where biased decisions can lead to unfair outcomes and reinforce harmful stereotypes. The example of a visually-impaired user asking a VLM to find a doctor, and the model relying on gender stereotypes, vividly illustrates this concern.
2. **Limitations of Current Bias Mitigation Methods:**
 - **Fine-tuning:** While effective, fine-tuning is computationally expensive for large models and does not offer dynamic, inference-time control over the bias-capability balance. Once a model is fine-tuned, its fairness parameters are static.
 - **Prompting:** Prompt engineering can guide behavior but often lacks a principled mechanism for adjusting the strength of intervention. It can also introduce additional latency and memory overhead.
 - **Existing Activation Steering:** While promising for inference-time control and efficiency, current steering methods struggle to achieve specific bias mitigation goals that require equiprobable outcomes across demographic groups. They often rely on predefined "directions" or heuristics that may not be directly optimized for nuanced bias reduction.
3. **Need for Adaptive and Contextual Fairness:** Fairness is acknowledged as a contextual and evolving concept. Inference-time controllability offered by steering provides a mechanism for human oversight and adaptable interventions, allowing models to align with changing norms and stakeholder expectations rather than enforcing a static definition of fairness.
4. **Efficiency and Minimal Overhead:** Steering offers minimal inference-time overhead by modifying activations on-the-fly, making it a more practical solution for deployed models compared to retraining or extensive prompting.
5. **Underexplored Area in VLMs:** While steering has gained traction in LLMs, its application to mitigate bias in VLMs remains largely unexplored, despite the significant societal impact of biased VLMs.

These motivations collectively drive the development of Direct Steering Optimization (DSO) as a method to achieve effective, controllable, and efficient bias mitigation in generative models.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology revolves around Direct Steering Optimization (DSO), a novel approach that uses reinforcement learning (RL) to learn optimal linear transformations for activation steering, tailored to mitigate occupation-gender bias while preserving model capabilities.

**Problem Setup:**
The authors define bias through stereotypical behavior.
- **Dataset:** A dataset $D = \{(x_i, \text{Img}_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ is used, where $(x, \text{Img})$ is a user prompt and an optional image. Each sample is annotated with an occupation $Ocp(x, \text{Img}) \in O$.
- **Stereotypical Behavior:** A decision $y$ is classified as "pro-stereotypical" or "anti-stereotypical" based on its alignment with societal gender-occupation stereotypes (sourced from the US Department of Labor).
- **Per-Occupation Bias (Primary Metric):** This is defined as $Bias(\pi, D) = \frac{1}{|O|} \sum_{o \in O} |\Delta(o)|$, where $\Delta(o)$ is the per-occupation stereotype gap, calculated as $Pr[S(y) = \text{pro}] - Pr[S(y) = \text{anti}]$ for occupation $o$. A zero value for this metric indicates fairness, meaning decisions are independent of gender-occupation stereotypes for every occupation.
- **Stereotype Gap (Secondary Metric):** $\Delta_{\text{pro-anti}}(\pi, D) = \sum_{o \in O} Pr[Ocp(x, \text{Img}) = o]\Delta(o)$. This metric summarizes the global bias direction but can be zero even with unfair behavior if pro- and anti-stereotypes cancel out.

**Activation Steering Mechanism:**
- Generative models are viewed as transformer blocks, each containing modules like layer normalization (ln), attention (attn), and multi-layer perceptrons (mlp).
- Steering modifies the output activations $h^{(l)}_{mod}(w)$ of a chosen module at layer $l$ by applying a linear transformation: $\hat{h}^{(l)}_{mod}(w) = h^{(l)}_{mod}(w) + \lambda (a^{(l)} \odot h^{(l)}_{mod}(w) + b^{(l)})$.
- Here, $a^{(l)}$ and $b^{(l)}$ are learnable steering parameters (vectors), $\odot$ is element-wise product, and $\lambda \in$ controls the intervention strength. The steered model is denoted $\pi_{a,b,\lambda}$.

**Direct Steering Optimization (DSO) Formulation:**
DSO has two main objectives: (i) reducing bias and (ii) preserving capabilities.
- **Initial Objective Formulation:** The problem is initially framed as minimizing bias while imposing constraints:
 $\min_{a,b} \text{Bias}(\pi_{a,b,\lambda=1}, D) + \alpha (\|a\|_1 + \|b\|_1)$
 s.t. $D_{KL}(\pi_{a,b,\lambda=1} \| \pi) \le \delta$
 where $\alpha$ controls sparsity (L1 penalty) and $\delta$ sets a maximum allowed Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from the base model $\pi$ to preserve capabilities.
- **Operationalizing with Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Directly optimizing the aggregated $Bias(\pi, D)$ is challenging for standard RL. Therefore, DSO reformulates this into an RL problem with a per-generation fairness reward:
 - **Fairness Reward $r_{\pi}(y, x, \text{Img})$:** For each occupation $o$, a "majority stereotype" $S^*_{\pi}(o)$ is identified (the more frequent pro- or anti-stereotypical prediction). The reward is +1 if the model's prediction *contradicts* $S^*_{\pi}(o)$ and -1 if it *matches* $S^*_{\pi}(o)$. This incentivizes an equilibrium where pro- and anti-stereotypical predictions occur equally often, leading to bias mitigation.
 - **RL Problem Definition (DSO):**
 $\max_{a,b} E_{y \sim \pi_{a,b,\lambda=1}}[r_{\pi_{a,b,\lambda=1}}] - \alpha (\|a\|_1 + \|b\|_1)$
 s.t. $D_{KL}(\pi_{a,b,\lambda=1} \| \pi) \le \delta$
 This formulation allows the use of standard policy-gradient methods.

**Theoretical Justifications:**
1. **Equivalence of RL Strategies (Theorem 1):** The paper provides a theoretical guarantee that the RL objective (maximizing expected fairness reward) is equivalent to minimizing the Per-Occupation Bias, given that each occupation has the same number of samples. This justifies using the reward-based formulation as a direct path to the intended fairness objective without introducing a surrogate gap.
2. **Capability Preservation (Theorem 2):** It is shown that a hard KL constraint $D_{KL}(\pi_{a,b,\lambda} \| \pi) \le \delta$ guarantees capability preservation. If a utility function $u$ (e.g., task accuracy) is $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, then the expected utility loss is bounded by $\sigma \sqrt{2f(\lambda)}$, where $f(\lambda) = D_{KL}(\pi_{a,b,\lambda} \| \pi)$. If $f(\lambda)$ increases monotonically with $\lambda$, then $\lambda$ provides a controllable trade-off between fairness and performance at inference time.

**Implementation Details:**
- **Target Modules:** DSO is applied to all LayerNorms within the transformer blocks of the LLM or the VLM backbone, as these are empirically found effective for linear neuron transformations.
- **Training:** The RL problem is solved using REINFORCE (with PPO's clipped surrogate objective and an entropy penalty). Training is performed using a small dataset of 600 ambiguous samples.
- **Hyperparameters:** Sparsity penalty $\alpha$ is set to $10^{-6}$. Crucially, the KL constraint $\delta$ is *not* enforced during training. Empirical analysis shows that Per-Occupation Bias monotonically decreases with increasing KL divergence (no "reward over-optimization" observed, unlike some RL for alignment tasks), implying that the fairness reward behaves like a "gold reward." Instead, the intervention strength $\lambda$ is used at inference time to control the bias-capability trade-off, as the KL divergence is empirically shown to increase monotonically with $\lambda$.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations demonstrate DSO's effectiveness in mitigating bias, maintaining model capabilities, and offering inference-time controllability across various generative models and tasks.

1. **Fairness Controllability at Inference Time (Fig. 3):**
 - DSO exhibits a consistent and monotonic decrease in Per-Occupation Bias as the intervention strength parameter $\lambda$ increases. This indicates that DSO provides predictable and stable control over bias mitigation.
 - In contrast, baselines such as Inference-Time Intervention (ITI) and Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) showed non-monotonic or inconsistent behavior, making their effects on bias less predictable. Prompt-based debiasing also lacked a principled way to control bias monotonically.

2. **Fairness vs. Accuracy Trade-Off in VLMs (Fig. 4, Table 1):**
 - DSO consistently achieved a superior fairness-accuracy trade-off, pareto-dominating other methods on both Gemma-3-4B and Qwen-VL-3B models. This means DSO could achieve greater bias reduction for a given level of accuracy preservation, or maintain higher accuracy for a target bias reduction.
 - For example, on Qwen-2.5-7B VL, a moderate DSO setting ($\lambda=0.2$) reduced Per-Occupation Bias by 10 percentage points (p.p.) and Stereotype Gap by 12 p.p. while maintaining Unambiguous and MMMU accuracy within 1.1 p.p. of the base model. A stronger intervention ($\lambda=1.0$) further reduced Per-Occupation Bias to 8.7% and Stereotype Gap to 0.1%, albeit with a 16 p.p. drop in Unambiguous accuracy.
 - Competing methods (CAA, ITI, Prompting) generally reduced the Stereotype Gap but were often ineffective at consistently reducing the Per-Occupation Bias, sometimes even worsening it (e.g., Llama-11B VL for Prompting and CAA). When effective in reducing Per-Occupation Bias, these methods typically incurred a higher performance degradation.

3. **Sparsity of Interventions (Fig. 5):**
 - DSO achieved significant Per-Occupation Bias reduction by intervening on a remarkably small fraction of model parameters. Specifically, modifying less than 0.005% of the total parameters (by intervening on 60% of LayerNorm neurons) resulted in nearly the same bias reduction as intervening on all LayerNorm neurons. This highlights DSO's efficiency and ability to target specific neurons.

4. **DSO for Bias Mitigation in LLMs (Table 2, Fig. 6):**
 - The method's generality was demonstrated through its effectiveness in mitigating bias in LLMs (Qwen 2.5, Llama 3.2) on the coreference resolution task using the SynthBias dataset.
 - For Qwen-2.5-3B, DSO ($\lambda=1$) reduced Per-Occupation Bias from 60.4% to 5.9% and Stereotype Gap from 62.0% to 4.1%, with Unambiguous accuracy increasing to 99.7%. This came with an increase in the "don't know" rate, indicating a more cautious model.
 - Similar to VLMs, DSO showed the best fairness-accuracy trade-off for LLMs, outperforming baselines which, although more effective in LLMs than VLMs, still caused a higher impact on accuracy.
 - A key observation was that LLM-native steering approaches (CAA, ITI, Prompting) did not generalize well to the VLM domain, whereas DSO maintained its robustness across both modalities.

5. **Additional Results (Appendix E, Tables 3-5):**
 - Consistent trends were observed across the SocialCounterfactuals hiring task, and the GenderBias-VL dataset for both occupation identification and hiring tasks. DSO with moderate $\lambda$ values consistently provided the most reliable and substantial bias reductions while maintaining high accuracy across various models and tasks. This further supports the robustness and efficacy of DSO.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Direct Steering Optimization (DSO) represents a significant advancement in the field of bias mitigation for generative models, particularly for vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs). Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Novelty in Steering Methodology:** DSO introduces a novel paradigm for activation steering by moving away from heuristic-based interventions towards a reinforcement learning (RL) approach that *directly optimizes* for desired behavioral outcomes (bias mitigation). This shift provides a more principled and effective way to control model behavior compared to previous methods.

2. **State-of-the-Art Fairness-Capability Trade-off:** The research demonstrates that DSO achieves a superior trade-off between reducing occupation-gender bias and preserving overall model capabilities. This is critical for practical deployment, as severe performance degradation often hinders the adoption of debiasing techniques. DSO's ability to minimize bias with minimal impact on accuracy makes it a highly practical solution.

3. **Inference-Time Controllability:** A key contribution is the provision of an interpretable intervention strength parameter ($\lambda$) that allows practitioners to dynamically control the balance between fairness and performance at inference time. This flexibility is crucial in real-world applications where varying contexts or user preferences may demand different levels of bias mitigation. It supports adaptive interventions reflecting evolving social norms and values.

4. **Efficiency and Sparsity:** DSO learns sparse linear interventions, affecting less than 0.005% of model parameters. This high degree of sparsity translates into computational efficiency and minimizes the risk of unintended side effects on other model capabilities, making it a lightweight and scalable solution for large foundation models.

5. **Generality Across Modalities:** The demonstrated effectiveness of DSO in mitigating bias in both VLMs (less explored domain for bias steering) and LLMs highlights its broad applicability across different types of generative models. This robustness is particularly impactful given the observation that many LLM-native steering techniques do not generalize well to the VLM domain for bias mitigation.

6. **Theoretical Justification:** The theoretical proofs establishing the equivalence of the RL objective to direct bias minimization and the guarantees for capability preservation strengthen the foundational understanding and reliability of DSO.

7. **Ethical and Societal Impact:** By enabling more effective and controllable bias mitigation, DSO contributes to the development of more responsible and fair AI systems. This is particularly vital for consequential applications like hiring, medical diagnostics, and assistive technologies, where biased decisions can have profound and detrimental real-world effects. The inference-time controllability also supports human oversight and participatory governance, aligning AI behavior with ethical guidelines.

8. **Direction for Future Research:** The success of DSO in directly optimizing for behavioral control is presented as an incentive for the broader research community to explore similar RL-based or direct optimization strategies for controlling other aspects of model behavior (e.g., toxicity, text style), moving beyond proxy objectives.

In summary, DSO offers a robust, efficient, and controllable solution for mitigating demographic biases in generative AI, with strong theoretical and empirical backing. Its ability to adapt to contextual fairness needs at deployment time marks a significant step towards more responsible and ethically aligned AI.
