# ZEST: Zero-shot Embodied Skill Transfer for Athletic Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> ZEST is a unified motion-imitation framework enabling legged robots to perform agile, whole-body movements by training controllers from diverse motion data. It successfully achieves zero-shot deployment of complex, multi-contact skills on Boston Dynamics' Atlas and Unitree G1 humanoids, and the Spot quadruped, demonstrating advantages over traditional model-based approaches for dynamic tasks.

## Problem

- Developing robust, human-like whole-body control for agile, contact-rich behaviors on legged robots, particularly humanoids.
- Overcoming the high engineering cost and lack of generality in existing robot control methods for acquiring diverse skills.
- Robustly utilizing heterogeneous and imperfect motion data (e.g., noisy video) for robot skill learning and ensuring zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Method

- A unified motion-imitation framework based on ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)==, trained in simulation with a minimal observation space and generic reward terms.
- A reference motion pipeline that processes diverse data sources (MoCap, video, animation) and employs kinematic retargeting without requiring contact labels.
- Integration of an asymmetric actor-critic architecture, domain randomization, adaptive reference state initialization, and an assistive wrench automatic curriculum to enhance robustness and sample efficiency for hardware deployment.

## Results

- Achieved zero-shot deployment of novel dynamic multi-contact skills (e.g., army crawl, breakdancing) on Atlas, expressive dances and scene interactions on Unitree G1 from video, and acrobatics on Spot.
- Maintained low mean absolute errors (MAE(q) for joint positions typically **0.04**-**0.08** rad) for tracking across diverse skills and data types, despite the challenges of dynamic and multi-contact tasks.
- Outperformed a state-of-the-art whole-body Model Predictive Control (MPC) baseline on dynamic behaviors for Atlas, successfully executing several complex, multi-contact sequences that MPC failed to complete.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A "minimal recipe" for motion imitation, free from complex components like contact labeling or extensive reward shaping, is sufficient for robust control of dynamic, multi-contact behaviors.
> - The framework effectively handles heterogeneous and imperfect motion data by tracking global motion in a relaxed manner, rather than strictly adhering to every detail of a noisy reference trajectory.
> - Progressive approximations for Parallel-Linkage Actuators and a principled approach to PD gain selection are crucial for minimizing sim-to-real mismatch on complex robotic hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot control, especially for humanoids, faces the grand challenge of achieving robust, human-like whole-body control for agile, contact-rich behaviors. Historically, two main paradigms have dominated:

- **Model-Based Control (MPC) / Trajectory Optimization**: Exemplified by Boston Dynamics' Atlas, this approach involves offline generation of dynamically feasible trajectories (often from keyframe animation) which are then tracked by online model-based controllers. While capable of impressive feats like parkour, these methods are "brittle," demanding heavy per-skill engineering, precise environment modeling, sophisticated state estimation, and predefined contact schedules. They struggle with high uncertainty, complex contact interactions, and generalizability across diverse behaviors.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**: More recently, RL has shown remarkable promise for agility and robustness by learning to manage complex dynamics and contacts implicitly. It allows training at scale in simulation and zero-shot transfer to hardware, leading to state-of-the-art performance in locomotion (e.g., over challenging terrains, high-speed running, parkour) and manipulation. However, *tabula rasa* RL is notoriously sample-inefficient and sensitive to reward design, often requiring extensive shaping to avoid unnatural behaviors.

**Motion Imitation Learning** bridges these two paradigms by using human motion data as a prior to regularize RL policy learning. Pioneered in computer graphics (e.g., DeepMimic, AMP), this approach guides controllers towards natural movements, simplifying reward engineering. Recent works have extended this to robotics, showing success in imitating animal gaits, stage performances, and contextual skills from videos, and even learning from optimized trajectories (Opt-Mimic, DTC).

ZEST significantly advances this landscape by presenting a **unified, generic, and minimal recipe** for motion imitation that addresses key limitations of prior work:

- **Heterogeneous Data Sources**: It robustly handles high-fidelity MoCap, noisy monocular video (ViCap), and non-physics-constrained keyframe animation, demonstrating resilience to diverse data imperfections. This contrasts with methods that rely on carefully curated or post-processed motion data.
- **Zero-Shot Hardware Deployment**: A core contribution is the reliable transfer of policies directly from simulation to hardware without any task-specific fine-tuning, across multiple robot morphologies (Atlas, Unitree G1, Spot). This is crucial for scalability.
- **Reduced Engineering Complexity**: ZEST explicitly avoids many common complexities in imitation learning pipelines, such as contact labeling, extensive reward shaping, future-reference windows, observation histories, and state estimators. This "minimal recipe" drastically lowers the barrier to deploying diverse skills.
- **Multi-Contact and Dynamic Behaviors**: It pushes the boundaries of demonstrated hardware capabilities, especially for multi-contact, whole-body, and highly dynamic skills on humanoids, which are notoriously difficult for model-based controllers and often magnify the sim-to-real gap for RL.

This work positions itself at the forefront of robust, data-driven control for complex robotic systems, seeking to create a scalable interface between biological movements and their robotic counterparts by leveraging the diversity and expressiveness of human motion. It competes with and demonstrates advantages over state-of-the-art MPC for dynamic tasks, and simplifies the learning process compared to many existing RL-based motion imitation frameworks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the ZEST project are twofold:

1. **To enable robust, human-like whole-body control on humanoid robots for agile, contact-rich behaviors.** This addresses the central challenge in humanoid robotics, where high degrees of freedom, intermittent multi-contact interactions, and modeling mismatches demand sophisticated control strategies.
2. **To develop a streamlined, unified motion-imitation framework that can train policies from diverse motion data sources and deploy them to hardware zero-shot.** The motivation here is to overcome the existing limitations of traditional approaches:
 - **High Engineering Cost**: Current methods often require heavy per-skill engineering and brittle controller tuning. ZEST aims for a single, general-purpose framework.
 - **Lack of Generality**: Many controllers are specialized for a single robot or behavior. ZEST seeks to generalize across behaviors and robot platforms.
 - **Data Imperfections**: Real-world motion data (especially video) is often noisy or kinematically inconsistent with physics. ZEST aims for robustness to these imperfections.
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap**: Dynamic, contact-rich behaviors are particularly susceptible to discrepancies between simulation and reality. ZEST strives for robust zero-shot transfer.
 - **Complex Pipelines**: Many successful RL-based approaches rely on intricate, multi-stage pipelines with handcrafted components (e.g., contact labels, extensive reward shaping, privileged observations for actors). ZEST aims for a minimal, single-stage recipe.

Ultimately, ZEST is motivated by the vision of enabling humanoid robots to operate effectively in human environments by performing a broad repertoire of coordinated, whole-body movements with human-like fluidity, leveraging existing human motion data as a source of general-purpose skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ZEST (Zero-shot Embodied Skill Transfer) is a unified motion-imitation framework built upon Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and deployed zero-shot to hardware. Its methodology can be broken down into several key components:

**A. Reference Motion Pipeline:**
ZEST processes diverse motion data sources:
- **Motion Capture (MoCap)**: High-fidelity human motion data (from Xsens and Vicon systems), requiring minimal post-processing.
- **Video-Captured (ViCap) Motions**: Reconstructed 3D human motion from monocular videos, using MegaSaM for camera/scene estimation and TRAM for human pose estimation. This pipeline is fast but produces noisier data with artifacts (jitter, foot-skidding).
- **Keyframe Animation**: Used for motions not humanly possible or for non-humanoid morphologies (e.g., Spot quadruped).
For human-centric data (MoCap, ViCap), a **kinematic retargeting** process maps the source motion to the target robot's skeleton. This involves a spacetime optimization minimizing root position/orientation tracking, bone alignment, and joint velocity regularization, while jointly optimizing for uniform scale and time resampling to ensure physical consistency with gravity. Crucially, ZEST **assumes no contact labeling** from any data source, relying purely on kinematic information.

**B. Systems and Modeling:**
The framework is evaluated on Boston Dynamics' Atlas (humanoid), Unitree G1 (humanoid), and Boston Dynamics' Spot (quadruped). A key technical contribution is the development of **progressive approximations for Parallel-Linkage Actuators (PLAs)**, commonly found in humanoid knees and ankles:
1. **Locally Projected Model (Massless-Links Approx.)**: Assumes PLA support links are massless, projecting their dynamics onto the main kinematic chain. This yields a simulator-compatible model with configuration-dependent effective motor armatures.
2. **Dynamic Armature Model (Diagonal Approx.)**: Addresses coupled PLAs (e.g., Atlas ankles) by decomposing the projected mass matrix into diagonal and off-diagonal components, treating the latter as a fictitious torque from the previous timestep (Jacobi approximation).
3. **Nominal Armature Model (Fixed-Configuration Approx.)**: For computational efficiency, armature values are computed once at a nominal configuration and fixed, simplifying simulation and providing a principled basis for PD gain design.
For **Spot**, a more detailed actuator model was developed to handle dynamic behaviors like continuous backflips, incorporating the robot's power-limiting algorithm and nonlinear effects (magnet saturation, transmission inefficiencies, friction).

**C. Training Setup and MDP Formulation:**
ZEST formulates control as a goal-conditioned Markov Decision Process (MDP) trained using **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** in the Isaac Lab simulator.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic Architecture**: The actor (policy) receives only proprioceptive observations and the next target state from the reference, ensuring hardware deployability without state estimators. The critic, however, receives additional "privileged information" (e.g., base linear velocity, contact forces, keybody positions/velocities, assistive wrench signals, curriculum-related signals) to accelerate value function estimation.
- **Observations (Actor)**: Includes instantaneous onboard measurements: torso angular velocity, normalized gravity vector in torso frame, joint positions, joint velocities, and the previous action.
- **Observations (Reference)**: Next target state from the demonstration: base height, absolute linear and angular base velocities, gravity direction in base frame, and reference joint positions. ZEST deliberately avoids future-reference windows beyond the immediate next target, as ablations showed no benefit.
- **Actions and Joint-Level Control**: The policy outputs **residual actions**, which are added to the reference joint positions. These combined targets are then sent to joint-level **PD controllers**. PD gains are selected using effective motor armature values derived from the PLA models, aiming for a critically damped response with a desired natural frequency. This minimizes sim-to-real mismatch.
- **Reward Terms**: The total reward is a combination of:
 - **Tracking Reward**: Dense, bounded exponential kernels penalizing errors in base position, orientation, angular velocity, linear velocity, joint positions, and keybody poses relative to the reference. Critically, these terms are *generic*, not task- or motion-specific, and do *not* rely on contact labels.
 - **Regularization Reward**: Penalties for action smoothness, large joint accelerations, and violations of joint position/torque limits.
 - **Survival Reward**: A constant positive term to encourage longer episodes.
- **Early Terminations**: Episodes terminate early if contact forces exceed thresholds or deviations from the reference are too large, guiding exploration.
- **Domain Randomization**: To ensure robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, training incorporates moderate domain randomization, including Gaussian noise in observations, random impulsive pushes, varying surface friction, and randomizing link masses.

**D. Training Techniques for Scalability and Robustness:**
- **Adaptive Reference State Initialization (RSI)**: To handle long-horizon trajectories and large motion libraries efficiently, demonstrations are partitioned into fixed-duration bins. Each bin maintains a "failure level" (based on an EMA of tracking performance), and a categorical sampler biases reset-state selection towards harder bins. A small floor probability prevents catastrophic forgetting of easier skills. This improves sample efficiency and ensures comprehensive skill mastery.
- **Assistive Wrench Automatic Curriculum**: For highly dynamic behaviors prone to early termination (e.g., handstands, backflips), a virtual spatial wrench is applied to the robot's base. This wrench, computed model-based using a PD term on base pose error and a feedforward component, is scaled by a gain that is modulated by the per-bin failure levels from the adaptive RSI. As tracking improves, the assistance automatically anneals to zero, gradually increasing task difficulty without manual curriculum design.

**E. Hardware Deployment Pipeline:**
ZEST utilizes an automated, framework- and hardware-agnostic pipeline for safe and consistent releases. Policy checkpoints (ONNX format) are logged with metadata, which is used to automatically configure the controller in an evaluation simulator (mirroring hardware software) and then deployed directly to the physical robot. This ensures consistency and minimizes manual errors for zero-shot transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The ZEST framework demonstrates remarkable generality and robustness across heterogeneous data sources and robot embodiments, achieving significant new capabilities in hardware:

**A. Zero-Shot Deployment Across Diverse Robots and Data Sources:**
- **Atlas Humanoid (Boston Dynamics)**: Successfully learns and performs dynamic, multi-contact skills (e.g., army crawl, breakdancing, forward roll, cartwheel) from MoCap data. It also transfers expressive dance routines and a soccer kick from noisy ViCap data, and a handstand invert from animation.
- **Unitree G1 Humanoid**: Transfers expressive dance (ballet sequence) and scene-interaction skills (e.g., jump onto box, climb up/down box) directly from videos. It also performs cartwheel and table-tennis from MoCap.
- **Spot Quadruped (Boston Dynamics)**: Achieves acrobatics, such as a continuous backflip and barrel roll, and a playful "happy dog" motion from keyframe animation, demonstrating cross-morphology extension.

**B. Quantitative Performance Metrics (Table 1):**
- **Robust Tracking**: Across all robots and diverse skills, ZEST maintains surprisingly low tracking errors, even with noisy ViCap data or dynamically aggressive animations. For example, many Atlas MoCap and ViCap skills show Mean Absolute Error (MAE(q)) for joint positions around 0.04-0.08 rad and Mean Angular Distance (MAD(R)) for base orientation below 0.1 rad.
- **Multi-Contact Challenges**: As expected, more dynamic, multi-contact skills (e.g., forward roll, breakdance on Atlas; box-climbing on G1) generally exhibit higher orientation and angular-velocity discrepancies, reflecting the inherent difficulty and higher sim-to-real gap in these regimes. However, the policy still completes these challenging maneuvers reliably.
- **Resilience to Data Imperfections**: Despite visible artifacts in ViCap references (pose jitter, foot skidding), the policy successfully transfers expressive motions, suggesting its ability to track the "global motion" in a relaxed way, rejecting obvious artifacts without forcing tight adherence to every detail of the reference root trajectory.

**C. Simulation-Based Evaluation and Ablation Studies:**
- **Effectiveness of Adaptive Curriculum (Assistive Wrench + Adaptive Sampling)**: Ablation studies confirm that the assistive wrench curriculum and adaptive sampling are critical for performance and sample efficiency, especially for long-horizon and highly dynamic behaviors. Removing these components significantly degrades mean success rates and, more importantly, lower-tail performance (p10 and minimum success), indicating less robust learning and undersampling of difficult motion segments.
- **Observation Windows and Action Space**:
 - Using longer reference or observation windows (e.g., 20 steps of history or future references) *reduces* performance and slows convergence, suggesting that the minimalist observation design is effective for this framework.
 - Employing *absolute actions* (instead of residual actions) yields the worst performance, highlighting the benefit of residual actions in leveraging the feedforward reference and simplifying the learning task.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic**: Stripping privileged observations from the critic significantly degrades value estimation and, consequently, policy updates, underscoring the importance of this architectural choice for accelerating training.
- **Robustness to Randomization (Figure S4)**: The policy shows reasonable robustness to various domain randomization factors (pushes, mass, friction, observation noise, initial state, actuator delay), even extrapolating beyond training distributions in some cases. Initial-state perturbations and push disturbances had the largest impact, particularly on lower-tail performance.

**D. Simulation-Based Benchmarking Against MPC:**
- **RL vs. MPC**: A multi-skill RL policy for Atlas was compared against a state-of-the-art whole-body MPC baseline developed at Boston Dynamics.
- **Performance Comparison**: For simpler gaits like walk, MPC and RL performed similarly. However, RL significantly *outperformed* MPC on more dynamic behaviors (e.g., cartwheel, jog). Crucially, several complex, multi-contact sequences (e.g., dance B, handstand invert, roll on all fours) *failed* under MPC due to difficulties in contact schedule extraction/accuracy or controller saturation, whereas the RL policy executed them successfully.
- **Elimination of Contact Schedules**: The learned controller does not require explicit contact labeling and is more tolerant of model/contact-timing mismatches, accommodating interactions with arbitrary body parts beyond feet and hands, a major advantage over the MPC baseline.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ZEST represents a significant advance in the field of agile robotic control, particularly for humanoids, by delivering three core contributions:

1. **Generic and Streamlined Motion-Imitation Recipe**: ZEST offers a "minimal recipe" that substantially reduces the engineering effort required for robotic skill acquisition. By training RL policies in a single stage from diverse and imperfect reference sources (MoCap, ViCap, animation) and deploying them zero-shot, it circumvents complex, domain-specific techniques such as contact labeling, extensive reward shaping, future-reference windows, and state estimators. This simplification makes the development and deployment of new robot skills more accessible and scalable.
2. **New State-of-the-Art Hardware Capabilities**: The work showcases unprecedented dynamic multi-contact behaviors on a full-size humanoid (Atlas), such as army crawls and breakdancing, which are exceptionally challenging for traditional controllers. Furthermore, it demonstrates the first physical motion transfer of highly dynamic, contact-rich skills (e.g., dancing, box climbing) directly from everyday video to a physical humanoid (Unitree G1). These achievements push the boundaries of what is possible with current humanoid robotics.
3. **Cross-Morphology Generality**: The consistent application of the same policy structure and training recipe across distinct robot morphologies (Atlas humanoid, Unitree G1 humanoid, and Spot quadruped) highlights a significant level of generality. This adaptability means the framework is not tightly coupled to a single robot or behavior, paving the way for more versatile and broadly applicable robot control solutions.

The potential impact of ZEST is substantial:
- **Accelerated Robot Skill Development**: By simplifying the pipeline from human motion data to robust robot control, ZEST could dramatically accelerate the process of endowing robots with a wide repertoire of human-like skills, reducing the need for laborious manual programming or per-task tuning.
- **Broader Applications for Humanoids**: The ability to perform complex, multi-contact, and scene-interactive behaviors directly from video unlocks new possibilities for humanoids to operate more flexibly and naturally in human environments, potentially enabling tasks that require intricate whole-body coordination or adaptation to unmodeled contacts.
- **Enhanced Robot Robustness and Agility**: The RL-based approach, combined with adaptive training strategies, yields controllers that are inherently more robust to disturbances and model uncertainties than traditional model-based methods, especially for dynamic, high-impact maneuvers.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: ZEST provides a robust foundation for future work on general tracking of unseen motions, compact motion embeddings, few-shot adaptation, and moving towards partially conditioned interfaces (e.g., keyframes, language commands), ultimately bridging behavior synthesis and real-time control.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations: lack of evaluation on generalization to *unseen* skills (beyond the training set), reliance on proprioception and flat/non-slippery terrain (limiting explicit perception of uneven environments), and the ongoing challenge of fully automated system identification for complex phenomena. Future work will focus on generalizing to unseen motions, developing compact motion embeddings, exploring sparse, human-interpretable control interfaces, and integrating high-level generative planners to further bridge user commands and agile robotic execution.
