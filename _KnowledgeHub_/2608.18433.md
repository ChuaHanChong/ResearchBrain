# The Embodiment Gap in Robot Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey defines the embodiment gap: the space between reusable robot-foundation-model structure (semantics, perception, actions, data) and the work still needed to execute it on a specific target robot's body and controller. It places 21 methods on a two-axis map, organizes literature into three sharing directions, and proposes a report card plus Embodiment Adaptation Curve for disclosing hidden adaptation work.

## Problem

- Robot foundation model papers report a single success rate, obscuring how much target-robot demonstration collection, calibration, or human intervention produced that number.
- No existing taxonomy separates what kind of structure is shared across robot bodies from the stage at which adaptation work still remains for execution.
- Safety, failure recovery, and intervention during real-robot deployment are rarely measured or reported, despite being central to practical autonomy.

## Method

- A ==two-axis qualitative map== crossing shared-structure type (semantics, perception, object interaction, action/skill, morphology-aware sharing) against where remaining work falls (skill connection, control-interface alignment, contact execution, safety/recovery), coded by three independent researchers over twenty-one method groups.
- A ==minimum report card== with eleven items (source and target robot, shared structure, modified components, target-robot data, model updates, calibration, operation, safety/intervention/recovery, evaluation rollouts, failure causes) for disclosing adaptation work behind a success rate.
- An **Embodiment Adaptation Curve** that plots performance against a stated adaptation-effort measure such as demonstration count or intervention count, paired with a four-category failure-cause taxonomy spanning semantics, data interfaces, correspondence, and execution.

## Results

- Coder agreement was high for what is shared (**76.2 percent** three-way agreement, Fleiss' kappa **0.78**) but much lower for where adaptation work remains (**47.6 percent** agreement, kappa **0.33**), showing the calibration-versus-contact boundary is genuinely ambiguous.
- Applying the report card to nine representative systems found safety, intervention, and recovery counts unreported for **seven of nine**, and calibration or setup details unclear for **three of nine**.
- **None** of the twenty-one coded method groups was placed in the Safety and Recovery category of the map, and reconstructed adaptation curves for MOTIF and Track2Act show most papers publish only one or two effort endpoints rather than full curves.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sharing the same representation type does not predict adaptation cost: two action-sharing methods can leave very different amounts of target-robot work, so representation category alone is not a reliable proxy.
> - Comparing success rates across papers without also comparing adaptation effort is misleading, since similar scores can hide very different amounts of demonstration, calibration, or intervention work.
> - Safety and recovery after failure remain an unaddressed and unmeasured blind spot in generalist robot policy research, systematically hidden by success-rate-only evaluation practices.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Robot Foundation Models (RFMs), including vision-language-action (VLA) policies and generalist robot policies, represents a significant direction in contemporary robotics research. This field is heavily influenced by the scaling paradigms observed in large language and vision models, characterized by increased data, larger model architectures, and broader benchmarks, with the objective of achieving enhanced generalization capabilities. Prominent examples of such RFMs include RT-X, Octo, OpenVLA, RoboCat, and the π0 family.

While existing research extensively explores the generalization potential of these models, this paper addresses a specific challenge that is often less visible: the practical work required to transition a generalized model from a theoretical or simulated success to functional execution on a physical robot with a particular body and control system. The paper introduces the concept of the "embodiment gap" to articulate this practical disconnect.

This work positions itself in relation to ongoing debates within the robotics community regarding the scalability of data and the roles of learning methods versus engineering. For instance, discussions by Goldberg (2025) and Amato et al. (2025) question whether robotics can achieve Internet-scale data comparable to language and vision, and emphasize the necessity of combining learning with robust engineering for reliable robot operation. This paper contributes to this discourse by seeking to clarify which aspects of the embodiment gap can be ameliorated through model or data scaling, and which necessitate dedicated engineering effort.

Furthermore, while numerous recent surveys cover RFMs, VLA policies, robot data, benchmarks, and evaluation methods (e.g., Firoozi et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2025), this paper distinguishes itself by focusing specifically on the **embodiment gap** in manipulation tasks. It extends beyond just the model itself to encompass the surrounding mechanisms, such as data and interface design, methods for accounting for body differences, and real-robot execution considerations, thus providing a more holistic perspective on cross-embodiment deployment challenges.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to characterize and analyze the "embodiment gap" in the context of Robot Foundation Models, proposing a framework for understanding and addressing the practical challenges associated with deploying generalized robot policies on diverse physical robots.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations within the field of robot learning:
1. **Discrepancy between generalization and practical deployment**: While RFMs demonstrate improved generalization across tasks and environments, their reusability often stops short of direct execution on a specific robot body. A shared model or representation typically requires additional work to connect it to the target robot's kinematics, control system, and physical interaction capabilities.
2. **Incompleteness of success rate as an evaluation metric**: Current evaluations for RFMs predominantly emphasize a robot's final task success rate. However, two systems achieving similar success rates may require vastly different amounts or types of preparatory work—such as calibration, control system alignment, or extensive real-robot fine-tuning—before reaching that performance. This "less visible work" is crucial for practical deployment but is not adequately reflected in reported success metrics. This issue is analogous to the tacit knowledge and experience employed by system integrators in industrial robotics.
3. **Lack of clarity on the impact of scaling**: There is an unresolved question regarding which components of the embodiment gap can be effectively reduced by scaling data or models, and which will consistently remain as essential engineering tasks specific to the target robot. Understanding this distinction is vital for directing future research efforts and resource allocation.
4. **Need for structured analysis and reporting**: To advance the field, a structured approach is necessary to identify where the embodiment gap manifests and to enable a more comprehensive comparison of cross-embodiment learning methods. Current reporting practices often omit critical details about adaptation and deployment efforts, hindering transparency and reproducibility.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines three specific contributions:
1. **Formal definition of the embodiment gap**: To precisely define the disconnect between reusable models, representations, or data and their conversion into executable actions on a particular robot, necessitating additional work post-transfer.
2. **Development of a two-axis qualitative map**: To systematically categorize existing methods based on "what is shared across robots" (distance from execution) and "where work remains on the target robot" (stage of adaptation needed). This map aims to visually represent the strengths and limitations of different research directions.
3. **Proposal of a reporting framework**: To introduce a standardized approach for documenting adaptation work and failure causes on the target robot. This framework, including a "report card" and "Embodiment Adaptation Curve" (EAC), is intended to complement success rates by providing insights into the effort and specific challenges encountered during real-robot deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper presents a conceptual analysis and survey of existing literature rather than an experimental study. Its methodology primarily involves:

1. **Operational Definition of the Embodiment Gap**: The authors define the embodiment gap (Section 2.1) as the work required when models, representations, or data reused across robots must be converted into executable actions that match the specific body and control system of a target robot. This includes problems requiring additional work on the target robot *after* transfer across embodiments. It clarifies its scope by distinguishing it from related concepts like domain shift, task adaptation, sim-to-real transfer, and general system integration, focusing specifically on challenges tied to a change in robot embodiment.

2. **Construction of a Two-Axis Qualitative Map**:
 - **Horizontal Axis: "What is Shared Across Robots"**: This axis categorizes shared structures by their distance from physical execution, ranging from abstract concepts to morphology-specific information. The categories are:
 - **Semantic / Task**: Goals, instructions, plans.
 - **Perception / Affordance**: Cues about objects and places for action.
 - **Object Interaction**: How manipulation should change an object or scene.
 - **Action / Skill**: Latent actions, action tokens, skills, trajectories.
 - **Morphology-Aware Sharing**: Body graphs, kinematics, body-specific conditions.
 - **Vertical Axis: "Where Work Remains on the Target Robot"**: This axis categorizes the type of adaptation work needed on the target robot, ordered by proximity to physical execution. The categories are:
 - **Skill / API Connection**: Connecting plans or meaning to executable skills.
 - **Calibration / Control Interface Alignment**: Aligning model outputs with the target robot’s control interface and action space.
 - **Contact / Force Execution**: Realizing motions as stable physical contact (e.g., grasping) and correcting deviations.
 - **Safety / Recovery**: Handling safe stopping, retrying, and recovery from failures.
 - **Placement Procedure**: 21 representative method groups were independently coded by three researchers according to these axes. Specific rules were developed for handling methods spanning multiple stages, such as prioritizing "Calibration / Control Interface Alignment" if a robot-specific action decoder or additional training is needed, versus "Contact / Force Execution" if the remaining difficulty is explicitly related to physical contact dynamics. Adjudication was performed for disagreements, particularly at boundary cases.

3. **Analysis through Three Research Directions**: The paper categorizes existing work into three overlapping research directions, analyzing how each addresses parts of the embodiment gap and what challenges remain:
 - **Sharing Semantics and Perception (Section 4)**: Focuses on leveraging large language and vision datasets for high-level reasoning.
 - **Sharing Robot Data and Interfaces (Section 5)**: Emphasizes standardization of data collection, formats, and interfaces for reusing physical robot data.
 - **Learning Correspondence Across Embodiments (Section 6)**: Explores methods that explicitly model relationships between different robot bodies, e.g., through object motion, abstract actions, or morphology.

4. **Proposal of a Reporting Framework**:
 - **Minimum Report Card (Table 1)**: A structured checklist for reporting details of cross-embodiment transfer, covering source/target robots, shared structures, modified components, target-robot data, model updates, calibration, real-robot operation, safety/intervention, evaluation rollouts, and failure causes.
 - **Embodiment Adaptation Curve (EAC) (Section 7.1)**: A conceptual visualization tool to plot performance against a quantifiable measure of adaptation effort (e.g., number of target-robot demonstrations, real-robot trials).
 - **Organizing Failure Causes (Section 7.2)**: A categorization scheme for observed failures (semantics/perception, robot data/interfaces, correspondence, execution on target robot) to provide diagnostic information beyond a mere failure rate.

5. **Literature Selection**: The survey systematically selected literature (surveys, papers, preprints, datasets) focusing on RFMs for manipulation, multi-robot data, cross-embodiment transfer, teleoperation, and related evaluation methods (Appendix A). Emphasis was placed on recent work and foundational studies necessary for context.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several findings, both conceptual and observational, derived from its analysis of the embodiment gap and existing literature:

1. **Formal Definition of the Embodiment Gap**: The paper formally defines the embodiment gap as the additional work required on a target robot to convert reused models, representations, or data into executable actions that match its specific body and control system. This definition provides a clear conceptual boundary for analyzing cross-embodiment transfer.

2. **Two-Axis Qualitative Map (Figure 2)**:
 - **Distribution of Methods**: The map effectively places 21 representative method groups, illustrating that as the "shared structure" moves closer to physical execution (rightward on the horizontal axis), the "work remaining on the target robot" generally shifts closer to direct physical interaction, such as contact and force execution (downward on the vertical axis). However, this relationship is not always one-to-one, as exemplified by OpenVLA and UMI/ALOHA, which both share actions but differ in where the primary remaining work lies (control alignment vs. stable contact).
 - **Identification of Research Gaps**: A significant finding from the map is that **no method group was placed in the "Safety / Recovery" row**. This suggests that while individual systems may handle safety and recovery, these aspects have not yet become a central research target or primary contribution of current RFMs, highlighting a critical unaddressed area for sustained real-world operation.
 - **Consistency in Coding**: Inter-coder agreement analysis revealed that the "what is shared" axis was coded with high consistency (Fleiss’ κ = 0.78), while the "where work remains" axis showed moderate agreement (κ = 0.33), with most disagreements occurring between adjacent categories (e.g., calibration vs. contact execution), underscoring the subtle distinctions in practical deployment challenges.

3. **Analysis of Three Research Directions**:
 - **Sharing Semantics and Perception (Section 4)**: This direction excels at providing high-level task understanding and visual cues, drawing from vast language and vision datasets. However, the main challenge (as shown on the map) remains connecting these abstract cues to concrete, stable actions executable by a specific robot. This often requires substantial work in "Skill/API Connection" and "Calibration/Control Interface Alignment."
 - **Sharing Robot Data and Interfaces (Section 5)**: This direction focuses on standardizing robot data collection and formats to create reusable training resources. While this brings shared information closer to action, the main remaining work lies in physically aligning these shared data and policies with the target robot’s behavior, often involving "Calibration/Control Interface Alignment" and "Contact/Force Execution." The traceability of data (e.g., action space, control frequency, camera alignment) is identified as crucial for diagnosing deployment mismatches.
 - **Learning Correspondence Across Embodiments (Section 6)**: This direction directly tackles body differences by learning relationships through object motion, abstract actions, or morphological information. While morphology-aware methods (e.g., Body Transformer, GET-Zero) can account for kinematic differences, challenges related to physical contact (force, slip, deformation) and recovery remain significant, requiring solutions in "Contact/Force Execution" even with internal body modeling.

4. **Reporting Framework (Section 7)**:
 - **Current Reporting Deficiencies**: The application of the proposed "Minimum Report Card" (Table 2) to representative claims reveals a consistent lack of detailed reporting on critical deployment aspects. Specifically, "safety, intervention, and recovery" counts are often not identifiable for many systems, and "setup/calibration" conditions are frequently underspecified. This substantiates the paper's claim that much of the practical work remains "less visible."
 - **Value of Structured Reporting**: The framework (Report Card, EAC, failure cause categorization) is presented as a mechanism to reveal the true deployment cost and effort, allowing for more informed comparisons of methods with similar success rates. The EAC (Figure 4) visually demonstrates how performance relates to adaptation effort, illustrating the current sparsity of such data in publications.
 - **Failure Cause Diagnosis**: The proposed categorization of failure causes (e.g., semantics/perception vs. execution on target robot) is intended to help researchers diagnose whether issues stem from model limitations or from the specific connection to the robot's physical embodiment, guiding future improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes several significant contributions with potential impacts on the direction and evaluation of robot learning research:

1. **Conceptual Clarity and Framework**: By formally defining the "embodiment gap" and providing a two-axis map for categorizing methods, the paper offers a novel conceptual framework for understanding the complexities of cross-embodiment transfer. This framework can serve as a common language and analytical tool for researchers to articulate the scope and limitations of their contributions more precisely.

2. **Paradigm Shift in Evaluation**: The proposed reporting framework (report card, Embodiment Adaptation Curve, failure cause categorization) advocates for a more comprehensive and transparent evaluation of RFMs. Moving beyond mere success rates, this framework encourages researchers to quantify and report the "less visible work" of adaptation, calibration, and real-robot operation. This could lead to:
 - **More Realistic Comparisons**: Allowing for a fairer comparison between systems by considering both performance and deployment effort.
 - **Increased Reproducibility**: Providing detailed context for reported results, making it easier for other researchers to reproduce or build upon prior work.
 - **Faster Deployment**: By highlighting the practical challenges, the framework can guide development towards more deployable and robust robot systems.

3. **Identification of Critical Research Gaps**: The finding that "Safety / Recovery" is largely unaddressed by current RFMs (as indicated by the empty row in the two-axis map) is a significant insight. This explicitly points to a crucial area where future research needs to concentrate, shifting focus from merely achieving a task to ensuring continuous, safe, and autonomous operation in real-world environments.

4. **Guidance for Future RFM Design and Data Collection**: The paper suggests four priorities for future work, which can profoundly impact RFM development:
 - **Process-Oriented Data**: Advocating for data collection that captures not just successful trajectories but also the process of adaptation, failure, and recovery. This could enable models to learn to adapt themselves.
 - **Adaptive RFM Systems**: Encouraging the design of RFMs as systems that actively assist in their own adaptation to target robots, potentially by learning where adjustment is needed or by integrating with higher-level control and supervisory agents.
 - **Shared Contact Behavior**: Emphasizing the need to incorporate tactile, force, and sound data into shared structures to learn contact-rich execution capabilities across diverse embodiments, addressing a current limitation of morphology-aware models.
 - **Comprehensive Benchmarking**: Promoting benchmarks that rigorously record adaptation work alongside performance, reinforcing the shift towards more holistic evaluation.

In essence, this work moves the discussion in robot learning from solely focusing on *what* a model can do in generalized settings to *how effectively and efficiently* that capability can be translated to physical execution on a diverse range of robot bodies. This emphasis on bridging the "embodiment gap" is crucial for the transition of RFMs from theoretical advancements to practical, deployable robotic systems.
