# Labimus: A Simulation and Benchmark for Humanoid Dexterous Manipulation in Chemical Laboratory

> [!summary] Summary
> Labimus provides the first simulation and benchmark platform for evaluating humanoid dexterous manipulation in precision-critical chemical laboratory tasks, revealing a "precision gap" where current robotic methods often complete tasks but fail to meet scientific quantitative tolerances. This platform reconstructs over 30 laboratory assets and simulates granular materials to enable realistic assessment of complex operations like solid weighing.

## Problem

- Current laboratory automation relies on customized layouts and fixed robotic arms, struggling with dynamic, precision-critical operations like solid-solid transfer at milligram-level accuracy.
- Existing humanoid and dexterous manipulation benchmarks focus on general-purpose tasks with binary success, lacking continuous precision metrics crucial for scientific experiments.
- Current laboratory simulation platforms universally use fixed robotic arms and lack support for humanoid dexterous manipulation, complex physical interactions, and continuous precision evaluation.

## Method

- Developed a high-fidelity simulation environment, **Labimus**, which reconstructs over 30 functionally faithful laboratory assets, incorporates ==particle-based powder physics==, and provides ==closed-loop instrument readouts==.
- Implemented an ==LLM-assisted SOP-to-simulation pipeline== to convert documented Standard Operating Procedures into executable simulation tasks with integrated, precision-aware scoring.
- Designed a ==three-tier evaluation hierarchy== (task completion, quantitative precision, long-horizon precision) with continuous precision metrics and four perturbation conditions for robust assessment of humanoid dexterity.

## Results

- Tier 1 task completion showed a clear difficulty gradient, with "Door open" achieving the highest success (ACT: **56.7%**) but "Tare press" resulting in near-zero success rates across all methods (ACT: **2.0%**, DP: **0.0%**, π₀: **0.0%**).
- For the "Grasp & place" task, ACT achieved a binary task success rate (S) of **5.3%** but a precision pass rate (P) of only **3.3%**, quantitatively demonstrating a **2.0** percentage point "precision gap."
- The π₀ policy for "Door open" exhibited a **7.3** percentage point performance drop under combined visual perturbations (from **47.3%** to **40.0%**) compared to individual perturbations, indicating reduced robustness to layered visual changes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A significant "precision gap" exists where policies achieve binary task completion but fail to meet the continuous quantitative tolerances required for scientific experimental validity.
> - Fine, single-finger manipulation tasks, such as pressing a small button, present a major challenge for current imitation learning and vision-language-action policies, resulting in near-zero success rates.
> - While policies show relative insensitivity to individual visual perturbations, compounded visual variations (lighting and texture combined) lead to a more substantial performance degradation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Laboratory automation has progressed through the development of robotic platforms and AI-driven scientific reasoning, enabling high-throughput and reproducible experiments. Current systems frequently employ customized laboratory layouts and redesigned tools for fixed robotic arms equipped with task-specific grippers. This paradigm is effective for predefined and standardized experiments but faces limitations when addressing inherently dynamic and precision-critical operations, such as solid–solid transfer in organic chemistry, which require real-time adaptation, multi-finger coordination, fine force control, and sustained tool-mediated contact. Such operations often demand milligram-level precision and are not easily automated by existing customized fixed-arm systems. Furthermore, these systems are typically confined to purpose-built facilities, limiting their deployability in ordinary laboratories.

Humanoid robots with dexterous hands offer a potential solution by operating directly within existing laboratory environments using standard tools and instruments, circumventing the need for environmental or hardware redesign. However, the application of humanoid dexterous manipulation to precision-critical laboratory tasks has remained largely unexplored.

Existing humanoid manipulation benchmarks, such as BEHAVIOR-1K and Genie Sim 3.0, and dexterous hand benchmarks like ManiSkill3 and DexJoCo, primarily focus on household or general-purpose tasks evaluated with binary success criteria. These benchmarks do not account for the continuous precision requirements typical of scientific experiments (e.g., transferring a specific mass of powder within a defined tolerance). Concurrently, laboratory simulation platforms, including LabUtopia, Chemistry3D, AutoBio, and MATTERIX, have advanced scene construction and chemical process modeling. However, these platforms universally utilize fixed robotic arms with parallel-jaw grippers, neglecting humanoid dexterous manipulation. AutoBio incorporates a 19-DOF hand, but its use is primarily configured for gripper-like operations, deferring full dexterous support. None of these existing laboratory benchmarks are grounded in documented experimental Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) or measure per-step execution precision with continuous metrics.

Labimus addresses this identified gap by introducing a benchmark that integrates humanoid dexterous hands, precision-critical laboratory tasks, and quantitative evaluation criteria within a high-fidelity simulation environment. This work positions itself as the first to specifically evaluate humanoid dexterity in scientific laboratory contexts, moving beyond binary task completion to assess experimental validity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of Labimus is to establish the first benchmark for humanoid dexterous manipulation within precision-critical chemical laboratory environments. This benchmark aims to enable the development and evaluation of humanoid robots capable of performing complex, contact-rich, and precision-dependent operations that are currently challenging for conventional automated systems.

The motivation for this work stems from several factors:

- **Limitations of current laboratory automation:** While robotic platforms have made strides in automating routine, standardized experiments, many core laboratory operations in fields like organic chemistry remain difficult to automate. These operations, such as solid–solid transfer, require dynamic adaptation to material properties (e.g., powder granularity, cohesion), multi-finger coordination, fine force control, and sustained tool-mediated contact (e.g., scooping, depositing with milligram-level precision). Existing fixed-arm robotic systems with parallel-jaw grippers are often insufficient for these tasks, requiring extensive customization of laboratory layouts and tools, which limits generalizability and deployment flexibility.
- **Potential of humanoid robots:** Humanoid robots with dexterous hands are motivated as a more general solution. They can be deployed in existing laboratory settings without requiring redesign of the environment or specialized tools, leveraging their human-like form factor and manipulation capabilities to interact with standard laboratory instruments. This offers the potential for broader applicability and integration into current scientific workflows.
- **Absence of suitable evaluation benchmarks:** Despite the potential of humanoids, no existing benchmark evaluates their dexterous manipulation capabilities in precision-critical laboratory contexts. Current humanoid and dexterous manipulation benchmarks focus on household tasks with binary success criteria (e.g., object grasped, drawer opened), which do not capture the continuous precision required for scientific experiments (e.g., transferring *exactly* 50 mg of powder). Similarly, existing laboratory simulation platforms, while advanced in scene and chemical process modeling, universally employ fixed robotic arms and lack support for humanoid dexterous manipulation and continuous precision evaluation.
- **Challenges in developing a high-fidelity benchmark:** The creation of such a benchmark involves specific challenges:
 - **Realistic physical simulation:** It requires modeling granular media (powders) at a particle level, incorporating articulated laboratory instruments, and providing real-time state readouts (e.g., digital mass displays on analytical balances) to ensure physically meaningful measurements and verifiable experimental outcomes.
 - **Complex contact-rich manipulation:** Tasks like scooping powder or manipulating glass doors demand coordinated multi-finger movements and precise force control, extending beyond simple pick-and-place operations.
 - **Precision-aware evaluation:** Evaluation must measure experimental quality against continuous precision metrics and defined tolerances (e.g., ±0.001 g), rather than merely binary task completion.

In response to these motivations and challenges, Labimus aims to reconstruct over 30 functionally faithful assets from real organic chemistry workstations, integrate advanced physics simulations for granular materials and articulated instruments with closed-loop readouts, define specific atomic operations and a complex workflow derived from real laboratory SOPs, and establish a multi-tiered precision-aware evaluation protocol. The overarching goal is to expose the "precision gap" where a robot may complete a task but fail to meet the quantitative tolerances necessary for scientific validity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Labimus is built upon a simulation foundation designed to address the unique requirements of humanoid dexterous manipulation in chemical laboratories. The methodology comprises three main components: laboratory scene construction and physics simulation, an SOP-to-simulation pipeline, and a teleoperation system for data collection.

#### 3.1. Laboratory Scenes, Assets, and Physics Simulation

- **Scene Layout:** Laboratory scenes are constructed around a fume hood, serving as a primary workbench. Assets, sourced from ArtVIP, feature high-quality meshes, realistic textures, and physically accurate collision geometry. Two types of scene layouts are provided:
 - **Digital-twin scenes:** These faithfully reproduce the spatial arrangements of real organic chemistry workstations.
 - **Procedural scenes:** These use parameterized layouts where instrument placement is randomized within axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) constraints, enabling systematic evaluation of generalization.
- **Functional Assets:** The asset library includes over 30 objects categorized as containers (e.g., flasks, beakers), tools (e.g., spatulas, funnels), and instruments (e.g., analytical balances, heating devices). Each asset is modeled with collision-ready meshes and accurate inertial properties. Instrument assets incorporate articulated joint definitions and measurable state variables, such as balance windshield doors with prismatic-joint constraints or tare buttons.
- **Powder Physics and Weighing Simulation:** Simulating solid weighing requires granular material physics. The standard Isaac Sim particle system was found unsuitable due to excessive fluidity and high computational cost for dry chemical powders. Labimus adopts a **micro-element approach**, representing each powder grain as a small rigid body. Interactions among particles and objects are resolved by the PhysX collision solver, with tuned parameters for real-time performance and stable powder accumulation. When particles land on the analytical balance pan, the balance calculates the total deposited mass in real-time, displaying it on a simulated digital readout. This closed-loop system from particle physics to instrument display provides the basis for continuous precision metrics.

#### 3.2. SOP-to-Simulation Pipeline

A four-stage, LLM-assisted pipeline converts documented Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) into executable simulation tasks with integrated scoring.

- **Stage 1: SOP Parsing:** An LLM (Qwen3.5-4B) with a domain-specific prompt template decomposes experimental procedures from structured text into manipulation-relevant steps. Each step is represented as a structured tuple containing action, target object, check method (e.g., joint state, spatial containment, mass reading), step weight, quantitative tolerance, and prerequisite steps. A fixed vocabulary ensures mapping to implemented simulator detectors.
- **Stage 2: Scene Assembly:** Task scenes are assembled from curated recipes, instantiating functional assets with pre-extracted poses from real workstations. Spatial, lighting, and texture variations are applied separately as perturbation conditions.
- **Stage 3: Prim Binding and Task Specification:** Logical object names (e.g., "balance door right") from the SOP are bound to concrete USD prim paths using a static object-to-prim library. The pipeline outputs a task configuration (scene path, robot initial pose) and a protocol specification in a lightweight TOML-based Domain-Specific Language (SOP-DSL), encoding the ordered, weighted step list with tolerances and bound prim paths.
- **Stage 4: Validation and Executable Task Generation:** A static validator checks the protocol specification for syntactic correctness, step weight normalization, and valid ordering references. The validated task configuration and protocol specification form the executable task for data collection and evaluation.

#### 3.3. Tianyi Humanoid Teleoperation and Data Collection

- **Humanoid Embodiment:** All demonstrations are collected using the Tianyi humanoid robot, an upper-body platform with dual 7-DOF arms and 6 active-DOF dexterous hands (BrainCo Revo 2). Its design matches an adult operator's workspace.
- **Manus-Glove and Isomorphic-Arm Teleoperation:** Demonstration trajectories are collected via a custom teleoperation system. Manus gloves capture operator finger joint angles and hand pose for direct kinematic mapping to Tianyi's dexterous hands. An isomorphic-arm setup mirrors Tianyi's arm kinematics, minimizing human-to-robot mapping gaps and enabling natural transfer of arm motions. This system refreshes at 90 Hz, supporting contact-rich manipulation.
- **Recorded Modalities:** Demonstrations include synchronized RGB observations from head-mounted and external cameras (1280x720, resized for policies) and proprioceptive states (2x7 arm joint positions, 2x6 hand joint angles, totaling 26 dimensions).

#### 3.4. Benchmark Design and Evaluation Protocol

Labimus defines a three-tier evaluation hierarchy with four perturbation conditions and specific metrics.

- **Task Suite:** The suite includes six atomic operations (door open, door close, grasp & place, tare press, tool pickup, scoop & weigh) and a seven-step solid-weighing workflow, all derived from SOPs and categorized by manipulation characteristics (e.g., contact type, arm configuration, dexterous-hand dependency, instrument interaction, precision target).
- **Three-Tier Evaluation Hierarchy:**
 - **Tier 1: Task Completion:** Evaluates atomic operations with binary success (did the intended state change occur?).
 - **Tier 2: Quantitative Precision:** Re-evaluates select Tier 1 operations (e.g., scoop & weigh, grasp & place) with continuous precision metrics, measuring deviation from SOP-specified tolerances (e.g., mass error $\le$ 0.001 g, position error $\le$ 15 mm). This tier quantifies the "precision gap."
 - **Tier 3: Long-Horizon Precision:** Evaluates the complete solid-weighing workflow, assessing compounding precision errors across sequential steps.
- **Evaluation Conditions:** Tasks are evaluated under four conditions:
 - **Standard:** Procedural layouts with randomized instrument/object placements (spatial distribution shift).
 - **+Lighting perturbation:** Randomized color temperature, intensity, and light source direction.
 - **+Texture perturbation:** Varied bench surface and background wall materials.
 - **+Combined perturbation:** All above perturbations applied simultaneously.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Task success rate (S):** Binary (0/1) task completion.
 - **Continuous precision error ($e_m, e_p$) and precision pass rate (P):** Fraction of episodes where task is completed *and* precision error is within tolerance ($\tau$). The gap ($S-P$) quantifies overestimation by binary success.
 - **Conditional precision pass rate ($P_{cond}$):** Precision quality exclusively among completed episodes, used for cross-tier comparisons.
 - **Step progress (SP):** SOP-weighted fraction of successfully completed protocol steps.
 - **Stage-level breakdown ($SP^{(k)}$):** Localizes failures to specific operational phases within multi-stage workflows.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted in Labimus evaluated several atomic operations across Tier 1 and Tier 2 under various perturbation conditions, using three representative policies: ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), and $\pi_0$.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks Evaluated:** Four atomic operations were assessed: Door open, Door close, Tare press (Tier 1, binary success), and Grasp & place (Tier 2, binary success and precision pass rate). Evaluation of other tasks (tool pickup, scoop & weigh) and the complete solid-weighing workflow (Tier 3) is noted as ongoing. Each task-condition pair involved 50 episodes across 3 random seeds (150 trials).
- **Baselines:** ACT (Transformer-based imitation learning), Diffusion Policy (diffusion-based imitation learning), and $\pi_0$ (vision-language-action foundation model) were used. All policies operated on the Tianyi humanoid with dexterous hands, sharing identical demonstration data and action spaces (26-dimensional joint positions). $\pi_0$ additionally conditioned on task instruction strings.
- **Training Protocol:** Baselines were trained on 100 teleoperation demonstrations per task in digital-twin scenes with fixed lighting, textures, and physical parameters. Testing used procedural layouts (Standard condition) with additional visual perturbations (lighting, texture, combined).
- **Observations:** RGB images (head-mounted, plus two external for grasp & place) and proprioceptive states (arm and hand joint angles).

#### 4.2. Results and Analysis

The experimental results revealed distinct patterns in task difficulty, baseline performance, and the crucial precision gap.

- **Task-Level Discrimination (Tier 1 Task Completion):**
 - A clear difficulty gradient was observed among the Tier 1 tasks. "Door open" consistently achieved the highest success rates (ACT: 56.7%, DP: 49.3%, $\pi_0$: 47.3%).
 - "Door close" showed moderate success rates, with $\pi_0$ performing notably higher (40.7%) than ACT (24.7%) and DP (6.7%).
 - "Tare press," which demands precise fingertip contact on a small button, resulted in near-zero success rates across all methods (ACT: 2.0%, DP: 0.0%, $\pi_0$: 0.0%). This indicates a shared challenge for current imitation learning and VLA methods in executing fine single-finger manipulation.

- **Baseline Differentiation:**
 - No single baseline consistently outperformed others across all tasks. ACT achieved the highest success for "Door open," while $\pi_0$ led for "Door close." The overall low success rates for "Tare press" suggest a common limitation rather than architecture-specific weaknesses.

- **Precision Gap (Tier 2 for ACT on Grasp & Place):**
 - For the "Grasp & place" task, ACT achieved a binary task success rate (S) of 5.3% $\pm$ 0.9%. However, its precision pass rate (P), which requires placement within $\pm$15 mm of the pan center, was 3.3% $\pm$ 1.9%.
 - This yielded a precision gap of 2.0 percentage points (S - P).
 - Among the 8 completed episodes across all seeds, only 5 satisfied the specified placement tolerance, resulting in a conditional precision pass rate ($P_{cond}$) of approximately 62.5%.
 - This finding quantitatively supports the paper's core assertion: a non-trivial fraction of "completed" manipulation tasks fail to meet the quantitative tolerances required for experimental validity, demonstrating that binary success metrics can overestimate true experimental validity.

#### 4.3. Robustness Analysis ( $\pi_0$ on Door Open)

- The robustness of the $\pi_0$ policy on "Door open" was evaluated under the four perturbation conditions.
- $\pi_0$ exhibited relative insensitivity to isolated visual perturbations. Performance under the "+Lighting" condition (41.3% $\pm$ 5.0%) and the "+Texture" condition (46.7% $\pm$ 4.1%) remained comparable to the "Standard" baseline (47.3% $\pm$ 7.4%).
- The "Combined" perturbation condition, which layered all visual variations simultaneously, resulted in the largest performance drop, reaching 40.0% $\pm$ 5.7%. This 7.3 percentage point decrease from the Standard condition suggests that compounded visual perturbations have a more substantial impact on VLA policy performance than individual perturbations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Labimus contributes to the fields of robotics and embodied AI by introducing a novel benchmark for humanoid dexterous manipulation in precision-critical laboratory environments.

The primary significance of this work lies in:
- **Addressing a critical gap:** Labimus is presented as the first benchmark specifically designed for humanoid dexterous manipulation in organic chemistry laboratories, covering operations that require high precision and complex physical interaction.
- **Exposing the precision gap:** The benchmark's multi-tiered evaluation protocol, which differentiates between binary task completion and quantitative experimental precision, reveals a consistent "precision gap." Policies that successfully complete a task may still fail to meet the specific quantitative tolerances required by scientific protocols. This finding highlights a fundamental disconnect that conventional binary success metrics cannot capture, providing a more granular understanding of robot performance in scientific contexts.
- **Providing a high-fidelity simulation environment:** The reconstruction of over 30 functionally faithful laboratory assets, combined with particle-based powder physics and closed-loop instrument readouts, creates a realistic simulation platform. This enables rigorous testing of embodied agents in scenarios involving granular materials, articulated instruments, and real-time measurement feedback.
- **Foundation for future development:** Labimus offers a testbed for developing reliable humanoid robots capable of scientific experimentation. It facilitates research into challenges such as fine-force powder transfer, milligram-level weighing, and contact-rich instrument interaction, which inherently demand high dexterity, coordination, and human-like embodiment.

The potential impact of Labimus includes:
- **Advancing laboratory automation:** By providing a structured evaluation framework for humanoid robots, Labimus can accelerate the development of autonomous systems capable of performing a wider range of complex and precise laboratory procedures, thereby extending automation beyond highly standardized tasks.
- **Catalyzing embodied AI research:** The benchmark encourages research into areas such as robust perception under varied conditions, fine motor control, multi-finger coordination, and closed-loop control based on instrument feedback, pushing the boundaries of embodied AI for scientific applications.
- **Improving sim-to-real transfer:** While the current version is simulation-based, its design for real-to-sim reconstruction lays a foundation for future sim-to-real validation, which is crucial for deploying robots in actual laboratories.
- **Broader scientific applications:** Although currently focused on organic chemistry, the framework is designed to be extensible to other scientific disciplines and more complex experimental workflows, potentially impacting fields such as materials science, biology, and pharmacology.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge that the current release (version 0) has several limitations, outlining a clear roadmap for future development:
- **Incomplete task evaluation:** Evaluation of some atomic operations (tool pickup, scoop & weigh) and the complete solid-weighing workflow (Tier 3) is ongoing.
- **Scope of tasks:** The current suite focuses on solid weighing. Future versions will expand to liquid handling, apparatus assembly, and multi-step synthetic procedures.
- **Simulation-only experiments:** Systematic sim-to-real transfer and real-world validation are identified as critical next milestones.
- **Single embodiment support:** The benchmark currently supports only the Tianyi humanoid; multi-embodiment support (alternative humanoid and dexterous-hand configurations) is planned for cross-platform comparison.

These limitations define a clear iterative development path to expand the benchmark's scope, complete its evaluation hierarchy, and validate its applicability in real-world scenarios.
