# A Corpus for Reasoning About Natural Language Grounded in Photographs

> [!summary] Summary
> The research introduces NLVR2, a dataset requiring compositional visual reasoning over natural language and real-world photographs, where captions are judged true or false against pairs of images. The dataset achieves high inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff’s α of 0.912) and reveals a substantial gap between human performance (96.1%) and state-of-the-art models (best at 53-55% accuracy).

## Problem

- Existing visual reasoning datasets often relied on synthetic images, synthetic language, or both, limiting linguistic expressivity and lexical diversity.
- Many datasets focused on simple object identification or restricted spatial relations, allowing models to perform well without deep compositional language understanding.
- Linguistic biases in prior datasets often permitted models to exploit superficial patterns rather than genuinely grounding reasoning in visual input.

## Method

- Developed **NLVR2**, a dataset of 107,292 examples comprising human-written natural language captions grounded in pairs of real-world web photographs.
- Employed a ==multi-stage crowdsourcing process== to collect images and sentences, with a design requiring each sentence to be true for two image pairs and false for two others within an 8-image set.
- Implemented a rigorous ==validation process== and ==dataset splitting strategy== to ensure high-quality, unbiased, and independent training, development, and test sets.

## Results

- The NLVR2 dataset contains **107,292** examples, **127,502** unique images, and **29,680** unique sentences, with a balanced distribution across true/false labels.
- Inter-annotator agreement on the dataset is high, with Krippendorff's α at **0.912** and human performance reaching **96.1%** (±2.6%).
- State-of-the-art visual reasoning models, including FiLM, achieved a maximum accuracy of **53-55%**, significantly underperforming human baselines and demonstrating the dataset's challenge.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - NLVR2 effectively mitigates linguistic biases by ensuring each unique sentence is associated with both true and false labels across different image contexts, forcing models to integrate both visual and linguistic information.
> - The dataset demands diverse and complex compositional reasoning skills, encompassing cardinality, quantifiers, coreference, comparisons, and spatial relations, significantly broadening linguistic diversity compared to prior benchmarks.
> - A substantial performance gap exists between human accuracy (96.1%) and the best-performing models (approximately 53-55%), indicating that current visual reasoning methods are insufficient for this challenging task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of NLVR2 is situated within the field of visual reasoning with natural language, an area focused on understanding compositional semantics by grounding linguistic expressions to visual elements such as objects, their properties, and relations within images. This capability is considered pertinent for human-computer interaction in visually complex environments, including robotic applications.

Prior research in language and vision (e.g., Antol et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2016) often concentrated on tasks like object property identification and limited spatial relations. Such resources, combined with data biases, often minimized the necessity for models to engage in compositional language understanding (Goyal et al., 2017). This limitation motivated the creation of datasets specifically designed to require compositional visual reasoning, such as NLVR (Suhr et al., 2017) and CLEVR (Johnson et al., 2017a,b). However, these datasets frequently rely on synthetic images, synthetic language, or both, which can restrict linguistic expressivity and lexical/semantic diversity.

NLVR2 addresses these identified limitations by offering a dataset that incorporates both human-written natural language and real-world photographs. This approach differentiates it from synthetic-data-based datasets while also distinguishing it from projects like CLEVR-Humans (Johnson et al., 2017b), which uses human-written paraphrases for synthetic images, and GQA (Hudson and Manning, 2019), which uses synthetic language for real-world photographs. The dataset's design, which pairs each sentence with multiple image contexts that yield different truth values, is aligned with recent efforts in visual question answering to counteract linguistic biases and compel models to integrate both visual and linguistic information effectively (e.g., Zhang et al., 2016; Goyal et al., 2017). The task of comparing and contrasting pairs of images in NLVR2 extends the multi-image setup employed in NLVR.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Natural Language Visual Reasoning for Real (NLVR2), a novel dataset designed to facilitate joint reasoning about natural language and real-world images. The core task in NLVR2 requires determining the truth value of a natural language caption concerning a pair of photographs.

The motivations and specific objectives behind the creation of NLVR2 are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Datasets:** The authors note that many existing visual reasoning datasets primarily focus on simple object identification or a restricted set of spatial relations. This simplicity, coupled with inherent biases in data, often allows models to perform well without fully grasping language compositionality. NLVR2 aims to overcome these issues by demanding more intricate reasoning.
- **Promoting Compositional Reasoning:** The dataset is specifically designed to elicit and require compositional visual reasoning, moving beyond rudimentary visual question-answering tasks. This includes reasoning about quantities, comparisons between objects or properties, and complex relations within and across image pairs.
- **Enhancing Semantic and Linguistic Diversity:** Unlike datasets utilizing synthetic images or synthetic language (e.g., CLEVR), NLVR2 employs real-world web photographs and human-written natural language captions. This is intended to yield a broader representation of linguistic phenomena, lexical diversity, and semantic richness that is characteristic of natural language.
- **Reducing Linguistic Biases:** A significant motivation is to mitigate unintentional linguistic biases often found in datasets. The data collection strategy in NLVR2 ensures that each unique sentence is associated with both "True" and "False" labels across different image pair contexts. This design aims to prevent models from exploiting superficial linguistic patterns to solve the task without genuinely grounding their reasoning in the visual input.
- **Utilizing Visually Rich Images:** The methodology emphasizes the selection of visually complex and detailed images that depict real-world scenes, often featuring multiple objects, activities, and rich environments. This is intended to support the generation of semantically diverse and challenging language.
- **Supporting Real-World Applications:** The compositional linguistic reasoning targeted by NLVR2 is presented as critical for practical applications in visually complex environments, such as natural language instruction following in robotics. While NLVR2 does not directly target an end-application, it aims to develop models capable of understanding this type of language.

In summary, the authors were motivated by the need for a robust benchmark that genuinely tests the compositional visual reasoning abilities of models, using ecologically valid natural language and real-world imagery, while systematically addressing dataset biases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for constructing NLVR2 involved a multi-stage crowdsourcing process designed to collect a large corpus of semantically rich descriptions grounded in image pairs, necessitating diverse types of reasoning.

#### 3.1. Image Collection

The initial step focused on identifying and acquiring suitable images that would support complex comparative and set-based reasoning.

- **Synset Identification:** From the ILSVRC2014 ImageNet challenge, 124 synsets (evenly distributed between animals and objects) were selected. These synsets were chosen because they frequently appear in visually rich contexts that could elicit complex language.
- **Query Generation and Image Retrieval:** For each synset, five search queries were generated using expansion heuristics (Table 1), such as adding numerical phrases (e.g., "two acorns"), hypernyms, similar words, or common activities (e.g., "beagles eating"). These queries were submitted to Google Images. For each of the top five results, the "Similar Images" tool was used to retrieve seven additional similar images, resulting in sets of eight similar images.
- **Image Pruning (Crowdsourcing Task 1):** Crowdworkers reviewed the downloaded image sets and removed low-quality images. Criteria for removal included incorrect loading, inappropriate content, non-realistic artwork, collages, or images not containing an instance of the corresponding synset. Sets with fewer than eight images after this stage were discarded.
- **Set Construction (Crowdsourcing Task 2):** This task further refined the image sets. Workers identified and removed duplicate images. They then marked "interesting" images based on criteria such as containing multiple instances of a synset, instances interacting with other objects, instances performing activities, or displaying diverse objects/features (Table 2). Sets with fewer than three interesting images were discarded. The remaining images were sorted by interestingness and then similarity, with the top eight forming the final image set for sentence writing.

#### 3.2. Sentence Writing

This stage involved generating natural language captions for the selected image sets.

- **Task Design:** Each set of eight images was randomly divided into four pairs. Crowdworkers were instructed to select two of these four pairs and write a single English sentence that was *True* for the two selected pairs and *False* for the remaining two pairs. This setup aimed to encourage compositional language by requiring workers to identify similarities and differences across image pairs.
- **Guidelines:** Specific instructions were provided to guide workers in crafting semantically rich sentences and to avoid simple descriptions or subjective content. Workers were discouraged from subjective opinions, discussing photograph properties, mentioning text within images, or writing sentences that could be trivially true/false (e.g., simple object existence if the object was only present in selected pairs). The goal was to promote language requiring joint reasoning.

#### 3.3. Validation

To ensure the quality and accuracy of the generated examples, a validation step was implemented.

- **Example Creation:** Each sentence written by a worker was paired with each of the four image pairs from the sentence-writing task, creating four distinct examples.
- **Truth Value Labeling:** Independent crowdworkers were presented with each example (sentence + image pair) and asked to label it as True, False, or Nonsensical.
- **Data Retention:** Examples were retained if the validator's label matched the initial intended truth value (based on the sentence-writer's selection). Nonsensical sentences or those with conflicting labels were removed.

#### 3.4. Dataset Splitting

The validated dataset was divided into training, development, and test sets to facilitate model evaluation.

- **Split Strategy:** A random 20% of the validated examples were allocated for development and testing. A crucial constraint was that examples originating from the same initial set of eight images were prevented from appearing across different splits, ensuring independent evaluation.
- **Quality Control for Dev/Test:** For development and test examples, four additional validation judgments were collected to assess agreement and human performance. Examples with two or more disagreements among these additional judgments were removed to create high-quality, reliable splits.
- **Final Splits:** The data was then organized into a training set, a development set, a public test set (Test-P), and an unreleased test set (Test-U), with approximately equal sizes for the dev and test sets.

#### 3.5. Data Collection Management

A tiered system with bonuses and qualification tasks was implemented to manage and incentivize crowdworkers, encouraging adherence to guidelines and the production of linguistically diverse sentences.

#### 3.6. Data Analysis

The collected data underwent both quantitative and qualitative analysis.

- **Agreement Metrics:** Krippendorff’s α and Fleiss’ κ were calculated on the development and test sets using the multiple validation judgments to quantify inter-annotator agreement.
- **Linguistic Analysis:** 800 sentences from the development set were qualitatively analyzed for the presence of various semantic and syntactic phenomena (e.g., cardinality, quantifiers, spatial relations, coreference, coordination, attachment ambiguities). This was compared against similar analyses for VQA, GQA, and NLVR.
- **Quantitative Metrics:** Sentence length distribution, vocabulary size, and synset distribution were analyzed.
- **Image Pair Reasoning:** A subset of sentences was analyzed to categorize the types of reasoning explicitly required over the pair of images (e.g., property holds in exactly one, both, or requires comparison).

#### 3.7. Evaluation Systems

Several baselines and state-of-the-art visual reasoning methods were evaluated on NLVR2 to establish performance benchmarks.

- **Baselines:** Included MAJORITY (predicting the most common label), TEXT (RNN-based text-only), IMAGE (CNN-based image-only), CNN+RNN (concatenating text and image features), and MAXENT (a feature-based classifier using detected objects and n-grams).
- **Advanced Models:** Three types of visual reasoning models were tested: End-to-End Neural Module Networks (N2NMN), Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM), and Memory, Attention, and Composition (MAC). These models were adapted to process pairs of images by concatenating their extracted features.
- **Metrics:** Model performance was assessed using per-example accuracy and consistency (the proportion of unique sentences for which all associated image pairs were correctly predicted).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded a new dataset and provided insights into its characteristics and the performance of current visual reasoning models.

#### 4.1. Dataset Statistics and Characteristics

- **Scale:** NLVR2 comprises 107,292 examples, 127,502 unique images, and 29,680 unique sentences.
- **Data Distribution:** Each unique sentence is paired with an average of 3.6 image pairs. The dataset is balanced, with the majority class (True) having 50.8-51.4% representation across splits.
- **Splits:** The dataset is divided into a training set (86,373 examples), a development set (6,982 examples), a public test set (Test-P, 6,967 examples), and an unreleased test set (Test-U, 6,970 examples).
- **Cost:** The mean cost per unique sentence was $0.65, and the mean cost per example was $0.18.

#### 4.2. Data Analysis

- **Inter-Annotator Agreement:** After removing low-agreement examples, Krippendorff’s α was 0.912 and Fleiss’ κ was 0.889, indicating high agreement among annotators.
- **Language Diversity:**
 - **Vocabulary Size:** NLVR2 features a vocabulary of 7,457 word types, which is notably larger than NLVR (262 types), reflecting increased lexical diversity.
 - **Sentence Length:** Sentences average 14.8 tokens in length, longer than VQA, GQA, and CLEVR-Humans, and comparable to MSCOCO and CLEVR, with a longer tail indicating more complex sentences than NLVR.
 - **Linguistic Phenomena:** A qualitative analysis of 800 development sentences demonstrated that NLVR2 contains a broad representation of semantic and syntactic phenomena (Table 5).
 - High prevalence was observed for hard cardinality (41.1%), soft cardinality (23.6%), existential quantifiers (23.6%), universal quantifiers (16.8%), coordination (33.3%), coreference (14.6%), spatial relations (49%), comparatives (8%), presupposition (20.6%), negation (9.6%), and preposition attachment ambiguity (11.5%).
 - Compared to NLVR, NLVR2 shows less hard cardinality but more soft cardinality, universal quantifiers, coordination, coreference, and comparatives. NLVR2 generally exhibits broader linguistic diversity than VQA and GQA across several categories.
- **Image Pair Reasoning:** Analysis indicated that sentences commonly require various types of reasoning across the image pairs (Table 10). For instance, 11% required a property in exactly one image, 19% in at least one, 11% in both, 26.5% referred explicitly to the left/right image, and 6% involved comparisons between images.

#### 4.3. Human Performance

Human performance, estimated from additional validation judgments, averaged 96.1% (±2.6) after pruning low-agreement examples. This provides a high benchmark for automated systems.

#### 4.4. Evaluation System Results

Performance on NLVR2 was measured by accuracy and consistency (Table 6).

- **Baselines:**
 - MAJORITY performance (predicting 'True') ranged from 50.8% to 51.4% accuracy, indicating a balanced dataset and minimal prior biases.
 - TEXT-only (RNN) and IMAGE-only (CNN) baselines performed similarly to MAJORITY (50.8-51.4% and 51.6-51.9% accuracy, respectively). This suggests that neither modality alone is sufficient to solve the task, compelling joint reasoning.
 - The CNN+RNN baseline achieved 52.4-53.4% accuracy and 11.0-12.2% consistency.
 - The MAXENT feature-based baseline was the highest-performing baseline, with 53.5-54.8% accuracy and 11.4-12.0% consistency.
- **Visual Reasoning Methods:**
 - FiLM (Perez et al., 2018) achieved the highest performance among the visual reasoning methods, with 52.1-53.0% accuracy and 9.8-10.6% consistency on the test sets.
 - MAC (Hudson and Manning, 2018) showed 50.8-51.4% accuracy and 11.0-11.4% consistency.
 - N2NMN (Hu et al., 2017) variants generally performed poorly, with accuracies ranging from 50.0% to 51.5% and consistencies from 2.3% to 9.8% on the development and test sets. N2NMN-RL specifically struggled to fit the training data.
- **Overall Challenge:** Despite high training accuracy for most complex models (e.g., CNN+RNN at 94.3%, MAC at 87.4%), their generalization performance on the development and test sets remained low (around 50-55% accuracy). All tested models performed substantially below human performance (96.1%), indicating that NLVR2 poses a significant challenge for current visual reasoning methods.
- **Contextualized Embeddings:** Experiments with BERT contextualized word embeddings paired with FiLM showed no observable benefit.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the NLVR2 corpus carries several significant implications and potential impacts for the research community in natural language processing and computer vision:

- **New Challenging Benchmark:** NLVR2 establishes a demanding benchmark for joint reasoning about natural language and real-world photographs. The substantial gap between human performance (96.1%) and the best-performing models (around 53-55% accuracy) indicates that current state-of-the-art visual reasoning systems struggle with this task, highlighting areas for future research and model development.
- **Addresses Limitations of Prior Datasets:** By combining visually complex, natural photographs with human-written natural language, NLVR2 moves beyond the limitations of synthetic datasets (like CLEVR) that have bounded expressivity and limited lexical/semantic diversity, and also enhances the realism compared to datasets with human language on synthetic images (like CLEVR-Humans). It provides a more ecologically valid environment for studying visual reasoning.
- **Mitigation of Linguistic Biases:** The data collection procedure, which explicitly pairs each unique sentence with both "True" and "False" image contexts, effectively minimizes unintended linguistic biases. This forces models to genuinely ground their reasoning in both modalities rather than relying on superficial linguistic cues, thereby promoting the development of more robust and generalizable visual-language understanding systems.
- **Promotion of Compositional Semantic Understanding:** The qualitative analysis confirms that NLVR2 requires diverse and complex compositional reasoning skills, including numerical expressions, quantifiers, coreference resolution, comparisons, spatial relations, and negation. This pushes the field to develop models capable of deeper semantic understanding and compositionality, moving beyond simpler tasks like object detection or attribute recognition.
- **Relevance for Grounded Language Applications:** The type of compositional reasoning required in NLVR2 is directly relevant to real-world applications such as robotic agents following complex natural language instructions in cluttered environments. While NLVR2 is not an application-oriented dataset, it serves as a foundational resource for building models with such capabilities.
- **Resource for Research Community:** The release of the training, development, and public test sets, along with evaluation scripts and a leaderboard, provides an accessible resource for researchers globally. This fosters comparative studies, encourages innovation in model architectures, and facilitates the collective advancement of visual reasoning and grounded language understanding. The availability of performance breakdowns by linguistic categories further enables fine-grained analysis of model strengths and weaknesses.
