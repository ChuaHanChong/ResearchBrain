# Towards Motion Turing Test: Evaluating Human-Likeness in Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the "Motion Turing Test" framework and the Human-Humanoid Motion (HHMotion) dataset to objectively evaluate the human-likeness of humanoid robot movements by disentangling motion from visual appearance using SMPL-X representations. It also develops PTR-Net, a specialized model that accurately predicts human-likeness scores, outperforming general-purpose Vision-Language Models.

## Problem

- A lack of a standardized, human-centered method to quantitatively evaluate the "human-likeness" of humanoid robot motions, beyond task-oriented metrics.
- The absence of a comprehensive dataset containing both human and diverse humanoid robot motions, specifically annotated for perceptual human-likeness.
- Current motion generation research prioritizes technical metrics (e.g., efficiency, accuracy) but often overlooks subjective human perception of naturalness and fluidity.

## Method

- Proposed the "==Motion Turing Test==" framework to evaluate human-likeness based solely on kinematic information (SMPL-X poses), removing visual appearance bias.
- Created the Human-Humanoid Motion (HHMotion) dataset, comprising 1,000 5-second SMPL-X motion clips (500 human, 500 humanoid) across 15 action categories, extensively annotated by 25 human evaluators with 0-5 Likert scale human-likeness scores.
- Developed PTR-Net, a Pose-Temporal Regression Network, as a specialized baseline model for automated human-likeness assessment, utilizing a temporal encoder and spatial-temporal graph convolution.

## Results

- PTR-Net achieved significantly lower MAE (**0.5813**) and RMSE (**0.7926**), and higher Spearman’s ρ (**0.6841**) compared to VLM-based and other motion-centric baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness in aligning with human judgments.
- Humanoid motions, particularly from real robots, consistently received lower human-likeness scores than human motions in the HHMotion dataset.
- The ablation study confirmed the critical contribution of PTR-Net's temporal encoder and attention pooling, with the full model achieving optimal performance and demonstrating generalization by accurately scoring an unseen robot's motion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human evaluators consistently identify a perceivable gap between human and humanoid robot motions, even when appearance cues are removed, with real robots typically performing less human-like than simulated ones.
> - Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like Gemini 2.5 Pro and Qwen3-VL-Plus are largely ineffective for nuanced motion human-likeness assessment without domain-specific training.
> - The human-likeness of robot motions varies significantly across action categories; dynamic actions (e.g., jumping, boxing) show the largest discrepancies, while smoother actions (e.g., walking, dancing) exhibit closer alignment to human performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen substantial advancements in developing motion generation and control systems, enabling robots to perform increasingly complex and fluid movements. This progress has led to robots exhibiting actions such as walking, running, dancing, and even gymnastics with notable fluidity. However, a significant gap exists in the systematic and quantitative evaluation of how "human-like" these robot motions are perceived by human observers.

Existing research in human and humanoid motion generation (e.g., using diffusion models, adversarial frameworks, imitation learning, and reinforcement learning with physics-based control) has primarily focused on achieving technical metrics like task completion rate, efficiency, robustness, or end-effector trajectory accuracy. These metrics do not inherently capture the subjective human perception of naturalness, fluidity, or anthropomorphism, which are critical for effective human-robot interaction.

Prior motion datasets, such as AMASS and Human3.6M, predominantly feature human movements and lack data on humanoid robots or human-likeness scores. While some efforts, like the LAFAN1 Retargeting Dataset, involve transferring human motions to robots, a notable "sim-to-real" gap often persists, and direct human-likeness evaluation remains unaddressed. Furthermore, methods for 3D human pose estimation (HPE) have progressed, utilizing parametric models like SMPL-X to reconstruct body pose and shape from visual inputs. However, robust 3D pose estimation for diverse humanoid robots, especially when aiming for direct comparison with human motion, presents challenges due to varying robot morphologies and kinematic constraints.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing the "Motion Turing Test" and introducing the Human-Humanoid Motion (HHMotion) dataset. By disentangling motion from visual appearance using SMPL-X representations and collecting extensive human annotations, the work establishes a new benchmark for evaluating human-likeness. This framework provides a standardized, perceptually-driven evaluation method, complementing existing task-oriented metrics and offering a pathway for the development of more human-like robot motions. The proposed baseline model, PTR-Net, further attempts to automate this subjective evaluation, positioning it as a tool for both motion generation assessment and as a potential reward function in reinforcement learning for humanoid control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a standardized and human-centered framework for evaluating the human-likeness of motions performed by humanoid robots. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives and motivations:

- **Propose the Motion Turing Test:** The core motivation is to create an intuitive, motion-centric evaluation inspired by Alan Turing's original concept. The goal is to determine if human observers can reliably distinguish between a humanoid robot's motion and a human's motion, using only kinematic information and without visual cues related to appearance. This test aims to move beyond task-oriented performance metrics to directly assess the perceptual quality of robot movements.

- **Develop the Human-Humanoid Motion (HHMotion) Dataset:** A critical motivation was the absence of a comprehensive dataset that includes both human and humanoid robot motions, specifically annotated for human-likeness. Existing datasets focus either exclusively on humans or lack the necessary subjective evaluations. The HHMotion dataset aims to fill this void by providing:
 - Diverse motion sequences from real-world humanoid robots (representing the state-of-the-art), simulated robots, and human subjects.
 - A standardized representation (SMPL-X) for all motions to eliminate the influence of visual appearance, ensuring that evaluations focus purely on kinematics.
 - Large-scale human annotations of human-likeness scores (0-5 Likert scale) to serve as ground truth for training and evaluating automatic assessment models.
 - Specific inclusion of human motions imitating robot behaviors to create ambiguous samples, further challenging the evaluation framework.

- **Analyze Current Humanoid Motion Limitations:** The researchers were motivated to quantitatively understand the current state of humanoid robot motion. By collecting and analyzing human-likeness scores, they sought to identify specific types of actions or motion characteristics where robots significantly deviate from human performance. This analysis would provide insights for future research and development in motion generation.

- **Formulate an Automatic Human-Likeness Evaluation Task:** Human annotation is resource-intensive. The motivation here was to formulate the subjective human-likeness evaluation as a quantitative regression task. This would enable the development of automated models capable of predicting human-likeness scores from motion data, thereby offering a scalable and objective alternative to human evaluators. Such an automated system could accelerate robot development cycles.

- **Propose and Benchmark a Baseline Model (PTR-Net):** Recognizing that advanced large vision-language models (VLMs) might not be optimally suited for the nuanced task of motion human-likeness assessment without specific training, the authors were motivated to develop a specialized, motion-centric baseline. PTR-Net aims to demonstrate that a model specifically designed for temporal and spatial kinematic patterns can effectively predict human judgments, and to establish a performance benchmark against contemporary, general-purpose VLM methods. This also serves to validate the necessity of domain-specific architectures for this particular challenge.

- **Provide Tools for Future Research:** Ultimately, the motivation includes making the dataset, code, and benchmark publicly available to foster community-wide research, accelerate progress in humanoid motion generation, and provide a valuable tool for evaluating and improving human-robot interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces the Motion Turing Test framework and develops the Human-Humanoid Motion (HHMotion) dataset, alongside a baseline model (PTR-Net) for automatic human-likeness evaluation.

#### 3.1. Motion Turing Test Framework

The Motion Turing Test conceptually evaluates whether human observers can distinguish between a robot's motion and a human's motion based solely on kinematic information, thus excluding appearance cues. The methodology involves:
- **Appearance Disentanglement:** All video data, from both humans and humanoid robots, is converted into 3D body pose sequences using the SMPL-X parametric body model. This process removes visual appearance (e.g., robot metallic shells, human clothing) to ensure evaluators focus purely on the quality and naturalness of the motion.
- **Human Annotation:** Human evaluators are presented with these appearance-agnostic pose sequences and asked to rate their human-likeness.

#### 3.2. HHMotion Dataset Construction

The HHMotion dataset comprises 1,000 5-second motion clips (500 human, 500 humanoid) across 15 action categories, totaling 21.7 hours of raw video data.

- **Humanoid Robot Data Collection:**
 - **Real-world:** Over 21 hours of video footage from major international events (World Robot Conference, World Artificial Intelligence Conference, World Humanoid Robot Games) were collected. This data features 11 robot models from 28 different teams.
 - **Simulated:** Motion sequences from the LAFAN1 Retargeting Dataset, visualized and recorded in a simulation environment, were also included.
 - **Processing:** Raw videos were segmented into 5-second clips, and low-quality clips (low-resolution, occluded, truncated) were removed. This resulted in 257 real humanoid clips and 243 simulated robot clips.

- **Human Motion Data Collection:**
 - **Volunteers:** 10 human subjects performed actions corresponding to the robot categories, both indoors and outdoors. These were segmented into 5-second clips (365 clips).
 - **Internet Sources:** Additional human motion videos were collected from YouTube to enhance diversity (135 clips).
 - **Human Imitating Humanoids:** A subset of human subjects intentionally mimicked robot-like movements to create ambiguous samples, aiming to challenge the distinction between human and robotic motion.

- **Human-Humanoid Pose Estimation:**
 - To achieve appearance disentanglement, all raw video clips were converted into SMPL-X representations.
 - Multiple state-of-the-art human pose estimation (HPE) and robot pose estimation (RPE) methods were compared.
 - GVHMR, originally designed for human motion, was selected due to its stability, temporal coherence, and generalization capability across both human and diverse humanoid subjects.
 - All reconstructed SMPL-X sequences underwent manual cross-validation to filter out noisy or failed samples, ensuring high quality and temporal smoothness.

- **Human-likeness Scoring:**
 - **Annotators:** 30 human evaluators were recruited to score each of the 1,000 SMPL-X motion sequences.
 - **Rating Scale:** A 0-5 Likert scale was used, where 0 indicated "completely inhuman-like motion" (robotic) and 5 indicated "indistinguishable from human motion" (completely human-like).
 - **Evaluation Focus:** Annotators were instructed to evaluate motions based on posture, rhythm, and coordination.
 - **Procedure:** Motion clips were randomized and shuffled, concealing their source (human or robot) to prevent bias. Each annotator spent approximately one minute per video, totaling over 500 hours of annotation.
 - **Quality Control:** An Inter-Annotator Consistency (IAC) check was performed, leading to the exclusion of 5 inconsistent annotators, retaining 25 evaluators for the final dataset.

#### 3.3. Robot Motion Turing Test Benchmark and PTR-Net Baseline

To enable automated evaluation, the task was formulated as a quantitative regression problem: predict a continuous human-likeness score (0-5) from a motion sequence.

- **Pose-Temporal Regression Network (PTR-Net):**
 - **Input:** Normalized SMPL-X pose sequences (local root coordinate frame).
 - **Architecture:**
 - **Temporal Encoder:** A two-layer bidirectional LSTM to capture long-range temporal dependencies in the motion.
 - **Spatial–Temporal Graph Convolution (ST-GCN):** Stacked ST-GCN blocks process the temporally encoded features, interpreting the human body as a graph. It alternates spatial graph convolution (to capture joint coordination) and temporal convolution (to capture motion evolution). A parameter-free adjacency design allows for adaptive feature aggregation.
 - **Attention Pooling and Regression Head:** A temporal attention module highlights significant motion segments, and a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) regressor outputs the final scalar human-likeness score.
 - **Training Objective:** Optimized using an L2 regression loss between predicted and human-annotated scores, combined with a regularization term (L_reg) to promote smoothness and stability in predicted scores over time.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics and Baselines

- **Metrics:** Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Spearman’s Rank Correlation (ρ) were used to quantify the alignment between model predictions and human judgments.
- **Comparison Baselines:**
 - **VLM-based:** Gemini 2.5 Pro and Qwen3-vl-plus were tested with various prompting strategies (Direct Evaluation, Context-Guided Evaluation, Prototype-Driven Evaluation, Direct Evaluation Chain-of-Thought, and Posture-Aware Chain-of-Thought) using rendered SMPL-X videos as input.
 - **Motion-centric:** MotionBERT (both frozen backbone and fine-tuned) and a lightweight Transformer-based model were used as stronger motion-specific baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. HHMotion Dataset Analysis and Human-likeness Scores

- **Overall Gap:** Analysis of human annotations revealed that human actions consistently received higher human-likeness scores than humanoid actions (refer to Fig. 5, left), indicating a perceivable gap between current robot capabilities and natural human movement.
- **Simulation vs. Real:** Humanoid actions performed in simulated environments generally received higher human-likeness scores compared to those from real humanoid robots (refer to Fig. 5, right), highlighting the persistent sim-to-real challenge.
- **Action Category Discrepancies:**
 - **Largest Differences:** Dynamic actions requiring high-frequency coordination and rapid limb transitions, such as jumping, boxing, running, ping-pong, and kicking ball, showed the largest score discrepancies between humans and robots. Humanoids struggled with these complex, reactive movements.
 - **Smallest Differences:** Smoother, more cyclic, or less dynamic actions like standing, sitting, skiing, walking, and dancing exhibited closer human-humanoid score alignment. Robots demonstrated reasonable fidelity in reproducing structured or rhythmically repetitive movements.
- **Conclusion:** Human evaluators could distinguish most humanoid motions from natural human movements, even with appearance cues removed.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Results of Automatic Human-likeness Assessment

- **PTR-Net Performance:** The proposed PTR-Net consistently outperformed all other baselines across the evaluation metrics. It achieved the lowest MAE (0.5813) and RMSE (0.7926) and the highest Spearman’s ρ (0.6841), demonstrating its effectiveness in aligning with human judgments.
- **VLM Limitations:** Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs), including Gemini 2.5 Pro and Qwen3-VL-Plus, exhibited significant deviations from human judgments. Even with sophisticated prompting strategies (e.g., PA-CoT), Gemini 2.5 Pro yielded substantially higher MAE and RMSE and much lower ρ values compared to PTR-Net. Qwen3-VL-Plus showed even less sensitivity, often providing nearly identical predictions. This indicates that current VLMs are inadequate for the specific, nuanced task of assessing motion human-likeness without task-specific training.
- **Room for Improvement:** An RMSE of 0.80 suggests that while PTR-Net is effective, there is still scope for further refinement in automatically predicting human-likeness.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Evaluation

- **Alignment with Human Judgment:** Representative examples illustrated that PTR-Net’s predictions closely aligned with human-annotated scores. For human actions, it accurately captured fluidity and coordination, assigning high scores. For humanoid actions, it correctly identified mechanical rigidity and rhythmic discontinuities, resulting in lower scores (refer to Fig. 8).
- **Interpretability:** The model appeared to capture interpretable motion cues such as fluency, coordination, and balance.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

- **Component Contributions:** The ablation study confirmed the contribution of each component of PTR-Net (refer to Tab. 4).
 - Removing the **Temporal Encoder** led to the most significant degradation in performance (increased MAE and RMSE, decreased ρ), highlighting the importance of capturing long-range temporal dependencies.
 - Disabling **Attention Pooling** also reduced correlation with human ratings, indicating its role in integrating global temporal context.
 - The **L_reg regularization term** contributed to more stable training and improved score consistency.
- **Full Model Efficacy:** The full PTR-Net model achieved the optimal balance across all metrics, validating its collaborative design.

#### 4.5. Discussion on Humans Imitating Humanoids

- **Ambiguity in Evaluation:** Analysis of the human-imitating-humanoid subset showed that human ratings exhibited partial overlap between imitating humans and actual humanoids. PTR-Net’s predictions largely mirrored these distributions (refer to Fig. 9).
- **Limitations of Kinematics:** This finding suggests that when humans intentionally mimic mechanical rigidity, kinematic cues alone may become insufficient for reliable discrimination. It implies that true human-likeness might encompass dimensions beyond mere smoothness and coordination, such as underlying intentionality and adaptability, which remain challenging for both generation and assessment.

#### 4.6. Out-of-Distribution Evaluation

- **Generalization:** PTR-Net demonstrated robustness and generalization capability by accurately predicting the human-likeness score for an unseen humanoid robot (XPeng IRON), with a prediction of 4.25 closely matching the human annotation average of 4.36 (refer to Fig. 10). This indicates the consistency and reliability of the learned scoring mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a standardized framework for evaluating humanoid robot motion, providing both a novel conceptual tool and practical resources for the robotics and AI communities.

- **Standardized Human-Centered Evaluation:** The proposal of the "Motion Turing Test" and the creation of the HHMotion dataset establish a new, perceptually-driven benchmark for assessing the human-likeness of humanoid robot movements. This moves beyond traditional task-oriented metrics to address the subjective quality of motion, which is crucial for human-robot interaction and acceptance. The public release of the dataset, code, and benchmark will enable reproducible and comparable research across the field.

- **Guidance for Robot Motion Development:** The detailed analysis of human-likeness scores across various action categories offers specific insights into current limitations. Identifying actions like jumping, boxing, and running as areas where robots significantly lag human performance provides clear targets for future research in motion generation and control algorithms. Conversely, categories like walking and standing, where robot performance is comparatively closer to human, indicate areas of relative strength. This diagnostic capability can guide developers in optimizing their systems more effectively towards human-like fluidity and coordination.

- **Automated Evaluation and Feedback System:** The development of PTR-Net demonstrates the feasibility of automating the assessment of motion human-likeness. This automated tool can significantly reduce the need for labor-intensive human evaluations, accelerating research cycles. Furthermore, PTR-Net's ability to quantitatively predict human-likeness scores means it can serve as an objective evaluation metric for new motion generation techniques. Its potential application as a reward model in reinforcement learning (RL) signifies a substantial impact, allowing RL agents to directly learn to synthesize more human-like motions, integrating human perception into the learning loop.

- **Revealing Limitations of General AI Models:** The finding that even advanced large vision-language models (VLMs) like Gemini 2.5 Pro and Qwen3-VL-Plus struggle with this specific task, even with elaborate prompting, highlights the necessity of specialized, motion-centric architectures. This suggests that while VLMs are powerful generalists, nuanced perceptual tasks in specific domains still require tailored approaches, guiding future research into more specialized AI for motion analysis.

- **Advancing Human-Robot Interaction:** By providing a means to quantify and improve the human-likeness of robot motions, this work contributes directly to enhancing the naturalness and effectiveness of human-robot interaction. Robots that move more naturally are likely to be perceived as more capable, trustworthy, and socially acceptable, fostering greater integration into human environments.

- **New Research Avenues:** The observation from the "human imitating humanoids" subset, indicating that kinematic cues alone may be insufficient to fully distinguish between intentionally rigid human movements and actual robot movements, opens new avenues for research. It suggests the need to explore more abstract dimensions of human motion, such as intentionality, adaptiveness, and context, in future evaluation frameworks and generation models.
