# Enhancing Visual Grounding for GUI Agents via Self-Evolutionary Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> SE-GUI is a reinforcement learning framework that enhances visual grounding for GUI agents using a self-evolutionary mechanism and dense point rewards. It achieves 47.3% accuracy on the ScreenSpot-Pro benchmark, outperforming larger models while utilizing only 3,018 high-quality training samples.

## Problem

- Existing GUI agents trained with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) struggle with data efficiency and generalization, particularly in complex, high-resolution environments.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches for GUI grounding face sparse reward issues, where binary success/failure signals impede effective early-stage training.
- The quality of initial training data for GUI tasks is often low due to noisy annotations and weak alignment, negatively impacting model learning.

## Method

- A rigorous three-fold ==data curation== process filters 300,000 samples down to 3,018 high-quality "seed" examples (**SE-GUI**-3k) for initial training.
- A dense point reward mechanism, combined with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), provides continuous feedback during RL training, allowing learning from imprecise predictions.
- A self-evolutionary reinforcement fine-tuning (SE-RFT) loop iteratively refines the model by using its own attention maps to filter low-quality training signals.

## Results

- SE-GUI-**7B** achieved **47.3%** accuracy on the ScreenSpot-Pro benchmark, surpassing the prior state-of-the-art UI-TARS-**72B** (**38.1%**) by **24.2%** while using significantly fewer parameters and less training data.
- The framework demonstrated strong generalization across other benchmarks, achieving **88.2%** on ScreenSpot and **90.3%** on ScreenSpot-v2, and outperforming baselines like GPT-4o and UGround-V1 in zero-shot agent evaluation tasks on AndroidControl and OmniACT.
- Ablation studies confirmed the efficacy of the components, showing the dense point reward alone led to a **4.21%** improvement, and the self-evolutionary process consistently improved performance across iterative stages.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-quality, meticulously curated seed data, even in small quantities, significantly improves the effectiveness of reinforcement learning for GUI agents.
> - Dense, continuous reward signals based on prediction proximity are crucial for guiding models to achieve precise localization in complex visual environments, overcoming the limitations of sparse rewards.
> - A self-evolutionary loop, where the model leverages its own attention maps to filter training data, enables continuous performance improvement and enhances the quality of learning signals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Evolution of GUI Agents

Graphical User Interface (GUI) agents are autonomous AI systems designed to understand and execute user commands by interacting with graphical interfaces, much like a human user would. Historically, GUI automation relied on programmatic agents that leveraged API calls or direct code access. However, with the rise of large foundation models, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), GUI agents have evolved to process visual information directly. This shift allows for greater flexibility and generalizability across diverse platforms (desktop, web, mobile), as agents can "see" and interpret the interface rather than relying on underlying structural data (like HTML or accessibility trees) alone. Pioneering systems like AppAgent, CogAgent, UI-TARS, and AgentS2 have significantly advanced GUI comprehension and interaction precision.

#### 1.2. Role of Reinforcement Learning (RL)

Initially, many successful GUI agents, such as UGround and OS-Atlas, were developed using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). SFT involves training models on large datasets of meticulously labeled (instruction, UI element, action) pairs. While effective in simpler scenarios, SFT faces two significant limitations, especially in complex, high-resolution professional environments:
1. **Data Dependency:** It requires vast volumes of diverse and high-quality labeled data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect.
2. **Weak Generalization:** Models fine-tuned via SFT often struggle to generalize to unseen interfaces or complex, nuanced tasks, exhibiting poor performance in "professional settings" with high visual information density.

Reinforcement Learning offers a promising alternative. RL allows models to learn from structured and incremental feedback (rewards) rather than relying solely on pre-labeled data. This paradigm is particularly appealing for GUI agents because it can guide the model towards more precise grounding and action execution by rewarding desired behaviors. Recent works like UI-R1 and GUI-R1 have begun to explore the potential of Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) in GUI tasks, demonstrating its efficacy even with limited data.

#### 1.3. Current Challenges and Limitations

Despite the promise of RL, existing approaches, including early RFT methods for GUI agents, face their own set of challenges:
1. **Sparse Rewards:** Many RL-based methods often rely on binary (0/1) rewards (success or failure). This sparsity impedes early-stage training, as incorrect predictions commonly receive identical zero rewards, leading to uniform advantage estimates and limited gradient information for effective optimization. The model struggles to learn from "near misses."
2. **Training Data Quality:** While RL can reduce the *quantity* of labeled data needed, the *quality* of the initial "seed" data remains critical. GUI datasets, often collected through automated pipelines, can be noisy, containing inaccurate bounding boxes or vague instructions that do not visually correspond to UI elements. This noise can mislead RL training.
3. **Precise Localization:** In complex, high-resolution interfaces, achieving precise grounding (identifying the exact UI element) is difficult. Current models often struggle with either roughly identifying the region but failing to localize precisely, or completely overlooking the relevant area due to weak alignment between instruction and ground truth.

This paper positions itself directly to address these limitations, proposing a framework that leverages RL to enhance visual grounding by tackling sparse rewards and ensuring data quality through a self-evolutionary mechanism.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enhance visual grounding for GUI agents**, specifically to improve their ability to accurately identify and localize interface elements based on natural language instructions. The authors are particularly motivated to achieve this in **complex, high-resolution, professional environments**, where existing methods, particularly Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), exhibit limitations in data efficiency and generalization.

The core motivations and specific problems the paper aims to solve are:

- **Overcoming SFT Limitations:** To move beyond the limitations of SFT, which demands vast, diverse datasets and struggles with generalization in nuanced, high-resolution settings.
- **Addressing Sparse Rewards in RL:** To mitigate the issue of sparse (binary) reward signals in conventional RL for GUI agents, which hinder effective early-stage training by providing insufficient gradient information.
- **Improving Training Data Quality for RL:** To recognize and address the problem of noisy, low-quality training data, which is prevalent in automatically collected GUI datasets and can negatively impact model learning. The authors found that model failures often stem from weak alignment between instructions and ground truth locations, motivating a focus on data quality.
- **Enhancing Localization Precision:** To enable the model to not only identify the general target region but also localize it precisely, even for small or visually subtle UI elements in complex interfaces.

By addressing these challenges, the authors aim to develop a more robust, data-efficient, and generalizable GUI agent framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named **SE-GUI (Self-Evolutionary GUI)**, integrates three core strategies to enhance visual grounding: seed data curation, a dense policy gradient mechanism, and a self-evolutionary reinforcement fine-tuning loop. The QwenVL2.5-3B/7B model serves as the base.

#### 3.1. Seed Data Curation

Recognizing the critical importance of data quality for effective RL training, the authors begin by curating a high-quality "seed" dataset. They started with approximately 300,000 open-source grounding examples from ShowUI, UGround, and AriaUI, spanning desktop, web, and mobile platforms. A three-fold filtering strategy was applied to this initial pool:

1. **Instruction Quality (IQ):** Regular expressions were used to remove generic or low-quality instructions (e.g., raw Python object names). A multi-modal large language model (MLLM) was then employed to score the remaining samples based on instruction clarity, alignment with UI elements, and ambiguity.
2. **Bounding Box Accuracy (BA):** To address misaligned or irrelevant bounding box annotations, a dedicated bounding box quality scorer was trained using the Qwen2.5-VL-7B model. This scorer was fine-tuned on a curated set of high-quality bounding boxes (positive examples) and randomly positioned ones (negative examples) to filter out inaccurate annotations.
3. **Task Difficulty (TD):** To remove overly simple tasks that offer little learning value, the Qwen2.5-VL-7B base model performed zero-shot testing on each data point. If all 8 generated outputs for an instruction were correct, the sample was deemed too simple and filtered out.

This rigorous curation process resulted in a final seed dataset of **3,018 high-quality samples**, referred to as SE-GUI-3k, which ensures linguistic consistency, balanced task complexity, and improved generalization.

#### 3.2. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with Dense Point Reward

To combat the problem of sparse binary rewards in RL, SE-GUI adopts Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and introduces a novel dense point reward mechanism.

- **GRPO Foundation:** GRPO, an alternative to PPO, eliminates the need for a separate critic model. Instead, it generates a group of *N* potential position predictions ({o_1..., o_N}) for a given instruction and evaluates each, comparing their rewards to compute relative advantages. The policy update is constrained by minimizing KL divergence between the updated and reference models for stability.
- **Dense Point Reward (R_p):** The key innovation here is replacing sparse binary rewards with a continuous reward that provides smoother feedback. This reward *R_p* is calculated based on the proximity of the predicted click point (x, y) to the ground truth bounding box (gt_bbox = [x1, y1, x2, y2]).
 - The core of the reward calculation is the normalized distance *d* between the predicted click point and the center of the ground truth bounding box.
 - If the predicted point is *inside* the bounding box, the base reward is 1.0, with an additional decaying term that reduces the reward as the point moves away from the box's center.
 - If the predicted point is *outside* the bounding box, the reward is solely based on *1 - (d/d_max)^2*, meaning points closer to the ground truth box's center still receive a higher reward than those farther away, even if they are outside. This provides a continuous gradient for learning from "near misses."
- **Final Reward Composition:** The overall response reward *R_o* is a weighted sum of the dense point reward (*R_Point*) and a format reward (*R_f*), i.e., *R_o = αR_f + βR_Point*. The format reward ensures adherence to the expected output format.

This dense reward mechanism allows the model to learn from imprecise predictions, gradually refining its grounding behavior by receiving informative feedback even for incorrect outputs.

#### 3.3. Self-Evolutionary Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (SE-RFT)

SE-GUI introduces an iterative, self-evolutionary learning loop that leverages the model's own attention maps to guide subsequent training, effectively allowing the model to "supervise" its own refinement.

1. **Initial Training:** An initial model is trained using the curated seed data.
2. **Attention Map Generation:** In each subsequent iteration, the best-performing model from the previous round is used to generate spatial attention maps for the current training samples. This involves extracting self-attention weights from the decoder across all transformer layers, normalizing them to retain visual token components, averaging across layers, and projecting them back onto the original image resolution. These maps highlight which visual tokens the model focuses on for a given instruction.
3. **Attention-Guided Loss Filtering:** The core idea is that if an attention map fails to focus appropriately on the correct UI element (indicating a lack of relevant prior knowledge or a challenging sample), then that data point might provide incorrect guidance. To address this, the loss for such data points is set to zero (i.e., they are ignored) for the current training iteration. This filtering is based on two criteria:
 - *P_peak*: Checks if there is any significant attention activation point within the ground truth target region.
 - *P_global*: Ensures that the average attention weight within the target region is higher than the global average attention across the entire map.
 Together, these ensure that attention is both concentrated and globally prioritized on the correct region.
4. **Iterative Refinement:** The model iteratively refines itself: as grounding accuracy improves, the quality of its attention maps also improves, enabling better self-supervision for the next iteration. This process continues until performance converges, yielding cleaner learning signals over time. The final loss function incorporates this attention-based filtering: *L(θ) = f(attn, gt_bbox, τ) × (E_t [- (π_θ(a_t|s_t) / π_old(a_t|s_t)) A_t + γ · KL[π_old(·|s_t), π_θ(·|s_t)]])*, where *f* is the attention filtering function, *A_t* is the advantage function, and *γ* controls the KL divergence penalty for stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SE-GUI was evaluated on six diverse benchmarks covering GUI grounding and agent tasks across desktop, mobile, and web environments.

#### 4.1. Grounding Capability

- **ScreenSpot-Pro:** This challenging benchmark focuses on high-resolution professional environments (23 applications, 5 industries, 3 OS).
 - SE-GUI-7B achieved a remarkable **47.3% accuracy**, which is **24.2% higher** than the previous state-of-the-art (SOTA) UI-TARS-72B (38.1%). This is particularly impressive given that UI-TARS-72B is a much larger model (72B parameters vs. 7B) and trained on massive data (14M vs. 3K).
 - SE-GUI-7B significantly outperforms Qwen2.5-VL-7B (26.8%) and other GUI-specific models like UGround-V1-7B (31.1%).
- **ScreenSpot & ScreenSpot-v2:** These benchmarks assess general GUI grounding performance across platforms.
 - SE-GUI-7B achieved **88.2% on ScreenSpot** and **90.3% on ScreenSpot-v2**. This demonstrates strong performance across mobile, desktop, and web, again outperforming most existing SOTA models like UI-TARS-2B/7B (82.3% on ScreenSpot, 84.7%/84.7% on ScreenSpot-v2).
These results underscore the effectiveness of SE-GUI's RL-based approach with minimal data, highlighting its data-efficient and scalable nature.

#### 4.2. Agent Evaluation

SE-GUI's generalization capabilities were tested on three GUI agent benchmarks: AndroidControl_High, AndroidControl_Low, and OmniACT.
- **AndroidControl (High/Low):** On a random sample of 500 actions from the test split, SE-GUI-7B significantly outperformed baselines.
 - In the low-level setting (fine-grained instructions per step), SE-GUI achieved **52.8% step accuracy (SR)**, surpassing GPT-4o (39.4%) and UGround-V1 (48.4%).
 - In the high-level setting (only task goal given), SE-GUI achieved **68.2% SR**, a +5.8% improvement over UGround-V1 (62.4%).
 - Notably, SE-GUI was trained purely on grounding data, unlike prior methods trained on AndroidControl and GUIACT, yet it generalized effectively to both task regimes.
- **OmniACT:** On this desktop/web benchmark for generating PyAutoGUI scripts, SE-GUI achieved an action score of **34.4**, outperforming GPT-4o (29.6) and UGround-V1 (32.8).

These agent evaluation results demonstrate SE-GUI's strong generalization and grounding capabilities in both mobile and desktop GUI environments, even in zero-shot settings for the agent tasks.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

The ablation studies confirmed the efficacy of the proposed components:

- **Image Resolution and Data Quality:**
 - Training on the **filtered, high-quality dataset** (SE-GUI-3k) yielded a **4.45% accuracy increase** compared to using unfiltered, lower-quality data (35.8% vs 31.31% on ScreenSpot-Pro, for one setup). This strongly validates the seed data curation strategy.
 - Increasing maximum pixel resolution from 1M to 5M consistently **improved performance**, suggesting that higher-resolution inputs provide valuable fine-grained visual information crucial for complex GUI tasks.
- **Effectiveness of Dense Point Reward:**
 - Using the **dense point reward** mechanism led to a **4.21% improvement** over sparse rewards on ScreenSpot-Pro (40.0% vs 35.8% in one setting). This confirmed that continuous feedback from near-misses is crucial for guiding early-stage training and better localization.
 - Ablations on reward weights showed that setting the format reward to point reward ratio to 1:2 (α:β) was optimal, suggesting less weight on format consistency encourages more focus on prediction accuracy.
- **Effectiveness of Self-Evolutionary Reinforcement Fine-Tuning:**
 - The iterative self-evolutionary process consistently **improved performance across stages**. The first stage (trained only on filtered data) achieved 39.97%. The second stage (with attention-guided supervision) improved by 3%. The third stage reached 46.55%, with minimal improvement in the fourth, indicating convergence.
 - Reward curves showed that each subsequent stage in the self-evolutionary process achieved higher rewards, validating the iterative refinement.
 - SE-GUI-7B showed significantly improved generalization in specialized professional environments (e.g., SolidWorks, Inventor) where UI-TARS-72B performed poorly, boosting accuracy by nearly 40% in some cases. This highlights the framework's ability to handle challenging scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Significance of Contributions

The work presented in "Enhancing Visual Grounding for GUI Agents via Self-Evolutionary Reinforcement Learning" makes several significant contributions to the field of AI and human-computer interaction:

- **Data Efficiency and Scalability:** A core contribution is demonstrating that state-of-the-art performance in complex GUI environments can be achieved with an exceptionally small, high-quality dataset (3,018 samples) when combined with an effective RL framework. This significantly reduces the reliance on large, expensive, and time-consuming supervised datasets, making GUI agent development more accessible and resource-efficient.
- **Robust Reinforcement Learning for GUI:** The introduction of a dense point reward mechanism effectively addresses the longstanding problem of sparse rewards in RL for precise localization tasks. By providing continuous feedback, the model can learn from subtle prediction errors, leading to more accurate and refined grounding.
- **Novel Self-Supervision Paradigm:** The self-evolutionary reinforcement fine-tuning (SE-RFT) mechanism is a highly innovative approach. By iteratively leveraging the model's own attention maps for self-supervision and data filtering, the framework enables continuous improvement without external human annotation. This 'bootstrap' learning process is a powerful concept for autonomous AI systems.
- **Superior Performance in Challenging Environments:** The exceptional performance on ScreenSpot-Pro, a benchmark for professional, high-resolution GUIs, is particularly impactful. Outperforming much larger models by a significant margin demonstrates that SE-GUI's approach is highly effective for complex, real-world applications where precise interaction is paramount.
- **Strong Generalization:** The ability of SE-GUI, trained purely on grounding data, to generalize effectively to agent evaluation benchmarks (AndroidControl, OmniACT) in zero-shot settings showcases its robust understanding of GUI elements and user intent, beyond mere point prediction.

#### 5.2. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge specific limitations, which also suggest clear avenues for future research:

- **Model Scale Constraints:** The current work primarily explores 3B and 7B parameter models due to hardware limitations. While these models achieved SOTA results within their size class, the authors hypothesize that applying the SE-GUI method to much larger models (e.g., 32B or 72B) could lead to even greater performance improvements, given the strong scalability observed.
- **Input Resolution Constraints:** For the 7B model, the maximum input resolution was limited to 2 million pixels. This constraint might lead to a loss of fine-grained details in very high-resolution images, potentially limiting performance in scenarios requiring extreme precision on minute UI elements. Future work could explore the impact of higher resolutions as computational resources become more available.
- **Single-Point Grounding:** The current focus is on click point prediction, which primarily deals with single-point grounding. Future work could extend this to more complex interaction types, such as drag-and-drop, range selection, or multi-element interactions, which are common in professional environments.
- **Dynamic UI Elements:** While the method shows strong generalization, handling highly dynamic or animated UI elements could pose further challenges. Incorporating temporal understanding or more sophisticated visual tracking mechanisms might be necessary.
- **Generalizability to Other Modalities:** The current framework focuses on visual grounding. Future research could investigate how to integrate other modalities (e.g., audio cues, haptic feedback) for a more comprehensive GUI agent.

Overall, this research provides compelling evidence for the effectiveness of a data-efficient, RL-based approach combined with self-evolutionary mechanisms for developing highly capable GUI agents. It sets a strong foundation for future advancements in automating complex digital interactions across diverse platforms.
