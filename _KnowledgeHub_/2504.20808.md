# SoccerDiffusion: Toward Learning End-to-End Humanoid Robot Soccer from Gameplay Recordings

> [!summary] Summary
> SoccerDiffusion, developed by the University of Hamburg, introduces a transformer-based diffusion model that learns end-to-end control policies for humanoid robots directly from 15 hours of real-world RoboCup gameplay recordings. The model successfully replicates fundamental behaviors like walking, kicking, and fall recovery, achieving a 95% success rate in physical robot fall recovery tests and enabling real-time inference on embedded hardware through distillation.

## Problem

- Traditional robot control systems involve extensive manual engineering, leading to strong inductive biases and limited adaptive behavior in dynamic environments.
- Existing end-to-end learning methods for robotics often depend on labor-intensive, handcrafted demonstrations to bootstrap basic skills.
- Modeling complex, multimodal action distributions (where multiple valid actions exist for a given state) is challenging for conventional behavioral cloning approaches, often resulting in averaged, suboptimal policies.

## Method

- The authors developed **SoccerDiffusion**, a transformer-based diffusion model trained on 15 hours (340 GB) of real-world multi-modal gameplay recordings from RoboCup competitions.
- The approach leverages diffusion models to capture and generate complex, multimodal distributions of joint command trajectories, addressing the challenge of diverse possible actions.
- A distillation technique is applied to compress the multistep diffusion process into a single-step inference model, enabling real-time execution on resource-constrained embedded robot hardware.
- The system processes synchronized multi-modal sensor inputs (images, joint states, IMU data) to predict future joint command trajectories.

## Results

- The model achieved a **95%** overall success rate in fall recovery tests on a physical robot (Wolfgang-OP platform) and **100%** in the Webots simulator, demonstrating robust recovery from various fall directions.
- SoccerDiffusion successfully learned fundamental behaviors including stable bipedal walking, turning, kicking, head-scanning for localization, and dynamic stabilization from real-world gameplay data.
- The distilled single-step inference model exhibited no noticeable qualitative difference compared to the original multistep diffusion model, enabling real-time performance on target hardware.
- The model showed a significant bias towards stillness if no prior motion was present in its context window and did not acquire high-level tactical behaviors like intentional goal-kicking or strategic field positioning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex and dynamic humanoid robot behaviors can be effectively extracted from large volumes of naturally occurring observational gameplay data, reducing the need for handcrafted demonstrations.
> - Transformer-based diffusion models are capable of learning and representing complex, multimodal action distributions for robust control in dynamic robotic tasks.
> - Distillation allows computationally intensive deep learning models, like diffusion models, to operate in real-time on embedded robot platforms, bridging the gap between advanced AI and practical robotics.
> - Policies learned exclusively from real-world data exhibit strong sim-to-real transferability, performing robustly in simulation without additional tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous robots capable of effective and adaptive behavior in dynamic environments is a significant challenge in robotics. The RoboCup Humanoid Kid-size League, where teams of autonomous humanoid robots compete in soccer matches, serves as a prominent benchmark for this challenge. Historically, achieving complex robot behaviors has involved manually engineering numerous abstractions and interfaces between various subsystems, which can introduce strong inductive biases and limit the system's expressive capacity. This traditional approach also presents complexities in design, integration, and handling of edge cases.

Consequently, end-to-end learning approaches, which aim to directly map sensor inputs to actuator commands, have emerged as a promising alternative. While end-to-end control has been demonstrated in simulation for humanoid football and in simplified real-world robot soccer scenarios, these methods often require a foundational set of handcrafted demonstrations to bootstrap the learning process for basic skills. This necessitates expert intervention and limits the scalability of learning complex, nuanced behaviors.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitation of relying on handcrafted demonstrations. Instead, it investigates the feasibility of learning end-to-end control policies directly from large amounts of data collected during real-world RoboCup competitions. This approach seeks to leverage existing, naturalistic gameplay data to train a base model that mimics typical robot behaviors, thereby potentially reducing the need for labor-intensive, task-specific demonstrations.

The work builds upon advancements in imitation learning (IL) and behavioral cloning (BC), which allow agents to learn from expert demonstrations. Traditional BC, while simple, can suffer from compounding errors and distributional shift problems in sequential prediction tasks, as learned predictions influence future states. Furthermore, multimodal action spaces, where multiple plausible actions exist for a given state, present challenges for simple point-estimate models (e.g., Mean Squared Error), which tend to produce averaged, suboptimal policies.

The research integrates two key architectural advancements to address these challenges:
- **Transformer Models:** Recognized for their ability to model long-range temporal dependencies and complex relationships in sequential data, transformers have seen successful application in robotics, making them suitable for handling the temporal aspects of robot control from sensor streams.
- **Diffusion Models (DMs):** These generative models excel at learning and sampling from complex, multimodal data distributions by reversing a noise-addition process. Recent work has applied DMs to robotics for imitation learning, particularly in short, multistep tasks for robot arms. This paper extends the application of DMs to a more complex and long-term task: end-to-end control in humanoid robot soccer.

By training a transformer-based diffusion model on real-world gameplay recordings, the authors aim to explore whether useful and diverse behaviors can emerge from such a dataset, serving as a robust foundation for subsequent, more advanced learning processes like reinforcement learning (RL) or preference optimization (PO). This differentiates it from prior works that either used handcrafted demos or were limited to simpler robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the feasibility of learning end-to-end control policies for humanoid robots in the context of RoboCup soccer, using data collected directly from real-world gameplay recordings. This objective is driven by several motivations:

Firstly, the development of autonomous robot control systems traditionally involves extensive manual engineering of software stacks, including various abstractions and interfaces. This process is complex, time-consuming, and can introduce strong inductive biases, potentially limiting the robot's capacity for adaptive and expressive behavior in dynamic, unpredictable environments like a soccer field. The authors are motivated to explore a data-driven approach that can circumvent the need for such explicit, rule-based programming.

Secondly, while existing end-to-end learning methods have shown promise in simulated or simplified real-world robot soccer, many still rely on a certain number of handcrafted demonstrations to bootstrap basic skills. The authors aim to move beyond this dependency by leveraging a large volume of pre-existing data from actual RoboCup competitions. This approach seeks to determine whether useful and robust behaviors can be extracted directly from observational data, rather than requiring expert-designed input for initial skill acquisition.

Thirdly, the research is motivated by the inherent challenges of modeling complex, multimodal action distributions in robotic tasks. In dynamic environments, a given sensory state might legitimately correspond to several different optimal or plausible actions (e.g., walking, turning, or preparing to kick). Traditional behavioral cloning methods that use simple point estimates (like mean squared error) often struggle with such multimodality, tending to collapse to an "average" policy that may not reflect any truly effective behavior. Diffusion models, with their capacity to capture complex and multimodal distributions, are chosen to address this specific challenge.

Fourthly, the study intends to provide a foundational "base model" for future, more advanced learning processes. By learning general behaviors from gameplay data, this model could serve as a pre-trained initialization for reinforcement learning (RL) or preference optimization (PO) algorithms, potentially accelerating their training and improving their performance by providing a more informed starting point than a randomly initialized policy. The immediate goal is not to surpass the performance of the highly optimized, manually programmed RoboCup software stack, but rather to establish the academic feasibility and utility of learning from existing data.

Finally, the authors are motivated to contribute to the broader research community by releasing the collected dataset, pre-trained models, and associated code. This aims to facilitate subsequent studies and enable other researchers to build upon their findings, especially in the areas of imitation learning, diffusion models in robotics, and humanoid robot control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study involves a data-driven approach utilizing a transformer-based diffusion model to learn end-to-end control policies for humanoid robots from real-world gameplay recordings. The process encompasses data collection, preprocessing, model architecture design, a distillation technique for real-time inference, and a specific trajectory execution strategy.

#### 3.1. Data Collection

The dataset consists of on-robot recordings from the Hamburg Bit-Bots RoboCup team during official RoboCup 2024 and RoboCup German Open 2025 competitions. A total of 88 recordings, amounting to approximately 15 hours of gameplay data, were utilized. Each recording represents a single sequence of data from a single robot during a match. Raw data was stored in ROS 2 Bag format (mcap files) and included multi-modal sensor measurements (vision, proprioception), intermediary representations, debug information, and the joint commands sent to the servo motors. Other recordings with insufficient image data sampling rates were excluded.

#### 3.2. Data Preprocessing

The raw mcap files underwent a preprocessing stage to ensure synchronized and uniformly sampled data across all modalities:
- **Resampling and Synchronization:** All joint and IMU-related data were resampled at 50 Hz, down from their original sampling rate of over 300 Hz. Image data was sampled at 10 Hz. To maintain causality, no future data was used for interpolation; instead, the last available data point before the sampling time was selected.
- **Image Processing:** Images were downsampled to a resolution of 480 × 480 pixels.
- **Game State Simplification:** Game state messages, originating from the human-controlled game controller software, were simplified into three discrete states: robot allowed to play (and move), robot not allowed to play, or unknown.
- **Data Storage:** The processed data was stored in a SQLite database, facilitating efficient synchronized querying during the training phase.
- **IMU Data:** Due to a recording error at RoboCup 2024, IMU data was reconstructed from an intermediary representation, providing only the robot's roll and pitch orientation. Accelerometer and gyroscope data were unavailable and therefore not used.
The final dataset totaled 340 GB, comprising 8,565,382 total samples across all modalities.

#### 3.3. Model Architecture: SoccerDiffusion

The core of the approach is SoccerDiffusion, a transformer neural network trained with a diffusion denoising objective. This architecture was chosen for its ability to learn complex, multimodal distributions (diffusion model) and model long-range temporal dependencies and combine multiple modalities (transformer architecture). Classifier-free guidance was not utilized.

The model architecture incorporates:
- **Multiple Encoders:**
 - **Joint and Rotation Encoders:** These process joint states, previous joint commands, and robot rotations. They employ a linear token embedding of input patches, followed by a transformer encoder. Their context window spans 2 seconds.
 - **Image Encoder:** This processes visual input. It uses a ResNet-18 architecture for feature extraction, followed by a transformer encoder to model temporal dependencies. Its context window spans 1 second.
 - The encoders perform a late fusion, encoding their respective modalities into a shared latent space.
- **Diffusion Denoising:**
 - **Diffusion Step Embedding:** The current step of the diffusion process is incorporated as a sinusoidal embedding.
 - **Game State Token:** The current game state (simplified as described in preprocessing) is included as a learned token.
- **Joint Command Trajectory Decoder:** This component is a transformer decoder that utilizes cross-attention to query the shared latent space produced by the encoders. Its role is to predict the noise residual given the latent encoder information and a noisy future joint command trajectory.
- **Sampling Algorithm:** The Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) algorithm is employed for sampling the joint command trajectories.
- **Positional Encodings:** All transformer components use sinusoidal position encodings to embed temporal information into the input tokens.
Hyperparameters, including 4 layers for Joint and Rotation Transformer Encoders, 2 layers for Image Transformer Encoder, 4 layers for Decoder Transformer, 8 attention heads, and 512 hidden dimensions, were selected to enable single-step inference on resource-constrained embedded platforms (specifically, the Ryzen 7 5700U APU of the Wolfgang-OP robot) after distillation. Training involved 300 epochs, 1000 diffusion steps for training, and 30 steps for sampling.

#### 3.4. Distillation

To achieve real-time inference on embedded hardware, a distillation technique was applied to reduce the multistep diffusion process to a single inference step.
- **Teacher-Student Approach:** The method follows a teacher-student paradigm. The student model's architecture is identical to the teacher model, but it is configured to perform only a single diffusion step.
- **Training Process:** The student model is initialized with the weights of the pre-trained teacher model. During distillation, the teacher model performs a deterministic 30-step DDIM sampling to generate target trajectories. The student model is then trained to predict these target trajectories directly from the input noise and the latent encoder information, using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss.
- **Efficiency:** To speed up the distillation, only the decoder of the student model is trained, while the encoder weights are frozen and shared with the teacher model. The same dataset used for training the teacher model is used for distillation.

#### 3.5. Trajectory Execution

The generated joint command trajectory is executed on the robot in a step-wise, delay-compensating manner.
- **Offset from Input:** The model's generated trajectory begins immediately after the latest available input data point.
- **Inference Time Compensation:** Recognizing that the model's inference time introduces a delay, the system only partially executes the generated trajectory. Initial points of the trajectory that would have elapsed during the inference time are truncated.
- **Overlapping Generation:** Trajectories are generated continuously and in an overlapping fashion. The system always executes commands from the most recently generated trajectory, which helps maintain the assumption of instantaneous inference during training by effectively handling execution delays.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SoccerDiffusion was conducted through both quantitative and qualitative assessments, performed on physical robot hardware (Wolfgang-OP platform) and in the Webots simulator.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Evaluation

A series of fall-recovery tests were performed to quantitatively assess a specific robust behavior. The robot was manually pushed to the ground from a standing position in four distinct directions (forward, backward, left side, right side). Each direction was tested ten times.
- **Success Rates:**
 - **Simulation:** SoccerDiffusion achieved a 100% success rate (40/40 trials) in the Webots simulator, consistently recovering from falls in all directions on the first attempt.
 - **Physical Robot:** On the physical robot, SoccerDiffusion demonstrated a 95% overall success rate (38/40 trials) on the first attempt. Specifically, it achieved 100% success for backward and left-side falls, 90% for right-side falls (1 failure), and 60% for forward falls (3 retries, 1 failure).
- **Comparison to Baseline:** The conventional software stack (baseline) achieved a 100% success rate for fall recovery in both simulation and on the physical robot.
- **Fall Detection:** The fall detection mechanism of SoccerDiffusion was successful in all cases during these tests.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Evaluation

Qualitative evaluation focused on observing a broader range of learned behaviors on the physical Wolfgang-OP robot and in simulation.
- **Fundamental Motions:** The robot demonstrated stable bipedal walking in various directions, turning motions, and kicking actions. It also successfully executed robust fall recovery sequences, which involved multistep motions (e.g., turning from its side to its front before standing up).
- **Dynamic Stabilization:** The model learned and replicated dynamic stabilization behaviors, mimicking strategies used by the Hamburg Bit-Bots' conventional software, such as PID-based Center of Mass control and adaptive gait phases, enabling it to recover from external disturbances during locomotion.
- **Head Motions:** The robot performed head-scan motions (left-to-right at various tilt angles) for environmental sensing, typically used for localization. It also tilted its head to observe the ball and its surroundings, mirroring head behaviors observed during ball manipulation in the training dataset. However, the robot did not exhibit active ball tracking with its head.
- **Distilled vs. Non-Distilled:** No noticeable qualitative difference in behavior was observed between the distilled, single-step inference model and the original, multistep diffusion model when both were run in a sub-real-time simulation environment. This indicates the distillation successfully preserved the learned policy's quality.
- **Real-to-Sim Transfer:** The learned model demonstrated robust transferability from the real world to simulation without requiring any additional training in the simulated environment. This contrasts with the Hamburg Bit-Bots' conventional walk engine, which typically requires specific parameter tuning for simulation.
- **Limitations:**
 - **High-Level Behavior:** The robot failed to exhibit high-level tactical behaviors, such as intentional kicking (e.g., aiming for a goal) or strategic positioning on the soccer field.
 - **Bias Towards Stillness:** A significant limitation identified was a strong bias for the robot to remain stationary if no prior motion was present in its context window. This behavior is attributed to the prevalence of "standing still" sequences within the collected dataset and the complexity introduced by team play dynamics where not all robots are constantly moving towards the ball. This often prevented the robot from initiating walking autonomously.

In summary, SoccerDiffusion successfully learned and replicated a range of fundamental, dynamic, and robust physical behaviors (walking, kicking, fall recovery, stabilization) directly from real-world gameplay data. It achieved real-time performance through distillation and demonstrated robustness to the sim-to-real gap. However, the model did not acquire complex, high-level tactical decision-making comparable to the conventional baseline.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics and artificial intelligence by demonstrating the potential of learning end-to-end control policies directly from large volumes of real-world gameplay recordings. The work addresses several key challenges in robot autonomy and offers implications for future development:

Firstly, the study provides empirical evidence for the feasibility of extracting complex and dynamic robot behaviors from naturally occurring observational data, collected during competitive events rather than meticulously curated, handcrafted demonstrations. This approach has the potential to reduce the laborious and expertise-intensive process of manually engineering control systems or generating task-specific demonstrations, thus accelerating the development cycle for complex robotic systems.

Secondly, the successful application of a transformer-based diffusion model to humanoid robot soccer highlights the utility of these architectures for handling multimodal sensor inputs and generating multimodal action distributions. Diffusion models' capacity to represent diverse, plausible actions for a given state addresses a known limitation of traditional behavioral cloning methods, which often struggle with averaging out behaviors in multimodal scenarios. This makes them particularly well-suited for the ambiguous and dynamic nature of robot soccer.

Thirdly, the implementation of a distillation technique to compress the multistep diffusion process into a single-step inference model is critical for practical deployment. This allows the learned policies to operate in real-time on resource-constrained embedded platforms, which are typical of physical humanoid robots. This innovation bridges the gap between computationally intensive deep learning models and the practical requirements of robot hardware, making the end-to-end learning paradigm more viable for real-world applications.

Fourthly, the demonstrated robustness of the learned model to the sim-to-real gap is a notable finding. The ability to transfer a policy trained exclusively on real-world data to a simulated environment without additional tuning, and for the robot to perform similarly, suggests that the model has learned generalizable and robust motion primitives. This characteristic could simplify development workflows by allowing for more extensive testing and refinement in simulation based on real-world learned behaviors.

Finally, by releasing the dataset, pre-trained models, and code, the authors foster open science and provide a valuable resource for the research community. This contribution can serve as a robust baseline for future studies, enabling researchers to build upon this work, explore different learning algorithms (such as more advanced reinforcement learning or preference optimization techniques), and investigate methods for learning higher-level tactical behaviors. While the current model did not achieve the same level of high-level tactical decision-making as the expertly engineered baseline, it lays a foundational layer of basic, stable, and dynamic motor control. This foundation is a critical prerequisite for building more sophisticated cognitive and strategic capabilities in autonomous humanoid robots, moving towards a future where robots learn increasingly complex behaviors directly from observation and interaction.
