# A Unified Framework for Trajectory Prediction with Explicit Planning and Reaction Decomposition

> [!summary] Summary
> Northeastern University researchers developed INTraJ, a unified framework that explicitly decomposes social influence into distinct planning and reaction stages for trajectory prediction. This framework achieves state-of-the-art performance on crowd benchmarks, reducing FDE to 9.87 pixels on SDD, and improves lightweight baselines for autonomous driving, yielding an ADE of 0.558m and FDE of 1.228m on AV2-ped.

## Problem

- Existing trajectory prediction models often entangle social influences from various sources and time scales, increasing complexity and vulnerability to spurious correlations.
- Current methods inadequately distinguish the functional roles of social influence in trajectory formation, treating it as an undifferentiated component.
- High-level representations in prior work frequently fail to explicitly capture a 'socially shaped planning representation' that agents form by anticipating others' future behaviors.

## Method

- Introduces **INTraJ**, a unified framework that explicitly decomposes social influence into a ==planning stage== and a ==reaction stage== for trajectory prediction.
- The planning stage generates an 'ideal reference trajectory' by integrating an agent's pure intent with anticipated future social information.
- The reaction stage calculates 'local adjustments' from the difference between a full-context prediction and the ideal reference, then fuses these adjustments using a learnable ==gating mechanism==.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art performance on crowd prediction benchmarks (ETH/UCY and SDD), reducing FDE to **9.87 pixels** on SDD.
- Delivered state-of-the-art results on the AV2-pedestrian dataset using a lightweight backbone, obtaining an ADE of **0.558m** and FDE of **1.228m**.
- Outperformed existing plug-in refinement methods on Argoverse 2, demonstrating larger improvements in metrics like minADE1, minFDE1, and MR1 when integrated with the QCNet backbone.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Trajectory formation in multi-agent environments involves a staged cognitive process where an overall plan, influenced by future social context, precedes local reactive adjustments.
> - Explicitly decomposing social influence into distinct planning and reaction stages is crucial for stable, coherent, and adaptive predictions.
> - This decomposition enhances model interpretability by separating long-term strategic interactions from short-term reactive behaviors, thereby improving robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Trajectory prediction is a fundamental task within artificial intelligence and robotics, essential for applications such as autonomous driving, pedestrian navigation, and crowd behavior analysis. The field aims to estimate the future motions of agents within a given scene, considering their historical movements and environmental context.

Historically, many mainstream approaches to trajectory prediction have adopted a "coupled formulation." In this design, individual motion tendencies, scene constraints (like map elements and lane topology), and social interactions are encoded into a shared latent space, leading to an end-to-end prediction. While this approach can provide dense information flow and expressiveness, the authors note that it often entangles social effects originating from different sources and time scales. This entanglement can increase model complexity and render it vulnerable to spuriously correlated neighbor patterns, potentially leading to less stable and interpretable predictions.

More recent research has explored the benefits of explicit decomposition in trajectory prediction. This includes methods that decouple high-level goal modeling, separate motion hierarchies, or employ structured representations in the time and frequency domains. These studies have demonstrated that isolating different factors, rather than unifying them, can be both reasonable and effective.

However, the authors identify a specific gap within these structured approaches: most existing methods still treat social influence as an undifferentiated component within a unified prediction process. They generally do not distinguish the functional role social influence plays in the formation of a trajectory. For instance, high-level representations in prior work typically focus on target endpoints, motion modes, or abstract latent variables, but they often do not explicitly correspond to a "socially shaped planning representation" that an agent might form after anticipating the future behaviors of surrounding entities.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by proposing a novel, unified framework called INTraJ. It addresses the identified gap by explicitly decomposing social influence into distinct planning and reaction stages. This decomposition is intended to align more closely with the cognitive process of agents, where an overall plan, shaped by anticipated social interactions, precedes local reactive adjustments during execution. By reframing trajectory prediction in this manner, INTraJ aims to provide a more structured, interpretable, and robust approach to modeling multi-agent interactions, applicable across diverse scenarios like autonomous driving and crowd forecasting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from an observation regarding human and agent behavior in multi-agent environments: agents typically do not react to their surroundings instantaneously or in an undifferentiated manner. Instead, they often engage in a staged process where an overall motion plan is first formulated, significantly influenced by anticipating the future behaviors of other agents, before making localized, reactive adjustments during the execution of that plan. This distinction highlights that "social interactions play staged roles, namely planning precedes reaction."

Based on this insight, the authors identify a critical limitation in existing trajectory prediction methods. Despite advancements in structured formulations and explicit social modeling, these methods are considered to "inadequately distinguish the functional roles of social influence in trajectory planning." Specifically, most current approaches, even those attempting some form of decomposition, tend to integrate social effects into a single, unified prediction process. The high-level representations used (e.g., target endpoints, motion modes, abstract latent variables) often do not explicitly capture a "socially shaped planning representation" that results from an agent anticipating the future actions of others. This lack of explicit functional distinction can lead to several challenges, including:
- **Entanglement of Social Effects:** Different types of social influence (e.g., long-term strategic planning vs. short-term collision avoidance) are conflated.
- **Increased Complexity:** A unified predictor struggles to disentangle and process these varied social cues effectively.
- **Vulnerability to Spurious Correlations:** The model may be misled by incidental patterns in neighbor behavior rather than capturing true causal or intentional social influence.

To address these limitations, the primary objective of this work is to propose INTraJ, a unified framework that explicitly decomposes social influence into two distinct functional stages:
1. **Planning Stage:** The objective here is to construct robust, high-level "reference trajectories." These trajectories are formed by integrating "future social information" (anticipating what other agents will do) with an agent's "pure intent," thereby creating a "socialized ideal trajectory" or overall plan. This stage aims to capture the broad path and timing an agent intends to follow, considering its social context.
2. **Reaction Stage:** The objective of this stage is to recover "local adjustments" or "reactive corrections." These adjustments are derived from the residual difference between a full-context trajectory prediction (from a standard backbone) and the planning-level reference trajectory. This stage focuses on immediate, fine-grained deviations from the plan, such as minor evasive maneuvers or speed changes, in response to real-time, local interaction dynamics.

A further key objective is to demonstrate that this two-stage decomposition is not limited to a specific type of prediction task but can be instantiated effectively under both multi-target and single-target prediction paradigms. The authors also aim to achieve consistent performance improvements, particularly in metrics like Final Displacement Error (FDE) and long-horizon consistency, across various standard benchmarks, ultimately seeking to establish state-of-the-art performance in several experimental settings. This is intended to validate the overarching hypothesis that "staged social modeling is critical for stable predictions."

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces INTraJ, a unified framework for trajectory prediction that explicitly decomposes social influence into a planning stage and a reaction stage. This framework is designed to be plug-in compatible with existing backbone networks and supports both multi-target and single-target prediction paradigms.

#### 3.1. Plan and Reaction Decomposition (Core INTraJ Module)

The core of INTraJ is the Plan-React module, which processes inputs to produce a final trajectory in two sequential stages:

1. **Planning Stage:**
 - **Pure Intent Extraction ($I_{pure}$):** An initial representation of the target agent's basic motion tendency, derived from its historical trajectory and local context, is extracted. This serves as a "self-intent planning seed," reflecting an agent's intrinsic motion goal without explicit consideration of others' future actions at this specific step.
 - **Socialized Intent Formation ($I_{social}$):** A "future social carrier" ($z$), which contains information about the anticipated future behaviors of surrounding agents, is integrated with $I_{pure}$. This integration is performed via a cross-attention mechanism, where $I_{pure}$ acts as the query and $z$ as the key/value. The output, $I_{social}$, represents the updated planning seed, now "socialized" by incorporating future social constraints.
 - **Ideal Reference Trajectory Decoding ($T_{ideal}$):** $I_{social}$ is then passed through a dedicated decoder to generate the "planning-level reference trajectory" ($T_{ideal}$). This trajectory is characterized as a smooth, low-frequency overall motion plan that explicitly accounts for future social interactions, emphasizing long-term navigability and mode-level consistency.

2. **Reaction Stage:**
 - **Full Prediction Generation ($T_{full}$):** The original backbone network, utilizing the full input context (ego agent history, neighboring trajectories, and scene context), generates its standard "full prediction" ($T_{full}$). This prediction typically implicitly includes all social and environmental factors in a coupled manner.
 - **Reactive Adjustment Calculation ($S_{high}$):** The structural difference between the full prediction and the ideal reference trajectory is computed: $S_{high} = T_{full} - T_{ideal}$. This difference is hypothesized to approximate the local reactive adjustments that deviate from the broader, pre-planned motion. These adjustments capture immediate responses like collision avoidance or short-term speed changes.
 - **Gated Fusion ($T_{final}$):** A learnable gating mechanism ($\alpha$) is introduced to selectively fuse the reactive adjustments ($S_{high}$) with the planning-level reference ($T_{ideal}$). The final output trajectory is produced as $T_{final} = T_{ideal} + \alpha \cdot S_{high}$. The gating weight $\alpha$ is designed to control the scale and presence of reactive adjustments, allowing them to vary across different prediction modes and future time steps based on contextual need.

#### 3.2. Paradigm-Specific Instantiations

The framework provides tailored designs for constructing $I_{pure}$ and $z$ depending on the prediction paradigm:

- **Multi-target Prediction Paradigm (e.g., QCNet backbone):**
 - $I_{pure}$: A lightweight self-history intent branch is used to extract $I_{pure}$ solely from the focal agent's historical trajectory and local context, without explicitly attending to others' future behaviors at this initial step.
 - $z$: The future social carrier ($z_{future}$) is constructed by first predicting the future trajectories of non-focal agents ($P_{others}$) from their scene-level representations (a natural output for multi-target backbones), and then encoding these predictions.
 - Cross-attention then merges $I_{pure}$ with $z_{future}$ to form $I_{social}$.

- **Single-target Prediction Paradigm (e.g., DeMo backbone):**
 - $I_{pure}$: An "ego-centered reference branch" with a "mask others" strategy is employed. This branch processes only the ego agent and map inputs, explicitly excluding neighbor conditions to derive a pure intent.
 - $z$: A "latent social response" ($z_{latent}$) serves as the social carrier. This is obtained from a "static self" branch where the ego agent's historical motion is set to static, allowing the model to encode potential future responses of surrounding agents as if the focal agent were stationary.
 - Cross-attention then combines $I_{pure}$ with $z_{latent}$ to form $I_{social}$.

#### 3.3. Training Objectives

The training process involves a composite loss function: $L = L_{pred} + \lambda_1 L_{ref} + \lambda_2 L_{aux}$.
- $L_{pred}$: The standard prediction loss from the original backbone for $T_{final}$ (e.g., best-of-K regression, mode classification).
- $L_{ref}$: A planning reference loss applied to $T_{ideal}$. It uses a smoothed version of the ground-truth future trajectory ($T_{smooth}$) as supervision and includes both trajectory regression loss and an endpoint loss to constrain the overall path and direction.
- $L_{aux}$: An auxiliary regularization term on the gating weight $\alpha$. This prevents $\alpha$ from collapsing to uniform values and encourages dynamic, context-dependent adjustments.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmarks:** Argoverse 2 (AV2), Argoverse 2-ped for autonomous driving, and ETH/UCY, Stanford Drone Dataset (SDD) for crowd prediction.
- **Metrics:** Average Displacement Error (ADE), Final Displacement Error (FDE), and on AV2, Miss Rate at 6m (MR6) and Miss Rate at 1m (MR1).
- **Backbones:** QCNet and DeMo for AV2, Resonance for ETH/UCY and SDD, and a lightweight Transformer/TCN baseline for AV2-ped. This selection aimed to demonstrate generality across different backbone architectures and prediction paradigms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across various benchmarks and backbones demonstrate the consistent effectiveness of the INTraJ framework.

#### 4.1. Results on Crowd Benchmarks (ETH/UCY, SDD)

- **Performance Improvements:** INTraJ, when integrated with the Resonance backbone, achieved state-of-the-art performance on both ETH/UCY and SDD datasets.
 - On SDD, the FDE significantly decreased to 9.87 pixels, outperforming other methods which generally remained above 10.
 - On ETH/UCY, INTraJ delivered a 7.2% improvement in overall FDE compared to the Resonance backbone. For the long-horizon prediction subsets of ETH/UCY, the FDE gain reached 12.2%.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Qualitative results on ETH/UCY (Fig. 3) illustrate that the final predictions (`final`) generated by INTraJ mitigate entanglement issues present in backbone-only predictions (`full`). The `final` trajectories maintained more coherent motion trends and better matched real crowd dynamics, particularly for complex and long-horizon scenarios. These findings indicate that the planning and reaction decomposition effectively models complex interactions and long-horizon trajectory evolution in pure crowd settings.

#### 4.2. Results on Autonomous Driving Benchmarks (Argoverse 2, Argoverse 2-ped)

- **Competitive Performance on AV2:** INTraJ achieved competitive results on the Argoverse 2 test set. While not uniformly achieving the best score across every metric, it consistently improved the performance of both the QCNet (multi-target) and DeMo (single-target) backbones. This suggests the framework's compatibility and benefit across different prediction paradigms within autonomous driving. For instance, integrating with DeMo resulted in minFDE6 of 1.17 and minADE6 of 0.61, comparable to or better than several state-of-the-art methods.
- **Effectiveness with Lightweight Backbones (AV2-ped):** To further assess the source of performance gains, INTraJ was integrated into a lightweight baseline (small Transformer encoder and TCN decoder) on the AV2-pedestrian dataset. Despite the simpler backbone, INTraJ achieved state-of-the-art performance across all metrics (e.g., ADE of 0.558m and FDE of 1.228m). This result supports the conclusion that the performance improvements are attributable to the structural decomposition inherent in INTraJ, rather than solely relying on the capabilities of a high-performance backbone.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Qualitative results on AV2 (Fig. 4) visually distinguished the "ideal" (planning-level reference) trajectory, the "full" (backbone-only) prediction, and the "final" trajectory. The `ideal` trajectory highlighted overall passing paths and long-horizon traversability, while the `final` trajectory, incorporating reactive adjustments, produced predictions that were more realistic and adaptive to local interaction changes. This visualization reinforces the concept of decoupling overall planning from local interaction responses.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Plug-in Refinement Methods

- **Superiority in Refinement:** When directly compared with plug-in post-processing methods like SmartRefine and LAKD, INTraJ demonstrated larger performance improvements across minADE1, minFDE1, and MR1 when integrated into the QCNet backbone. For example, QCNet with INTraJ achieved a minFDE1 of 4.14 and MR1 of 0.56, outperforming QCNet + SmartRefine (4.17 FDE1, 0.58 MR1) and QCNet + LAKD (4.15 FDE1, 0.58 MR1).
- **Stronger Generalization:** INTraJ exhibited stronger cross-task generalization, showing consistent gains in both autonomous driving and pure crowd scenarios. This contrasts with many plug-in methods that are primarily validated on specific datasets like the Argoverse series, indicating INTraJ's contribution is a more generalizable structured formulation for trajectory formation.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

An ablation study with QCNet on Argoverse 2 confirmed the necessity of the proposed two-stage design:
- Removing either the planning-level reference or the reaction-stage adjustment led to performance degradation, indicating both components are crucial.
- The future social influence update and the gating mechanism were also found to be critical for stable performance.
- The study confirmed that the gains arise from the conceptual framework and improved formulation of trajectory formation, rather than simply from additional parameter stacking.

#### 4.5. Computational Overhead Analysis

INTraJ introduces moderate additional computational costs, maintaining a favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off:
- Parameters increased from 7.66M (QCNet) to 8.46M (QCNet + INTraJ).
- Inference latency increased from 37.94ms to 44.64ms.
- FLOPs increased from 76.80G to 77.85G.
These overheads are comparable to or better than other refinement methods (e.g., SmartRefine, which resulted in 8.00M parameters, 40.45ms latency, and 77.34G FLOPs).

#### 4.6. Robustness to Imperfect Predicted Others

An analysis of robustness to errors in "Pred Others" (predicted future trajectories of surrounding agents) showed that INTraJ is tolerant to small corruptions. Performance degraded smoothly with increasing Gaussian noise, remaining better than or comparable to the backbone under reasonable levels of inaccuracy in social future prediction. This suggests the framework's practical viability in real-world scenarios where perfect predictions for surrounding agents are not guaranteed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The INTraJ framework introduces a hierarchical social influence modeling view that re-conceptualizes trajectory prediction as a planning-driven two-stage process. This reframing holds several significant implications and potential impacts on the field:

#### 5.1. Conceptual Advancement in Social Interaction Modeling

The core significance lies in explicitly decomposing social influence into distinct planning and reaction stages. This moves beyond treating social interaction as a monolithic factor within a unified predictor. By distinguishing the functional roles of social influence—first in shaping an agent's overall plan and then in prompting local adjustments—INTraJ offers a more nuanced and psychologically plausible model of multi-agent behavior. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how agents navigate complex environments.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Interpretability and Robustness

The explicit separation of planning and reaction can lead to more interpretable prediction models. Researchers and developers can analyze the "ideal" planning-level trajectories and the "reactive" deviations separately, providing insights into the model's decision-making process. This structured approach may also enhance robustness by disentangling long-term strategic interactions from short-term reactive behaviors, potentially making predictions less susceptible to spurious correlations that can plague coupled models.

#### 5.3. Broad Generalizability and Applicability

INTraJ demonstrates consistent performance gains across diverse settings, including both autonomous driving and pure crowd scenarios. Its compatibility with both multi-target and single-target prediction paradigms, and its effectiveness when integrated into various backbone architectures (from lightweight baselines to state-of-the-art models), highlight its broad generalizability. This suggests that the "planning precedes reaction" principle is a fundamental and transferable paradigm for modeling multi-agent interactions, applicable across different domains and specific prediction tasks.

#### 5.4. Practical Impact on Real-World Systems

- **Autonomous Driving:** More stable, coherent, and adaptive trajectory predictions can directly contribute to safer and more efficient autonomous driving systems. By providing a clear long-term plan and accurate local adjustments, self-driving vehicles could better anticipate and react to complex traffic situations, improving planning and decision-making.
- **Crowd Analysis and Management:** Improved prediction of human trajectories can benefit applications in crowd management, urban planning, public safety, and human-robot interaction in crowded spaces. It can aid in identifying potential conflicts, optimizing pedestrian flow, or designing more responsive robotic systems.

#### 5.5. Foundation for Future Research

The proposed "planning-before-reaction" paradigm offers a new avenue for future research in trajectory prediction and multi-agent systems. It encourages further exploration into:
- More sophisticated models for extracting pure intent and future social information.
- Dynamically adapting the planning and reaction stages based on environmental uncertainty or agent characteristics.
- Integrating this hierarchical view with other decomposition techniques (e.g., causal disentanglement).
- Developing evaluation metrics that specifically assess the quality of planning-level consistency and reactive adaptiveness.

In summary, INTraJ reframes a fundamental problem in trajectory prediction with a conceptually grounded, empirically validated, and broadly applicable framework. Its emphasis on staged social modeling provides a pathway towards more stable, interpretable, and effective prediction systems for complex interactive environments.
