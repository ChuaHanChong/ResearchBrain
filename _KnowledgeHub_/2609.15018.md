# G-ray: Ray-Level Relative Geometric Position Encoding in Multi-View Vision Transformers under Camera Heterogeneity

> [!summary] Summary
> G-ray is a relative position encoding for multi-view vision Transformers that parameterizes rotary phases by camera-local ray angles instead of image-plane coordinates, so the same ray pair induces the same relative phase under any field of view or projection model. It adds no learned parameters, drops into RoPE, GTA, or RayRoPE, and cuts mean pointmap error 45.8% over MapAnything at 50 views.

## Problem

- Feed-forward multi-view Transformers dropped the explicit camera model, inheriting single-image positional encodings that say where a token sits but nothing about its camera.
- Absolute camera encodings such as Plucker rays and CamRay tokens attach geometry per token, so cross-view relative structure must be inferred indirectly with no guaranteed positional consistency.
- Relative encodings like GTA, PRoPE, and RayRoPE still build rotary phases from image-plane coordinates, so equal pixel offsets encode different ray-angle differences across projections.

## Method

- Image-to-ray interface unprojects each positional sample to a camera-local unit ray, in closed form from pinhole intrinsics or through a per-pixel unit-ray map for non-pinhole views.
- ==Ray-angle rotary encoding==: horizontal and vertical atan2 angles drive a geometric frequency bank scaled by alpha, rotating queries, keys, and values, with an inverse query rotation on the output.
- Coordinate replacement swaps grid indices in ==RoPE== and ==GTA==, or query-camera projected pixels in ==RayRoPE==, for ray angles while retaining pose, depth, and disparity components.

## Results

- With supplied calibration, **G-ray** leads all six dataset-averaged 50-view metrics: **0.0744** pointmap relative error versus **0.1372** for MapAnything, and **58.1%** versus **37.5%** point inliers.
- On mixed-projection ScanNet++ the pinhole-trained model reaches **0.0583** pointmap error and **75.1%** point inliers without retraining, roughly **74%** below calibrated MapAnything.
- For view synthesis, RayRoPE plus G-ray gains **1.10** to **1.13 dB** PSNR on RE10K; removing value and output rotations raises ScanNet++ ray error **33%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The positional coordinate is a design lever independent of architecture: replace it, keep the host encoding's geometry, and gain invariance without any learned parameters.
> - Angular coordinates have a bounded, resolution-independent range, so they extrapolate above training resolution where growing grid indices push positions outside the trained range.
> - Payoff depends on which heterogeneity you face, being largest under mixed projections and unseen viewpoints, and the whole scheme rests on accurate calibration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-view 3D perception seeks to infer scene geometry, camera poses, depth, or novel views from multiple images. It is relevant to autonomous driving, robotics, augmented reality, mobile mapping, surveillance, and embodied intelligence. Traditional methods such as structure-from-motion and multi-view stereo explicitly convert image measurements into camera rays and then optimize scene structure. These approaches can accommodate different camera models, but they often require iterative optimization, reliable correspondences, and accurate calibration.

Recent feed-forward vision Transformers, including VGGT, MapAnything, and related systems, replace much of this optimization with learned multi-view aggregation. They are computationally more direct, but many were developed under homogeneous imaging assumptions: the views generally share a pinhole projection, similar fields of view (FoVs), or a fixed camera configuration. In real deployments, cameras may differ in focal length, FoV, device type, distortion, or projection model. A multi-camera rig may combine wide-angle and telephoto cameras, while a variable-FoV camera may change its imaging geometry over time.

The paper focuses on positional encoding, a specific but important component of Transformer attention. Standard positional encodings associate tokens with image-grid coordinates. Camera-aware methods have subsequently introduced absolute ray or point features, or relative encodings involving camera transformations and projected coordinates. However, methods such as GTA and RayRoPE still use image-plane coordinates for at least part of their rotary phase computation. The authors identify a resulting “cross-projection phase mismatch”: the same pair of physical viewing rays can have different pixel or grid separations under different FoVs or projection models. G-ray addresses this issue by representing positions using camera-local ray angles rather than image-plane coordinates.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to develop a relative positional encoding that gives the same physical ray relationship the same positional phase across heterogeneous cameras. The authors seek to achieve four related goals.

First, the method should make relative attention geometrically consistent when images have different focal lengths, FoVs, or projection models. In a pinhole camera, a fixed pixel displacement corresponds to different angular displacements depending on focal length and image location. This relationship is also altered by fisheye or other non-pinhole projections. Consequently, pixel-based relative positions do not have a consistent physical interpretation across views.

Second, the method should preserve the advantages of existing camera-aware encodings rather than replacing their entire geometric formulation. GTA, for example, contains camera-transformation information in addition to image-grid coordinates. RayRoPE uses projected geometric samples, depth-related quantities, and camera-center information. G-ray is designed to replace only the projection-dependent positional coordinate while retaining these complementary components.

Third, the authors evaluate whether the same encoding can support multiple tasks. They test G-ray for feed-forward 3D reconstruction and for novel-view synthesis (NVS), using both a direct RoPE implementation and integrations with GTA and RayRoPE.

Fourth, the paper examines practical robustness. It evaluates mixed pinhole and non-pinhole inputs, controlled FoV changes, resolution increases beyond the training resolution, calibration errors, estimated calibration, and computational overhead.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Ray-Angle Representation

For each token position, G-ray uses a view-specific image-to-ray map \(U_c\). For a calibrated pinhole camera, the map is obtained from the intrinsic matrix:

\[
r_{c,i}=\frac{K_c^{-1}p_{c,i}}{\|K_c^{-1}p_{c,i}\|_2},
\]

where \(p_{c,i}\) is the image-plane token location in homogeneous coordinates. For non-pinhole cameras, \(U_c\) is supplied as a calibrated per-pixel unit-ray map or derived from a projection model.

The unit ray is represented by horizontal and vertical camera-local angles:

\[
\theta_x=\operatorname{atan2}(r_x,r_z), \qquad
\theta_y=\operatorname{atan2}(r_y,r_z).
\]

These angles are expressed in radians and form the positional coordinates used by the rotary encoding. Unlike pixel coordinates, they describe viewing direction directly. The same ray pair therefore produces the same angular difference even if it is sampled by wide-angle, telephoto, pinhole, or fisheye cameras.

#### 3.2. Rotary Relative Attention

G-ray constructs block-diagonal rotations for the horizontal and vertical angles. Each angle is represented using multiple frequency components, following the multi-frequency structure of RoPE. Queries, keys, and values are rotated according to their ray-angle coordinates, and the inverse query rotation is applied to the attention output.

Because rotations compose by angle subtraction, the query-key interaction depends on:

\[
\Delta \phi_k=\omega_k(\theta_{j}-\theta_i),
\]

where \(\omega_k\) is the frequency for channel pair \(k\). Thus, relative phase is determined by angular separation rather than image-grid displacement.

The paper also analyzes a content-free attention probe in which query and key features are held constant. This probe shows the positional prior produced by the encoding alone. Grid-index RoPE produces a prior based on lattice offsets, whereas G-ray favors tokens with similar camera-local viewing directions.

#### 3.3. Integration with Existing Encodings

The method is evaluated in three forms:

1. **Direct G-ray RoPE for 3D reconstruction.** G-ray replaces grid-index RoPE in a VGGT-based architecture. The channel dimension is divided between horizontal and vertical ray angles.
2. **GTA + G-ray for NVS.** G-ray replaces GTA’s image-grid coordinates while retaining GTA’s camera-transformation subspace and other geometric conditioning.
3. **RayRoPE + G-ray for NVS.** G-ray replaces RayRoPE’s query-camera projected pixel coordinates while retaining camera-center, depth, interval, and uncertainty-related components.

The encoding introduces no learned parameters. Its main external requirement is view-specific calibration or a predicted ray map.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design

For 3D reconstruction, the authors use a VGGT-based model with a DINOv2 ViT-L/14 backbone, alternating frame and global attention, pointmap prediction heads, and pose regression. Training uses pinhole images from a mixture of indoor, outdoor, synthetic, driving, and multi-view datasets. The model is trained in two resolution stages, at 224-pixel and 518-pixel long-side inputs, using eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

Evaluation covers three heterogeneous settings:

- **ScanNet++:** mixed non-pinhole DSLR and pinhole iPhone images.
- **PandaSet:** a rigid six-camera automotive rig with camera-specific intrinsics and FoVs.
- **ETH3D:** controlled FoV variation generated through cropping and resizing.

Metrics include pointmap relative error, ray-direction error, depth relative error, point and depth inlier rates, and pose ATE. Homogeneous pinhole benchmarks are also used to assess whether G-ray harms conventional inputs.

For NVS, the authors use the LVSM backbone and train separate models on RealEstate10K and Objaverse. FoV changes are introduced through controlled cropping and resizing in RealEstate10K and through rendering protocols in Objaverse. Performance is evaluated using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Heterogeneous 3D Reconstruction

With supplied calibration, G-ray obtains the lowest averaged value on all six reported metrics across ScanNet++, PandaSet, and ETH3D at 50 views. Its average pointmap relative error is **0.0744**, compared with **0.1372** for MapAnything, corresponding to a 45.8% reduction. Its average point inlier rate is **58.1%**, compared with 37.5% for MapAnything.

The largest difference appears on mixed-projection ScanNet++. With calibrated ray maps, G-ray achieves a pointmap relative error of **0.0583**, ray-depth relative error of **0.0449**, and point inlier rate of **75.1%**. MapAnything records 0.2246, 0.1729, and 26.2%, respectively. G-ray also has the lowest ray-direction error and pose error in this comparison. These results indicate that a model trained on pinhole imagery can process mixed pinhole and fisheye inputs at inference when appropriate ray maps are supplied.

The results are less uniform on the other datasets. On PandaSet, π³-X obtains lower point and depth errors, while G-ray has the lowest ray-direction error at **0.27 degrees**. On ETH3D, VGGT-Ω performs better on several geometry metrics, while G-ray has the lowest ray-direction error at **0.15 degrees**. Thus, the benefit depends on the type of heterogeneity and the metric being measured.

Without external calibration, G-ray driven by AnyCalib-estimated ray maps has the lowest average pointmap error, ray-direction error, and pose ATE among the compared methods. VGGT-Ω remains better on average depth error and inlier rates. This shows that estimated calibration can support G-ray, but with a measurable performance loss relative to ground-truth calibration.

#### 4.2. Homogeneous Pinhole Reconstruction

G-ray remains competitive when all input views use a homogeneous pinhole model. It improves over calibrated MapAnything on TUM pose estimation and on the reported indoor reconstruction accuracy columns for NRGBD and 7-Scenes. However, VGGT-Ω generally performs better, and VGGT has lower error on several video-depth and DTU metrics. The results suggest that G-ray’s angular parameterization does not require heterogeneous inputs to remain usable, although it does not dominate general-purpose homogeneous benchmarks.

#### 4.3. Novel-View Synthesis

On RealEstate10K, both GTA and RayRoPE benefit from replacing image-plane coordinates with G-ray under FoV variation. At a maximum zoom factor of 2.5, GTA + G-ray reaches **20.12 dB PSNR**, compared with **13.44 dB** for GTA. RayRoPE + G-ray improves over RayRoPE by 1.11, 1.13, and 1.10 dB at maximum zoom factors of 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5, respectively. It also produces better SSIM and LPIPS values in these settings.

On Objaverse, GTA + G-ray improves PSNR by 0.78 dB for FoV change, 0.44 dB for novel viewpoints, and 0.88 dB when novel viewpoints are combined with additional FoV change. RayRoPE + G-ray improves by 0.48 dB for novel viewpoints and 1.32 dB for novel viewpoints with additional FoV change. However, RayRoPE + G-ray performs 0.46 dB worse than RayRoPE when the target shares a context pose and only FoV changes. This indicates that the method’s benefit depends on the target-view configuration and host encoding.

#### 4.4. Robustness and Efficiency

G-ray degrades more gradually than comparison methods as zoom heterogeneity increases to a factor of five and as input resolution increases to twice the training scale. In contrast, calibrated MapAnything and image-only VGGT show larger drops in point inliers and pose accuracy.

The method is sensitive to calibration quality. A 5% focal-length perturbation increases average ray-direction error from **0.191 to 0.995 degrees**, while a 20% perturbation increases it to **3.552 degrees**. Assigning intrinsics to the wrong views causes a much larger degradation. Runtime and memory are nearly unchanged: at 50 views, G-ray uses 12.403 GiB and 6.504 seconds, compared with 12.401 GiB and 6.494 seconds for grid-index RoPE.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It identifies a specific incompatibility between image-plane rotary phases and heterogeneous camera geometry, then addresses it through a small coordinate-level modification rather than a new architecture or additional learned module. The representation connects Transformer relative position encoding with the classical geometric concept of camera rays.

Potential applications include multi-camera autonomous systems, mobile mapping, robotics, surveillance networks, and view synthesis using cameras with different lenses or FoVs. The reported pinhole-to-fisheye transfer is particularly relevant for systems that combine consumer cameras, wide-angle sensors, and specialized imaging devices.

The main practical limitation is calibration dependence. G-ray requires accurate and correctly associated image-to-ray geometry, and estimated calibration produces lower accuracy than ground-truth calibration. The experiments also rely heavily on controlled cropping and resizing; real optical zoom may introduce changes in distortion, point-spread function, exposure, and image quality that were not fully tested. The method’s gains are not uniform across all NVS regimes, projection types, resolutions, or datasets. Further evaluation on panoramic, catadioptric, rolling-shutter, and dynamically changing cameras would be needed to establish broader applicability.
