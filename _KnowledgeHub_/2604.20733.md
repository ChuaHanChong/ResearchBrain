# Near-Future Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Near-Future Policy Optimization (NPO) introduces a mixed-policy Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) scheme that leverages verified trajectories from a model's own near-future self to guide current policy training. The automated variant, AutoNPO, achieved an average performance of 63.15% across eight multimodal reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating improved convergence speed and exploration compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Pure on-policy exploration in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) suffers from sparse correct trajectories in early stages and premature convergence in later stages.
- Existing mixed-policy RLVR methods face a fundamental trade-off between the quality of the learning signal (Q) from auxiliary trajectories and the gradient variance (V) incurred when using them.
- Approaches using external teachers offer high Q but incur high V, while replaying past trajectories maintains low V but provides limited Q.

## Method

- **Near-Future Policy Optimization (NPO)** guides the current policy using verified trajectories generated by a ==near-future checkpoint== from the same training run, aiming for an optimal balance between signal quality (**Q**) and variance cost (**V**).
- For prompts where the current policy struggles, a verified correct trajectory from the near-future guide is strategically injected into the rollout group, with optional ==importance sampling correction==.
- **AutoNPO** automates this process by adaptively triggering interventions based on ==training reward and entropy signals==, dynamically selecting the optimal checkpoint distance (Δ) to maximize the effective learning signal **S = Q/V**.

## Results

- **AutoNPO** achieved an average performance of **63.15%** across eight multimodal reasoning benchmarks, surpassing the base model (**57.88%**), pure on-policy **GRPO** (**60.25%**), and other mixed-policy baselines.
- The method demonstrated **accelerated convergence** and maintained **higher policy entropy** during training, leading to higher final validation plateaus compared to standard on-policy RL.
- Ablation studies confirmed NPO variants with and without IS correction performed nearly identically and both significantly outperformed **GRPO**, supporting the dispensability of exact IS correction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An optimal balance between signal quality (Q) and variance cost (V) in mixed-policy RLVR is achieved by leveraging a 'near-future self' checkpoint, empirically demonstrating an interior maximum for the effective learning signal S=Q/V.
> - Importance sampling correction for off-policy trajectories in NPO is largely unnecessary due to the 'near-policy' nature of the guide, simplifying implementation and reducing computational overhead.
> - NPO interventions actively re-expand exploration by preserving higher policy entropy, which prevents premature convergence and enables models to break through performance plateaus.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a post-training methodology for enhancing the capabilities of reasoning models. However, pure on-policy exploration in RLVR faces two primary limitations. During early training phases, the sparsity of correct trajectories can hinder efficient learning. In later stages, the rollout distribution may narrow, leading to premature convergence and performance plateaus.

To mitigate these issues, researchers have explored mixed-policy approaches that incorporate auxiliary trajectories from various sources to enrich the learning signal. These efforts generally fall into two categories:
1. **External Source Trajectories**: Methods like LUFFY, expert prefixes, or interleaved supervised corrections aim to import "stronger" trajectories from external teachers or fully trained models. While these trajectories can offer high signal quality (Q), their distributional divergence from the current policy often introduces significant gradient variance (V), making them challenging to integrate effectively.
2. **Replayed Past Trajectories**: Approaches such as experience replay or restart-style methods reuse successful trajectories generated during earlier stages of the same training run. These trajectories are typically "closer" to the current policy, resulting in lower variance (V). However, their quality (Q) is inherently limited by the capabilities of earlier checkpoints, which may not facilitate substantial gains beyond the current policy's performance.

The tension between signal quality (Q) and variance cost (V) represents a central challenge in mixed-policy RLVR. Existing methods often optimize for one factor at the expense of the other, leading to suboptimal trade-offs. This research positions itself by proposing that an optimal balance can be achieved by leveraging "near-future" checkpoints from the same training run, aiming to provide trajectories that are both sufficiently strong and distributionally close.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the challenge of finding auxiliary trajectories that are simultaneously strong enough to teach the current policy new knowledge and close enough to be readily absorbed, thereby maximizing the effective learning signal in RLVR. The authors formalize this as optimizing a trade-off between two quantities associated with any off-policy trajectory source:
- **Signal Quality (Q)**: Defined as the fraction of prompts on which the current policy fails, for which the auxiliary source can produce a verified-correct trajectory. Q is hypothesized to increase as the guide policy becomes stronger (i.e., further along the optimization path).
- **Variance Cost (V)**: Represents the gradient variance incurred when trajectories from a different policy are incorporated using importance weighting (π_θ / π_off-policy). V is hypothesized to increase, approximately exponentially, as the distributional divergence between the current policy and the auxiliary source grows.

The effective learning signal is defined as **S = Q/V**. The core motivation is that existing mixed-policy methods occupy suboptimal regions in this Q-V trade-off space. External teachers offer high Q but incur high V, leading to a diminished effective S. Past-trajectory replay methods maintain moderate V but are limited by modest Q. Pure on-policy RLVR avoids variance but provides no Q beyond the current policy's exploration.

The authors hypothesize that a "near-future" checkpoint from the *same training run* offers a natural solution. Such a checkpoint is considered:
1. **Stronger**: Being further along the optimization path, it possesses greater capability and can generate more verified-correct trajectories (higher Q) than the current policy or past checkpoints.
2. **Closer**: Sharing the same initialization, architecture, and optimization history, its parameter distance from the current policy remains relatively small and controllable. This helps in keeping the importance-weighting variance (V) low.

By providing a tunable handle on this trade-off (the step distance ∆ to the near-future checkpoint), the research aims to identify and leverage an optimal ∆ that maximizes S. This approach is motivated by the potential to accelerate RLVR convergence and raise the final performance ceiling, thereby overcoming the structural limits of pure on-policy exploration and the inefficiencies of existing mixed-policy methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Near-Future Policy Optimization (NPO), is a mixed-policy scheme designed to guide a current policy using verified trajectories from its own near-future self.

#### 3.1. Core Operation of NPO

NPO modifies the standard RLVR training loop by adjusting how the rollout group is formed.
- **Guide Policy Selection**: For a training step `t` with current policy `π^(t)`, NPO identifies a near-future checkpoint `π^(t+∆)` from the same training run. `∆` is the number of optimization steps separating the current and guide policies.
- **Guidance Trajectory Generation**: Prior to an NPO segment, `π^(t+∆)` is used to generate trajectories for each prompt `x` offline. These trajectories are then verified, and one correct trajectory per prompt (`o'_x`) is cached to serve as guidance. Prompts for which `π^(t+∆)` cannot produce a correct trajectory are excluded from the guidance set. This caching is performed once per segment, reducing online computational overhead.
- **Rollout Group Modification**: At step `t`, the current policy `π^(t)` samples `n` on-policy trajectories. A cached guidance trajectory `o'_x` is injected into the rollout group *only if* the current policy is struggling on prompt `x` (i.e., its on-policy pass-rate `ˆp(x)` falls below a threshold `τ_gate`) and `o'_x` is available. If these conditions are met, the `n`-th slot of the rollout group is replaced by `o'_x`; otherwise, the original on-policy rollout `o_n` is retained.
- **Objective Function**: Group-relative advantages are computed over the modified rollout group. The learning objective remains the standard clipped objective (Equation 4), with importance sampling (IS) correction applied only for the guidance slot where `o'_x` originated from `π^(t+∆)`. The authors note that, empirically, due to the near-policy nature of the guide, the IS correction can be marginal, and treating `o'_x` as on-policy yields similar performance.

#### 3.2. Two Manual Interventions (Validation Probes)

To validate NPO's effectiveness across different training phases, two manual interventions were implemented:
- **Early-Stage Bootstrapping**: A short "scout run" of the base model is performed, and its final checkpoint (`π^(t+∆)`) is used to guide a fresh training run from the same base weights during its initial, sparse-reward window. This aims to accelerate early convergence.
- **Late-Stage Plateau Breakthrough**: When a training run plateaus, it is continued for a short duration to obtain a stronger checkpoint (`π^(t+∆)`). The plateaued segment is then replayed, guided by this stronger checkpoint on the same prompts, with the goal of breaking through the performance ceiling.

#### 3.3. AutoNPO: Adaptive Intervention

Building on the insights from manual interventions, AutoNPO automates the process of intervention timing and guide selection.
- **Mistake Pool Maintenance**: A lightweight mistake pool (B) is maintained, storing identifiers and failure steps for prompts where the current policy's group accuracy falls below a threshold. This pool is critical for triggering interventions, estimating the effective learning signal, and defining prompt sets for guidance.
- **Intervention Triggering**:
 - **Warning Stage (Zero-cost)**: Monitors the Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of training reward and policy entropy. A warning fires if reward stagnates and entropy declines concurrently, indicating potential exploration collapse.
 - **Confirmation Stage**: If the warning persists, the current policy `π^(t)` is rolled out on a small subset of the mistake pool (B). If the resulting pass-rate `ˆp` exceeds a threshold `τ_pass`, an intervention is confirmed.
- **Rollback Distance Selection (∆^*)**: Upon confirmation, AutoNPO calculates `∆^* = arg max_∆ ˆQ(∆)/ˆV(∆)`.
 - `ˆQ(∆)` is estimated as the pass-rate of `π^(t)` on `B_∆`, which are the prompts from the mistake pool that failed during the segment `t - ∆` to `t`.
 - `ˆV(∆)` is estimated using a variance proxy derived from the per-token KL divergence between `π^(t)` and `π^(t-∆)`, following a predicted exponential form.
 - The `ˆQ(∆)` values are obtained from the single confirmation rollout, minimizing overhead.
- **Execution**: The current policy `π^(t)` (which becomes the guide) generates verified-correct trajectories for prompts in `B_∆^*`. Training then rolls back to checkpoint `t - ∆^*` and resumes with the NPO mechanism, using the cached guidance. Unlike manual NPO, AutoNPO restricts cache generation and substitution to `B_∆^*` for efficiency. After the replayed segment, a cooldown period `T_cool` is initiated before the controller re-arms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted using Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct as the base policy, trained on MMFineReason-123K, and evaluated across eight multimodal reasoning benchmarks including MathVista, MMMU-Pro, and ZeroBench. Baselines included GRPO (pure on-policy), LUFFY (external teacher), ExGRPO (historical replay), and RLEP (far future replay).

- **Empirical Validation of Q-V Trade-off**: The theoretical prediction of an optimal effective learning signal S(∆) = Q(∆)/V(∆) was empirically supported. Measurements showed Q(∆) rising concavely and V(∆) growing exponentially with checkpoint distance ∆. Their ratio, S(∆), exhibited a distinct interior maximum, confirming that an ideal guide is neither too close (low Q) nor too far (explosive V). Specifically, S peaked at ∆* ≈ 20 steps from the base policy and ∆* ≈ 70 steps from a mid-training policy.
- **Performance Improvements**:
 - NPO variants consistently outperformed existing baselines. The average performance on reasoning tasks increased from 57.88% (Base Model) and 60.25% (GRPO) to 62.84% with NPO (early + late-stage interventions) and **63.15% with AutoNPO**.
 - LUFFY, despite leveraging an external teacher, performed as the weakest RL-trained model, regressing below the base model on one benchmark (WeMath), which provided empirical evidence for the issue of high variance cost (V) overwhelming signal quality (Q).
 - Replay-based methods (ExGRPO, RLEP) showed improvements over GRPO but plateaued below NPO, supporting the claim that their signal quality (Q) is capped by earlier checkpoints.
 - NPO's early-stage intervention alone surpassed all other baselines on average (62.12%) and significantly improved performance on ZeroBench (26.35% vs. GRPO's 22.60%), a benchmark characterized by sparse correct trajectories.
 - The late-stage intervention, when combined, yielded further gains on benchmarks requiring deeper reasoning (e.g., WeMath, MathVerse), demonstrating its ability to break through performance plateaus.
 - AutoNPO achieved the highest overall average score and secured the top position on five of the eight evaluation tasks, indicating that its adaptive mechanism can identify and leverage intervention opportunities at least as effectively as manual scheduling.
- **Training Dynamics**:
 - AutoNPO demonstrated accelerated convergence, with training reward rising more quickly and maintaining higher levels throughout optimization compared to GRPO.
 - A key mechanism identified was AutoNPO's ability to preserve higher policy entropy. While GRPO showed a steady decline in entropy, indicative of exploration collapse, AutoNPO's interventions actively re-expanded exploration, preventing premature narrowing of the rollout distribution. This re-expanded exploration contributed to a higher late-stage validation plateau.
 - The improvements in training reward were observed as discrete, step-wise gains following intervention windows, suggesting targeted rather than diffuse optimization.
- **Importance-Sampling Correction Ablation**: An ablation study confirmed that exact importance sampling (IS) correction is largely unnecessary for NPO. Both NPO variants (with and without IS correction) significantly outperformed GRPO and were nearly indistinguishable from each other in performance. This is attributed to the "near-policy" property of the guide, which keeps the `π_θ / π_off-policy` ratio close to one. This finding allows for practical simplifications in implementation, saving memory and compute, a benefit that is specific to NPO and not applicable to mixed-policy methods with distributionally distant guides.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Near-Future Policy Optimization (NPO) as a contribution to the field of reinforcement learning for large language and multimodal models.

- **Theoretical Framework**: The work formalizes the quality-variance trade-off in mixed-policy RLVR using the effective learning signal S = Q/V. It theoretically and empirically establishes that a "near-future self" within the same training run offers an optimal balance between signal quality and variance cost, identifying a unique optimal checkpoint distance. This conceptualization provides a new lens for analyzing and designing mixed-policy reinforcement learning strategies.
- **Improved Performance and Efficiency**: NPO consistently demonstrates superior performance over existing mixed-policy baselines and pure on-policy RLVR, both in accelerating convergence during early training and in raising the final performance ceiling. The observed gains across diverse multimodal reasoning benchmarks suggest the robustness and generalizability of the approach. Furthermore, the finding that importance sampling correction can be largely omitted due to the near-policy nature of the guide offers practical advantages in terms of reduced computational overhead and simplified implementation.
- **Adaptive and Automated Learning**: AutoNPO represents a significant advancement by automating the intervention process. By leveraging online training signals (reward stagnation, entropy decline) and empirically estimating the optimal effective learning signal, AutoNPO removes the need for manual tuning of intervention timing and rollback distance. This adaptive framework enhances the practical applicability and scalability of the method across different tasks and longer training runs.
- **Contribution to Self-Taught RLVR**: This work is explicitly framed as a contribution to the broader "Self-Taught RLVR" research program, which explores how reasoning models can learn from themselves. By focusing on the "temporal self" (a near-future version guiding a past self), NPO bridges mixed-policy RLVR and self-distillation/self-taught approaches. This opens new avenues for future research into other dimensions of self-guidance, such as the "informed self" or "parallel self."
- **Future Directions**: The paper encourages further exploration of alternative mechanisms for injecting learnable signals from the near-future self, specifically mentioning On-Policy Distillation as a potential area for follow-up work. This suggests a broader impact on how models might leverage their own evolving capabilities for continuous improvement.
