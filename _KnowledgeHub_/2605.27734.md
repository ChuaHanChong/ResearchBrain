# Learn from your own latents and not from tokens: A sample-complexity theory

> [!summary] Summary
> This work presents a sample-complexity theory demonstrating that learning from a model's own latent representations significantly improves data efficiency for hierarchical structures. It shows that iterative latent prediction achieves a sample complexity proportional to the vocabulary size and branching factor, constant with respect to hierarchy depth, in contrast to the exponential dependency found in token-level prediction methods.

## Problem

- Large language models and other generative AI require orders of magnitude more training data than biological learning systems for comparable competence.
- Previous theoretical analyses of learning hierarchical structures showed sample complexity growing exponentially with the hierarchy's depth for supervised and token-level self-supervised methods.
- A quantitative understanding of the data efficiency benefits of latent-prediction self-supervised learning and the role of hierarchical stacking remained limited.

## Method

- Researchers utilized the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM), a simplified probabilistic context-free grammar, to theoretically analyze sample complexity.
- They designed an Iterative Latent Clustering (ILC) algorithm that recursively learns hierarchical latents by clustering 'cousin context vectors,' and validated it with an end-to-end Stacked Latent-Clustering (SLC) neural network.
- A theoretical framework was developed to explain how `data2vec`, a prominent ==self-supervised== method, implicitly performs hierarchical latent prediction through its teacher-student mechanism.

## Results

- Theoretical proof for the ILC algorithm shows it recovers the RHM latent tree with a sample complexity of P∝vm3P \propto vm^3P∝vm3, independent of hierarchy depth L.
- Numerical simulations of ILC and experiments with the SLC network empirically confirmed the P∝vm3P \propto vm^3P∝vm3 sample complexity for recovering the top-level latent, even when using local learning rules.
- `data2vec` pretrained on RHM data empirically demonstrated a root-classification sample complexity of P∝vm3P \propto vm^3P∝vm3, consistent with implicit hierarchical latent prediction and more efficient than token-level methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning from latent representations preserves the statistical signal strength for synonym correlations across all hierarchical levels, preventing the signal attenuation seen in token-level prediction.
> - Hierarchical latent prediction reduces sample complexity from an exponential dependence on hierarchy depth (L) to a constant factor (proportional to vm3vm^3vm3) for learning the full latent tree.
> - General self-supervised methods like `data2vec` can implicitly discover multi-scale latent structures, suggesting that explicitly hierarchical architectures may not always be necessary for these benefits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a disparity in data efficiency between contemporary generative models (e.g., large language models, diffusion models) and biological learning systems. Current state-of-the-art models, while achieving high performance, require training data volumes orders of magnitude larger than those encountered by biological learners for comparable competence. For instance, frontier Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on 10^13–10^14 tokens, which exceeds a child's linguistic exposure by more than five orders of magnitude.

Several hypotheses attempt to explain this discrepancy, with two prominent lines of inquiry: multimodal and grounded learning, and learning within abstract latent spaces. This paper focuses on the latter, investigating the efficiency benefits of learning from latent representations rather than directly from raw input tokens.

This approach builds upon a paradigm in self-supervised learning (SSL) where networks are trained to predict their own latent representations of related data views or masked regions, exemplified by methods such as BYOL, DINO, data2vec, and JEPA. This concept also aligns with predictive coding theories in computational neuroscience, which propose that the cortex predicts its own future activity. While these latent-prediction methods have demonstrated empirical success, a comprehensive theoretical understanding of their data efficiency benefits and the utility of hierarchical stacking remains limited.

The authors position their work within the context of previous theoretical analyses of the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM), a simplified Probabilistic Context-Free Grammar (PCFG). Prior studies using the RHM have provided quantitative theories for neural scaling laws and compositional generalization in deep networks, demonstrating that supervised learning and token-level SSL on RHM data require sample complexities exponential in the depth of the latent hierarchy. This paper extends this analytical framework to explore the sample complexity of self-supervised learning methods that utilize latent prediction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research is to contribute to understanding the significant data efficiency gap between biological learning and current high-performing generative artificial intelligence models. The paper seeks to investigate an alternative learning paradigm where models predict their own latent representations, which is hypothesized to be more data-efficient than predicting raw input tokens.

Specifically, the research pursues two key objectives:

1. **Quantify Data Efficiency Improvement:** To determine the extent to which training networks to predict their own latent representations (as opposed to raw tokens) improves data efficiency. This involves providing a quantitative measure of the sample complexity required for such latent-prediction methods compared to token-level prediction objectives.
2. **Evaluate Benefits of Hierarchical Stacking:** To assess whether stacking multiple latent-prediction modules into multi-scale hierarchies offers additional benefits, particularly in terms of data efficiency. This objective aims to clarify the role of explicit architectural hierarchies in these learning paradigms.

To address these objectives, the authors chose to utilize the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM). The RHM is a tractable probabilistic context-free grammar designed to capture the compositional structure found in natural language and images. This model is motivated by its ability to generate data with a well-defined hierarchical latent structure and by its previous utility in yielding quantitatively predictive theories for the sample complexity of supervised and token-level self-supervised learning. Prior work on RHM demonstrated that recovering the latent tree using supervised or token-level SSL requires a number of samples exponential in the latent tree's depth (L). The authors hypothesize that latent prediction methods can overcome this exponential dependence, thereby offering significant data efficiency gains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combination of theoretical analysis, algorithmic design, and empirical validation using neural networks. The core of the methodology revolves around the **Random Hierarchy Model (RHM)**, a simplified probabilistic context-free grammar.

#### 3.1. The Random Hierarchy Model (RHM)

The RHM generates strings of visible tokens (at level 0) by recursively applying production rules along a tree of hidden symbols (latent variables) of depth L. Key parameters include:
- **Depth (L):** The number of latent levels in the tree.
- **Branching factor (s):** The number of children a parent node can have.
- **Vocabulary size (v):** The number of unique symbols at each level.
- **Production rules:** For each level $\ell$, $vm$ distinct tuples from $V_{\ell}^s$ are chosen and partitioned into $v$ labeled groups of size $m$, one for each parent symbol in $V_{\ell+1}$. This results in $m$ synonymous choices for each parent. The grammar is unambiguous, meaning each grammatical tuple has a unique parent.
- **Generation:** Proceeds top-down, with the root symbol chosen uniformly, and subsequent child tuples sampled uniformly from rules associated with their parent.

#### 3.2. Correlation-Based Learning in the RHM

The authors leverage a prior observation that learning the RHM hierarchy corresponds to identifying invariances under synonym exchange. Statistical signals for these synonym classes are derived from correlations between a tuple of tokens and an external observable (Z). Each "unresolved production rule" in the path between the tuple and Z attenuates the signal by a factor of $m$, increasing the required sample complexity.
- **Supervised learning:** Observable Z is the root label. Sample complexity $P \sim vm^{L-\ell}$ for learning level-($\ell$+1) rules, with overall complexity $\sim vm^L$.
- **Token-level self-supervision (e.g., Masked Language Modeling):** Observable Z is a masked token. Learning level-1 latents requires $P \sim vm^3$. However, learning higher-level latents (level $\ell \ge 1$) still involves predicting a surface token, leading to an attenuation factor of $m$ per additional latent level. This results in $P \sim vm^{\ell+2}$, and an overall sample complexity of $P \sim vm^{L+1}$.

The central hypothesis of this work is that "learn-from-your-own-latent" methods remove this "residual token bottleneck." By lifting the prediction target to an abstract latent space, the statistical signal strength for synonym clustering remains constant across all hierarchical levels. This suggests a sample complexity of $P \sim vm^3$ for all levels, irrespective of L.

#### 3.3. Iterative Latent Clustering (ILC) Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

The authors formalize their hypothesis with the ILC algorithm, a theoretical, level-by-level clustering procedure:
1. **Initialization:** Visible tokens are set as level-0 latents ($\hat{h}^{(0)}_i = x_i$).
2. **Iteration (for $\ell = 0$ to $L-2$):**
 - Form all observed level-$\ell$ tuples ($\hat{T}^{(\ell)}_u$).
 - Estimate the support of grammatical tuples.
 - For each observed tuple $\nu$, compute an **empirical cousin context vector** $\hat{\phi}_{\ell}(\nu)$. This vector represents the conditional expectation of a one-hot encoding of a fixed level-$\ell$ element in a *cousin tuple* (sharing the $\ell+2$ grandparent with $\hat{T}^{(\ell)}_u$), given $\nu$.
 - Cluster these context vectors into $v$ groups, where each group corresponds to a synonym class.
 - Define the next-level latent label ($\hat{h}^{(\ell+1)}_u$) by the cluster identity.
3. **Assumptions:** The theoretical analysis of ILC relies on two assumptions:
 - **Balanced and separated grammar:** The grammar ensures that all grammatical tuples occur with similar probability, and the true parent context vectors are sufficiently separated in space.
 - **Stable clustering module:** The clustering algorithm can correctly partition points if they are sufficiently close to their true cluster centers and centers are sufficiently separated.

#### 3.4. Stacked Latent-Clustering (SLC) Network

To validate the ILC theory with a neural architecture, the authors propose the SLC network, an end-to-end differentiable model:
- **Architecture:** Consists of L-1 identical modules, each mirroring an ILC step.
- **Module Components:**
 - **Predictor (p):** A CNN-based subnetwork that takes an $s$-tuple of level-$\ell$ tokens and outputs a categorical distribution over its cousin tokens. Trained with a cross-entropy loss.
 - **Clusterer (C):** A CNN-based subnetwork that maps the predictor's output vector to a soft assignment in a discrete codebook. Trained with a contrastive objective that encourages similar predictions to cluster together and dissimilar ones to separate, along with a sparsity regularization.
- **Training:** Uses a teacher-student framework, where teacher weights are an exponential moving average (EMA) of student weights, for stability against representation collapse. Gradients are aggregated via the UPGrad algorithm for multi-objective optimization.
- **Local Learning Rules:** Experiments include variants with stop-gradients between modules and between predictor/clusterer subnetworks to test biological plausibility.

#### 3.5. Analysis of data2vec

The paper provides a theoretical and empirical analysis of data2vec, a prominent self-supervised latent-prediction method:
- **Mechanism:** In a teacher-student setup, the student network predicts the averaged last K block activations of a teacher network applied to an unmasked input, at masked token positions. The teacher weights are an EMA of the student's.
- **Theoretical Connection:** The authors propose that data2vec implicitly performs hierarchical latent prediction by arguing:
 - **(A1) Targets carry the learned latents:** Teacher targets linearly encode the RHM latents that the encoder has already learned.
 - **(A2) Correlation learning:** Gradient descent extracts features whose correlations with the target become detectable.
- **Phased Learning:** The EMA teacher implicitly enables a sequence of learning phases. In each phase, the teacher incorporates newly learned latents from the student, effectively lifting the prediction task to a higher latent level, analogous to the ILC.
- **Empirical Validation:** Data2vec is pretrained on RHM data, and a linear probe is used to evaluate its ability to classify the root label from its learned representations. Direct measurements of "synonym clustering score" within data2vec's encoder are also performed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several findings regarding the data efficiency of latent-prediction self-supervised learning on hierarchical data:

1. **Theoretical Sample Complexity for Latent Prediction:** The study proves that for the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM), an iterative latent clustering algorithm (ILC) can recover the full non-root latent tree with a sample complexity of $P \propto vm^3$, up to logarithmic factors. This indicates that the required number of samples is constant with respect to the depth (L) of the latent hierarchy. This result stands in contrast to previous findings for supervised learning ($P \propto vm^L$) and token-level self-supervised learning ($P \propto vm^{L+1}$), both of which show an exponential dependence on L. The constant $vm^3$ scaling is attributed to the fact that learning from latents maintains the statistical strength of synonym correlations across all hierarchical levels, preventing the attenuation factor $m$ per level observed in token-level objectives.

2. **Validation of Iterative Latent Clustering (ILC):** Numerical simulations of the ILC algorithm, utilizing k-means for clustering, confirm the theoretical prediction. The clustering reconstruction accuracy for all non-root levels collapses onto a single curve when the number of samples is rescaled by $vm^3$, indicating that the threshold for recovery is indeed independent of L and scales as $m^3$.

3. **End-to-End Stacked Latent-Clustering (SLC) Network Performance:** An end-to-end differentiable neural network architecture, the Stacked Latent-Clustering (SLC) network, was developed to mimic the ILC algorithm. When trained with gradient descent, this network also achieved a sample complexity of $P \propto vm^3$ for recovering the top-level latent variable (root classification).
 - **Local Learning Rules:** The study also demonstrated that this $vm^3$ scaling is maintained even when stop-gradients are introduced between modules (layer-wise training) or between the predictor and clusterer within a module. This suggests that explicit global backpropagation is not strictly necessary for this data efficiency, supporting the biological plausibility of local learning rules.
 - **Training Dynamics:** Analysis of training dynamics showed that learning progresses layer-wise, with the successful clustering at one level enabling the learning of the next higher level.

4. **Analysis of data2vec:**
 - **Empirical Sample Complexity:** Pretraining data2vec on the RHM empirically demonstrated a root-classification sample complexity of $P \propto vm^3$. This result is incompatible with the $vm^{L+1}$ scaling expected for token-level prediction and is even more efficient than $vm^L$ for supervised learning.
 - **Theoretical Explanation:** The authors provide a theoretical framework explaining data2vec's $vm^3$ scaling. This framework posits that data2vec implicitly performs hierarchical latent prediction. Through a teacher-student mechanism with exponential moving average (EMA) teacher updates, the teacher targets progressively incorporate the latents learned by the student. This effectively transforms the problem at each hierarchical level into a local clustering task identical to that solved by the ILC algorithm, thereby preserving the $vm^3$ sample complexity across levels.
 - **Internal Synonym Clustering:** Direct measurement of the "synonym clustering score" within data2vec's encoder layers confirmed that the network collapses synonymous tuples onto common representations. These clustering scores for various latent levels also collapse onto a common curve when samples are rescaled by $vm^3$, providing direct evidence that data2vec's encoder implements the same recursive clustering mechanism as the ILC.
 - **Latent Encoding in Teacher Targets:** It was observed that teacher targets linearly encode the RHM latents at different levels, and this encoding becomes detectable at the $vm^3$ sample scale.

5. **Implications for Explicit Stacking:** The findings suggest that general self-supervised methods like data2vec may already implicitly discover multi-scale latent structures within data. This observation weakens the argument for explicitly hierarchical architectures such as H-JEPA, as a single-module latent prediction system can achieve similar benefits without explicit architectural stacking.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of representation learning and self-supervised methods, with implications for both artificial intelligence and computational neuroscience.

**Fundamental Understanding of Data Efficiency:** The primary significance lies in providing a quantitative theoretical explanation for the data efficiency benefits of "learn-from-your-own-latent" self-supervised learning. By rigorously demonstrating that latent prediction can reduce sample complexity from an exponential dependence on hierarchical depth (L) to a constant ($P \propto vm^3$), the work establishes a foundational principle: latents at the same hierarchical level are more strongly correlated with each other than with raw tokens. This amplified signal allows for more efficient learning compared to token-level prediction, which dilutes this signal.

**Implications for Large Language Models and Generative AI:** The findings suggest that current generative models, particularly large language models (LLMs) trained with next-token prediction, might be operating suboptimally in their use of training data. If the principles derived from the RHM generalize to real-world data, integrating own-latent self-supervision could lead to systematic improvements in data efficiency, potentially enabling the training of competent models with significantly less data. This could offer a pathway to "break" existing neural scaling laws, which often dictate proportional increases in performance with computational resources and data.

**Architectural Design and Redundancy of Stacking:** The analysis of data2vec's implicit hierarchical learning challenges the conventional wisdom that explicit architectural stacking (like in H-JEPA) is always necessary for multi-scale representation learning. The results suggest that sophisticated single-module latent prediction methods can inherently perform multi-scale latent discovery. This could guide the design of more efficient and simpler network architectures for hierarchical data.

**Biological Plausibility and Cognitive Science:** The demonstration that the Stacked Latent-Clustering (SLC) network can achieve optimal sample complexity even with local learning rules (e.g., via stop-gradients, without end-to-end backpropagation) enhances the biological plausibility of such mechanisms. This aligns with theories like predictive coding in the brain, suggesting that the human brain's remarkable data efficiency might stem from similar local, latent-space predictive processes.

**Future Research Directions:**
- **Empirical Validation on Real Data:** The work motivates controlled empirical comparisons between latent-supervised generative models and token-level prediction baselines on natural language and image datasets. Such experiments could test if the predicted sharp divergence in data efficiency at small datasets and convergence at large datasets holds true in more complex settings.
- **Generalization of RHM:** Extending the theoretical analysis of the RHM to more complex grammars (e.g., variable tree topologies, recursive rules, context-dependent rules) would bring the theory closer to natural data and refine the understanding of different architectures' strengths and weaknesses.
- **Analysis of Other Hierarchical Architectures:** The paper identifies other recent self-supervised architectures (e.g., V-JEPA 2.1, Bootleg) that employ multi-scale teachers. Theoretically studying these architectures in the RHM framework could provide further insights into their learning mechanisms and sample complexity.
- **Synthetic Data as a "Laboratory":** The research highlights the value of synthetic data models like the RHM as controlled environments for designing, testing, and theoretically understanding novel machine learning architectures before large-scale deployment.
