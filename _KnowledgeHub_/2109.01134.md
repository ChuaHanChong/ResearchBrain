# Learning to Prompt for Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Context Optimization (CoOp) automates prompt engineering for large pre-trained vision-language models like CLIP by learning continuous context vectors. This method achieves superior few-shot performance on diverse image recognition tasks and surprisingly enhances domain generalization capabilities compared to hand-crafted prompts and linear probing.

## Problem

- Manually crafting effective textual prompts for vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP is time-consuming, highly sensitive to minor wording changes, and requires specialized domain expertise.
- Adapting powerful pre-trained VLMs to specific downstream tasks often requires extensive fine-tuning or designing new classifiers, which can be computationally expensive and data-intensive.
- There was a lack of automated, data-efficient methods to leverage the strong zero-shot capabilities of VLMs while also improving their performance on task-specific data.

## Method

- ==Context Optimization (CoOp)== replaces hand-crafted textual prompt contexts with ==learnable, continuous vectors== that are optimized directly from a small amount of labeled data.
- The method keeps the entire pre-trained vision-language model (specifically, CLIP's image and text encoders) frozen during training, only updating the minimal set of context vectors.
- CoOp explores two main context designs: a ==unified context shared across all classes==, and a class-specific context (CSC) where each class learns its own set of context vectors.

## Results

- Context Optimization (CoOp) consistently outperforms zero-shot CLIP with hand-crafted prompts, achieving average gains of approximately **15%** in 16-shot scenarios across **11** diverse datasets.
- CoOp significantly enhances CLIP's robustness to domain shifts, outperforming zero-shot CLIP and linear probe models on out-of-distribution datasets like ImageNet-V2, -Sketch, -A, and -R.
- Unified context generally performs better in low-data regimes (e.g., **1-8 shots**), while class-specific context (CSC) can be more effective for fine-grained classification tasks when more training data is available.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing continuous prompt vectors directly from data is a highly effective and parameter-efficient strategy for adapting large vision-language models (VLMs) to diverse downstream tasks.
> - Learned prompts can surprisingly enhance VLM robustness to domain shifts, outperforming even zero-shot methods on out-of-distribution benchmarks, which challenges prior assumptions about transfer robustness.
> - The benefits of prompt learning are consistent across different VLM vision backbones (e.g., ResNet, ViT), indicating its broad applicability beyond specific architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the crucial intersection of large-scale pre-trained models, vision-language understanding, and efficient model adaptation, a rapidly evolving area in artificial intelligence.

**The Rise of Vision-Language Models (VLMs):** Traditionally, visual recognition systems relied on training models with discrete labels for a fixed set of categories. This "closed-set" paradigm limits adaptability to new categories, as it necessitates retraining or extensive fine-tuning with new data. The emergence of large pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like OpenAI's CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) and Google's ALIGN marked a paradigm shift. These models learn to align images and raw texts in a shared feature space by training on massive datasets of image-text pairs. This contrastive learning objective enables unprecedented "zero-shot" transferability, where a model can classify unseen categories by simply comparing image features with text-generated classification weights (e.g., "a photo of a [CLASS]"). This capability has democratized access to powerful visual representations.

**The Challenge of Prompt Engineering:** While zero-shot transfer via prompting is revolutionary, a significant practical hurdle quickly emerged: "prompt engineering." This refers to the process of manually crafting the textual descriptions (prompts) that guide the VLM for a specific downstream task. As the paper highlights, identifying the "right" prompt is non-trivial, time-consuming, and highly sensitive to subtle wording changes. A minor alteration in a prompt (e.g., adding "a" or specifying "a type of texture") can drastically affect performance. This manual tuning requires domain expertise and an intuitive understanding of the model's biases, making VLM deployment cumbersome.

**Inspiration from NLP Prompt Learning:** The authors draw direct inspiration from parallel advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP). Large language models (LLMs) like GPT-3 also exhibited sensitivity to prompt wording, leading to the development of "prompt learning" or "prompt tuning" techniques. These methods automate the process of finding effective prompts, often by optimizing "soft prompts" or "continuous prompts"—learnable vectors in the embedding space rather than discrete words. This paper is among the first to successfully adapt and apply these continuous prompt learning principles to vision-language models, bridging a gap between recent NLP and computer vision innovations.

**Context of Foundation Models:** The work also aligns with the broader movement towards "foundation models." These are massive, pre-trained models (like CLIP, GPT-3, Florence) that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks. The paper explicitly states its goal of facilitating the "democratization of foundation models," making them "easier and cheaper to adapt for the wider community." By automating prompt engineering, CoOp contributes directly to lowering the barrier for leveraging these powerful, yet often unwieldy, models. It shifts the focus from bespoke model training to efficient model adaptation, which is becoming a dominant theme in contemporary AI research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **automate the process of prompt engineering for large pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP, enabling more efficient and effective adaptation for diverse downstream image recognition tasks.**

This core objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Overcoming the Bottleneck of Manual Prompt Engineering:** The authors critically identify manual prompt engineering as a major practical challenge for deploying VLMs. They demonstrate that subtle variations in prompt wording can lead to significant performance fluctuations (e.g., over 5% accuracy difference on Caltech101 by simply adding "a"). This manual, trial-and-error process is time-consuming, requires specialized domain knowledge, and does not guarantee optimal performance. CoOp aims to eliminate this bottleneck by making the process programmatic.

2. **Enhancing Data Efficiency for Downstream Tasks:** While VLMs offer zero-shot capabilities, performance can often be boosted with some task-specific data. The paper is motivated to turn these models into "data-efficient visual learners." By learning optimal prompts from a small number of labeled examples (few-shot learning), CoOp seeks to achieve substantial performance gains over purely zero-shot methods and even strong baselines like linear probing, without requiring extensive fine-tuning of the large pre-trained model.

3. **Leveraging Advances in NLP Prompt Learning:** Recognizing the similar challenges faced by large language models, the authors are motivated to transfer the successful paradigm of "continuous prompt learning" from NLP to the vision-language domain. This involves optimizing context vectors in an embedding space rather than searching for discrete words, offering greater flexibility and leveraging the gradient-based optimization power of deep learning.

4. **Improving Model Transferability and Generalization:** A crucial motivation is to not only enhance performance on known downstream tasks but also to maintain or even improve the robustness of VLMs to domain shifts. While zero-shot CLIP has shown strong out-of-distribution generalization, learning-based adaptation methods often risk overfitting to the source distribution. The authors explicitly investigate whether prompt learning can achieve strong transfer performance *without* sacrificing generalization ability, ideally even improving it.

5. **Democratization of Foundation Models:** The ultimate underlying motivation, as articulated by the authors, is to make powerful "foundation models" (like CLIP) more accessible and easier to deploy for a wider range of users and applications. By simplifying the adaptation process, CoOp contributes to lowering the expertise and resource requirements for leveraging these advanced AI capabilities.

In essence, CoOp's motivation is to bridge the gap between the theoretical promise of powerful pre-trained VLMs and their practical, efficient deployment across various real-world computer vision tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Context Optimization (CoOp)**, addresses the prompt engineering problem by replacing hand-crafted textual context with learnable continuous vectors. The core idea is to optimize these context vectors directly from a small amount of labeled data, while keeping the parameters of the large pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM) (specifically, CLIP) entirely frozen.

#### 3.1. Vision-Language Pre-training (CLIP Overview)

The paper first provides a brief overview of CLIP, the foundational VLM used in their experiments. CLIP consists of two encoders: an image encoder (e.g., ResNet-50, ViT) and a text encoder (Transformer-based). It's trained with a contrastive loss on a massive dataset of image-text pairs to align image and text embeddings in a common feature space.
For zero-shot inference, classification weights for new categories are synthesized by feeding natural language prompts (e.g., "a photo of a [CLASS].") to the text encoder. The image features are then compared against these text-generated weights using cosine similarity to determine classification probabilities.

#### 3.2. Context Optimization (CoOp) Details

CoOp's central innovation lies in modeling the prompt's context words as learnable vectors.
Let `g(·)` denote the text encoder. Instead of a fixed textual prompt like "a photo of a [CLASS].", CoOp constructs a prompt `t` with `M` learnable context vectors `[V]1, [V]2..., [V]M`.

**Main Implementations:**

1. **Unified Context:**
 - **Structure:** The prompt takes the form: `t = [V]1 [V]2 ... [V]M [CLASS]`. Here, all classes share the *same* set of `M` context vectors.
 - **Flexibility:** The paper also explores placing the `[CLASS]` token in the middle of the context vectors: `t = [V]1 ... [V]M/2 [CLASS] [V]M/2+1 ... [V]M`. This allows the context to surround the class token, potentially capturing richer relationships.
 - **Rationale:** This version is simpler, has fewer parameters, and is expected to generalize well across categories within a dataset.

2. **Class-Specific Context (CSC):**
 - **Structure:** For each class `i`, a *separate* set of context vectors `[V]i1, [V]i2..., [V]iM` is learned. Thus, `[Vi1] ... [ViM]` is independent of `[Vj1] ... [VjM]` for `i ≠ j`.
 - **Rationale:** This offers greater expressive power, allowing the model to learn highly specialized contexts for each individual class, which can be beneficial for fine-grained classification tasks where class distinctions are subtle. However, it requires more data and has more parameters, making it potentially prone to overfitting in low-data regimes.

**Training Process:**

- **Fixed Pre-trained Parameters:** Crucially, the entire CLIP model (both image and text encoders) remains frozen during training. Only the `M` context vectors (or `K * M` vectors for CSC, where K is the number of classes) are updated.
- **Optimization:** Training is performed by minimizing a standard classification loss (cross-entropy) based on the prediction probabilities derived from the cosine similarity between image features `f` and text-generated class weights `g(ti)`.
- **Gradient Flow:** The gradients from the classification loss back-propagate through the text encoder `g(·)` to optimize the continuous context vectors. This allows CoOp to "distill the rich knowledge encoded in the [CLIP] parameters for learning task-relevant context."
- **Initialization:** The context vectors are initialized by drawing from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution (standard deviation 0.02) or, for comparison, with embeddings of actual words (e.g., "a photo of a").
- **Hyperparameters:** Key hyperparameters include the number of context tokens `M` (typically 16) and the position of the `[CLASS]` token. Training uses SGD with cosine annealing learning rate schedule and a warmup period to mitigate gradient instability.

**Comparison to Other Fine-tuning Methods:** CoOp is distinct from traditional fine-tuning where the entire pre-trained model (or parts of it) are updated. It also differs from linear probing, which trains a linear classifier on fixed features. CoOp focuses on adapting the *prompting mechanism* itself, making it a parameter-efficient approach tailored to VLMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted on 11 diverse image classification datasets and several domain generalization benchmarks, reveal several key findings about CoOp's effectiveness:

1. **Superior Few-Shot Learning Performance:**
 - **Vs. Hand-crafted Prompts:** CoOp significantly outperforms zero-shot CLIP, which relies on hand-crafted prompts. Even with as few as one or two shots, CoOp achieves a "decent margin." With 16 shots, the average gain over hand-crafted prompts is around 15%, with the highest gains reaching over 45% (e.g., EuroSAT). This highlights CoOp's ability to learn task-relevant prompts effectively from minimal data.
 - **Vs. Linear Probe:** CoOp generally demonstrates clear advantages over the linear probe model, especially in the extreme low-data regimes (1-2 shots). The linear probe often requires more data to even match zero-shot performance, whereas CoOp quickly surpasses it. This confirms CoOp's high data efficiency.

2. **Impact of Context Design (Unified vs. Class-Specific):**
 - **Unified Context:** On average, unified context performs better, particularly in challenging low-data scenarios (fewer than 8 shots). It's generally preferred for generic objects, scenes, and actions.
 - **Class-Specific Context (CSC):** CSC can be more effective for some fine-grained categories (e.g., StanfordCars, Flowers102, FGVCAircraft) and specialized tasks (DTD, EuroSAT) when more training shots (e.g., 16 shots) are available. This is attributed to its higher expressive power, but it is more susceptible to overfitting with limited data due to its larger number of parameters.

3. **Surprising Domain Generalization Capabilities:**
 - Despite being a learning-based approach, CoOp surprisingly enhances CLIP's robustness to distribution shifts. On ImageNet-V2, -Sketch, -A, and -R datasets, CoOp consistently outperforms zero-shot CLIP (which uses manual prompts) and significantly outperforms the linear probe model. This is a counter-intuitive but highly significant finding, as learning-based methods are often expected to be less robust to out-of-distribution data than zero-shot ones.
 - The study also notes that using *fewer* context tokens (e.g., M=4) often leads to *better* domain generalization, suggesting a trade-off between source domain performance (where more tokens might help) and robustness (where fewer tokens might prevent overfitting).

4. **Consistency Across Vision Backbones:**
 - CoOp's performance improvements over hand-crafted prompts are consistent across different image encoder architectures (ResNet-50, ResNet-101, ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16). This indicates that the benefits of prompt learning are not tied to a specific vision backbone but are generally applicable to CLIP-like models.

5. **Superiority Over Prompt Ensembling and Other Fine-tuning Methods:**
 - CoOp even outperforms prompt ensembling, where multiple carefully designed hand-crafted prompts are combined. This suggests the learned continuous prompts capture more optimal semantic information than any combination of discrete manual prompts.
 - Compared to other fine-tuning strategies (e.g., fine-tuning the image encoder, adding transformation/bias layers to text encoder output), CoOp exhibits significantly better performance, particularly in terms of both accuracy and robustness. Fine-tuning the image encoder, for instance, performs poorly.

6. **Initialization and Interpretability:**
 - **Initialization:** Random initialization of context vectors performs comparably to initializing them with word embeddings (e.g., "a photo of a"), suggesting that the optimization process is robust to the initial state.
 - **Interpretability:** The learned continuous prompts are difficult to interpret directly by mapping them to the nearest vocabulary words. While some nearby words show loose relevance, connecting them doesn't form coherent phrases, indicating that the learned vectors might encode meanings beyond discrete human language.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Learning to Prompt for Vision-Language Models" paper by Zhou et al. holds significant implications for the future development and deployment of large pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) and the broader field of foundation models.

1. **Democratization and Accessibility of Foundation Models:** This is arguably the most critical impact. By automating prompt engineering, CoOp drastically lowers the barrier to entry for using powerful VLMs like CLIP. Previously, effectively leveraging these models required significant domain expertise and time-consuming manual tuning. CoOp transforms VLMs into "data-efficient visual learners" that are "easier and cheaper to adapt." This is vital for the widespread adoption and real-world application of foundation models across various industries and research domains, moving them from specialized labs to a broader community.

2. **Paradigm Shift in Model Adaptation:** CoOp introduces prompt learning as a highly effective and parameter-efficient adaptation strategy for VLMs in computer vision. Unlike traditional fine-tuning (which updates millions of parameters) or linear probing (which learns a simple linear classifier on fixed features), CoOp demonstrates that optimizing a minimal set of context vectors can yield superior performance. This represents a new paradigm, focusing on adapting the input interface (the prompt) rather than the model's core architecture. This efficiency is particularly valuable as foundation models continue to grow in size.

3. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The results clearly show CoOp's ability to achieve substantial performance gains with very few labeled examples (few-shot learning). This is crucial for real-world scenarios where large amounts of labeled data are often expensive or impossible to acquire. CoOp enables practitioners to quickly adapt powerful VLMs to new tasks with minimal data overhead.

4. **Unexpected Robustness to Domain Shifts:** The finding that CoOp improves CLIP's robustness to distribution shifts is profoundly significant. It challenges the prevailing assumption that zero-shot transfer is inherently more robust than learning-based adaptation. CoOp demonstrates that a well-designed learning strategy can actually distill more generalizable knowledge from the pre-trained model, leading to better performance on unseen domains. This opens up new avenues for research into robust adaptation methods.

5. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The paper explicitly positions itself as a foundational study, hoping its findings and open-source code will "inspire and facilitate future research." It identifies several promising directions:
 - **Cross-dataset transfer:** Investigating how prompts learned on one dataset might transfer to others.
 - **Test-time adaptation:** Exploring methods to adapt models directly during inference.
 - **More generic adaptation methods:** Developing even broader strategies for mega-size vision models.
 The work also implicitly encourages further research into the interpretability of continuous prompts, a common challenge in many deep learning methods.

In conclusion, CoOp represents a major step forward in making large vision-language models practical and broadly applicable. By tackling the critical issue of prompt engineering, it not only enhances performance and data efficiency but also reveals surprising improvements in model robustness, charting a clear course for the future of foundation model adaptation in computer vision.
