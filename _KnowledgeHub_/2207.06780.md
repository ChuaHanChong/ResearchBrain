# An Empirical Evaluation of Four Off-the-Shelf Proprietary Visual-Inertial Odometry Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> An empirical evaluation compared four off-the-shelf proprietary Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems—Apple ARKit, Google ARCore, Intel RealSense T265, and Stereolabs ZED 2—across diverse indoor and outdoor real-world environments. The study identified Apple ARKit as the most consistently robust and accurate, while Stereolabs ZED 2 showed the highest drift and inconsistency.

## Problem

- A lack of comprehensive, standardized performance evaluations for widely used closed, proprietary Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems.
- Existing comparative studies often focus on open-source VIO algorithms or are limited to simple, short-range, and controlled environments.
- Engineers and researchers face difficulty in selecting suitable off-the-shelf VIO solutions for real-world robotic and AR/VR applications due to unclear performance characteristics under challenging conditions.

## Method

- Conducted a systematic empirical evaluation of four popular proprietary VIO systems: ==Apple ARKit==, Google ARCore, Intel RealSense T265, and Stereolabs ZED 2.
- Developed a custom data collection rig to capture synchronized motion data across all four devices in six distinct challenging indoor (corridors, stairs) and outdoor (campus, parking lot, urban roads) environments.
- Evaluated VIO performance using quantitative Final Drift Error (FDE) and qualitative trajectory consistency assessments against architectural floorplans or Google Maps, with all SLAM features like loop closure deactivated for a pure VIO comparison.

## Results

- Apple ARKit consistently achieved the lowest Final Drift Error (FDE) and most stable trajectories across nearly all tests, including high-speed urban roads (FDE of **2.68** m) and multi-story stairs (FDE of **0.19** m).
- Google ARCore performed well in indoor walking scenarios (e.g., FDE of **0.09** m for hallways) but experienced significant degradation or failure in high-speed vehicular tests or under abrupt lighting changes (e.g., FDE of **140.08** m for urban roads).
- Intel RealSense T265 showed reasonable indoor performance but suffered from scale inconsistency, while Stereolabs ZED 2 consistently displayed the highest FDE values and most severe rotational drift across most sequences (e.g., FDE of **409.25** m for urban roads).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Proprietary VIO systems exhibit significant variability in robustness, accuracy, and consistency across different real-world scenarios, particularly under rapid motion, poor lighting, or repetitive visual features.
> - Apple ARKit demonstrated superior stability and consistency in 6-DoF pose estimation across almost all challenging conditions, including high-speed vehicular movements, compared to other commercial solutions.
> - Limitations specific to different commercial systems include scale inconsistency (Intel RealSense T265), sensitivity to high speeds and illumination changes (Google ARCore), and significant rotational drift (Stereolabs ZED 2).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems constitute a significant area within robotics and computer vision research, aiming to determine the 6-Degrees of Freedom (6-DoF) position and orientation (ego-motion) of a camera-IMU rig by integrating visual information from cameras with inertial data from an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU). This capability is crucial for autonomous navigation, augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and various robotic applications where accurate and consistent pose estimation is required without reliance on external localization systems like motion capture or Global Positioning Systems (GPS).

The VIO field has reached a level of maturity, leading to the development of numerous algorithms. These can broadly be categorized into open-source, academic VIO methods (e.g., MSCKF, OKVIS, VINS-Mono) and closed, proprietary VIO algorithms integrated into commercial products and platforms. While open-source solutions allow for detailed algorithmic understanding and modification, proprietary systems offer off-the-shelf, often highly optimized, pipelines ready for end-user integration.

Existing research includes comparative experiments on various VIO approaches. However, the current landscape exhibits several limitations regarding comprehensive evaluations, particularly concerning proprietary systems. Many comparative studies focus predominantly on open-source VIO algorithms, often evaluating them on publicly available datasets like EuRoC, which may not fully represent real-world challenging environments. Some studies have included a subset of proprietary platforms, but these comparisons are frequently limited to simple indoor settings, short movement distances, or focus on developing new datasets rather than providing extensive performance evaluations of commercial VIO solutions themselves.

Specifically, there is a noted absence of comprehensive benchmarking for popular closed proprietary VIO platforms, such as those utilized in augmented reality frameworks (Apple ARKit, Google ARCore) or dedicated tracking devices (Intel RealSense T265, Stereolabs ZED 2). These commercial systems are widely deployed in various industrial applications, consumer products (e.g., Pokémon GO, IKEA Place AR), and even in significant challenges like the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, underscoring their practical importance. The lack of standardized, robust comparisons across diverse and challenging real-world scenarios makes it difficult for researchers and engineers to select the most suitable off-the-shelf VIO system for their specific robotic or AR/VR applications. This paper directly addresses this gap by providing an empirical evaluation of four frequently used proprietary VIO systems in a range of challenging indoor and outdoor environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to address the deficiency in comprehensive performance evaluation of off-the-shelf, proprietary Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems. Despite the widespread use of commercial VIO platforms in various robotic, AR/VR, and industrial applications, there has been a lack of benchmark studies that rigorously assess their positioning stability, consistency, and accuracy in challenging real-world environments.

The paper aims to fulfill the following key objectives:

1. **Comprehensive Evaluation**: To conduct a thorough and systematic empirical evaluation of four popular proprietary VIO systems: Apple ARKit, Google ARCore, Intel RealSense T265, and Stereolabs ZED 2. This represents the first comparative study of these specific four platforms across diverse indoor and outdoor scenarios.
2. **Performance Assessment in Challenging Environments**: To assess the selected VIO systems in a variety of challenging real-world conditions, including long and narrow corridors, large open spaces, repetitive stairways, underground parking lots with insufficient lighting, and complex urban traffic environments with vehicular movement. These environments are chosen to reflect realistic use cases and expose potential limitations of VIO algorithms under difficult circumstances (e.g., scarce or repetitive visual features, rapid motion, sudden illumination changes).
3. **Benchmarking for the Research Community**: To present the complete experimental results as a benchmark comparison. The goal is to provide a reliable reference for researchers and engineers who require an off-the-shelf 6-DoF state estimation solution and need guidance on selecting a VIO platform suitable for their specific robotic platforms and autonomous vehicles.
4. **Focus on Proprietary Systems**: To specifically concentrate on closed, proprietary VIO/SLAM products that are generally easier to operate and integrate than open-source academic algorithms, which often present higher barriers to entry for implementation and understanding. This focus is driven by the practical interest of many engineers and researchers in readily deployable commercial solutions.
5. **Assessment of Stability, Consistency, and Accuracy**: To quantitatively and qualitatively evaluate the stability, consistency, and accuracy of the 6-DoF pose estimates generated by each VIO system across the defined test sequences.

The motivation stems from the observation that while commercial VIO systems are frequently utilized, there is an unclear understanding of which platforms offer the most reliable performance for varied robotic applications. This research seeks to provide clarity by empirically testing and comparing these systems under conditions that simulate realistic operational demands.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study involved a systematic empirical evaluation of four proprietary VIO systems under various challenging indoor and outdoor conditions.

#### 3.1. Visual-Inertial Odometry Systems Under Test

The study focused on four commercial, off-the-shelf VIO systems frequently used in robotic applications:

- **Apple ARKit**: Apple’s Augmented Reality (AR) platform, integrated into iOS devices. It incorporates a filtering-based VIO algorithm, described as similar to MSCKF, which includes a sliding window filter, bundle adjustment, and motion/structure marginalization modules. Data was collected using a custom iOS data collection app on an iPhone 12 Pro Max (iOS 14.7.1) at 60 Hz, providing 6-DoF camera poses, RGB images, and IMU measurements.
- **Google ARCore**: Google’s AR platform for Android OS smartphones. It utilizes a Multi-State Constraint Kalman Filter (MSCKF)-style VIO/SLAM algorithm known as Concurrent Odometry and Mapping (COM). Data was acquired using a custom Android OS app based on Google’s ARCore example on an LG V60 ThinQ (Android 10.0.0, ARCore 1.29) at 30 Hz.
- **Intel RealSense T265**: A dedicated, stand-alone VIO and SLAM tracking device. It features an embedded processor (Vision Processing Unit) that performs all position computations on the device using image sequences from stereo fisheye cameras and fused sensor information. 6-DoF motion tracking results were collected at 200 Hz using the Intel RealSense SDK 2.0, connected to an Intel NUC mini PC.
- **Stereolabs ZED 2**: A hand-held stereo camera with a built-in IMU, designed for neural depth sensing and 6-DoF VIO/SLAM. The specifics of its proprietary VIO algorithm are not publicly detailed. It requires an external NVIDIA GPU for positional tracking. Data was collected at 30 Hz using ZED SDK 3.5.2 on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano onboard computer.

The study specifically excluded open-source VIO methods and non-inertial visual SLAM algorithms, focusing solely on commercial, off-the-shelf products.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup and Data Collection

A custom-built capture rig was designed to mount all four VIO devices (iPhone 12 Pro Max, LG V60 ThinQ, Intel T265, ZED 2) simultaneously. This rig ensured that all devices experienced the same motion trajectory during data collection. For indoor experiments, the rig was carried by a person, with onboard computers (Intel NUC, NVIDIA Jetson Nano) and batteries stored. For outdoor vehicular tests, the rig was rigidly fixed to the front passenger seat of a car.

Before commencing experiments, the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of all cameras were calibrated by capturing multiple views of a checkerboard to ensure that motion tracking results could be interpreted within a consistent reference coordinate frame. All VIO platforms were operated with their default parameter settings. Capabilities related to SLAM, such as loop closure, were deactivated across all systems to ensure a fair comparison focused purely on VIO performance.

#### 3.3. Benchmark Dataset and Environments

The benchmark dataset comprised sequences recorded in six distinct challenging locations, categorized into three indoor and three outdoor scenarios. The total length of these sequences ranged from 83 m to 3051 m, designed to evaluate medium and long-range VIO performance.

- **Indoor Sequences**:
 - **Indoor Corridor**: A long U-shape corridor (approx. 145 m), including pure rotational movements and varied textures.
 - **Indoor Hallway**: Open hallway spaces (approx. 84 m), also with pure rotational movements and textures.
 - **Indoor Multistory Stairs**: A multi-floor staircase environment (approx. 114 m), involving repetitive rotational motion while climbing from the 2nd basement to the 5th floor. These indoor cases included temporary occlusions and areas lacking visual features.
- **Outdoor Sequences**:
 - **Outdoor University Campus**: An outdoor campus area (approx. 513 m) with rapid topography changes and narrow return paths.
 - **Underground Parking Lot**: A dark underground parking lot (approx. 450 m) with poor visual conditions, collected via car at low speed (5-15 km/h).
 - **Urban Outdoor Roads**: Public automobile roads near Seoul Station, Seoul (approx. 3.1 km), characterized by complex urban traffic, moving people, cars, large vehicles, and high-speed driving conditions (sometimes exceeding 60 km/h).

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

Quantitative and qualitative methods were used for evaluation:

- **Quantitative Evaluation**: To assess performance quantitatively without an external motion capture system, all experiments were designed such that the start and end points of the movement trajectories coincided. The primary metric was the **Final Drift Error (FDE)**, defined as the end point position error in meters. An ideal FDE value would be 0, with larger values indicating less accurate position estimates relative to the defined origin.
- **Qualitative Evaluation**: The consistency, stability, and reliability of each VIO system were qualitatively evaluated by overlaying the estimated 6-DoF VIO trajectories onto architectural floorplans of buildings or Google Maps. This allowed for visual assessment of how well the estimated paths matched the actual movements and environmental geometry, particularly concerning rotational drift, scale consistency, and overall trajectory shape.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of the four proprietary VIO systems revealed distinct performance characteristics across the diverse indoor and outdoor challenging environments. The Final Drift Error (FDE) served as the primary quantitative metric, supplemented by qualitative observations of trajectory consistency and stability.

#### 4.1. Indoor Long Corridors and Open Hallway Sequences

- **Apple ARKit**: Demonstrated the most consistent motion tracking. Its estimated trajectories matched the building floorplan well, maintaining orthogonality and scale, with starting and ending points closely meeting without significant rotational drift.
- **Google ARCore**: Achieved the smallest FDE values (0.12 m for Corridor, 0.09 m for Hallway), indicating high positional accuracy at the endpoint. However, qualitatively, its estimated trajectory did not always align as consistently with the floorplan's shape as ARKit.
- **Intel RealSense T265**: Showed accurate 3-DoF rotational motion but exhibited a problem with scale consistency, often estimating trajectories larger than the actual movements. FDE values were higher than ARKit and ARCore (1.88 m, 0.61 m).
- **Stereolabs ZED 2**: Presented the least accurate and most inconsistent positioning performance. Significant rotational motion drift accumulated over time, leading to substantial trajectory distortions and large FDE values (1.44 m, 4.58 m).

#### 4.2. Indoor Multistory Stairs Sequence

- **Apple ARKit**: Displayed the best performance with highly consistent 6-DoF motion tracking. Its top and side views showed overlapped and consistent trajectories, with starting and ending points nearly matching in the xy-plane (FDE of 0.19 m). The estimated height of each floor was also approximately identical to the ground-truth blueprint.
- **Google ARCore**: Diverged more significantly than ARKit, with an FDE of 3.98 m. The estimated loop closing was not as precise.
- **Intel RealSense T265**: Showed better performance than ARCore and ZED2 but still diverged (FDE of 1.49 m).
- **Stereolabs ZED 2**: Exhibited the most severe trajectory distortion, particularly in the z-axis (height) direction, with an FDE of 4.76 m, indicating poor consistency in repetitive rotational motion.

#### 4.3. Outdoor University Campus Sequence

- **Apple ARKit**: Along with ARCore, it demonstrated trajectories that matched well with Google Maps, with start and end points meeting. Its FDE was 2.01 m.
- **Google ARCore**: Achieved the smallest FDE of 0.07 m, indicating high positional accuracy for the endpoint. Its trajectory shape also aligned well with Google Maps.
- **Intel RealSense T265**: While its trajectory shape was similar to ARKit and ARCore, it suffered from a scale inconsistency issue, estimating a path smaller than the actual movements (FDE of 4.08 m). This is a common issue for monocular VIO configurations.
- **Stereolabs ZED 2**: Showed the most severe distortion of the actual movement trajectory, with its orthogonality compromised due to inaccurate rotation estimation. Its FDE was significantly higher at 206.38 m.

#### 4.4. Outdoor Urban Roads and Parking Lot Sequences (Vehicular Tests)

- **Apple ARKit**: Performed with high accuracy and consistency even under challenging high-speed driving conditions (exceeding 60 km/h) and complex urban environments. Its trajectories overlaid on Google Maps showed accurate loop closing (FDE of 2.68 m) and stable 6-DoF motion tracking. It was the only system to produce stable results in the vehicular test.
- **Google ARCore**: Occasionally failed when car speed increased or under abrupt light variations. In the urban roads sequence, it registered a substantial FDE of 140.08 m. In the parking lot, it showed better performance than T265 and ZED2 but still diverged.
- **Intel RealSense T265**: Its VIO algorithm diverged or failed if the car stopped temporarily or drove too fast in the urban roads, indicated by "×" (failure) in Table I, and showed a large FDE of 9.01 m in the parking lot.
- **Stereolabs ZED 2**: Continuously accumulated rotational drift error over time, leading to highly inaccurate motion estimation results and significant FDEs (409.25 m for urban roads, 10.85 m for parking lot). Its positioning results were the most deviating from actual movements in the underground parking lot.

#### 4.5. Overall Performance Summary

- **Apple ARKit**: Consistently demonstrated the most robust, accurate, and stable motion tracking across nearly all indoor and outdoor scenarios, including challenging conditions like rapid camera movements, abrupt illumination changes, and high-speed vehicular travel. It rarely experienced tracking failure or motion jumps.
- **Google ARCore**: Performed well in indoor sequences and with walking motion data, exhibiting accurate and consistent tracking. However, its performance deteriorated sharply or diverged when confronted with rapid movements or poor lighting conditions, particularly in vehicular tests.
- **Intel RealSense T265**: Showed reasonable indoor performance, positioning itself behind Google ARCore. Its primary limitation was a consistent scale inconsistency issue and occasional tracking failures when moving too slowly or too fast.
- **Stereolabs ZED 2**: Was the most inconsistent and inaccurate among the four devices. It suffered from severe accumulation of rotational error over time, leading to significant trajectory distortion and large endpoint drift. Its performance was particularly poor in maintaining straight paths and under fast motion, exhibiting the highest FDE values in most sequences.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a unique and valuable contribution to the field of visual-inertial odometry by offering the first comprehensive, back-to-back empirical comparison of four prominent proprietary VIO systems: Apple ARKit, Google ARCore, Intel RealSense T265, and Stereolabs ZED 2. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Addressing a Research Gap**: The study directly addresses a notable gap in the existing literature, which has largely focused on open-source VIO algorithms or provided limited evaluations of commercial proprietary systems. By focusing exclusively on off-the-shelf commercial products in challenging real-world environments, the paper provides insights into the practical performance of systems that are widely deployed but whose internal workings are often opaque.
2. **Benchmark for System Selection**: The detailed benchmark results serve as a crucial reference for researchers, engineers, and developers. It offers empirical evidence regarding the stability, consistency, and accuracy of each VIO platform across diverse scenarios, from walking indoors to high-speed vehicular motion outdoors. This information can guide the selection of an appropriate VIO solution for specific robotic platforms, autonomous vehicles, augmented reality applications, and other industrial uses, saving significant development and evaluation time.
3. **Insights into Real-World Performance**: The use of six challenging indoor and outdoor environments, including long corridors, multi-story stairs, underground parking lots, and complex urban roads, provides realistic insights into how these systems perform under varying conditions (e.g., repetitive features, poor lighting, rapid motion, urban clutter). This goes beyond the scope of many existing benchmarks that rely on controlled or public datasets, offering a more robust understanding of system limitations and strengths in practical deployments.
4. **Identification of Strengths and Weaknesses**: The study meticulously details the performance characteristics of each system, highlighting Apple ARKit's consistent robustness across nearly all scenarios, ARCore's strong indoor performance but sensitivity to high speed/lighting changes, T265's scale inconsistency, and ZED 2's pronounced rotational drift. Such specific observations are critical for optimizing system integration and anticipating potential failure modes in real-world applications.
5. **Implications for Robotics and AR/VR Development**: The findings have direct implications for developers of autonomous robots, drones, and AR/VR applications. For instance, the superior performance of Apple ARKit in high-speed vehicular tests suggests its potential for applications like autonomous driving or large-scale mapping, even if officially not explicitly supported. Conversely, understanding the limitations of other systems (e.g., ZED 2's rotational drift) can inform design choices or necessitate additional sensor fusion techniques to compensate for VIO inaccuracies.
6. **Foundation for Future Research**: This benchmark can serve as a baseline for future research aiming to improve proprietary VIO systems or to develop new open-source algorithms that can match or exceed the performance of leading commercial solutions in diverse environments. It also highlights the need for further exploration into robust VIO performance under extreme conditions (e.g., very high speeds, extreme lighting, featureless environments).

In conclusion, this empirical evaluation contributes significantly to the practical understanding of commercial VIO system capabilities, providing a valuable resource for guiding technological choices and fostering advancements in autonomous navigation and perception technologies.
