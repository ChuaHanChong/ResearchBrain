# Human-in-the-loop Online Rejection Sampling for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Hi-ORS introduces a post-training method for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) robotic models that enhances stability and robustness for real-world manipulation. It utilizes human-in-the-loop online rejection sampling to achieve efficient fine-tuning and error recovery for contact-rich tasks, mastering them in as little as 1.5 hours.

## Problem

- Pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often cannot be directly deployed in real-world robotics due to domain shifts and specific task requirements, necessitating further post-training.
- Real-world Reinforcement Learning (RL) for fine-tuning high-capacity VLAs is unstable and sample-inefficient, hindered by inaccurate value estimation (Q-function overestimation) and sparse supervision for intermediate model computations.
- Imitation Learning (IL), while stable, is an offline method prone to compounding errors and catastrophic failures when robots encounter states not present in the initial demonstration dataset.

## Method

- **Hi-ORS** employs a ==rejection sampling== objective, inspired by LLMs, which uses an ==outcome-based reward filter== to select only successful real-world trajectories for policy updates, thus stabilizing value estimation without relying on unstable Q-functions.
- The method integrates ==reward-weighted supervised learning== using a ==flow matching-based objective==, providing ==dense supervision== across all intermediate inference steps of the VLA model to leverage its full computational potential.
- An ==asynchronous actor-learner architecture== facilitates concurrent data collection and policy learning, supported by flexible ==human interventions== for error recovery and ==data filters== to ensure training stability and sample efficiency.

## Results

- **Hi-ORS** consistently outperformed strong baselines like Behavior Cloning and existing real-world RL methods (HIL-SERL, Q-Chunking) across **3** robotic manipulation tasks, achieving higher asymptotic success rates and mastering contact-rich tasks in just **1.5 hours** of real-world training.
- Fine-tuned **Hi-ORS** policies demonstrated strong test-time scalability and robust error-recovery capabilities (e.g., re-grasping dropped objects), with success rates improving monotonically when given retry budgets during evaluation.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical contribution of each **Hi-ORS** component, including rejection sampling, human correction, and data filters, to its superior performance, efficiency, and stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rejection sampling, when adapted to robot learning with outcome-based reward filtering, offers a robust alternative to learned value functions for identifying high-quality data and stabilizing policy improvement.
> - Providing dense supervision across all intermediate inference steps of a flow matching-based VLA model, weighted by trajectory rewards, significantly improves learning efficiency and stability compared to traditional sparse RL signals.
> - Strategic, filtered human interventions (e.g., teleoperation for error recovery) are essential for quickly teaching complex behaviors and obtaining positively rewarded samples, preventing contamination of the training data by suboptimal actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen significant advancements with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, often pre-trained on massive teleoperation datasets, aim to provide generalist robot policies capable of understanding high-level language instructions and executing complex tasks. However, the paper identifies a critical challenge: VLA models, despite their powerful pre-training, cannot be directly deployed in real-world scenarios without further post-training or fine-tuning due to domain shifts and specific task requirements.

The two primary paradigms for post-training in robotics are Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL).
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** IL, particularly behavior cloning (BC), trains policies by maximizing the likelihood of expert actions in collected states. It is straightforward to implement and train but suffers from an "offline nature." This means IL policies are prone to compounding errors when encountering states not present in the initial demonstration dataset, leading to catastrophic failures in dynamic real-world environments. Human-in-the-loop IL methods attempt to mitigate this by collecting interventions, but these often remain heavily human-dependent.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** RL, in contrast, incorporates online exploration and trial-and-error to optimize actions and maximize expected returns. This approach has shown promise in producing robust real-world manipulation policies. However, the paper highlights that applying RL to fine-tune high-capacity VLAs in real-world settings is notoriously unstable and sample-inefficient. Key issues include:
 - **Inaccurate Value Estimation:** RL algorithms rely on neural networks to approximate action-value functions (Q-functions). These approximations can be unstable and prone to overestimation, especially in high-dimensional action spaces (e.g., with action chunking, where multi-step actions are predicted simultaneously).
 - **Inefficient Supervision:** Modern VLAs often involve intermediate computations (e.g., iterative denoising in diffusion-based policies). Traditional RL typically supervises only the final action, providing sparse learning signals and neglecting the rich information from these intermediate steps.
 - **Sample Inefficiency and Safety:** Real-world RL requires extensive exploration, which is costly, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe for robots, especially with limited sample budgets.

The authors position Hi-ORS as a method that bridges the gap between the stability of IL and the robustness of RL, addressing the inherent instabilities of real-world RL for VLA fine-tuning. It draws inspiration from techniques in Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically rejection sampling, which has been successfully used for iterative self-improvement and filtering in language models. By adapting rejection sampling to robot learning, Hi-ORS aims to achieve stable, efficient, and robust online post-training. This work contributes to the growing area of "post-training" or "online alignment" for foundation models in robotics, seeking to make these powerful models practical for real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a stable and flexible online post-training method for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotic manipulation tasks. The authors are primarily motivated by two critical challenges that plague existing post-training approaches:

1. **Instability of Real-world Reinforcement Learning (RL) for VLAs:** While RL offers the potential for robust policies through online exploration, its application to high-capacity VLAs in real-world settings is severely unstable. This instability stems from two identified sources:
 - **Inaccurate Value Estimation (I1):** Neural network approximations of action-value functions (Q-functions) are susceptible to overestimation, particularly in high-dimensional action spaces like those produced by action chunking (where multiple future actions are predicted). This makes reliable policy improvement difficult.
 - **Inefficient Supervision (I2):** VLAs often perform complex intermediate computations (e.g., iterative denoising in diffusion policies). Standard RL objectives typically only supervise the final action, providing sparse and inefficient learning signals, thus failing to leverage the full potential of these models.
2. **Limitations of Imitation Learning (IL):** Despite its training stability, IL (e.g., behavior cloning) is an offline method that cannot inherently correct for compounding errors during real-world execution. If the robot deviates into states not covered by the training data, performance degrades, leading to failures.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of Hi-ORS are:
- **Stabilize value estimation:** Replace high-variance Q-function approximations with a more reliable mechanism for identifying high-value actions or trajectories.
- **Provide dense intermediate-step supervision:** Leverage the internal workings of VLAs to provide richer, more frequent learning signals.
- **Incorporate human guidance efficiently:** Allow human operators to flexibly intervene and guide the learning process, especially for error recovery, without contaminating the training data with suboptimal corrections.
- **Achieve high robustness and sample efficiency:** Develop a method that enables VLAs to master contact-rich manipulation tasks with minimal real-world training time and exhibit strong generalization and error-recovery capabilities.

In essence, the paper aims to combine the best aspects of IL (training stability) and RL (robustness through online interaction) while mitigating their respective drawbacks, thereby making VLA fine-tuning practical and effective for real-world robotic applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Hi-ORS (Human-in-the-loop Online Rejection Sampling) is proposed as a simple yet effective post-training method that leverages rejection sampling, reward-weighted supervised training, and flexible human interventions within an asynchronous infrastructure. The approach is designed to stabilize VLA fine-tuning by addressing the two primary sources of instability in RL: inaccurate value estimation and inefficient supervision.

The methodology can be broken down into four core components:

**A. Rejection Sampling for Robotic Manipulation:**
At its core, Hi-ORS employs a rejection sampling objective, inspired by its success in LLMs, to overcome the instability of learned value functions. It operates in a two-phase structure analogous to Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI):
1. **Evaluation Phase:** The current policy generates trajectories in the real world. For each trajectory $\tau$, a cumulative reward $R(\tau)$ is calculated (using a simple, manually annotated binary reward model in experiments, though a learned model is also discussed). A trajectory is "accepted" if its reward meets or exceeds a dynamically adjusted reward threshold $m$, represented by an indicator function $I_m(\tau) = 1_{R(\tau) \geq m}$. This process directly filters out negatively rewarded or unsuccessful rollouts, effectively acting as an outcome-based value estimation that avoids the pitfalls of approximate Q-functions (addressing instability I1). The threshold $m$ progressively increases over training iterations ($m_1 \leq m_2 \leq \dots \leq m_N$) to ensure continuous improvement on increasingly higher-quality data.
2. **Improvement Phase:** The policy is updated using a reward-weighted supervised learning objective. For flow matching-based VLAs (like $\pi_0$), this objective is designed to provide dense supervision across all intermediate inference steps (addressing instability I2). The loss function is formulated as:
 $L_{\text{Hi-ORS}}(\theta) = E_{\tau \sim p_{\pi_\theta}(\cdot), x_0 \sim N(0,I), u \sim Unif} [ I_m(\tau) \cdot \|v_\theta(u, s_t, x_u) - (x_1 - x_0)\|_2^2 ]$
 Here, $I_m(\tau)$ acts as a weight, ensuring that only successful trajectories contribute to the policy update, and the flow matching loss term provides dense supervision for the vector field $v_\theta$ at all intermediate integration times $u$. This combined objective effectively mitigates both identified sources of instability.

**B. Varied Frequency for Human Corrections:**
Recognizing the high sample complexity of real-world robotic tasks, Hi-ORS strategically incorporates human interventions to guide exploration and demonstrate error recovery. Human operators can intervene at any timestep via teleoperation, targeted resets, or injecting corrective segments. Crucially, only human intervention episodes that achieve positive rewards (i.e., pass the rejection sampling filter $I_m(\tau)$) are retained in the accepted buffer, preventing the contamination of training data with suboptimal human actions.
To optimize data efficiency and maintain smooth execution, Hi-ORS uses an adaptive interaction frequency:
- $f_{high}$ during human intervention periods to capture fine-grained corrective behaviors.
- $f_{low}$ during autonomous control periods to ensure consistent policy execution.

**C. Asynchronous Infrastructure:**
To manage the computational demands of VLA inference and accelerate training, Hi-ORS employs an asynchronous actor-learner architecture.
- **Actor Node:** Typically runs on one GPU, responsible for online inference (generating trajectories) and streaming data to the learner node.
- **Learner Node(s):** Uses the remaining GPUs (G-1 GPUs) for policy updates. Distributed training, orchestrated with ZeRO-2, handles large-scale VLAs.
This asynchronous design allows data collection and policy learning to happen concurrently, significantly improving training throughput (approx. 2x) and enabling learning even when the robot is physically paused.
Additional filters are implemented for training stability:
- **No-ops Action Filter:** Discards actions where the norm of the relative transform falls below a threshold, preventing the policy from getting "stuck" at the beginning of an episode.
- **Short Episode Filter:** Discards very short episodes to prevent incorrect action chunking and ensure informative rollouts.

The total latency (model inference, communication, sequential execution) and training time per iteration are carefully considered to maintain an Update-to-Data (UTD) ratio of around 1.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Hi-ORS was systematically evaluated across three real-world robotic manipulation tasks with two different embodiments: "Raise-Hand" (Paxini Tora One, target pose reaching), "Pack-Detergent" (Paxini Tora One, pick-and-place), and "Insert-Moisturizer" (Dobot X-Trainer, precise insertion). The tasks covered varying levels of complexity, including contact-rich manipulation.

**A. Real-world Performance Comparison (Q1):**
- **Overall Superiority:** Hi-ORS consistently outperformed strong baselines: vanilla Behavior Cloning (BC), HIL-SERL (a real-world RL method with human-in-the-loop corrections), and Q-Chunking (an RL method designed for action chunking).
- **Effectiveness:** Hi-ORS achieved higher asymptotic success rates across all tasks. For instance, on "Raise-Hand," it matched HIL-SERL's best performance without the late-stage regressions HIL-SERL exhibited. On "Pack-Detergent" and "Insert-Moisturizer," Hi-ORS reached significantly higher success levels.
- **Efficiency:** Hi-ORS converged faster and required fewer real-world interactions to reach target success levels, mastering contact-rich manipulation in just 1.5 hours of real-world training.
- **Addressing RL Limitations:** The paper demonstrated that HIL-SERL, while performing well on simpler tasks, struggled with harder tasks due to RL's inherent instability issues (inaccurate value estimation, BPTT issues). Q-Chunking, though improved by distillation, still underperformed Hi-ORS, highlighting the advantage of outcome-based value estimation over learned Q-functions. Compared to BC, Hi-ORS showed an average improvement of 23.3% due to its online data collection and acceptance filter, mitigating compounding errors.

**B. Learning Dynamics (Q2):**
- **Test-time Scalability:** Hi-ORS policies exhibited strong test-time scalability, particularly in the "Insert-Moisturizer" task. When given larger trial budgets during evaluation (allowing for retries), the success rate monotonically improved, indicating the policy's ability to effectively recover from intermediate errors. This contrasts sharply with behavior cloning, which showed little to no test-time scaling.

**C. Spatial Generalization:**
- Hi-ORS demonstrated great spatial generalizability. Policies trained with objects starting at "chess points" (specific locations) successfully handled test cases where objects were in "middle areas," and vice-versa, even for extreme initial object placements far from the robot's home position. This generalization is attributed to the online data collection nature, allowing for quick fixes and adaptation.

**D. Error Recovery:**
- The fine-tuned policies showcased impressive complex error-recovery behaviors, such as re-grasping a dropped object, re-inserting an object that missed its target, and performing compensating insertions when the initial grasp was suboptimal. Human corrections were instrumental in rapidly teaching these skills, as an offline BC model quickly failed in similar scenarios.

**E. Ablation Studies (Q3):**
A comprehensive ablation study confirmed the critical contribution of each component of Hi-ORS:
- **Rejection Sampling & Reward-Weighted Supervision:** Replacing the outcome-based reward with a learned reward classifier (as in HIL-SERL) led to lower success rates, suggesting that false positive rewards from learned models can confuse training, validating the direct reward-based filtering.
- **Human Correction:** Removing human intervention drastically reduced the success rate to 0%, highlighting its essential role in providing explicit guidance for error recovery and obtaining positively rewarded samples efficiently.
- **Data Filters:** Both the "no-ops action filter" and "short episode filter" were crucial. Removing the former dropped success to 20% and slowed training, while removing the latter reduced success to 60%, emphasizing their importance for sample efficiency and learning stability.
- **Varied Execution Frequency:** The adaptive frequency strategy ($f_{high}$ for human interventions, $f_{low}$ for autonomous execution) was confirmed effective; fixed lower or higher frequencies both resulted in performance degradation.
- **Learning Scheduler:** A cyclical scheduler, while hypothesized to improve training, showed only minor effects, leading to its removal in the final version based on Occam's Razor.

Overall, Hi-ORS demonstrated a superior trade-off, achieving 80% success rate within 1.5 hours, with each design choice contributing materially to its performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, with the introduction of Human-in-the-loop Online Rejection Sampling (Hi-ORS), holds significant implications for the field of robotic manipulation and the practical deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

**Primary Significance:**

- **Bridging the Gap between IL and RL:** Hi-ORS effectively synthesizes the strengths of imitation learning (training stability) and reinforcement learning (robustness through online interaction) while mitigating their respective weaknesses. This is a crucial advancement, as previous methods often struggled to achieve both stability and high performance in real-world VLA fine-tuning.
- **Stable and Efficient VLA Fine-tuning:** By addressing the fundamental instabilities of real-world RL—inaccurate value estimation and inefficient supervision—Hi-ORS provides a robust and sample-efficient method. Its ability to fine-tune a base policy for complex, contact-rich tasks in just 1.5 hours of real-world training significantly reduces the cost and time associated with robot deployment. This efficiency is critical for accelerating the development and iteration cycle of robotic applications.
- **Novel Application of Rejection Sampling:** The paper successfully translates the concept of rejection sampling, previously impactful in Large Language Models (LLMs) for self-improvement, to the domain of robotic learning. This cross-domain inspiration demonstrates a powerful new paradigm for identifying and leveraging high-quality data in reinforcement learning settings without relying on complex and unstable value function approximations.
- **Enhanced Error Recovery Capabilities:** The integration of flexible human-in-the-loop corrections is a practical and effective way to guide policies in mastering error-recovery behaviors. This allows robots to learn from "near-misses" and failures, dramatically improving their resilience and ability to handle unexpected situations, which is essential for real-world autonomy. The demonstrated test-time scalability, where policies improve with retries, highlights this capability.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robot Deployment:** The reduced training time and enhanced robustness offered by Hi-ORS can significantly lower the barrier to deploying VLA models for new or specialized robotic tasks. This could lead to faster development cycles for a wide range of industrial, service, and research robotics applications.
- **More Reliable Robotic Systems:** By enabling policies to gracefully recover from errors and exhibit greater robustness, Hi-ORS contributes to the creation of more reliable and trustworthy robotic systems. This is particularly important for applications in unstructured or dynamic environments where perfect execution is rarely achievable.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** Hi-ORS provides a strong baseline and a new direction for fine-tuning VLAs. Future work could build upon this by exploring more sophisticated acceptance threshold schedulers for stochastic environments, extending the method to multi-task and longer-horizon manipulation, or investigating how to automate aspects of human intervention.
- **Inspiration for Generalist Robot Learning:** The approach could inspire further research into how to combine human intuition and guidance with autonomous learning for creating truly generalist robotic agents that can adapt quickly to novel situations and recover from unforeseen challenges.

In conclusion, Hi-ORS represents a significant step forward in making advanced VLA models practical and robust for real-world robotic manipulation. Its elegant solution to the instability of RL, combined with efficient human collaboration, positions it as a highly impactful contribution to the field.
