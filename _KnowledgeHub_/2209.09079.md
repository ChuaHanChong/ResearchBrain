# MSVIPER: Improved Policy Distillation for Reinforcement-Learning-Based Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Maryland and NIST developed MSVIPER, a framework that distills black-box deep reinforcement learning policies into interpretable decision trees for robot navigation, using multi-scenario sampling to produce smaller, more robust policies. The approach enables post-training direct modification of these decision trees to address navigation issues like freezing, oscillation, and vibrations on real robots, achieving up to a 95% reduction in freezing behaviors.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policies for robot navigation are typically neural networks, which are opaque and difficult to analyze, verify, or debug.
- Existing DRL policies often exhibit suboptimal behaviors or errors (e.g., freezing, oscillation, vibration) that are costly and time-consuming to correct, requiring full retraining.
- The lack of mechanisms for post-training policy modification prevents efficient adaptation to new errors or changing conditions without extensive re-training.

## Method

- The **MSVIPER** framework comprises a three-stage pipeline involving training an expert DRL policy, distilling it into an interpretable decision tree, and then directly modifying the tree.
- The expert neural network policy is distilled into a decision tree using multi-scenario trajectory generation, which samples from diverse simulated environments of varying complexities to create a broader dataset.
- Algorithms are applied for direct decision tree modification to identify and alter specific nodes to correct undesirable behaviors like robot freezing, oscillation, and vibrations without requiring retraining of the original expert policy.

## Results

- MSVIPER generated decision trees were significantly smaller (e.g., 411 nodes vs. 2651 for SSVIPER) and achieved higher average rewards than previous distillation methods, closely mimicking the expert policy.
- On real robots, tree modifications reduced freezing behaviors by up to **95%** (from **100%** to **5%**) and oscillations by up to **90%** (from **96%** to **6%**) in indoor navigation tasks.
- For outdoor navigation, the approach reduced robot vibrations (e.g., from **0.53** to **0.48**, an approximate **17%** improvement) and oscillations, leading to smoother and more stable movement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distilling DRL policies into decision trees using diverse, multi-scenario sampling improves the robustness and interpretability of the resulting policy while yielding smaller, more efficient tree structures.
> - Direct, post-training modification of interpretable decision tree policies can effectively resolve specific navigation errors and sub-optimal behaviors in robots, eliminating the need for computationally expensive retraining.
> - Targeted structural changes to decision trees, such as modifying actions in specific leaf nodes or adjusting branch thresholds, can significantly enhance robot navigation performance and stability in real-world scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses several interconnected challenges within the fields of robotics, artificial intelligence, and machine learning, particularly focusing on robot navigation. Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) have increasingly demonstrated capabilities in enabling robots to navigate complex indoor and outdoor environments, perform obstacle avoidance, and adapt to dynamic scenes or uneven terrains. However, policies learned through DRL are often represented by neural networks, which typically operate as "black boxes." This characteristic makes them difficult to analyze, verify, debug, or interpret, posing significant hurdles for deployment in safety-critical real-world applications.

Existing research has explored methods for interpreting neural networks or distilling their knowledge into more transparent models. Policy distillation involves transforming a policy from one format to another while preserving its essential input-output behavior. Approaches like VIPER (Verifiable Reinforcement Learning via Policy Extraction) have aimed to distill neural network policies into decision trees, offering improved interpretability. However, the application of such methods to complex, real-world robotic tasks has been limited, and they often lack mechanisms for addressing errors or suboptimal behaviors discovered post-training without requiring a complete retraining process.

This paper extends previous work, particularly VIPER, by proposing a new method, MSVIPER, that aims to overcome some of these limitations. It situates itself in the context of improving the efficiency and robustness of policy distillation for practical robot navigation. Furthermore, it addresses the critical need for post-training policy modification, allowing for error correction and performance enhancement without the time-consuming and resource-intensive process of full retraining. This aligns with a growing interest in verifiable and interpretable machine learning for autonomous systems, where human feedback and domain knowledge can be integrated efficiently into learned policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research stem from the practical limitations encountered when deploying Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policies in real-world robot navigation scenarios. The authors identify several key issues that MSVIPER seeks to address:

1. **Interpretability and Analysis of Learned Policies**: Policies learned via DRL are typically represented as neural networks. These models are often opaque, making it challenging to understand the underlying logic behind a robot's decisions. This "black-box" nature hinders analysis, debugging, and verification, which are critical for safety and reliability in autonomous systems. The authors are motivated to develop approaches that produce decision-tree (DT) policies, as DTs offer inherent transparency; the cause-and-effect relationship between state and action is readily apparent, making them easier to analyze and understand.

2. **Addressing Sub-optimality and Errors in Learned Policies**: DRL policies, despite their learning capabilities, may exhibit errors or suboptimal behaviors in certain scenarios. Identifying and correcting these shortcomings is crucial for robust robot operation. The existing methods often require extensive retraining, which can be computationally expensive, time-consuming, and sometimes impossible if the original training data or environment setup is no longer available.

3. **Enabling Post-Training Policy Modification without Retraining**: A central motivation is the ability to modify and improve a learned policy *after* its initial training and deployment, without necessitating a complete retraining cycle. This capability is highly valuable in real-world applications where new errors might be discovered, environmental conditions change, or specific performance metrics need fine-tuning. For instance, a software engineer might need to fix a specific faulty behavior without deep knowledge of RL algorithms. The decision-tree structure, unlike neural networks, is naturally amenable to direct, targeted modifications.

4. **Improving Robot Navigation Performance**: The research specifically targets common failure modes in RL-based robot navigation. These include:
 - **Freezing Robot Problem**: In indoor environments with dynamic obstacles (e.g., pedestrians), robots might become immobile to avoid collisions, failing to reach their goal.
 - **Oscillation Behaviors**: Robots might exhibit jerky or oscillatory movements, especially when navigating around obstacles, leading to inefficient and undesirable trajectories.
 - **Vibrations on Uneven Terrain**: In outdoor environments with complex, uneven terrain, robot motion can be jerky, leading to high vibrations and reduced stability.

5. **Enhancing Sample Efficiency and Robustness of Policy Distillation**: The authors seek to improve upon existing policy distillation methods like VIPER. They aim to create smaller, more efficient decision trees that accurately mimic expert policies while also being more robust to diverse scenarios. This involves addressing limitations in how previous methods sampled trajectories, which often relied on single simulated environments.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to bridge the gap between powerful but opaque RL agents and the need for interpretable, verifiable, and adaptable robot control policies for complex real-world tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MSVIPER (Multiple Scenario Verifiable Reinforcement Learning via Policy Extraction) method comprises a three-stage pipeline: learning an initial "expert" policy, distilling this policy into a decision tree, and then modifying the decision tree to optimize performance without retraining.

#### 3.1. Reinforcement Learning for Initial Navigation Policy

The process begins by training an "expert" navigation policy using a standard Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) algorithm. The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is specified for this purpose. A crucial aspect of this stage is the use of **Curriculum Learning (CL)**. Instead of training the agent in a single complex environment, it is trained in a succession of increasingly difficult environmental stages. The policy learned from an earlier, simpler scenario serves as the initialization for training in a subsequent, more complex scenario. This multi-stage training aims to produce a robust neural network (NN) expert policy capable of handling diverse indoor and outdoor navigation tasks, including navigating to static/dynamic goals, avoiding collisions, and achieving smooth trajectories. This expert policy is treated as a black box that cannot be retrained during subsequent stages. The state space is represented as a numerical array, incorporating goal location, previous action, and a radial occupancy grid derived from lidar data, spanning 213 dimensions. The action space is discrete, consisting of motion primitives with varying linear and angular velocities.

#### 3.2. Policy Distillation: Conversion to Decision Tree Policy (MSVIPER)

The core contribution of MSVIPER lies in its improved policy distillation technique, which converts the black-box NN expert policy into an interpretable Decision Tree (DT) policy. This stage enhances the VIPER approach by introducing multi-scenario sampling:

- **Multi-Scenario Trajectory Generation**: Unlike prior methods (referred to as Single-Source VIPER or SSVIPER) that sample trajectories from a single simulated environment, MSVIPER samples trajectories generated by applying the expert policy across *multiple simulated environments (E)* of varying complexities and types (e.g., different indoor layouts, dynamic obstacles, uneven outdoor terrains). This diversity in sampling ensures that the resulting dataset of state-action pairs (D) covers a broader range of critical states and scenarios, leading to more robust DTs.
- **Dataset Aggregation and Sampling**: For each scenario `e` in the set of environments `E`, the expert policy `π*` is run for `N` iterations, generating trajectories of length `l_t`. All collected state-action pairs `(s, a)` are aggregated into a single set `D`. From this aggregated set `D`, a smaller dataset `D'` of `n_s` pairs is randomly sampled.
- **Decision Tree Training**: The sampled dataset `D'` is used to train a decision tree `ˆπ` in a supervised learning manner using the CART (Classification and Regression Tree) algorithm. In this process, state features are inputs, and actions are labels.
- **Policy Selection**: This process results in `N` candidate decision trees. These trees are evaluated in simulation over `n_cv` trials, and the best-performing tree (highest average reward) is selected as the final MSVIPER policy `ˆπ`.

The theoretical analysis (Theorem 1, Lemma 1, Theorem 2, Theorem 3) suggests that MSVIPER can decrease the upper bound of the loss function compared to single-scenario VIPER, implying better imitation performance and equivalent or superior sample complexity by increasing the likelihood of sampling critical states.

#### 3.3. Tree Modification: Policy Optimization & No Retraining

Once the DT policy `ˆπ` is obtained, MSVIPER enables direct analysis and modification of its structure to improve navigation characteristics without retraining the original expert policy. The modifications are designed to be minimal to preserve the learned behavior while addressing specific issues.

- **Indoor Navigation Modifications**:
 - **Freezing Robot Problem**: An algorithm (Algorithm 2) identifies leaf nodes in the DT where the action is "Stop" (`a_F`) and the associated state subspace indicates static obstacles (occupancy grid shows minimal movement). To alleviate this (Algorithm 3), these problematic nodes' actions are modified to a rotation (`a_L` or `a_R`) to allow the robot to extricate itself. The direction of rotation is determined by the majority obstacle location.
 - **Oscillation**: An algorithm (Algorithm 4) detects sequences of alternating left-right rotations by running the policy in simulation. The corresponding leaf nodes contributing to this behavior are identified. To alleviate oscillation (Algorithm 5), such nodes are either directly assigned a lower magnitude velocity action or converted into branch nodes with new child nodes, one for problematic states (lower velocity) and one for non-problematic states (original action).

- **Outdoor Navigation Modifications**:
 - **Vibration**: Two methods are proposed to reduce robot vibrations on uneven terrain, defined by `V_b` (Equation 1) involving angular velocities in roll and pitch.
 1. **Threshold Adjustment (Method 1)**: An algorithm (Algorithm 6) identifies branch nodes that split on features related to angular velocity. Their split thresholds are then adjusted by a configurable increment `h` (Algorithm 7).
 2. **Action Modification (Method 2)**: An algorithm (Algorithm 8) identifies nodes whose abstract state space intersects with a defined "Vibration Space" (a hyperdimensional object described by the vibration equation). For these nodes, their leaf descendants' actions are modified to motion primitives with reduced linear and/or angular velocities (Algorithm 9).
 - **Oscillation**: Similar to indoor navigation, specific nodes causing oscillation are identified and modified to reduce angular velocity magnitude.

These modifications are quantified using "Objective Efficiency" (`e_O`) and "Relative Efficiency" (`e_R`) metrics (Equation 5), which measure improvement in a target metric (e.g., freezing percentage, oscillation percentage, vibration level) relative to the number of nodes modified or the percentage of total nodes modified.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents detailed findings from applying MSVIPER in both simulated and real-world robot navigation scenarios.

#### 4.1. Environments and Robot Platforms

Experiments were conducted across three distinct environments:
1. **Indoor Obstacle Avoidance**: A robot navigates among static and dynamic obstacles (pedestrians) to a random goal.
2. **Indoor Find and Follow**: A robot, after exiting a room, must locate and follow a human in a complex multi-room warehouse. This task involves three stages of increasing difficulty.
3. **Outdoor Uneven Terrain Navigation**: A robot navigates over challenging inclined or uneven terrain between a start and a goal location.

The policies were validated on real-world robot platforms: a Clearpath Jackal and a Turtlebot for indoor scenarios, and a Clearpath Husky for outdoor navigation.

#### 4.2. MSVIPER Performance vs. SSVIPER and Expert Policy

MSVIPER demonstrated improvements in several key areas compared to the single-source VIPER (SSVIPER) and successfully mimicked the expert neural network policy:

- **Higher Average Reward**: In the indoor obstacle avoidance environment, MSVIPER-generated decision trees achieved an average reward of 0.30, closely matching the expert NN policy (0.29), significantly outperforming SSVIPER (0.23). In the Find & Follow scenario, MSVIPER's reward (0.19) was also higher than SSVIPER's (0.097), although both were below the expert NN (0.464).
- **Smaller Decision Trees**: MSVIPER consistently produced smaller decision trees. For obstacle avoidance, MSVIPER yielded trees with 411 nodes, substantially smaller than SSVIPER's 2651 nodes. In the Find & Follow environment, MSVIPER trees had 1681 nodes compared to SSVIPER's 2073 nodes. (Table II, Figure 3). Smaller trees are beneficial for interpretability and modification.
- **Faster Runtime**: MSVIPER exhibited faster runtime during the distillation phase. For obstacle avoidance, it completed in 7.59 seconds, faster than SSVIPER's 8.84 seconds. In Find & Follow, MSVIPER took 34.02 seconds, whereas SSVIPER took 51.00 seconds.
- **Improved Sample Diversity and Reduced Overfitting**: The multi-scenario sampling approach of MSVIPER contributed to increased sample diversity, covering more critical states and reducing the chances of overfitting, leading to more robust policies.

#### 4.3. Indoor Navigation Results (with Tree Modification)

On real Jackal and Turtlebot robots navigating in lab environments with moving pedestrians and furniture, tree modifications applied to MSVIPER policies resulted in:

- **Reduced Freezing Behaviors**: Significant reductions in freezing instances were observed. For a policy with an initial 100% freezing rate in problematic scenarios, tree modification reduced it to 5% (a 95% reduction).
- **Reduced Oscillations**: Policies exhibiting excessive oscillation (96% oscillation rate) were improved to a 6% oscillation rate (a 90% reduction). (Table III).

#### 4.4. Outdoor Navigation Results (with Tree Modification)

Using a Clearpath Husky robot on uneven outdoor terrain, the tree modifications yielded:

- **Reduced Vibrations and Oscillations**: The initial expert-mimicking trees performed similarly to the expert policy. Post-modification, a policy with excessive vibration (0.53 initial value) was reduced to 0.48, representing an improvement of approximately 17%. Similarly, oscillation was reduced from 0.092 to 0.076. These changes contributed to more stable and smoother robot operation. (Table IV).

#### 4.5. No-Retraining Tree Modification: Efficiency Metrics

The effectiveness of tree modification was quantified using two metrics:

- **Objective Efficiency (e_O)**: Measures the improvement in a target metric per node modified.
- **Relative Efficiency (e_R)**: Measures the improvement in a target metric per percentage of nodes modified.

MSVIPER demonstrated higher efficiency in tree modification compared to SSVIPER. For example, in indoor oscillation reduction, MSVIPER achieved `e_O = 0.085` and `e_R = 121.7`, which were higher than SSVIPER's reported values (`e_O = 0.032`, `e_R = 32.9`). This indicates that MSVIPER policies required fewer node modifications (e.g., 11 nodes for oscillation vs. 31 for SSVIPER) to achieve desired improvements, reflecting their initial quality and smaller size. In some cases, MSVIPER policies at smaller sample sizes (e.g., 1K samples) resulted in no freezing behavior, implying fewer initial errors to correct.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MSVIPER framework and its associated methodologies present several significant advancements and hold potential for considerable impact in the development and deployment of autonomous robotic systems:

1. **Enabling Post-Training Policy Improvement**: The most substantial contribution is the ability to analyze and modify robot navigation policies *after* the initial training phase, without necessitating a complete retraining of the expert model. In real-world scenarios, retraining can be prohibitively expensive in terms of computational resources, time, and access to the original training setup or data. MSVIPER offers a practical solution to address newly discovered errors, adapt to changing conditions, or fine-tune performance, thus extending the lifecycle and utility of deployed RL policies.

2. **Increased Interpretability and Verifiability**: By distilling complex neural network policies into more transparent decision trees, MSVIPER inherently improves the interpretability of robot decision-making. This transparency is crucial for understanding why a robot takes certain actions, which is vital for debugging, auditing, and building trust in autonomous systems, especially in safety-critical applications. The decision tree structure also lends itself to formal verification techniques, potentially leading to more reliable and predictable robot behaviors.

3. **Improved Robot Navigation Performance and Robustness**: The demonstrated reductions in undesirable behaviors such as freezing, oscillation, and vibrations highlight MSVIPER's ability to directly enhance navigation quality. These improvements were validated on real-world robotic platforms in both indoor and outdoor environments, showcasing the practical efficacy of the approach for creating smoother, more efficient, and safer trajectories.

4. **Empowering Non-RL Experts**: The tree modification techniques are designed to be intuitive, allowing software engineers or developers without specialized knowledge in reinforcement learning to diagnose and correct specific policy shortcomings. This lowers the barrier to entry for policy customization and maintenance, potentially fostering broader adoption and application of RL in robotics.

5. **Efficient Policy Distillation**: MSVIPER's multi-scenario sampling approach produces smaller, more robust decision trees that better mimic the expert policy compared to previous methods like SSVIPER. This efficiency in distillation leads to more manageable policies for analysis and modification, and potentially reduces the computational resources needed during the distillation phase itself.

**Potential Impact**: The ability to modify policies without retraining could significantly accelerate the development and deployment cycles for autonomous robots. It offers a pathway to create human-machine collaborative systems where machine learning provides initial capabilities, and human expertise refines and corrects them in a targeted, efficient manner. This could lead to more adaptable and resilient autonomous systems in various domains beyond navigation, wherever interpretable and modifiable intelligent agents are beneficial.

**Limitations**: The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- MSVIPER currently requires a discrete action space.
- The state space must be formatted as a numerical array.
- The method, like its predecessors, relies on hyperparameter tuning (e.g., sample size, number of iterations, trajectory length).
- It may not be suitable for tasks requiring prohibitively large decision trees.
- Being an imitation learning process, it may still face challenges related to distribution shift.

**Future Work**: Future research could explore extending MSVIPER to other reinforcement learning applications beyond robot navigation. Additionally, the developed tree modification techniques could be applied to decision trees generated through other means, further broadening their utility.
