# Evolutionary Continuous Adaptive RL-Powered Co-Design for Humanoid Chin-Up Performance

> [!summary] Summary
> The EA-CoRL framework optimizes humanoid robot hardware and control policies simultaneously, enabling the RH5 robot to perform a chin-up, a high-effort task previously unfeasible due to actuator limitations. The approach integrates continuous adaptive reinforcement learning with evolutionary strategies, identifying optimal actuator gear ratios for legs and wrists to achieve a 3.85x increase in wrist torque and a 3.51x increase in leg torque, leading to superior performance and broader design space exploration compared to a baseline method.

## Problem

- Traditional robot design separates hardware finalization from control algorithm development, leading to sub-optimal performance for complex, dynamic tasks.
- Existing co-design methods lack robustness to environmental disturbances/uncertainties (optimal-control-based) or face challenges in efficient policy adaptation to changing designs and limited design-space exploration beyond simple link lengths (RL-based).
- Many high-effort tasks remain unachievable for current humanoid robots due to inherent actuator limitations, necessitating costly and extensive hardware redesigns.

## Method

- ==Introduces Evolutionary Continuous Adaptive RL==-based Co-Design (EA-CoRL), a generic framework that integrates an outer loop of design evolution (using CMA-ES) with an inner loop of continuous policy adaptation (using PPO).
- Optimizes actuator gear ratio factors for specific joint groups (lower limb, shoulder, elbow, wrist) of the RH5 humanoid, using a design-aware, model-agnostic control policy continuously fine-tuned from a pre-trained base policy for evolving hardware configurations.
- Evaluates the framework using a challenging whole-body humanoid chin-up task, focusing on maximizing performance and minimizing a fitness score derived from RL rewards.

## Results

- EA-CoRL consistently achieved a significantly lower final fitness score (higher reward) than the PreTraining-FineTuning (PT-FT) baseline, with its continuous policy adaptation mechanism yielding the highest task reward across diverse robot configurations.
- The framework identified optimal gear ratio factors for the RH5, notably increasing wrist pitch/yaw linear actuator ratios by **3.85x** and hip/knee linear actuator ratios by **3.51x**, while making smaller adjustments to shoulder and elbow actuators.
- The co-designed RH5 humanoid successfully performed the chin-up task, which was previously unfeasible with its original actuator configuration, demonstrating the framework's capability to enable high-effort motions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous policy adaptation within the co-design loop is critical for achieving robust task performance, preventing premature convergence, and enabling a more thorough exploration of the hardware design space.
> - Strategic co-optimization of decoupled actuator groups (e.g., gear ratios in legs and wrists) enables high-effort, complex motions via efficient load distribution and non-intuitive design-control synergies, without major mass or hardware increases.
> - Model-agnostic RL combined with evolutionary strategies offers a generalized co-design approach applicable to various robotic systems and dynamic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is engaged in developing robots capable of performing increasingly complex and demanding physical tasks, such as lifting heavy loads, executing assembly operations, and performing highly dynamic movements like jumping or parkour. Historically, robot design has followed a sequential process where hardware is finalized before control algorithms are developed. This traditional approach often results in sub-optimal performance, as it may not fully exploit the robot's hardware capabilities and relies heavily on designer intuition.

Recent advancements have shifted towards co-design methodologies, which advocate for the parallel optimization of both hardware design and control algorithms. This integrated approach aims to create a synergistic relationship where the robot's physical structure is optimized concurrently with its control strategy for specific goals or motion capabilities. Early co-design strategies, often based on optimal control (OC), demonstrated successes in minimizing energy consumption or enhancing range of motion. However, these OC-based methods typically rely on precise models and exhibit limitations in robustness when faced with environmental disturbances, system uncertainties, or variations from idealized conditions, thereby restricting their applicability in real-world scenarios.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a promising alternative for co-design due to its model-free nature, which inherently offers greater adaptability and robustness. RL-based approaches can leverage techniques such as domain randomization to expose policies to diverse conditions, improving their resilience to model inaccuracies and environmental uncertainties. A primary challenge in RL-based co-design, however, is the close coupling between learned policies and the specific design parameters they were trained on, often necessitating adaptation or retraining when designs change. Previous works in RL-based co-design have explored meta-RL for quadruped locomotion, co-optimization of parallel elastic joints, and structural co-design for bipedal robots, often focusing on aspects like link lengths or specific actuator types. While these contributions have advanced the field, there remains a need for more comprehensive studies that consider broader design spaces and develop versatile, generalized learning-based co-design frameworks applicable to a wider range of robotic systems with minimal architectural modifications.

This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a novel framework, Evolutionary Continuous Adaptive RL-based Co-Design (EA-CoRL), which aims to integrate continuous control policy adaptation directly into the co-design process. The work specifically targets high-effort tasks for humanoid robots, exemplified by the chin-up, which are often unachievable with existing hardware due to actuator limitations. By focusing on the co-design of actuator parameters (gear ratios) and control policies, the research seeks to enable the execution of such tasks without requiring extensive or costly hardware redesign, thereby broadening the practical scope of co-design in humanoid robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and validate a generic framework, termed Evolutionary Continuous Adaptive RL-based Co-Design (EA-CoRL), for the simultaneous optimization of robot hardware design and its corresponding control policy. This framework integrates reinforcement learning with evolutionary strategies to enable continuous adaptation of the control policy to evolving hardware configurations.

The motivation for developing EA-CoRL stems from several challenges inherent in traditional robot design and existing co-design approaches:
1. **Addressing Limitations of Sequential Design:** Traditional robot design, which separates hardware development from control algorithm development, often leads to sub-optimal performance. This sequential process can prevent robots from fully exploiting their hardware capabilities, especially for demanding, dynamic tasks.
2. **Enhancing Robustness and Adaptability:** While previous co-design methods, particularly those based on optimal control, show promise, they often lack robustness to environmental disturbances and system uncertainties due to their reliance on precise models. RL-based methods offer a model-free alternative but require efficient policy adaptation to design changes.
3. **Broadening Design Space Exploration:** Existing RL-based co-design efforts have explored specific design parameters, such as link lengths. There is a need for frameworks that can explore a wider range of design aspects, such as actuator properties, to achieve more comprehensive optimization.
4. **Enabling Previously Unfeasible Tasks:** Many high-effort tasks (e.g., heavy lifting, complex dynamic movements) remain challenging or unfeasible for current humanoid robots due to actuator limitations. The research aims to demonstrate that co-design, by optimizing actuator characteristics alongside control, can enable these tasks without necessitating significant and costly hardware modifications. This specifically targets improving performance consistency and reducing the chances of premature convergence typically observed in methods with static policies.
5. **Developing a Generic and Model-Agnostic Framework:** The objective includes creating a co-design algorithm that is model-agnostic and broadly applicable to various robotic systems, minimizing the need for extensive modifications when applied to different robot architectures or tasks.

The main contributions of this work, reflecting these objectives, are:
- The proposal of EA-CoRL, a novel RL-based framework for the co-design of both robot model parameters and control policies.
- The integration of a continuous adaptive co-design process within EA-CoRL, designed to enable broader exploration of the design space, enhance performance consistency, and mitigate premature convergence compared to baseline approaches.
- The development of a model-agnostic humanoid chin-up policy, utilized as a case study to validate the co-design capabilities for whole-body humanoid motion.
- A demonstration that a high-effort task, previously unfeasible for the RH5 humanoid due to actuator limitations, can be achieved through the co-design of motor gear ratios and an RL policy optimized on hardware parameters, thereby broadening the scope of co-design while minimizing hardware costs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Evolutionary Continuous Adaptive RL-based Co-Design (EA-CoRL) framework synergistically optimizes robot design and control policy by combining an evolutionary strategy with reinforcement learning (RL). The framework is structured into two main interconnected components: Design Evolution (outer loop) and Policy Continuous Adaptation (inner loop).

**A. Design Evolution: Outer Loop**
This phase is responsible for exploring the space of robot design parameters, denoted as $D$. The Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) is employed for this purpose due to its efficacy in black-box optimization. In each iteration of CMA-ES, candidate designs are sampled from a multivariate Gaussian distribution within $D$. Each candidate design's performance is then evaluated based on a "Fitness Score" ($J_{pop}$), which is derived from the RL training results of that specific design. The candidates exhibiting the highest performance are selected to evolve into the next generation, progressively refining the designs towards an optimized solution. Upon completion of the design evolution iterations, the best-performing robot design ($d^*$) is identified based on its maximized policy reward, which corresponds to a minimized Fitness Score ($J^*$).

**B. Continuous Adaptation: Inner Loop**
This phase focuses on the continuous fine-tuning of the control policy for new design candidates generated by the Design Evolution phase. Parallel simulation environments are instantiated within the NVIDIA Isaac Gym dynamic simulator for this purpose. This phase comprises several key elements:

1. **Base Policy ($\pi_{\theta_0}$):** An initial policy is pre-trained on a set of initial robot designs ($D_0$) generated during the early stages of the Design Evolution phase. This base policy serves as a "warm start," providing a foundational control strategy for the subsequent fine-tuning processes.
2. **Fine-tuned Policy ($\pi_{\theta^*}$):** At the beginning of the Design Evolution (outer loop), this policy is initialized with the Base Policy ($\pi_{\theta_0}$). In each subsequent iteration of the Design Evolution, $\pi_{\theta^*}$ is fine-tuned for the new population of robot designs ($D_i$). The reward obtained from training this fine-tuned policy is then used to evaluate the designs $D_i$. If the computed Fitness Score ($J_{pop,i}$) for the current population is lower (indicating a higher reward) than the best fitness score found so far ($J^*$), then $\pi_{\theta^*}$ is updated with the newly obtained fine-tuned policy.
3. **Task-Adapted Policy ($\pi_{\theta}$):** This policy is a dynamically updated version of the fine-tuned policy. It is derived from $\pi_{\theta^*}$ at each subsequent iteration of the Design Evolution, following the fine-tuning stage. If the task-adapted policy achieves a higher reward for a given design, $\pi_{\theta^*}$ is updated. This dynamic update mechanism facilitates the exploration of maximized motion performance across diverse robot configurations, leading to a refined final design ($d^*$) and an optimized policy ($\pi_{\theta^*}$).
4. **RL Training:** The problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), characterized by states ($S$), actions ($A$), transition probabilities ($P$), rewards ($r$), and a discount factor ($\gamma$). The objective is to learn a design-aware policy ($\pi_{\theta^*}$) that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted return. The model-free Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is utilized for this training. By training candidate designs in parallel, the policy learns to generalize effectively throughout the specified design space $D$, adapting to different robot configurations.

**C. Best Robot Design ($d^*$):**
Upon completion of all Design Evolution iterations, the robot design variables that yielded the highest policy reward (i.e., the lowest fitness score $J^*$) throughout the entire co-design process are selected as the best robot design $d^*$.

**Experimental Setup Details:**
- **Robot Model:** The third-generation whole-body humanoid RH5 (55 kg, 187 cm, 33 DoFs) is used as the evaluation platform. The robot features a series-parallel hybrid design, but its original configuration faces challenges in high-force tasks due to actuator limitations.
- **Task:** The chin-up exercise is selected as a benchmark to assess whole-body strength and dynamic performance. The task involves lifting the robot's body until its feet leave the floor and its head rises above a bar, a motion unachievable by the original RH5 model within its joint limits.
- **Design Space:** The design parameters consist of gear ratio factors for four groups of RH5 actuators: lower limb ($d_{leg}$, 5 DoFs), shoulder ($d_{shoulder}$, 6 DoFs), elbow ($d_{elbow}$, 2 DoFs), and wrist ($d_{wrist}$, 4 DoFs). These factors are bounded between 0.5 and 4.0. The gear ratio factor directly influences the joint torque limits ($\tau_{max}(d) = \tau_{default} \times d$) and joint velocity limits ($\dot{q}_{max}(d) = \dot{q}_{default} / d$) of the robot, modeling realistic actuator changes.
- **Observation Space:** The agent's observations ($o_t$) comprise standard proprioceptive data (e.g., target position distance, command, gravity projection, joint positions, velocities, previous actions) augmented with privileged information about the current gear ratio factors. These factors are encoded into a latent space and stacked with proprioceptive data for the actor-critic network.
- **Action Space:** The action space ($a_t$) includes continuous position and velocity commands for 17 of RH5's 33 DoFs. This subset includes 6 actuated DoFs per arm, the body pitch joint, and the knee and hip flexion-extension joints in each leg. A PD controller translates these commands into torques.
- **Reward Function:** The reward function $R_t$ is a weighted sum of multiple terms (Table I). It incentivizes successful chin-up motion (head position, feet clearance, hollow cylinder interaction), penalizes joint limit overshoots (position, velocity, torque limits), sudden control changes (action rate, joint acceleration), excessive torque output, motion asymmetry, and non-optimal orientation.
- **Deployment Workflow:** CMA-ES generates populations of 50 candidate designs. These designs are expanded across 4000 parallel simulation environments (80 rollouts per design instance) in Isaac Gym to mitigate reward noise. An initial base policy is pre-trained for 5000 iterations. In subsequent iterations, the fine-tuned policy (initialized from the previous best policy) is trained for 2500 steps. The fitness score is calculated as the negative mean reward per population. The 10 lowest fitness scores guide the next CMA-ES update. If a new best score is found, the fine-tuned policy is updated. The process runs for 50 evolution iterations on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, requiring approximately four days.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of EA-CoRL involved six independent runs with different random seeds and a comparison against a PreTraining-FineTuning (PT-FT) baseline, an RL-based co-design algorithm where a universal policy is pre-trained and then fine-tuned for specific designs without continuous adaptation.

**A. Best Robot Designs and Gear Ratios:**
- **EA-CoRL's Best Design (Figure 3, Table III):** The EA-CoRL framework determined that the leg and wrist actuator groups require significantly higher gear ratio factors compared to the shoulder and elbow groups to successfully execute the chin-up motion. For instance, the wrist pitch/yaw linear actuator's screw pitch was reduced from an original 1.00 mm to 0.26 mm (a gear ratio factor of approximately 3.85), and hip/knee linear actuators from 10.00 mm to 2.85 mm (factor of approximately 3.51). Conversely, the shoulder rotational actuator ratio changed from 1:100 to 1:112, and the elbow linear actuator from 2.00 mm to 1.17 mm (factor of approximately 1.71). This allocation indicates that the lower body contributes momentum and the wrists are critical for stability during the chin-up, compensating for the original RH5's relatively weak wrist actuators.
- **PT-FT's Best Design (Figure 3, Table III):** The PT-FT method yielded a design with lower gear ratio factors for the leg group (e.g., hip/knee at 5.88 mm screw pitch) but higher ratios for the shoulder (1:349), elbow (0.96 mm), and wrist (0.29 mm) groups. This suggests a different load distribution, with PT-FT favoring higher torque in the upper body and wrists more uniformly.
- **Interpretation:** EA-CoRL's ability to decouple and optimize these gear ratio groups resulted in a whole-body design that efficiently utilizes the lower body for momentum while minimizing the torque demands on the shoulder actuators, aligning with the task's biomechanics for the RH5 robot. The derived gear ratios represent substantial adjustments that could be implemented without significantly increasing system mass.

**B. Design Space Exploration and Robustness:**
- **Correlation Heatmaps (Figure 4):** Six 2D heatmaps illustrating the fitness score distributions across the design space revealed correlations between gear ratio factor groups. The blue stars representing EA-CoRL's best designs consistently fell within high-reward regions (darker areas), and these regions surrounded the optimal point, indicating robustness. In contrast, the green crosses representing PT-FT's best designs were often found in regions with lower rewards, particularly for shoulder and wrist ratios, suggesting a less optimal exploration of the design space.

**C. Performance Trajectories and Learning Efficiency:**
- **Fitness Score Trajectories (Figure 5):** When averaged over six random seeds, EA-CoRL consistently achieved a significantly lower final fitness score (corresponding to a higher reward) compared to PT-FT across the entire design evolution process. PT-FT's fitness score trajectory showed signs of stagnation after initial improvement. This outcome supports the effectiveness of EA-CoRL's continuous policy updates, which allow for more dynamic adaptation and a better balance between exploration and exploitation.
- **RL Learning Curves (Figure 6):** The learning curves for the RH5 chin-up task demonstrated that EA-CoRL's best design policy achieved the highest final task reward, directly benefiting from its continuous adaptation mechanism. PT-FT, while converging quickly, showed its policy remaining "frozen" within a pre-trained region, limiting further performance improvement. Both methods, however, exhibited faster convergence compared to training policies from scratch, validating the efficiency of fine-tuning from a warm-start or pre-trained policy.

**D. Actuator Load Distribution:**
- Analysis of the best EA-CoRL design revealed that the shoulder yaw actuator operated at only 18% of its torque limit, in contrast to the shoulder roll and pitch actuators, indicating its limited contribution to the chin-up motion. The increased gear ratios in the wrist and leg actuators underscored their critical roles, suggesting that RH5, unlike humans who rely on powerful back muscles, compensates for structural limitations by increasing torque in the wrists and lower body.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the Evolutionary Continuous Adaptive RL-based Co-Design (EA-CoRL) framework, which represents an advancement in the co-design of robotic systems by integrating continuous policy adaptation with evolutionary design optimization.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:
- **Enabling High-Effort Tasks:** A primary impact is the demonstration that EA-CoRL can facilitate the execution of complex, high-effort tasks previously considered unfeasible for existing humanoid robots, such as the RH5 performing a chin-up, due to actuator limitations. This is achieved by strategically co-optimizing actuator gear ratios with the control policy, rather than requiring extensive and potentially costly hardware redesign. This expands the repertoire of dynamic movements humanoids can perform.
- **Improved Performance and Robustness:** The comparative analysis against the PreTraining-FineTuning (PT-FT) method indicates that EA-CoRL achieves superior task performance (higher rewards) and exhibits greater robustness through a broader exploration of the design space. The continuous policy adaptation mechanism prevents premature convergence, allowing for more thorough optimization of both hardware and control.
- **Broader Design Space Exploration:** By addressing actuator characteristics (gear ratios) across multiple joint groups, the framework moves beyond optimizing simpler parameters like link lengths, demonstrating a more comprehensive approach to robotic co-design. This capability is crucial for discovering non-intuitive design-control synergies.
- **Model-Agnostic and Generalizable Framework:** The proposed EA-CoRL framework is designed to be model-agnostic, implying its potential applicability to a wide range of other humanoid and legged robotic systems. This generality could accelerate research and development in diverse robotic platforms without requiring substantial modifications to the core methodology.
- **Cost Efficiency:** By demonstrating that significant performance gains for demanding tasks can be achieved through software-driven co-design of actuator parameters (which might involve simple gear changes) rather than entirely new hardware builds, the work offers a pathway to more cost-effective robotic development and adaptation.
- **Advancement in RL for Robotics:** The integration of continuous policy adaptation within an evolutionary loop enhances the utility of RL in robotic co-design. It underscores the importance of dynamic policy refinement in scenarios where hardware parameters are constantly evolving.

Future implications of this research include the potential for:
- **Multi-Task Optimization:** Extending the framework to co-design robots for a benchmark set of diverse tasks, moving beyond single-task optimization.
- **Refined Reward Formulations:** Incorporating biomechanical theories and more sophisticated performance metrics into reward functions to better capture the complexities of physical interaction and design effectiveness.
- **Advanced Optimization Techniques:** Integrating techniques such as Bayesian optimization or imitation learning to further enhance the efficiency and precision of the co-design process.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** Addressing computational challenges, particularly in dynamic simulations (e.g., convex hull collision computations), to improve scalability and facilitate seamless transfer of co-designed robots from simulation to real-world deployment.

In summary, this work contributes to the advancement of humanoid robotics by providing a robust and adaptable framework for co-design, opening new avenues for developing high-performance robots capable of executing a wider array of dynamic and challenging tasks with greater efficiency and lower hardware costs.
