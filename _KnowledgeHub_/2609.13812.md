# GeomVLA: Unifying Scene, Motion, and Action in 3D

> [!summary] Summary
> GeomVLA is a vision-language-action model that lifts pretrained VLM features into robot-centric 3D scene tokens using depth and camera calibration, then predicts latent future 3D point motion with a task-conditioned Scene Trajectory Denoiser whose intermediate tokens condition a flow-matching 3D action denoiser through geometry-aware attention. It reaches state-of-the-art 4.624 on CALVIN and 62.5% real-world success without robot-action pretraining.

## Problem

- Most VLAs reason over 2D image features while manipulation needs metric geometry, contact and motion in 3D, creating a mismatch between representation and control.
- Existing 3D VLAs align only static scene representation with action generation, leaving unaddressed how intermediate future reasoning should be represented inside the control pipeline.
- Future-prediction policies forecast videos, optical flow or traces in pixel space while actions execute in 3D, so motion reasoning and control share no common frame.

## Method

- Lifts Florence-2 patch features into robot-base-frame ==3D scene tokens== via depth and camera calibration, with ==3D rotary positional embeddings== keeping scene, motion and action in one metric frame.
- **3D Scene Trajectory Denoiser**: a ==flow-matching decoder== over a 20x20 anchor grid predicting per-step 3D displacements for 15 future steps, trained on SpatialTrackerV2 pseudo-labels with a ==motion-weighted rectified-flow loss==.
- One ==velocity-field== evaluation at pure noise yields intermediate motion tokens, pooled by ==temporal attention== and fused into scene tokens through a zero-initialized ==geometry-aware cross-attention== layer feeding the ==3D action denoiser==.

## Results

- CALVIN ABC-D average length **4.624** versus **4.480** for FLOWER and **4.430** for X-VLA; LIBERO **98.4%**, RoboTwin2.0 five-task **84.8%**, all without robot-action pretraining.
- Real-world ALOHA2 eight-task mean **62.5%** versus **44.4%** without motion and **29.4%** for LoRA-tuned pi-0.5; the joint-angle variant scores **59.4%** against **28.8%** for its 2D counterpart.
- Ablations: image-space motion with 3D actions drops to **4.085**, fully denoised traces to **4.047**, a frozen random denoiser to **4.443**; motion adds **61 ms**, **27.9%** latency overhead.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Future-motion prediction pays off mainly when scene, motion and action share one metric frame; mixing image-space reasoning with 3D control performs worse than staying fully 2D.
> - Early-stage denoiser latents beat finalized trajectories as conditioning: the policy wants the direction of scene evolution, not a committed open-loop plan to track.
> - Longer prediction horizons hurt; extending 15 to 32 steps raised trajectory error and lowered success, so harder auxiliary tasks can degrade the learned representation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) models connect visual observations and natural-language instructions to robot actions. Recent systems have used pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) to transfer semantic knowledge from image and language data into robotic manipulation. This has improved the ability of robots to interpret object identities, task descriptions, and high-level instructions. However, many VLA systems continue to represent observations mainly as two-dimensional image tokens, even though manipulation depends on metric three-dimensional relationships, including object position, depth, orientation, contact, and collision avoidance.

A parallel research direction has developed 3D manipulation policies that represent scenes and actions in point-cloud, voxel, feature-field, or Cartesian coordinate spaces. These methods generally provide stronger spatial grounding than image-only policies, but they may not retain the broad semantic knowledge of large pretrained VLMs as directly as conventional VLAs. More recent 3D VLA systems attempt to combine semantic VLM features with geometric representations by lifting image features into 3D or using separate visual and 3D processing streams.

A third line of work introduces future prediction or motion reasoning as an intermediate signal for control. Such methods predict videos, optical flow, visual traces, object trajectories, or gripper paths before or alongside action generation. Future prediction can provide information about task dynamics and help with long-horizon manipulation. Nevertheless, much of this reasoning is performed in image space, while the resulting robot actions are executed in 3D. GeomVLA addresses the relationship among these three areas by placing scene representation, future scene motion, and action generation in a common robot-centric 3D coordinate frame.

The paper’s central claim is not simply that future-motion prediction is useful, but that it is more useful when its representation is geometrically consistent with both perception and control. The method therefore focuses on integration rather than on adding a standalone motion-prediction auxiliary task.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Addressing the 2D-to-3D Control Mismatch

The primary motivation is the mismatch between image-centric perception and the metric geometry required for manipulation. A two-dimensional image can indicate that one object is visually near another, but it does not directly specify their three-dimensional distance, relative height, or pose. Camera viewpoint and depth ambiguity can make image-space relationships unstable across scenes and robot configurations.

GeomVLA aims to preserve the semantic features learned by a pretrained VLM while attaching each feature to a calibrated 3D position. This allows the model to reason about both what an image region represents and where the associated scene content is located relative to the robot.

#### 2.2. Integrating Future Motion With Control

The second objective is to provide a representation of how the scene is expected to change while the robot performs the instruction. Instead of predicting only the next action, the model predicts future 3D displacement trajectories for sampled scene points. These trajectories can encode effects such as an object being lifted, moved, inserted, or displaced during a multi-step task.

The predicted trajectories are not directly executed as an open-loop plan. Instead, GeomVLA extracts intermediate hidden states from the trajectory-prediction module and uses them as latent motion tokens for action generation. This design is intended to allow the policy to benefit from future-motion reasoning without requiring the motion predictor to produce a fully accurate, executable plan.

#### 2.3. Improving Long-Horizon and Contact-Rich Manipulation

The authors specifically target tasks involving delayed effects, precise placement, insertion, stacking, and multiple sequential actions. In such settings, the action that should be taken at the current time may depend on the expected future arrangement of objects. The evaluation is consequently conducted on benchmarks covering long-horizon manipulation, compositional generalization, single-arm and dual-arm control, and real-world tabletop tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Inputs and 3D Scene Representation

GeomVLA receives multi-view RGB-D observations, a language instruction, and robot proprioception. A pretrained Florence-2 VLM encodes the RGB images and instruction into visual patch tokens and language tokens. Depth values are assigned to image patches by interpolation, and camera intrinsics and extrinsics transform the corresponding image locations into positions in the robot base frame.

The resulting lifted scene tokens combine visual semantics, image coordinates, and 3D position. Local geometry-aware attention is applied over neighboring 3D points, allowing the model to integrate nearby scene structure. Proprioceptive information is represented by learned tokens anchored at the current end-effector pose. Global visual and language tokens are retained as additional conditioning information.

#### 3.2. 3D Scene Trajectory Denoiser

The main new component is the **3D Scene Trajectory Denoiser**. From a front-camera RGB-D image, the model samples a regular 20-by-20 grid, producing **400 3D scene anchors**. For each anchor, it predicts a sequence of future 3D displacement increments over a horizon of **15 downsampled time steps**. Since the videos are temporally downsampled by a factor of three, this corresponds to approximately 45 original video frames.

The predicted future position of a point is obtained by cumulatively adding its displacement increments to its initial 3D position. The trajectory decoder is conditioned on local scene tokens, global image features, and language features. Its attention structure alternates between spatial attention over scene anchors and temporal attention over future steps. Spatial attention is implemented through serialized windows along a 3D Morton, or Z-order, curve to reduce computational cost while preserving spatial locality.

Training uses pseudo-label trajectories generated by SpatialTrackerV2 from benchmark demonstration videos. The model is trained with conditional rectified flow matching. Gaussian noise is interpolated with the target trajectory, and the decoder learns the velocity field that transforms the noise into the target displacement sequence. A motion-weighted loss gives greater emphasis to points that move while retaining a nonzero contribution from static points. Invalid or low-confidence tracks, including those affected by occlusion or poor depth, are masked or downweighted.

#### 3.3. Latent Motion Features and Action Generation

During policy inference, GeomVLA does not need to fully denoise and integrate a trajectory before producing an action. It evaluates the trajectory denoiser once at the initial flow state, where the input is Gaussian noise. The decoder’s intermediate tensor contains features for each scene anchor and future time step. A learned temporal-attention operation pools the time dimension into one motion token per anchor.

These motion tokens are fused with the lifted 3D scene tokens using a geometry-aware cross-attention layer. The resulting motion-aware scene representation is then provided to the action denoiser. The action denoiser represents noisy end-effector trajectories as spatial action tokens and alternates among cross-attention to scene and language tokens, self-attention over the action sequence, and conditioning on flow time and proprioception. Three-dimensional rotary positional embeddings are used throughout the spatial attention layers.

The model predicts end-effector translation and rotation trajectories, together with binary gripper states, using a rectified-flow objective for continuous actions and binary cross-entropy for gripper predictions. An optional **joint-angle denoiser** uses the motion-aware scene representation and intermediate end-effector action features to produce joint-angle commands instead of directly executing Cartesian actions.

#### 3.4. Training Procedure

Training occurs in two stages. First, the trajectory denoiser is trained from SpatialTrackerV2 pseudo-labels while the VLM remains frozen. Second, the action denoiser, trajectory denoiser, and VLM are jointly fine-tuned using the action loss. The trajectory pseudo-label loss is not retained during this second stage. The system uses a pretrained Florence-2 VLM but does not use large-scale robot-action pretraining; it is trained on the demonstrations provided by each benchmark.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Benchmarks

On **CALVIN**, GeomVLA obtains a score of **4.624** on the ABC-D long-horizon evaluation. This exceeds the reported scores of FLOWER at 4.480 and X-VLA at 4.430. The action-only 3D variant, GeomVLA without motion reasoning, scores 4.508, indicating that the motion module provides an additional improvement beyond the underlying 3D policy.

On **LIBERO**, GeomVLA achieves an average success rate of **98.4%**. The paper reports this as the best result among the compared methods that do not use reinforcement-learning fine-tuning. The gains are reported to be particularly relevant for longer-horizon and contact-sensitive tasks.

On a five-task subset of **RoboTwin2.0**, GeomVLA reaches an average success rate of **84.8%**, compared with 81.2% for the cited π₀.₅ baseline. The selected tasks range from approximately 100 to 300 action steps and include hammering, bottle placement, moving a can into a pot, handover, and bowl stacking. Across all 50 RoboTwin2.0 tasks, GeomVLA obtains **78.6% under Easy randomization and 76.2% under Hard randomization**, compared with 75.9% and 75.7% for π₀.₅. The paper notes, however, that the five-task comparison is not a fully controlled ablation because different variants were trained on different task sets.

#### 4.2. Ablation Findings

The ablations identify several distinct effects. Adding future-motion reasoning to a 2D policy increases the CALVIN score from 4.411 to 4.437, while adding it to the 3D action policy increases performance from 4.508 to 4.624. This suggests that motion prediction can provide temporal information beyond direct action imitation.

The representation and point at which motion information is injected are important. Conditioning on the fully denoised 3D trajectory produces a score of 4.047, while conditioning on a fully denoised latent produces 4.334. Reading the motion latent at a slightly later flow state produces 4.484. The full method, which extracts intermediate features from the initial noisy state, reaches 4.624. Using only the trajectory encoder reaches 4.443. These results indicate that early denoising states may provide a useful abstraction of intended scene evolution without committing to a finalized trajectory.

Geometric consistency is also supported by the ablations. Using image-space future motion while retaining a 3D action denoiser reduces the CALVIN score to 4.085. A variant using image-plane coordinates with metric depth reaches 4.462, below the full 3D formulation. In contrast, expressing scene, motion, and action in a shared arbitrary rigid frame produces 4.596. This suggests that consistency among coordinate representations is more important than using the robot base frame specifically.

The trajectory predictor is instruction-sensitive. Replacing the correct instruction with a mismatched one increases endpoint error by 3.40 cm on CALVIN, 5.44 cm on LIBERO, and 1.64 cm on RoboTwin2.0. Increasing the trajectory horizon from 15 to 32 steps worsens mean error from 2.46 cm to 3.35 cm and reduces the CALVIN score from 4.624 to 4.272.

#### 4.3. Real-World Evaluation

The real-world experiments use an ALOHA2 platform with two elevated Azure Kinect cameras and a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense camera. Eight single-arm tabletop tasks test insertion, stacking, placement, reorientation, object transfer, uncapping, color ordering, and spatial arrangement.

GeomVLA achieves an average success rate of **62.5%**, compared with 44.4% for the version without motion reasoning and 29.4% for π₀.₅. It exceeds the π₀.₅ baseline on seven of eight tasks. Its strongest reported results include 19/20 for stacking blocks, 20/20 for uncapping a marker, and 17/20 for standing a bottle upright. Ranking bottles by color remains difficult, with only 2/20 successful trials.

The joint-angle variant achieves **59.4%**, compared with 28.8% for a matched 2D joint-angle model. This comparison indicates that the geometric representation contributes even when the final control output is not Cartesian.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GeomVLA contributes an architectural principle for robotic VLA systems: semantic perception, future-motion reasoning, and action generation should be connected through a common metric geometry. The results suggest that simply adding a motion-prediction objective or a 3D module is insufficient if the intermediate representations remain inconsistent with the action space.

Potential applications include manipulation tasks requiring precise spatial relationships, object insertion, stacking, and multi-stage execution. The joint-angle extension also indicates that 3D reasoning can serve as an intermediate representation for different robot control interfaces and embodiments.

Several limitations affect its broader applicability. The method depends on accurate depth and camera calibration, which can be problematic in real environments. It is trained only on benchmark-scale demonstrations and lacks large-scale robot-action or cross-embodiment pretraining. Its future reasoning is latent and geometric rather than language-explicit, so it does not directly provide interpretable plans or hierarchical task decomposition. Finally, the motion module adds computational cost: inference time increases from approximately 219 to 280 milliseconds per action chunk, a 27.9% overhead. Future work could address calibration robustness, larger and more diverse datasets, explicit language-based planning, and improved integration of geometric reasoning with failure recovery.
