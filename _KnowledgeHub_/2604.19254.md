# ShadowPEFT: Shadow Network for Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> ShadowPEFT presents a Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) framework that employs a shared shadow network for centralized, layer-level adaptation within Large Language Models. This approach achieves competitive or superior performance against LoRA and DoRA on generation and understanding benchmarks, while offering architectural decoupling for flexible deployment, including efficient detached inference on edge devices.

## Problem

- Traditional fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs) incurs substantial computational costs for task-specific adaptation.
- Existing low-rank PEFT methods like LoRA suffer from 'linear-local parameterization,' leading to fragmented and depth-inconsistent adaptation across the model layers.
- Current PEFT approaches are often tightly coupled to the LLM's backbone weight structure, hindering modularity, reusability, and flexible deployment options.

## Method

- **ShadowPEFT** augments a frozen LLM backbone with a lightweight, shared ==shadow model== that maintains and evolves a parallel hidden state.
- A ==Shadow Injection Module== modulates the backbone's hidden state using the shadow state, while a ==GRU-like Shadow Update Module== continuously updates the shadow state across Transformer layers.
- The adaptation mechanism is centralized within a single, portable functional module, allowing ==architectural decoupling== from the backbone's internal weights.

## Results

- ShadowPEFT achieved competitive or superior average performance (e.g., Qwen3 8B, **76.92**) compared to LoRA (**76.51**) and DoRA (**75.99**) on generation and understanding benchmarks, often with fewer trainable parameters.
- Pretraining the shadow model enabled a practical detached inference mode, retaining an average performance of **62.11** for a Qwen3 8B backbone with a **0.5B** shadow, demonstrating its standalone utility for edge devices.
- ShadowPEFT exhibited improved out-of-distribution generalization and effectively leveraged larger trainable parameter budgets (up to **0.4B** shadow for 8B backbone), unlike the performance degradation observed in DoRA.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Centralizing adaptation within a shared, stateful 'shadow network' operating at the layer level can provide more consistent and effective task adaptation than distributed low-rank perturbations.
> - Architecturally decoupling the adaptation module from the LLM backbone enables enhanced modularity, standalone deployment, and efficient 'detached shadow-only' inference for resource-constrained environments.
> - Pretraining the shadow model facilitates cross-scale adaptation and knowledge transfer, allowing a smaller pretrained model to effectively serve as an adaptive module for a larger backbone.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid growth of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to substantial computational costs associated with their fine-tuning for specific downstream tasks. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods have emerged as a solution to this challenge by enabling adaptation of LLMs by updating only a small subset of parameters while freezing the extensive pretrained backbone.

Existing PEFT approaches generally fall into three categories:
1. **Prompt- and Prefix-based methods:** These methods, such as Prompt Tuning, Prefix Tuning, and P-Tuning, optimize a small set of continuous prompt parameters. While highly parameter-efficient, their expressive capacity can be limited by context length, and they do not achieve persistent task-specific adaptation in the same way as model weight modifications.
2. **Adapter-based methods:** Approaches like those introduced by Houlsby et al., AdapterSoup, and Compacter insert lightweight bottleneck modules into Transformer blocks. These methods are modular and effective but typically involve independently optimized adapters at each layer, potentially leading to redundancy and fragmented adaptation across the network's depth.
3. **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and its variants:** LoRA, along with its extensions like QLoRA and DoRA, has become a dominant choice due to its simplicity and effectiveness. LoRA injects trainable low-rank updates into selected linear projections of the frozen pretrained weights.

This paper identifies a limitation in LoRA-style PEFT, characterizing it as a "linear-local parameterization." This means that each selected linear layer receives an independent trainable update, and task adaptation arises from the aggregate effect of many separate perturbations distributed across the model's depth. This fragmented approach, where each linear layer learns a distinct transformation without explicit cross-layer sharing of an adaptation state or function, can lead to inconsistent depth-wise adaptation. Furthermore, LoRA's adaptation mechanism is closely tied to the backbone's internal weight structure, limiting its architectural decoupling and standalone deployment.

ShadowPEFT aims to address these limitations by proposing an alternative PEFT design. Instead of distributing independent low-rank perturbations, ShadowPEFT centralizes adaptation within a shared functional module that operates on the hidden representations of Transformer layers. This work positions itself as a development that shifts the focus from decentralized linear-level perturbations to a centralized, layer-level refinement process, thereby offering enhanced modularity, reusability, and flexible deployment options that are not inherent to conventional low-rank PEFT methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce an alternative paradigm for Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) that deviates from the conventional approach of distributing small, independent parameter updates across a large language model's (LLM) internal weights. The core motivation stems from perceived limitations of existing methods, particularly Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and its variants.

The detailed objectives and motivations are:

- **Address Fragmented Adaptation in LoRA-style PEFT:** The authors argue that LoRA's strategy of injecting independent low-rank perturbations into individual weight matrices at various points across the LLM's architecture results in a "linear-local parameterization." This means each modification operates somewhat in isolation, leading to a fragmented adaptation mechanism where task adaptation emerges from the aggregate effect of many uncoordinated local changes. The research aims to explore a "centralized" approach to overcome this, where adaptation is managed by a single, shared functional module.
- **Decouple Adaptation from Backbone Weight Structure:** Conventional low-rank PEFT methods tightly couple the adaptation parameters to the backbone's internal weight structure. This strong coupling limits the independent utility and deployability of the adaptation modules. A key objective for ShadowPEFT is to architecturally decouple the adaptation mechanism from the frozen backbone, allowing it to function as a standalone, portable component.
- **Propose Layer-Level Refinement via a Depth-Shared Shadow Module:** Instead of weight-space perturbations, the paper aims to implement layer-level refinement. This involves augmenting the frozen backbone with a "shadow network" that maintains a parallel hidden state and iteratively refines it across the Transformer layers. This shifts the locus of adaptation from distributed weight changes to a shared, functional refinement process in the layer space.
- **Enable Modular and Detachable Deployment:** By decoupling the shadow module, the authors seek to enable two distinct deployment benefits:
 - **Modular deployment and independent versioning:** The shadow module can be attached or detached from the base model without modifying the backbone weights, offering flexibility in how adaptation is applied and updated.
 - **Benefits for edge computing:** The detached shadow model can be deployed independently, allowing for lightweight inference on resource-constrained edge devices, while the larger backbone (with attached shadow) can reside on a cloud server for more complex tasks.
- **Facilitate Cross-Scale Adaptation and Reusability through Pretraining:** The research aims to demonstrate that the shadow model can be initialized from a smaller pretrained model (e.g., a 0.5B model serving an 8B backbone). This allows the shadow model to act as a reusable adaptation module across different scales of backbone models, potentially leveraging existing compact models' pretraining knowledge.
- **Achieve Competitive or Superior Performance:** Despite its architectural differences and additional deployment flexibilities, a core objective is to ensure that ShadowPEFT matches or surpasses the performance of established low-rank PEFT methods like LoRA and DoRA under comparable or even slightly smaller trainable parameter budgets on various benchmarks.
- **Demonstrate Generalization and Efficiency:** The work also seeks to show that the proposed centralized adaptation improves out-of-distribution generalization and introduces minimal inference latency overhead compared to existing PEFT methods, thus making it practical for real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ShadowPEFT proposes a centralized PEFT framework that augments a frozen pretrained Large Language Model (LLM) backbone with a lightweight, shared "shadow model." This shadow model operates at the Transformer layer level, maintaining and evolving a parallel hidden state that provides task-adaptive refinement signals to the backbone.

#### 3.1. ShadowPEFT Overview

The framework processes input sequences `x` through an LLM backbone composed of `L` Transformer decoder layers. For each layer `ℓ`, the base model produces a hidden state `h_out^(ℓ)`. The shadow model maintains a parallel "shadow state" `s^(ℓ)`.
The initial shadow state `s^(0)` is generated by a shadow backbone `f_shadow(x; θ_shadow)`. For each base layer from `ℓ = 1` to `L-1`, ShadowPEFT executes three sequential steps:

1. **Shadow Injection:** The shadow model injects trainable parameters into the previous base hidden state using the current shadow state.
2. **Base Encoding:** The frozen base layer processes this refined hidden state.
3. **Shadow Update:** The shadow model updates its internal shadow state based on the newly obtained base hidden representation.

This process conceptualizes adaptation as a depth-wise state-space process where a single, portable adaptation pathway is reused across layers, rather than independent local weight perturbations.

#### 3.2. Shadow Injection Module

Before base layer `ℓ` processes its input (`h_out^(ℓ-1)`), the Shadow Injection Module modulates `h_out^(ℓ-1)` using `s^(ℓ-1)`. This is the point where the shadow's influence is directly applied to the backbone.
The module calculates the representational difference, or discrepancy, between the current backbone state and the shadow state:
`δ^(ℓ) = h_out^(ℓ-1) - s^(ℓ-1)`
This discrepancy is then projected through a lightweight trainable low-rank bottleneck to produce a task-specific correction `˜δ^(ℓ)`:
`˜δ^(ℓ) = Dropout(δ^(ℓ) W_down^(ℓ)) W_up^(ℓ)`
Here, `W_down^(ℓ) ∈ R^(d×r)` and `W_up^(ℓ) ∈ R^(r×d)` are layer-dependent trainable projection matrices, with `r ≪ d`. `W_down^(ℓ)` is initialized from `N(0, σ^2)` and `W_up^(ℓ)` is zero-initialized, ensuring `˜δ^(ℓ) = 0` at initialization. The low-rank bottleneck ensures parameter efficiency and filters the discrepancy, learning which components are useful for adaptation.
The corrected representation `h^(ℓ)` is then obtained via a residual connection:
`h^(ℓ) ← h_out^(ℓ-1) + α ˜δ^(ℓ)`
where `α > 0` controls the injection strength. This `h^(ℓ)` is then fed into the base layer `ℓ`.

#### 3.3. Shadow Update Module

After base layer `ℓ` produces its output `h_out^(ℓ)`, the Shadow Update Module updates the shadow state `s^(ℓ-1)` to `s^(ℓ)`. This step allows the shadow to absorb new information from the base model, maintaining a task-adaptive reference across depth.
A gated residual update mechanism is employed, similar to a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU):
`t^(ℓ) = T^(ℓ)(h_out^(ℓ))`
`g^(ℓ) = σ(G^(ℓ)(h_out^(ℓ)))`
`s^(ℓ) = (1 - g^(ℓ)) ⊙ s^(ℓ-1) + g^(ℓ) ⊙ t^(ℓ)`
where `⊙` is element-wise multiplication, and `σ` is the sigmoid function. `T^(ℓ)(·)` and `G^(ℓ)(·)` are implemented as lightweight two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). `T^(ℓ)(·)` maps the base output to a candidate shadow representation, while `G^(ℓ)(·)` predicts an element-wise gate to control the interpolation between the previous shadow state and the current base state.

#### 3.4. Base-Shadow Joint Training

ShadowPEFT is trained end-to-end, with the base model remaining frozen. The trainable components include the shadow backbone, the injection projections (`W_down`, `W_up`), the update networks (`T^(ℓ)`, `G^(ℓ)`), and a shadow prediction head.

- **Causal language modeling objective:** For tasks like language generation, the training loss combines the standard cross-entropy loss from the base model's final hidden state with an auxiliary cross-entropy loss from the shadow model's initial state:
 `L = L_CE(h_base W_lm, y) + λ L_CE(s^(0) W_shadow, y)`
 where `λ` is a scalar hyperparameter (default 0.05). The auxiliary shadow loss acts as a regularizer, stabilizing optimization and encouraging the shadow to encode task-relevant information independently.
- **Sequence classification objective:** For classification tasks, the final shadow hidden state `s^(L)` is pooled into a single vector, then scored by a shadow classifier head `W_shadow_cls`. The joint classification loss is:
 `L_CLS = L_CE(W_cls h_base, y) + λ L_CE(W_shadow_cls ¯s^(L), y)`

#### 3.5. Inference Modes

ShadowPEFT supports two distinct inference configurations:

1. **Shadow-attached inference:** The full ShadowPEFT pipeline is executed, with the primary prediction originating from the shadow-attached base model. The shadow head can optionally provide an auxiliary output.
2. **Detached shadow-only inference:** Only the shadow backbone and its prediction head are utilized. This mode bypasses the large base model, enabling lightweight deployment, particularly beneficial for edge computing scenarios, while still leveraging the task-adaptive representations learned during fine-tuning.

#### 3.6. Centralized Shadow Model Designs (Appendix A and B)

The shadow model can be initialized in two ways:
- **Implicit setting:** Derived from the base model's configuration by reducing layers, intermediate width, and attention heads.
- **Explicit setting:** An independently provided, compatible architecture, enabling cross-model knowledge transfer.
To avoid duplicate embedding tables, ShadowPEFT shares the base model's frozen embedding layer. If the shadow model has a different hidden dimension than the base model, a learned linear projection aligns the shadow output.

For pretraining, the paper describes reusing a Qwen3 0.6B model as a shadow for a Qwen3 8B base model. To bridge their different hidden dimensions, a linear projection matrix `P` is introduced. `P` is initialized using the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse of the 8B model's language model head and the 0.6B model's original head, providing a warm start that approximates the original output distribution. This shadow model is then further pretrained on a subset of FineWeb-Edu (English) and Wudao (Chinese) corpora.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ShadowPEFT involved comparisons against LoRA and DoRA across three backbone scales of the Qwen3 family (0.6B, 4B, 8B) on a set of generation and understanding benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Main Performance Comparison (Table 1)

ShadowPEFT demonstrated competitive or superior average performance across all tested backbone scales, often with a slightly smaller trainable parameter budget than its baselines.
- **Qwen3 0.6B:** ShadowPEFT achieved an average score of 62.27, outperforming LoRA (61.81) and DoRA (62.08).
- **Qwen3 4B:** ShadowPEFT scored 75.43 on average, surpassing LoRA (74.55) and DoRA (74.85).
- **Qwen3 8B:** ShadowPEFT obtained an average of 76.92, which was higher than LoRA (76.51) and DoRA (75.99).
The performance gains on individual datasets were generally consistent, indicating the effectiveness of centralized layer-level adaptation.

#### 4.2. Impact of Shadow Pretraining and Detached Inference

- **Randomly Initialized Shadow (Qwen3 8B + 0.5B Shadow):** When using a randomly initialized 0.5B shadow model, ShadowPEFT in attached mode performed competitively, though marginally lower than the default compact shadow configuration (76.60 vs 76.92 average). The detached shadow-only inference mode with random initialization resulted in a substantial performance collapse (average ~35-41 across scales), indicating it lacked standalone capability.
- **Pretrained Shadow (Qwen3 8B + 0.5B Shadow):** Pretraining the 0.5B shadow model significantly improved performance. In the attached mode, it yielded the best overall result (77.11 average), showing notable gains on reasoning tasks like GSM8K (from 80.21 to 82.18) and SQuAD v2 (from 87.39 to 87.78). Critically, the detached shadow-only inference with the pretrained shadow retained a reasonable average performance of 62.11, surpassing fine-tuned Qwen3 0.6B models with LoRA and DoRA. This finding supports the concept of shadow pretrainability and its utility as a standalone lightweight model.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study on Shadow Update Module

Removing the shadow update module in ShadowPEFT (on Qwen3 4B) resulted in a performance drop of 2.43 points on GSM8K (79.00 to 76.57), while performance on the Amazon sentiment classification task remained nearly unchanged. This suggests that the continuous update of the shadow's internal state is particularly crucial for generation tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, but less so for simpler classification.

#### 4.4. Generalization Performance (Out-of-Distribution, OOD)

ShadowPEFT consistently achieved the highest OOD performance across all evaluated training conditions (training on one dataset and evaluating on others with 2-shot demonstrations). For instance, when trained on GSM8K, ShadowPEFT achieved an OOD average of 50.61, compared to LoRA's 50.40 and DoRA's 48.57. This indicates that ShadowPEFT not only maintains in-distribution performance but also preserves generalization capabilities.

#### 4.5. Trainable Parameter Scaling

An analysis of trainable parameter scaling (0.1B to 0.5B for the shadow model with Qwen3-8B backbone) showed distinct behaviors:
- **ShadowPEFT:** Consistently benefited from a larger shadow model, with GSM8K accuracy increasing from 81.35 (0.1B) to a peak of 82.12 (0.4B), then marginally decreasing at 0.5B (81.80). This indicates its ability to effectively absorb additional capacity.
- **LoRA:** Showed nearly flat performance across all shadow sizes.
- **DoRA:** Experienced a monotonic degradation in performance as parameter scale increased (81.12 at 0.1B to 77.79 at 0.5B), consistent with known limitations of low-rank methods where excessive rank can hurt generalization.

#### 4.6. Efficiency (Inference Latency)

ShadowPEFT introduced minimal inference overhead compared to LoRA, with latency increases ranging from 3.7% to 5.9% across the Qwen3 0.6B, 4B, and 8B models. DoRA, in contrast, incurred substantially higher costs. The low overhead for ShadowPEFT is attributed to its parallel shadow forward pass and lightweight residual connections for injection and updates.

#### 4.7. System-Level Evaluation (Robot Intent Understanding)

A system-level experiment on a Unitree Go2 robot dog for intent understanding demonstrated ShadowPEFT's practical benefits:
- **Lowest Latency and Highest Accuracy:** ShadowPEFT achieved superior accuracy (99.35% vs. 97.7% for LoRA/DoRA) and the lowest latency.
- **Detachable Design Advantage:** The detached shadow model could resolve routine robot skills locally on the edge device, leading to low latency. Complex or open-domain requests were safely forwarded to the full ShadowPEFT model on a cloud server (indicated by a `[REMOTE]` tag), preventing local hallucinations. LoRA and DoRA, lacking this detached mode, always relied on the full model.
This confirms the practicality of ShadowPEFT's detached and attached modes, particularly for edge computing with a robust remote fallback mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ShadowPEFT introduces a distinct approach to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) that deviates from prevailing low-rank adaptation methods. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Conceptual Shift in PEFT Design:** This work reframes PEFT from a process of injecting fragmented, linear-local weight perturbations to one of centralized, stateful, and functional layer-level adaptation. By introducing a shared shadow network that maintains and evolves a parallel hidden state across Transformer layers, ShadowPEFT proposes an alternative mechanism for guiding LLM behavior. This shift could inspire further research into functional overlays as a general strategy for model adaptation.

- **Enhanced Modularity and Portability:** A core impact of ShadowPEFT is the architectural decoupling of the adaptation module (the shadow network) from the frozen backbone. This design enables the shadow module to be treated as a standalone component. It can be independently trained, stored, versioned, and deployed, offering a level of modularity not inherently available in methods where adaptations are deeply intertwined with the backbone's weight matrices.

- **Flexible Deployment Strategies, Particularly for Edge Computing:** The ability to detach the shadow model for inference has significant implications for resource-constrained environments like edge devices. The "detached shadow-only inference" mode allows for low-latency processing of routine tasks locally, reducing reliance on cloud resources. For complex or out-of-scope queries, the system can intelligently forward tasks to the full ShadowPEFT model deployed in the cloud, preventing local failures or hallucinations. This hybrid edge-cloud deployment strategy offers a practical solution for real-world applications requiring both responsiveness and advanced reasoning capabilities.

- **Cross-Scale Adaptation and Knowledge Reusability:** The demonstration that a smaller pretrained model can serve as an effective shadow for a larger backbone opens possibilities for cross-scale adaptation. This approach could facilitate the transfer of pretraining knowledge from compact models to serve as adaptive modules for more powerful LLMs, promoting resource efficiency and potentially democratizing access to LLM fine-tuning.

- **Competitive Performance with Added Benefits:** ShadowPEFT achieves performance that is competitive with, and often surpasses, established baselines like LoRA and DoRA, even with comparable or slightly fewer trainable parameters. This demonstrates that its novel architectural design does not come at a performance cost, while simultaneously providing enhanced flexibility and deployment options.

- **Improved Generalization and Parameter Scaling:** The findings regarding improved out-of-distribution generalization suggest that centralized adaptation might lead to more robust fine-tuned models. Furthermore, ShadowPEFT's ability to effectively leverage increased parameter budgets within its shadow model, contrasting with the performance degradation observed in low-rank methods beyond certain thresholds, indicates a more scalable approach to enhancing adaptation capacity.

In summary, ShadowPEFT presents a viable and flexible alternative to conventional low-rank PEFT. Its emphasis on a centralized, stateful, and detachable adaptation module has the potential to influence the design of future PEFT methods, particularly in scenarios demanding modularity, efficient deployment across diverse computational environments (from edge to cloud), and effective cross-scale knowledge transfer.
