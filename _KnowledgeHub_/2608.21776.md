# SpatialDiff: 3D-Aware Object Movement via Implicit Spatial Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> SpatialDiff is an instruction-driven image editing method that injects implicit 3D spatial priors into a diffusion transformer via a 3D geometry encoder and cross-attention connector, plus latent-space depth supervision during training, enabling precise object relocation across depth layers and occlusions in complex scenes, reaching GPT-O 0.843 vs 0.766 for the next-best baseline.

## Problem

- 2D-only diffusion editing methods lack spatial understanding, so they cannot reliably execute instruction-level 3D relocation like moving an object behind or between others.
- Explicit 3D-aware editing methods rely on single-image 3D reconstruction, which is ill-posed under unknown viewpoint, occlusion, and incomplete geometry, degrading edits.
- Existing methods lack a dataset and mechanism for instruction-driven spatial object movement across depth layers in complex multi-object scenes with occlusion.

## Method

- ==Implicit 3D Spatial Modeling== extracts geometry-aware tokens from the input image using a frozen 3D Visual Geometry Encoder (VGGT backbone) without explicit depth or reconstruction heads.
- A **Connector module** uses learnable query tokens with cross-attention to align the 3D geometry tokens into the diffusion transformer's latent token space for joint conditioning.
- ==Global Spatial Supervision== applies an MSE loss between a spatial decode head's output and the VAE-encoded target depth map, trained latent instead of pixel space, within a two-stage training strategy.

## Results

- On SpatialBench, **SpatialDiff** scores GPT-O **0.843** and Qwen-O **0.807**, beating Qwen-Image-Editing (**0.766/0.717**) and Flux-Kontext (**0.498/0.447**).
- Ablations show implicit 3D modeling raises GPT-SC from **0.398** to **0.518** over LoRA fine-tuning alone, and latent depth supervision jumps GPT-O to **0.837** versus **0.673** for pixel-space depth supervision.
- A 35-participant user study of 4200 votes gives SpatialDiff H-O **4.715** versus **3.562** for the next-best method, and it also beats commercial Nano Banana 2 on Qwen-O (**0.807** vs **0.706**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Injecting 3D priors as implicit latent tokens rather than through explicit single-image reconstruction sidesteps the ill-posedness that limits geometry-based editing pipelines.
> - Supervising spatial features in VAE latent space rather than pixel space avoids over-constraining non-edited regions while still enforcing spatial consistency.
> - Extra conditioning tokens for 3D priors roughly double inference time, so training-time-only 3D grounding without inference-time geometry tokens is a promising efficiency direction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The field of image editing has seen rapid advancements, particularly with the rise of diffusion-based generative models. These models enable a range of tasks, including style transfer, object removal and insertion, and fine-granular content editing. However, achieving precise and natural control over object movement, especially in complex scenes involving varying depths, occlusions, or specific spatial relationships, continues to be a challenge. The existing approaches can generally be categorized into 2D-based and 3D-aware methods, each with distinct advantages and limitations.

#### 1.1. Instruction-based 2D Image Editing

Many contemporary image editing methods operate predominantly in the 2D domain. These approaches, often leveraging pretrained diffusion models, allow users to manipulate images via natural language instructions. They offer flexibility and efficiency, as the editing process occurs in image or latent space without requiring explicit 3D reconstruction. Examples include methods that construct transformation paths, model editing as continuous video sequences, or integrate vision-language models (VLMs) and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to enhance instruction understanding. While these methods demonstrate improved controllability and semantic consistency, they inherently lack an understanding of 3D spatial structures. This limitation prevents them from consistently executing fine-grained spatial movements that require reasoning about depth, relative object positions, or occlusions, leading to potential inconsistencies in 3D space.

#### 1.2. 3D-Aware Image Editing

To address the spatial inconsistencies of 2D methods, several works have integrated explicit 3D reasoning into diffusion frameworks. These approaches aim to infer object geometry, camera viewpoint, depth, and spatial relationships, often by performing 3D reconstruction or spatial lifting from a single image. Techniques involve lifting diffusion activations to 3D using estimated depth maps, synthesizing novel views for 3D shape alignment, or utilizing input 3D layouts and camera poses to guide object repositioning. While 3D-aware designs offer the promise of strong geometric control and spatial consistency, they face significant limitations when applied to single-image complex scene editing. Explicit 3D reconstruction from a single 2D image is an ill-posed problem, suffering from ambiguities in depth and viewpoint, incomplete geometry, and the absence of multi-view supervision. These factors can degrade reconstruction quality, thereby impairing subsequent editing and limiting the practical applicability of these 3D-based pipelines in general 2D image editing scenarios.

#### 1.3. Research Gap Addressed

The current research landscape presents a dilemma: 2D-based methods are flexible but lack spatial understanding, hindering accurate instruction-level spatial movement, while 3D-based methods promise spatial reasoning but struggle with the practical challenges of single-image inputs and complex scene conditions. This paper identifies a critical gap: how to effectively integrate the benefits of 3D spatial priors into a 2D image-editing diffusion framework *implicitly*, without requiring full 3D reconstruction, thereby enabling the diffusion model to understand and control object spatial positioning. SpatialDiff aims to bridge this gap by enabling precise and spatially consistent object movement in complex scenes while operating within a largely 2D diffusion framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an instruction-driven image editing method capable of performing flexible and precise spatial movement of objects in complex scenes. The motivation stems from the limitations of current image editing techniques:

1. **Inadequate 3D Spatial Understanding in 2D Methods:** Existing 2D image editing methods, largely trained on 2D datasets, primarily leverage planar features. Consequently, they struggle with spatial movement instructions in complex scenes where objects span different depth layers or are partially occluded. They lack the intrinsic understanding of true 3D spatial relationships necessary for accurate object repositioning.
2. **Challenges of Explicit 3D Reconstruction:** While 3D-aware methods theoretically offer solutions, their reliance on explicit 3D reconstruction from a single image is problematic. This process is inherently ill-posed due to unknown viewpoints, depth ambiguities, occlusions, and incomplete geometry, leading to reconstruction errors that undermine editing quality.
3. **Need for Implicit 3D Integration:** The core motivation is to find a way to imbue diffusion models with 3D spatial awareness implicitly, avoiding the computational and reconstruction hurdles of explicit 3D modeling, yet still benefiting from 3D geometric priors. This approach seeks to enable a diffusion transformer (DiT) to understand and control object spatial positioning relations through internal representations.

To achieve this, the authors propose two core innovations:
- **Implicit 3D Spatial Modeling (ISM):** To enable the model to build an internal, comprehensive understanding of 3D spatial structure by leveraging 3D prior knowledge.
- **Global Spatial Supervision (GSS):** To constrain latent spatial features, allowing the model to perceive and correctly implement changes in object spatial positions due to editing operations, thereby maintaining scene consistency.

The overarching goal is to significantly improve the accuracy and fidelity of spatial movement within complex scenes by integrating 3D spatial structures into the image editing process in a robust and coherent manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SpatialDiff proposes an implicit modeling approach to embed 3D spatial information into an instruction-driven image editing framework. It integrates two main components: Implicit 3D Spatial Modeling (ISM) and Global Spatial Supervision (GSS), trained using a two-stage strategy. The base model for instruction-driven image editing is Flux-Kontext.

#### 3.1. Implicit 3D Spatial Modeling (ISM)

ISM aims to provide the diffusion backbone with a spatially grounded understanding without explicit 3D reconstruction.

##### 3.1.1. 3D Visual Geometry Encoder (3D-VGE)

To introduce 3D-aware features, SpatialDiff utilizes a 3D Visual Geometry Encoder (3D-VGE). This encoder is based on the Transformer backbone of VGGT, a 3D foundational model designed to infer key 3D attributes (camera parameters, point maps, depth maps, 3D point tracks) from a single image. Given an input image `x0`, the 3D-VGE extracts a spatial-aware representation `S`. By using only the backbone features of VGGT, SpatialDiff leverages implicit 3D geometric priors to guide the editing process, avoiding the need for explicit depth or surface normal reconstruction.

##### 3.1.2. Connector for Spatial Alignment

The features `S` extracted by the 3D-VGE (`R^(l' x d')`) have a different format than the latent representation used by the Diffusion Transformer (DiT). A Connector module is introduced to align these spatial-aware features with the DiT's latent space. The Connector employs a set of learnable query tokens `Q_l` (`R^(l x d)`) corresponding to the DiT's token sequence length and dimension. A cross-attention mechanism processes `Q_l` with `S`, distilling spatial information into an aligned representation `Q_l` that is compatible with the DiT's latent space.

##### 3.1.3. Token Integration

The aligned 3D spatial tokens `Q_l` are then concatenated with other conditional tokens, specifically the latent tokens of the input image `x` and the instruction tokens `I`. This combined sequence `C = [x, Q_l, I]` forms the complete latent sequence, which is then fed into the multimodal layers of the DiT backbone. This integration allows the DiT to process local appearance features, spatial cues, and language instructions concurrently. Through this multimodal interaction, the model implicitly learns and utilizes 3D spatial structures, including object depth, relative positioning, and inter-object relationships, facilitating precise manipulations in complex scenes.

#### 3.2. Global Spatial Supervision (GSS)

While ISM introduces 3D knowledge, it primarily reinforces static spatial memory. To ensure dynamic spatial updates and maintain overall scene consistency during object movement (e.g., preventing residual traces or semantic shifts), Global Spatial Supervision (GSS) is introduced. GSS uses depth-guided supervision during training to align the original 3D spatial tokens with the spatial positions of the target image.

##### 3.2.1. Latent Depth Supervision

The authors explored two strategies for depth supervision: explicit (pixel-level consistency) and latent (VAE latent space consistency). Latent Depth Supervision (LDS) was adopted because explicit depth supervision was observed to lead to unnatural editing results and loss of content in non-edited regions. LDS emphasizes high-level spatial and semantic relationships, providing a smoother and more robust training signal less sensitive to fine details.

Formally, the depth map `d_tgt` of the ground-truth edited image `x_tgt` is obtained using a pretrained monocular depth estimator (DepthAnything). During training, the DiT-processed spatial tokens (`s`) are mapped into the VAE latent space via a learnable spatial decode head `D`. Consistency is enforced between `D(s)` and the encoded target depth representation `E(d_tgt)` using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, `L_GSS = || D(s) - E(d_tgt) ||^2_2`. This approach utilizes explicit depth information only as an auxiliary supervision signal during training, preserving the implicit nature of the spatial modeling during inference. This design allows 3D information to be dynamically updated, encoding geometry features consistently without requiring explicit 3D reconstruction at inference time.

#### 3.3. Training Strategy

A two-stage training strategy is employed to ensure stable convergence and optimize the Connector module:

1. **Stage 1: Connector Alignment:** In the first stage, only the Connector module is optimized. This is guided by the standard diffusion loss (`L_Align`), aiming to align the 3D spatial features with the DiT latent space.
2. **Stage 2: Joint Optimization:** In the second stage, the DiT backbone, the Connector module, and the spatial decoder head (for GSS) are jointly optimized. The training objective for this stage combines the diffusion loss with the Global Spatial Supervision loss (`L_GSS`), weighted by a factor `λ = 0.01`. This ensures that the model not only performs editing but also maintains spatial and geometric consistency throughout the generation process.

The model is trained on a custom "movement dataset" of 20k (reference image, instruction, target image) triplets, derived from OBJect-3DIT images with instructions generated by Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct. The training resolution is 512x512, using AdamW optimizer on 8 GPUs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of SpatialDiff was evaluated through quantitative comparisons, qualitative analysis, ablation studies, and a user study on a newly constructed complex scene benchmark called SpatialBench, in addition to the OBJect-3DIT dataset.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Comparison

SpatialDiff was compared against state-of-the-art instruction-driven image editing models, including Flux-Kontext, OmniGen2, Step1X-Edit, BAGEL, and Qwen-Image-Editing. The evaluation used GPT-5 and Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct to score Semantic Consistency (SC), Perceptual Quality (PQ), and an Overall Score (O).

On the SpatialBench dataset (Table 1), SpatialDiff achieved the highest scores across all metrics:
- **Semantic Consistency (SC):** GPT-SC of 0.803 and Qwen-SC of 0.778, indicating superior instruction interpretation and execution for spatial movement.
- **Perceptual Quality (PQ):** GPT-PQ of 0.886 and Qwen-PQ of 0.838, demonstrating strong preservation of object consistency and natural visual appearance.
- **Overall Score (O):** GPT-O of 0.843 and Qwen-O of 0.807, confirming a balanced improvement in both semantic accuracy and visual fidelity.

In contrast, other methods either struggled with instruction adherence despite high image quality (e.g., Qwen-Image-Editing) or showed sub-optimal performance in balancing editing fidelity and object consistency. Similar trends were observed on the OBJect-3DIT test dataset, where SpatialDiff maintained overall best performance (Table 4). A comparison with a commercial baseline (Nano Banana 2) also showed SpatialDiff achieving higher scores in spatial instruction adherence and overall accuracy (Table 5).

#### 4.2. Qualitative Comparison

Qualitative results (Figure 4, 8, 9) illustrate SpatialDiff's precision and stability in spatial movement:
- **Accurate Spatial Relocation:** SpatialDiff successfully relocates objects based on instructions (e.g., moving a table to the "left side" of a sofa, interpreting "left" relative to the sofa's 3D orientation), while maintaining appearance and scene consistency. Other methods often failed to achieve correct spatial relocation, leading to structural distortions or inconsistent object shapes.
- **Scene Coherence and Background Integrity:** The method maintained background textures, shadows, and spatial relationships naturally. Unedited regions remained visually consistent without redundant texture generation or blurring. In examples like moving a cat between a rabbit and a dog, competitor methods produced object deformations, incorrect content merging, ghost artifacts, or removed unedited objects.
- **3D Spatial Understanding and Depth Consistency:** SpatialDiff correctly captured front–back relationships and depth ordering, positioning objects naturally (e.g., a sculpture in front of a door). Other approaches, while appearing reasonable in 2D, often failed to maintain depth coherence, resulting in weak spatial integration.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study on SpatialBench (Table 2, Figure 5) systematically analyzed the contribution of each component:
- **Baseline (Flux-Kontext fine-tuned with LoRA, Model A):** Fine-tuning improved SC but slightly decreased PQ, indicating a trade-off.
- **Implicit 3D Spatial Modeling (ISM, Model B):** Introducing 3D knowledge via a 3D foundation model (VGGT) and aligning it significantly boosted SC (0.398 → 0.518 GPT-SC) and O (0.563 → 0.620 GPT-O), validating the role of 3D-aware features in strengthening spatial reasoning.
- **Explicit Depth Supervision (EDS, Model C):** Applying pixel-space depth supervision led to a slight improvement in O but introduced over-constrained learning, affecting non-edited regions and causing visual stability issues (e.g., removal of objects).
- **Latent Depth Supervision (LDS, SpatialDiff):** When supervision was applied in the VAE latent space, the model achieved the best overall balance, with substantial gains across all metrics (GPT-SC: 0.804, GPT-PQ: 0.871, GPT-O: 0.837). This indicates that latent-level guidance provides smooth, semantically coherent constraints, preserving global scene consistency and high perceptual quality.
- **Additional Ablation (Table 6):** Replacing VGGT features with learnable queries (Full model w/o ISM) caused a significant drop in instruction following, confirming the critical role of ISM. The comparison to "Full model w/o LDS" demonstrated that LDS acts as a linker, coupling depth supervision with VGGT priors for spatial consistency.

#### 4.4. User Study

A user study involving 35 participants evaluated images based on Semantic Consistency (H-SC) and Perceptual Quality (H-PQ) on a 5-point scale. SpatialDiff consistently achieved higher human-evaluated scores across all three metrics (H-SC: 4.711, H-PQ: 4.720, H-O: 4.715, Table 3), confirming the quantitative and qualitative findings regarding its superior instruction following, image quality, and overall performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Contributions

SpatialDiff contributes a novel approach to instruction-based image editing by implicitly integrating 3D spatial awareness into diffusion models.
- The **Implicit 3D Spatial Modeling** mechanism, leveraging a 3D Visual Geometry Encoder and a Connector module, enables the diffusion model to develop an internal understanding of 3D spatial structures from a single 2D image without requiring explicit 3D reconstruction during inference. This addresses a key limitation of prior 2D-based methods that lacked spatial intelligence and 3D-aware methods that struggled with reconstruction fidelity.
- The **Global Spatial Supervision** strategy, specifically through Latent Depth Supervision, provides a robust and coherent training signal that dynamically guides the model to maintain spatial consistency and prevent artifacts during object movement. This latent-level constraint is shown to be more effective than explicit pixel-level supervision for preserving semantic coherence and visual quality.
- The method demonstrates **state-of-the-art performance** in object movement tasks, exhibiting superior instruction following, perceptual quality, and global scene consistency, even in complex scenes with occlusions and multi-depth structures.

#### 5.2. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its advancements, SpatialDiff has identified limitations. The model may struggle with precise physical reasoning, as evidenced by occasional incorrect perspective scaling or incomplete shadow removal (Figure 7). Accurate distance control also remains challenging, partly due to the inherent ambiguity in natural language instructions.

From a computational efficiency standpoint, the additional conditioning tokens from the 3D Visual Geometry Encoder increase the computational burden during both training and inference (inference time increased from 29s to 59s with 1024 spatial tokens). A potential direction for future research involves integrating these 3D priors directly into the model's module parameters, allowing the extra conditioning computation to be removed during inference. This would entail training with 3D prior tokens but relying solely on 2D image tokens for conditioning at inference time, potentially improving efficiency.

This study opens new avenues for integrating 3D understanding into diffusion models, paving the way for more spatially perceptive and robust image editing systems that can better understand and manipulate real-world scenes.
