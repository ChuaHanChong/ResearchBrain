# Learning Social Affordance for Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> Social affordance learning represents human-human and human-object-human interactions as hierarchical structures over body-joint skeletons: latent sub-events, selected affordable joints grouped into functional clusters, and spatial plus motion potentials per group. An MCMC algorithm learns these weakly supervised from RGB-D videos, letting a robot synthesize reactive full-body motion averaging 0.073 m joint error, comparable to Kinect ground truth in user ratings.

## Problem

- Prior affordance work models a single agent manipulating an object, treating affordance as a unary function of object location and trajectory, ignoring multi-agent interaction.
- Symbolic activity-representation methods for robot imitation learn high-level structure but never explicitly model the low-level body-joint trajectories needed to execute motion.
- Graphics motion-synthesis models generate single-agent motion from clean motion-capture input, so they neither handle reactive two-agent interaction nor noisy RGB-D skeletons.

## Method

- A hierarchical generative ==parse-graph== factors an interaction into latent sub-events, with duration priors, sub-event transition priors, plus spatial (Weibull and von Mises) and motion potentials over joints.
- Joint selection and grouping uses a Bernoulli prior to pick affordable joints and a ==Chinese Restaurant Process== to cluster them into functional groups per sub-event.
- A two-loop **MCMC** learner: outer Metropolis-Hastings proposes sub-event parsing by merge, split, and relabel moves; inner Gibbs sampling optimizes joint grouping; dynamic programming drives online motion synthesis.

## Results

- Full model averages **0.0730 m** joint distance to ground truth versus **0.2816 m** for a multi-level HMM baseline, across five interactions on a new RGB-D dataset.
- Ablations confirm both components matter: removing joint selection and grouping raises error to **0.0899 m**, removing latent sub-events raises it to **0.1132 m**.
- In a **14-subject** study, synthesized handshake and throw-catch sequences passed equivalence tests against ground truth on all three questions, and slightly exceeded ground-truth ratings on goal achievement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Affordance is relational, not unary: modeling it as spatial relations between grouped body joints of two agents captures social context a single-agent formulation cannot.
> - Explicit latent sub-goal decomposition plus joint grouping can denoise weak sensor data, since averaging structure across demonstrations recovers contact events occlusion hides in any single recording.
> - Learned human joint trajectories still assume humanoid body configuration; transferring to robots with different kinematics and mechanical constraints remains an unsolved motion-transfer problem.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The concept of "affordance," originally defined by Gibson in 1979 as the "action possibilities" of things, has garnered substantial attention across robotics, computer vision, and human-robot interaction (HRI) research. Computational modeling of affordances aims to enable robots to learn what activities are possible (semantic-level affordances) and how/where to execute them (spatial- and motion-level affordances) from human examples. This capability is fundamental for robot planning and replication of human activities.

Previous work in robot affordance learning has largely focused on scenarios involving a single agent, such as a robot or human manipulating an object. In these contexts, affordance is often treated as a unary function, dependent primarily on an object's spatial location, trajectory, and the robot's intended action. Examples include systems that infer object affordances from human demonstrations or for object manipulation tasks.

However, a significant gap exists in scenarios requiring collaborative tasks and rich interactions between multiple agents (e.g., humans and robots). In such multi-agent environments, computing single-robot object manipulation affordances is insufficient. Robots need to understand complex interactions and react appropriately within social contexts, meaning an action may only be afforded under specific social cues (e.g., a robot should only grasp a human hand during a handshake, not arbitrarily).

This paper addresses this gap by introducing the concept of "social affordances." It aims to extend affordance learning to dynamic agents with multiple body parts involved in human-human and human-object-human interactions. This focus on "the richest and most elaborate affordances," as noted by Gibson, represents a novel direction for robot learning.

Related fields include vision-based hierarchical activity recognition and human-human interaction recognition. While these areas study recognizing complex activities and interactions, they typically do not model affordances as action possibilities for a robot. Similarly, symbolic representation learning methods for robotic motion planning have been explored for single-agent activities, but often lack explicit modeling of low-level joint trajectories. In computer graphics, motion synthesis techniques exist, but they predominantly focus on single-agent motion and often rely on highly accurate motion capture data, unlike the noisy RGB-D inputs considered here.

This work distinguishes itself by:
1. Considering activities involving multiple agents.
2. Decomposing these activities into hierarchical sub-events and sub-goals, with affordances grounded in skeleton sequences.
3. Learning both spatial and motion affordances for multiple body parts involved in these interactions.

The paper aims to lay a foundation for robots to understand and replicate complex social behaviors, moving beyond isolated object manipulation towards more natural and collaborative human-robot interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and develop an approach for robot learning of **social affordance** from human activity videos. This learning is specifically framed within the context of human-robot interaction, enabling a robot to infer its own movements in response to human body motion, thereby replicating human-like interactions.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations of existing affordance learning paradigms and the requirements for more sophisticated human-robot collaboration:

- **Insufficiency of Unary Affordance Models:** Traditional affordance learning primarily focuses on a single agent interacting with an object, defining affordance as a unary function. This object-centric view is inadequate for collaborative tasks where multiple agents interact with each other and potentially with objects. For a robot to operate effectively in human environments, it must understand interactions between agents, not just agent-object relationships.
- **Need for Context-Dependent Interaction Understanding:** Social interactions are governed by context and intent. A robot cannot simply act upon a perceived affordance (e.g., a human hand being "grabbable") without considering the social context (e.g., the human intending a handshake). The robot requires a mechanism to activate affordances appropriately within a social script.
- **Requirement for Structural Representation of Interactions:** To replicate complex human behaviors, a robot needs a detailed understanding of how interactions unfold. This involves learning structural representations that describe:
 - **Decomposition:** How an overall activity can be broken down into discrete sub-events or sub-goals.
 - **Key Body Parts:** Which body parts of each agent are critical for performing each sub-event.
 - **Motion Patterns:** How these body parts should move in relation to each other, including maintaining specific spatial relations.
- **Challenges of Learning from Real-World Data:** The learning process faces several challenges:
 - **Noisy Sensor Data:** Human skeletons extracted from RGB-D videos (like those from Kinect) are often noisy due to occlusions and tracking inaccuracies, complicating the learning of precise motion patterns.
 - **Complex Temporal Dynamics:** Human interactions exhibit more intricate temporal dynamics compared to simple, isolated actions, requiring models capable of capturing these complexities.
 - **Weak Supervision and Limited Data:** The learning often relies on small datasets with only weak supervision (e.g., overall activity labels, but no explicit sub-event annotations), necessitating robust learning algorithms that can discover latent structures.

By addressing these motivations and challenges, the research aims to provide robots with a foundational capability for intelligent and socially aware interaction, paving the way for more natural and effective human-robot collaboration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a generative model for weakly supervised learning of social affordance from human demonstration videos, specifically designed to address human-robot interaction scenarios.

**Representation and Formulation:**
The social affordance is represented using a hierarchical graphical model grounded in skeleton sequences.
- **Skeleton Sequences:** An interaction instance is defined by the 3D joint positions of two agents over time, denoted J_t. If an object is involved, its position O_t is also included. To manage noise from RGB-D cameras, a simplified set of five key joints per agent (base, left/right wrists, left/right ankles) is used.
- **Interaction Label (c):** Each interaction instance is assigned a predefined category label.
- **Latent Sub-Events:** Complex interactions are decomposed into a sequence of non-overlapping sub-intervals, each corresponding to a latent sub-event (s_k). The number of sub-events is specified, but their temporal parsing and specific type are learned automatically. A sub-event is characterized by its sub-goal (achieved at the end of the interval) and its motion patterns.
- **Joint Selection and Grouping:** Within each latent sub-event, the model identifies "affordable joints" that contribute significantly to the sub-event. These affordable joints are then clustered into functional groups (h_1..., h_∞) based on rigid spatial relations, while non-contributing joints are assigned to a "Null" group. This selection and grouping (Z_s) varies across different sub-event types.
- **Sub-goals and Motion Patterns:**
 - **Sub-goals:** Defined by spatial potentials (Ψ_g) among joints within functional groups at the end of a sub-event. These potentials describe relative positions (e.g., horizontal/vertical distances modeled by Weibull distributions, angles by von Mises distributions).
 - **Motion Patterns:** Described by motion potentials (Ψ_m) characterizing the movement of affordable joints throughout the sub-event. Similar distributions (Weibull for distances, von Mises for orientation) are used for active joints, while exponential distributions are used for Null group joints to encourage static behavior.

**Probabilistic Modeling:**
A joint probability p(G, Z_c) is defined, where G represents the parse graph for an interaction instance and Z_c represents the common joint selection and grouping for interaction category c.
- The probability of a parse graph given joint selection/grouping, p(G|Z_c), incorporates:
 - A likelihood term (p({J_t}|Z_sk, s_k, c)) composed of spatial and motion potentials.
 - Priors for the interaction category (p(c), assumed uniform), sub-event transition (p(s_k|s_{k-1}, c), learned from frequencies), and sub-event duration (p(s_k|c), modeled by a log-normal distribution).
- The prior for joint selection and grouping, p(Z_c), uses a Bernoulli distribution for individual joint selection (Null vs. active) and a Chinese Restaurant Process (CRP) prior for grouping active joints into functional classes.

**Learning Algorithm:**
A Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) based algorithm with two nested loops is proposed to maximize p(G, Z_c) from training data (skeleton sequences with interaction labels).
- **Outer Loop (Metropolis-Hasting for Sub-Event Parsing):** This loop optimizes the temporal parsing of sub-events (S). At each iteration, a new parsing S' is proposed through one of three dynamics:
 - **Merging:** Combines two consecutive, skeletally similar sub-events.
 - **Splitting:** Divides a sub-event into two distinct ones, encouraging splits where skeletons are dissimilar.
 - **Re-labeling:** Assigns a new label to a uniformly sampled sub-event.
 The proposal is accepted based on an acceptance rate incorporating the current and proposed probabilities and proposal distributions.
- **Inner Loop (Gibbs Sampling for Joint Selection and Grouping):** Given a sub-event parsing, this loop optimizes the joint selection and grouping (Z_c). It iteratively updates each joint's assignment (to a functional group or the Null group) based on its conditional probability, derived from the likelihood and the CRP/Bernoulli priors.

**Motion Synthesis (Online Simulation):**
The learned social affordance model is used to synthesize a robot's skeleton sequence (second agent) in real-time, in reaction to an observed human (first agent) and potentially an object.
- The process iteratively predicts robot motion in small time steps.
- **Dynamic Programming (DP):** At each time step, a DP algorithm infers the current latent sub-event based on all observed human and previously synthesized robot skeletons, maximizing the probability of the parse graph.
- **Joint Location Prediction:** For the inferred current sub-event, the model predicts the sub-event's end time and samples multiple possible future locations for the five key affordable joints of the robot.
- **Selection:** The set of joint locations that maximizes the spatial and motion potentials within the current sub-event is selected.
- **Full Body Fitting:** To generate a complete robot skeleton, clustered full-body skeletons (pre-computed via K-means) are fitted to the predicted key joint locations by rotating limbs.
- The robot's skeleton for the current time step is then updated, and the process continues.

**Dataset:**
The authors collected a new RGB-D video dataset, the UCLA Human-Human-Object Interaction (HHOI) dataset. It includes 5 interaction types: 3 human-human (shake hands, high-five, pull up) and 2 human-object-human (throw and catch, hand over a cup). The dataset features 8 actors, an average of 23.6 instances per interaction, captured using a MS Kinect v2 sensor.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the model's ability to learn and apply social affordances for generating robot motion.

**Learned Affordance Representations:**
The MCMC-based learning algorithm successfully discovered semantically meaningful social affordance representations from RGB-D videos. This included:
- **Latent Sub-Event Discovery:** The model automatically identified critical steps (sub-events) within complex interactions.
- **Joint Selection and Grouping:** It learned which body parts are important for each sub-event and how they functionally group together. For example, in "shake hands," the system grouped the wrist joints of both agents. In "throw and catch," a group comprising both agents' wrist joints and the object was identified, indicating crucial interactions. Figure 3 visually confirms these learned sub-events and joint groupings.
- **Spatial and Motion Patterns:** The learned potentials effectively captured the required spatial relations at sub-event goals and the associated motion patterns.

**Quantitative Performance (Experiment 1: Skeleton Synthesis Accuracy):**
The proposed full model was evaluated against several baselines using the average joint distance (in meters) between synthesized robot skeletons and ground truth (GT) human skeletons.
- **HMM Baseline:** A multi-level Hidden Markov Model yielded an average joint distance of 0.2816 m.
- **Model Variants:**
 - V1 (Ours without joint selection and grouping): 0.0899 m.
 - V2 (Ours without latent sub-events): 0.1132 m.
- **Full Proposed Model:** Achieved the lowest average joint distance of 0.0730 m.

These results indicate that the full hierarchical generative model, incorporating both latent sub-events and functional joint grouping, provides a substantial improvement in synthesis accuracy compared to baselines and its own ablative variants. The performance is comparable to the noise range of the Kinect 2 sensor (50-100 mm). Figure 4 visually illustrates that the synthesized skeletons from the proposed method are closer to the GT compared to the HMM baseline.

**Qualitative Performance (Experiment 2: User Study on Naturalness):**
A user study involving 14 subjects rated synthesized and GT interactions on a 1-5 scale across three questions:
- **Q1: "Is the purpose of the interaction successfully achieved?"**
 - The ratings for synthesized sequences were generally comparable to GT, with "shake hands" and "throw and catch" passing a 90% confidence equivalence test with a 0.5 margin. Other interactions passed with a 1.0 margin, except for "pull up."
- **Q2: "Is the synthesized agent behaving naturally?"**
 - Synthesized "shake hands" and "throw and catch" received slightly *higher* mean ratings than their GT counterparts. This was attributed to the model's ability to correctly learn and synthesize critical sub-goals like "hand touching," which were often poorly captured or missed in the noisy Kinect-based GT data due to occlusion.
 - For other interactions, synthesized results were comparable to GT, with "pull up" showing lower ratings due to noisier training sequences.
- **Q3: "Does the synthesized agent look like a human rather than a robot?"**
 - Generally, GT sequences received higher "human-like" scores. However, synthesized sequences for most interactions (except "pull up") still passed equivalence tests (with a 1.0 margin), suggesting a reasonable level of perceived human-likeness.

The user study highlighted that the model effectively learned the *goals* of the interactions, sometimes surpassing the fidelity of noisy single-instance GT recordings by generalizing from multiple examples. The ability to correctly model hand touching, a critical social cue, demonstrated the effectiveness of the joint grouping mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces the concept of **social affordance** for robot learning, representing a notable advancement beyond traditional object-centric or single-agent affordance models. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Advancement in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** By enabling robots to understand and learn complex, context-dependent social interactions from human demonstrations, this work pushes HRI beyond simple object manipulation. It provides a foundation for robots to engage in more natural, intuitive, and collaborative interactions with humans, crucial for deployment in shared workspaces and social environments.
- **Novelty in Affordance Learning:** This is identified as the first work to specifically study robot learning of affordances for social activities, explicitly considering multiple agents and decomposing interactions into hierarchical sub-events with associated spatial and motion patterns. This extends the scope of affordance theory to the social domain.
- **Robustness to Real-World Data Challenges:** The proposed generative model demonstrates robustness in learning from noisy RGB-D skeleton data, which is a common challenge in real-world computer vision and robotics applications. The MCMC-based learning algorithm and its hierarchical structure effectively discover latent components (sub-events, joint groupings) despite weak supervision and imperfect sensor input.
- **Improved Motion Synthesis and Goal Achievement:** The ability to synthesize appropriate full-body motion for an interacting agent, accurately achieving interaction sub-goals (e.g., precise hand-touching for a handshake), is a direct result of the learned social affordances. The user study demonstrated that the model can generate motions that are not only quantitatively accurate but also qualitatively comparable to, and in some cases perceptually superior to, noisy ground truth for achieving interaction objectives.
- **Foundation for Collaborative Robotics:** By allowing a robot to infer its own movement in reaction to human motion and maintain social spatial relations, the work provides a critical component for developing truly collaborative robots. This could enable robots to anticipate human actions, adapt their behavior, and participate seamlessly in shared tasks.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper implicitly sets the stage for future research into motion transfer from human models to diverse robot platforms. While the current work focuses on the learning and synthesis of human-like skeletons, the authors acknowledge the need to adapt these learned behaviors to robots with different physical configurations and kinematic constraints. This points to a practical pathway for deploying the learned social affordances on physical robots.

Overall, this research represents a foundational contribution to the field of social robotics, offering a method for robots to acquire a deeper understanding of human social behavior and act accordingly, thereby facilitating more sophisticated and natural human-robot partnerships.
