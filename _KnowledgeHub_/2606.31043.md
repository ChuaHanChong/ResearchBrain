# Warp RL: Reshaping Base Policy Distributions for Dynamics Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> Warp RL introduces a post-training policy adaptation framework that utilizes invertible, state-conditioned transformations to reshape base policy action distributions. This approach generalizes additive residual reinforcement learning, achieving higher success rates in challenging simulated manipulation tasks and reducing real-robot task completion time by 30% without sacrificing success.

## Problem

- Robot policies frequently experience performance degradation when deployed in environments with altered dynamics due to unmodeled real-world phenomena or parameter shifts.
- Traditional additive residual reinforcement learning, a prominent post-hoc adaptation method, is limited to translating the mean of an action distribution, unable to modify its shape, scale, or state-dependent geometry.
- This limitation can lead to suboptimal performance or even degradation below the unadapted policy under dynamics shifts requiring more complex adjustments, such as altered variance or non-uniform corrections.

## Method

- **Warp RL** replaces the additive correction of residual RL with an ==invertible, state-conditioned transformation==, a = Tθ(z, s), directly operating on samples z from a frozen base policy.
- The transformation Tθ is instantiated as a ==rational-quadratic (RQ) spline flow==, which provides monotonic, analytically invertible, and differentiable transformations for each action dimension.
- The framework is designed for ==identity initialization==, ensuring the adapted policy initially performs identically to the base policy, and is compatible with policy-gradient methods (PPO, SAC) and ==Evolution Strategies (ES)== due to tractable log-probability computations.

## Results

- In challenging simulated manipulation tasks (e.g., PegInsertionSide, LiftPegUpright), Warp-ES consistently outperformed residual RL, achieving up to a **70.6%** success rate on PegInsertionSide compared to **57.4%** for Residual-PPO.
- Warp-ES achieved full performance recovery (matching or exceeding source dynamics performance) on approximately **50%** of task-seed combinations, significantly outperforming Warp-PPO and Residual-PPO.
- On a real-robot peg-insertion task, Warp RL achieved comparable success rates to residual correction while reducing the median task completion time by **30%** (from **4.65 seconds** to **3.25 seconds**), indicating more efficient and direct task execution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Additive residual RL's limitation to pure translation restricts its ability to adapt to dynamics shifts requiring changes in action distribution variance (F1), miscalibrated confidence (F2), or non-uniform action-dependent corrections (F3).
> - Structured, invertible transformations like RQ-spline flows can effectively reshape complex action distributions, offering a strictly more expressive adaptation mechanism than simple additive corrections.
> - Combining expressive warping (RQ-spline flow) with gradient-free optimization (Evolution Strategies) yielded consistently superior performance across diverse dynamics shifts in simulation, outperforming gradient-based methods and other architectural choices.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot policies developed under specific conditions frequently encounter performance degradation when deployed in environments with altered dynamics. Such changes, encompassing variations in object mass, contact properties, actuator responses, or unmodeled real-world phenomena, can render a trained policy ineffective, even when the task objective remains constant. Retraining policies from an initial state can be computationally intensive and impractical, particularly for physical robotic systems.

Existing approaches to address this issue include domain randomization, where training data incorporates variations to anticipate deployment conditions, and methods that integrate adaptability into the policy itself, such as meta-learning for rapid gradient-based adaptation or conditioning policies on estimated dynamics parameters. These methods generally require foresight during the initial training phase to account for potential adaptation needs.

An alternative strategy is post-hoc policy adaptation, where an arbitrary, pre-trained policy remains frozen, and a compact, lightweight module is trained to provide corrections. Residual reinforcement learning (residual RL) is a prominent example within this category. In residual RL, a learned state-dependent additive correction is applied to the actions sampled from a base policy. This method is noted for its simplicity, stability, and natural identity initialization, where the correction module initially outputs zero, preserving the original policy's behavior.

This work positions itself as an advancement within the post-hoc policy adaptation paradigm, specifically by addressing a structural limitation of conventional additive residual RL. While residual RL effectively shifts the mean of a policy's action distribution, it cannot modify its shape, scale, or state-dependent geometry. The paper identifies scenarios where this limitation can lead to suboptimal performance, or even degradation below the unadapted policy, under dynamics shifts that require more complex distributional adjustments. Warp RL is introduced as a generalization of residual RL, aiming to provide a more expressive adaptation mechanism that maintains the benefits of a compact, identity-initialized correction module. It integrates concepts from normalizing flows, which are invertible transformations capable of reshaping probability distributions, into the policy adaptation context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a policy adaptation method that overcomes the structural limitations of additive residual reinforcement learning while preserving its advantages, particularly in scenarios involving dynamics shifts. The authors identify specific shortcomings of residual RL, which they formalize as follows:

- **Additive Formulation as a Translation Operator:** Residual RL applies an additive offset to actions sampled from a base policy, *a = z + δ<sub>θ</sub>(s)*. If the base policy is Gaussian, this translates the mean of the action distribution while preserving its variance, shape, and number of modes. This means that residual RL can reposition the action distribution but cannot alter its fundamental geometry. This restriction is sufficient when adaptation primarily requires correcting systematic biases or calibration offsets.

- **Formalized Failure Modes Under Dynamics Shift:** The paper details three specific failure modes where the translation-only capability of residual RL becomes insufficient:
 - **F1. Wrong variance:** When shifted dynamics demand highly precise or committed actions, but the base policy exhibits a broad action distribution (indicating high uncertainty), an additive correction cannot narrow this distribution to meet the new requirement. Similarly, if the base policy is overly confident (narrow distribution) when broader exploration is needed, translation cannot widen it.
 - **F2. Miscalibrated confidence:** If the base policy's action distribution concentrates probability mass around an action region that was optimal under the original dynamics but is suboptimal under the shifted dynamics, an additive translation merely shifts this tightly concentrated mass to another potentially suboptimal region. It cannot diffuse or re-center the confidence appropriately.
 - **F3. Non-uniform correction:** The required corrective action may vary across different magnitudes or regions of the base action distribution. For instance, high-magnitude actions might be excessively aggressive under new dynamics, while moderate actions remain appropriate. An additive offset applies the same shift to all samples from the base policy, failing to provide an action-dependent, non-uniform adjustment.

- **Empirical Observation of Degradation:** The authors observe empirically that under such dynamics shifts, residual correction can lead to stagnation or even a decrease in performance below that of the unadapted base policy. This observation motivates the need for a more versatile adaptation mechanism.

The key motivation for Warp RL is to enable adaptation that can reshape the scale, skew, and general nonlinear geometry of the action distribution, rather than just translating it. This is pursued while adhering to two crucial constraints:
1. **Frozen Base Policy:** The pre-trained base policy remains unchanged, avoiding costly full re-training.
2. **Identity Initialization:** The adaptation module should naturally start as an identity mapping, ensuring that initial training iterations do not degrade a known-good base policy. This allows the system to begin with the performance of the unadapted base policy and only deviate when improvements are learned.

By addressing these limitations, Warp RL aims to provide a strictly more expressive framework for policy adaptation that can robustly handle a wider range of dynamics shifts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Warp RL proposes to replace the additive correction of residual reinforcement learning with an invertible, state-conditioned transformation of the base policy’s action distribution. Instead of executing *a = z + δ<sub>θ</sub>(s)*, Warp RL executes *a = T<sub>θ</sub>(z, s)*, where *z ∼ π<sub>base</sub>(· | s)*.

#### 3.1. Warping Framework

- **Core Idea:** The transformation *T<sub>θ</sub>(·, s)* directly operates on samples *z* from the frozen base policy *π<sub>base</sub>*. At each timestep, a sample *z* is drawn from *π<sub>base</sub>(· | s)*, then transformed into the executed action *a = T<sub>θ</sub>(z, s)*.
- **Exact Sampling and Tractable Density:** This approach provides exact samples from the warped distribution. Due to the invertibility and differentiability of *T<sub>θ</sub>*, the log-probability of the warped policy *π<sub>θ</sub>(a | s)* can be calculated in closed form using the change-of-variables formula: *log p<sub>a</sub>(a) = log p<sub>z</sub>(T<sup>-1</sup><sub>θ</sub>(a)) + log det (∂T<sup>-1</sup><sub>θ</sub>/∂a)*. This tractability is crucial for policy-gradient methods.
- **Generalization of Residual RL:** The framework demonstrates that additive residual RL is a specific, degenerate case of a general transformation. A pure translation *T(z, s) = z + δ(s)* is a monotonic bijection with a unit Jacobian, which can be represented within the proposed transformation family. More generally, affine bijections *T(z, s) = α(s) ⊙ z + δ(s)* allow for learnable per-dimension scaling, addressing "wrong variance" and "miscalibrated confidence." Nonlinear monotonic transformations further subsume these, enabling "non-uniform correction."
- **Identity Initialization:** The transformation *T<sub>θ</sub>* is initialized to the identity, meaning *T<sub>θ=0</sub>(z, s) = z*. This ensures that the adapted policy initially performs identically to the base policy, preventing early degradation during training.

#### 3.2. Instantiation with State-Conditioned RQ-Spline Flow

- **Choice of Flow:** The authors instantiate *T<sub>θ</sub>* as a rational-quadratic (RQ) spline flow. This choice is motivated by its properties:
 - **Monotonicity:** RQ-splines apply an independent monotonic bijection per action dimension.
 - **Analytic Invertibility:** They have analytic inverses, essential for tractable log-probability calculations.
 - **Diagonal Jacobian:** This simplifies the computation of log-determinant of the Jacobian to a sum over action dimensions (O(d)).
- **Bounded Action Domain:** RQ-splines are typically defined on bounded domains. For Gaussian base policies, which produce unbounded samples, an element-wise `tanh` squash is applied to map samples into the bounded domain before the spline transformation. This bounding map is also invertible and differentiable, preserving the overall tractability.
- **State Conditioning:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) maps the observation *s* to the parameters of the spline (bin widths, bin heights, knot derivatives). The number of parameters scales with the number of spline bins *K* per action dimension.
- **Identity Initialization:** Identity initialization is achieved by setting the output layer of the conditioning MLP to zero. This configuration results in uniform spline bins and unit knot derivatives, effectively recovering the identity map.

#### 3.3. Training

The Warp RL framework is optimizer-agnostic due to the compact and self-contained nature of the correction module.
- **Warp RL with PPO:** The analytic invertibility of the RQ-spline allows direct integration with policy-gradient algorithms like PPO. The log-probability of the warped policy is computed by taking the log-density of the base Gaussian sample and subtracting the log-Jacobians of both the `tanh` squash and the RQ-spline. This enables direct application of the PPO clipped surrogate objective. Entropy is estimated via Monte Carlo samples.
- **Warp RL with SAC:** Similarly, the same log-probability computation makes Warp RL compatible with off-policy algorithms like SAC. In the real-world (sim-to-real) setting, the spline output is used as a bounded correction added to a clipped base-policy mean (*a = clip(μ<sub>base</sub> + correction, -1, 1)*). This structural symmetry facilitates comparison with additive residual correction in that context.
- **Warp RL with Evolution Strategies (ES):** ES treats the spline as a black box. It perturbs the spline parameters with Gaussian noise and evaluates each candidate via rollouts. This method does not require inverse, Jacobian, or log-probability computations, simplifying the implementation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Warp RL was evaluated across both simulated manipulation tasks and a real-robot peg-insertion task.

#### 4.1. Controlled Simulation Adaptation

- **Setup:** Four ManiSkill3 manipulation tasks (PushCube, PushT, LiftPegUpright, PegInsertionSide) were used. A PPO base policy was trained in source dynamics and then frozen. Task-specific dynamics shifts (e.g., mass multiplication, clearance reduction) were introduced. Three identity-initialized correction methods were compared: Residual-PPO (additive correction with PPO), Warp-PPO (RQ-spline warp with PPO), and Warp-ES (RQ-spline warp with Evolution Strategies).
- **Results (Table 1):**
 - **PushCube:** All correction methods, including Residual-PPO and Warp RL variants, effectively recovered performance, reaching success rates comparable to or exceeding the source dynamics. This suggests that for this task, adaptation primarily required translation, which Warp RL subsumes.
 - **PegInsertionSide:** Residual-PPO performed worse than the unadapted base policy under shifted dynamics (57.4% vs. 59.6%). This indicates a failure mode where an additive, state-only offset was insufficient and detrimental, likely due to the need for action-dependent adjustments (F3: non-uniform correction). Warp-ES achieved the highest success rate (70.6%) on this challenging task.
 - **LiftPegUpright:** Warp-PPO substantially improved over Residual-PPO (87.3% vs. 75.0%), and Warp-ES achieved the highest performance (98.5%). This suggests the task required variance adjustment (F1: wrong variance) that pure translation could not provide.
 - **Overall Dominance of Warp-ES:** Warp-ES consistently outperformed all other baselines on every task, indicating that the expressive warping coupled with gradient-free optimization was effective across diverse dynamics shifts.
- **Recovery Profile (Figure 3):** An aggregated recovery analysis showed that Warp-ES achieved full recovery (performance equal to or better than the original policy in source dynamics) on approximately 50% of task-seed combinations, significantly outperforming Warp-PPO and Residual-PPO.
- **Ablation Study (Table 2):** An ablation comparing different correction architectures (Residual-ES, Affine-ES, MLP-ES, Warp-ES) under the ES optimizer demonstrated the importance of the structured spline parameterization.
 - Residual-ES failed on tasks requiring more than translation (e.g., PushT, PegInsertionSide).
 - Affine-ES and MLP-ES, while introducing more capacity or per-dimension scaling, often performed comparably to or worse than Residual-ES on some tasks, suggesting that unconstrained nonlinearity or mere affine transformation without the specific spline structure did not yield consistent improvements.
 - The results implied that the gains of Warp-ES were attributable to the structured spline, which provides monotonic, invertible transformations, rather than solely to the ES optimizer or generic nonlinear capacity. MLP-ES, despite similar parameter counts, performed worse than Warp-ES on three out of four tasks.

#### 4.2. Real-World Adaptation

- **Setup:** A PPO base policy for PegInsertionSide, trained in simulation, was deployed on a physical SO101 6-DOF robot arm. An RLPD/SAC pipeline was used to train lightweight corrections online for 50 episodes, comparing additive residual correction against Warp RL. Both methods produced bounded adjustments to the clipped base policy mean.
- **Results (Table 3):**
 - **Success Rate:** Both Residual and Warp corrections improved significantly over the unadapted base policy (from 45% to 65% and 70% respectively), demonstrating that online correction can bridge the sim-to-real gap for this task. The success rates between Residual (65%) and Warp (70%) were comparable given the sample size (n=20).
 - **Cycle Time:** Warp correction substantially reduced the median cycle time (episode duration) to 3.25 seconds, a 30% reduction compared to Residual (4.65 seconds) and the Base policy (4.80 seconds). The higher variance in Warp cycle times (0.93s vs 0.19s) suggested sharper specialization, leading to faster episodes but occasionally longer ones if the tight insertion tolerance was missed.
- **Interpretation:** The reduction in cycle time for Warp RL suggests that its ability to reshape the action distribution resulted in more direct and efficient insertion trajectories. Instead of merely shifting the mean action, the warp could concentrate the action probability around a narrow, effective approach corridor, leading to faster task completion without sacrificing success rate.

#### 4.3. Additional Baselines (Appendix F)

- Comparison with full fine-tuning (FT-KL) and last-layer fine-tuning (FT-Last) showed that Warp-ES consistently achieved the best results on every task, despite only adapting a small correction module on top of a frozen policy. This suggests that output-space adaptation of a competent frozen base can be more effective than retraining the policy network itself, even with reduced capacity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents Warp RL as a significant advancement in policy adaptation for robotic systems, particularly when confronted with dynamics shifts between training and deployment environments. Its primary significance lies in formally identifying and addressing the structural limitations of conventional additive residual reinforcement learning. By replacing additive corrections with invertible, state-conditioned spline flows, Warp RL offers a strictly more expressive adaptation mechanism capable of reshaping the scale, skew, and state-dependent geometry of action distributions, moving beyond simple translation.

The potential impact of Warp RL is multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Adaptation Capabilities:** The ability to handle complex distributional changes (wrong variance, miscalibrated confidence, non-uniform correction) allows Warp RL to recover or surpass performance in scenarios where additive residuals degrade or stall. This broader adaptability could make robotic systems more robust to unforeseen dynamics variations in real-world applications.
- **Improved Efficiency and Performance:** In simulation, Warp-ES demonstrated superior performance across multiple manipulation tasks, often achieving higher success rates than residual methods and even full fine-tuning of the base policy. In real-world peg-insertion, Warp RL achieved comparable success rates to residual methods while significantly reducing task completion time (30% faster median cycle time). This efficiency gain has direct practical implications for robot throughput and operational costs in industrial or service robotics.
- **Flexible Integration:** The framework is designed as a drop-in replacement for additive residuals in existing policy adaptation pipelines, such as those used in sim-to-real transfer. This facilitates its adoption and integration into current robotics development workflows without requiring extensive re-engineering of base policy training or deployment infrastructure.
- **Principled Adaptation:** The use of identity-initialized normalizing flows ensures that adaptation begins from a known-good base policy and only deviates when supported by learned improvements. This provides stability and a clear starting point for training.
- **Broader Applicability:** While currently demonstrated with PPO-trained Gaussian base policies, the conceptual framework could potentially be extended to other base policy types (e.g., behavior-cloned, chunked-action, or diffusion-style policies), broadening its utility across different reinforcement learning paradigms.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. The RQ-spline operates on a bounded domain, which might affect tasks with commands near saturation. The current implementation focuses on PPO-trained Gaussian bases, suggesting future work to extend it to other base policy types. The per-dimension, per-timestep warp does not model cross-dimensional or temporal correlations beyond state conditioning, indicating potential for autoregressive or sequence-level warps. Finally, the real-robot evaluation, while impactful, was limited to a single task with 20 episodes per method, suggesting that more extensive evaluations would strengthen statistical conclusions. Addressing these aspects could further enhance the robustness and generalizability of Warp RL.
