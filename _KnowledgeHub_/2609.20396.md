# Imagine-TAMP: Imagination-Guided Task and Motion Planning in Partial Observability

> [!summary] Summary
> Imagine-TAMP is an interleaved, partially observable task-and-motion planner for finding occluded targets in clutter. A vision-language model reshapes a particle belief over target locations, SAM 3D imagines hidden scene geometry, and candidate plan skeletons are ranked by non-unit costs combining arm effort and rendered target visibility before motion refinement. On constrained shelves, success rises from 46% to 84%; real-robot planning time drops 32%.

## Problem

- Conventional TAMP assumes the relevant world state is known before planning, yet realistic cluttered scenes hide targets behind objects or in unobserved regions.
- PO-TAMP planners rank skeletons by symbolic plan length or unit costs, so they favor short DETECT-first plans even when the viewpoint barely exposes the target.
- Semantic belief-shaping methods like COCO-TAMP estimate where the target likely is, but not how alternative sensing or manipulation actions would expose it; exhaustive geometric refinement is too expensive.

## Method

- **VLM-based semantic belief shaping**: a VLM ranks visible occluders by likelihood of hiding the target; scores reweight particles inside each occluder's ==conical occlusion region== as a soft prior.
- **Geometric imagination with belief-guided determinization**: ==SAM 3D== reconstructs plausible hidden geometry, and a ==ghost target== at the maximum-weight particle yields a deterministic scene for ==BFS== enumeration of K candidate skeletons.
- **Non-unit skeleton cost**: sums normalized ==joint-displacement effort== for PICK/PLACE and a ==visibility-aware DETECT cost== from 64x64 rendered target pixels, propagated through hypothetical scene changes; only the argmin skeleton gets ==CUTAMP== refinement.

## Results

- Shelf, frontal-only viewpoints (50 paired trials): Motion-Cost lifts success **46.0%** to **84.0%** and cuts replans **11.50** to **4.50**; at 15 occluders Unit-Cost falls to **35%** versus **85%**.
- Visibility-weight ablation: success stays **84.0%** for all weights, but raising the visibility weight from 0 to 2.5 cuts removed occluders **2.46** to **2.12** and trajectory length **2.63** to **2.37**.
- Cluster scenes: Motion-Cost plus Gemini 3.8 Flash reaches **100%** success with **1.40** replans versus **83.3%/6.30** without VLM; Unit-Cost plus Gemini only **73.3%**. Real-robot planning time: **232.0s** to **158.5s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Plan length is a poor proxy under partial observability: cheap geometric estimates of sensing quality can justify longer manipulate-then-observe strategies that unit-cost planners systematically reject.
> - Generative models can serve as ranking heuristics rather than ground truth: imagined geometry need only order candidate strategies, while a model-based solver still verifies final motions.
> - Semantic priors and geometric cost are complementary, not substitutes: better beliefs alone cannot fix a planner biased toward short skeletons; semantics decide where to look, geometry decides how.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses task and motion planning under **partial observability**, a setting in which a robot must act despite incomplete knowledge of object locations, scene geometry, or object relationships. Conventional task and motion planning (TAMP) combines symbolic decisions, such as picking, placing, and detecting objects, with continuous motion planning. However, many TAMP systems assume that the relevant scene state is already known. This assumption is unsuitable for cluttered environments in which a target object may be hidden behind other objects or outside the robot’s current field of view.

Partially Observable TAMP (PO-TAMP) extends planning to include uncertainty, sensing, belief updates, and replanning. Existing approaches include belief-space planning, determinization followed by online replanning, risk-aware information gathering, active perception, and manipulation-based object search. These methods allow robots to decide whether to gather information or alter the environment, but the choice is often influenced by symbolic plan length, fixed action costs, or computationally expensive geometric planning.

The central problem identified by the paper is that **short symbolic plans are not necessarily informative or physically efficient**. For example, a planner may prefer repeatedly executing a short `DETECT` action because it contains fewer symbolic steps than a plan that first removes an occluding object. Yet, if the target remains hidden from the available viewpoints, repeated detection actions provide little information and consume the replanning budget.

The proposed framework builds on two recent research directions. First, generative scene-completion models can infer plausible geometry in unobserved parts of a scene. Second, vision-language models (VLMs) can provide semantic priors about likely object relationships, such as the possibility that a soup can is hidden behind other food containers. The paper combines these capabilities with model-based TAMP. Its distinction from direct foundation-model planning is that the VLM shapes a probabilistic belief and the generative model supplies planning hypotheses; neither model directly generates or verifies the final robot motion.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to enable a robot to select more appropriate strategies for searching for and retrieving hidden objects. In particular, the system must decide between:

1. **Information gathering**, such as moving the camera to obtain another observation.
2. **Scene manipulation**, such as picking up and relocating an object that may be blocking the target.
3. **Direct target interaction**, when the target is sufficiently likely to be accessible.

The motivation is that these choices depend on more than symbolic action count. A sensing action should be evaluated according to whether its viewpoint is likely to reveal the target. Similarly, a manipulation action should be evaluated according to both its physical effort and its expected effect on target visibility. These quantities are difficult to estimate from the currently observed scene because the target and surrounding geometry may be hidden.

The paper therefore pursues three related goals. First, it seeks to determine whether non-unit, geometry-informed costs improve the choice between sensing and manipulation. Second, it investigates whether imagined visibility changes can identify which occluders are worth removing. Third, it evaluates whether semantic information from a VLM can concentrate the belief over likely target locations and reduce unnecessary exploration.

The authors formulate the task around an unknown target pose \(x^\star\) and a belief distribution \(b_t(x^\star)\). The robot can execute actions such as `PICK`, `PLACE`, and `DETECT`. The objective is to select a plan that completes the manipulation task while minimizing cumulative physical and sensing costs under uncertainty.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Determinize-and-Replan Architecture

Instead of solving the complete belief-space TAMP problem, which would require reasoning over all possible future observations and continuous motions, Imagine-TAMP uses a **determinize-and-replan** strategy. At each planning iteration, the robot:

1. Updates its belief over the target location.
2. Applies semantic guidance from a VLM.
3. Constructs a plausible hidden-scene hypothesis.
4. Selects a representative target hypothesis.
5. Generates several symbolic plan skeletons.
6. Evaluates them using approximate geometric and visibility costs.
7. Fully refines only the selected skeleton.
8. Executes it and replans when new observations become available.

This structure limits expensive motion planning to the most promising task-level strategy.

#### 3.2. Particle Belief and Semantic Belief Shaping

The target belief is represented by weighted particles, each corresponding to a possible target pose. Initially, particles are distributed over geometrically feasible surfaces or volumes. After a sensing action, particles that should have been visible but did not produce a detection are downweighted. The update uses an estimated visibility function and a nominal detection probability.

The VLM receives the current RGB image, the target description, and information about visible objects. It is asked which visible objects are most likely to conceal the target. The response is converted into semantic scores. Higher-ranked objects receive larger scores, while unranked or less likely objects receive smaller or zero scores.

Each target particle is associated with the occluding object whose approximate occlusion cone contains that particle. The particle weight is then multiplied by a semantic likelihood term. This process biases the belief toward locations behind semantically plausible occluders without eliminating other geometrically possible locations. A small constant ensures that a VLM error delays exploration rather than making the true target location impossible under the belief.

#### 3.3. Imagined Hidden Geometry

The system uses a generative 3D scene model to infer plausible geometry in currently unobserved regions. The generated geometry is conditioned on the current image and is used to estimate the consequences of hypothetical actions. It is not treated as ground truth and is not directly used for robot execution.

The highest-weight target particle is inserted into the imagined scene as a temporary “ghost” target. This produces a deterministic planning scene containing both a hypothesized target and plausible occluding geometry. The ghost target allows the planner to evaluate whether candidate sensing viewpoints would expose the target and whether moving an occluder would improve visibility.

#### 3.4. Candidate Skeleton Generation

A breadth-first symbolic planner generates candidate action sequences in increasing depth. Each skeleton may contain actions such as detecting the target, picking an occluder, placing it elsewhere, and then detecting the target again. Up to a specified number of goal-reaching skeletons are retained.

Because breadth-first search naturally favors shorter sequences, the candidate skeletons are subsequently reranked using approximate costs. This is the mechanism that allows a longer manipulation-first strategy to be selected over a shorter but uninformative detection-first strategy.

#### 3.5. Approximate Manipulation and Detection Costs

For `PICK` and `PLACE`, the planner samples possible grasp and placement parameters. Parameters that do not admit an inverse-kinematics solution are discarded. The cost of a feasible action is approximated by normalized joint displacement from the current robot configuration.

For `DETECT`, the system samples possible camera viewpoints. For tabletop scenes, viewpoints are drawn from a hemisphere around the hypothesized target. For shelf scenes, viewpoints are restricted to a frontal grid, representing a more constrained sensing problem. Each viewpoint is evaluated by rendering a low-resolution segmentation image of the imagined scene. The number of target pixels provides an estimate of target visibility. Visibility cost decreases as the target becomes more exposed.

The viewpoint cost combines visibility and arm-motion effort. Candidate viewpoints are ranked by this score and passed to the underlying motion planner in best-first order. Importantly, the approximate evaluator does not assume that its best viewpoint will necessarily be motion-feasible; the full motion planner still verifies collision-free execution.

The cost of a complete skeleton is the sum of its approximate action costs. Action outcomes are propagated through the imagined scene, so a detection action after removing an occluder is evaluated in the hypothetical post-manipulation state rather than the initial state.

#### 3.6. Continuous Refinement and Execution

Only the lowest-cost skeleton undergoes full continuous refinement using the underlying TAMP system, CUTAMP. This stage computes feasible grasps, placements, camera poses, and collision-free trajectories. After execution, new observations update the belief and scene model. The process repeats until the target is detected and the task is completed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Non-Unit Costs Improve Decisions Under Restricted Sensing

The most pronounced benefits occurred in shelf environments, where sensing was limited to frontal viewpoints. In 50 paired trials with 9–15 occluders, unit-cost PO-TAMP achieved **46.0% success**, whereas the motion-cost method achieved **84.0%**. Average replanning steps decreased from **11.50 to 4.50**, and the paired success difference was statistically significant with \(p = 3.8 \times 10^{-6}\).

Unit-cost planning removed no occluders because it repeatedly selected short detection-first plans. The motion-cost methods removed approximately two occluders per successful task, reflecting a willingness to alter the environment when sensing alone was unlikely to reveal the target.

On tabletop scenes, where the robot could choose viewpoints over a hemisphere, unit-cost planning already achieved **96.0% success**, and motion-cost planning achieved **100.0%**. The smaller difference indicates that non-unit evaluation is most useful when the sensing geometry is constrained.

In the shelf clutter sweep, unit-cost success declined from **80.0% with five occluders to 35.0% with fifteen**. Motion-cost planning remained between **85.0% and 90.0%**. Replanning increased sharply for unit-cost planning, reaching 14.35 steps with fifteen occluders, while motion-cost planning required 4.75 steps.

#### 4.2. Visibility Prediction Improves Manipulation Efficiency

The visibility-weight experiment separated the value of manipulation from the value of predicting which occluder would be most useful to remove. Allowing manipulation primarily restored success under severe occlusion: success remained **84.0%** across the tested visibility weights.

However, adding visibility prediction improved efficiency. Compared with effort-only planning, the default visibility-weighted method reduced trajectory length from **2.63 m to 2.37 m**, replans from **4.84 to 4.50**, and removed occluders from **2.46 to 2.12** on average. Increasing the visibility weight beyond 2.5 produced no further improvement in these experiments.

#### 4.3. Semantic Belief Shaping Complements Geometric Planning

In cluster-based experiments, the target was hidden within one of three spatially separated clusters, and its shape corresponded to the cluster composition. Unit-cost planning without semantic guidance achieved **53.3% success**. Adding Gemini-based semantic belief shaping increased success to **73.3%**, but unit-cost planning still failed in some cases because it retained a preference for detection-first strategies.

Motion-cost planning without semantic guidance achieved **83.3% success**, but required an average of **6.30 replans** and **3.90 occluder removals**. Combining motion-cost planning with Gemini semantic guidance achieved **100% success**, with only **1.40 replans** and **0.40 removals** on average.

Other VLMs produced weaker results. For example, MC combined with GPT-4o-mini or Qwen3-VL:32B reached **93.3% success**, while MiniCPM-V produced **83.3%**. The results suggest that semantic guidance is dependent on model quality, but that it provides information complementary to geometric evaluation.

#### 4.4. Real-World Validation

The complete system was tested on real tabletop and shelf scenes using a RealSense camera, FoundationStereo for depth, SAM 3 for segmentation, SAM 3D for reconstruction, and FoundationPose for object pose estimation. Across four trials, the full system and the geometry-only ablation both completed the tasks. However, the full system reduced average replanning steps from **3.25 to 1.50** and total planning time from **232.0 seconds to 158.5 seconds**, a reported **32% reduction**.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological. It shows how generative perception and semantic reasoning can be used as **planning heuristics within a model-based robotics system**, rather than as replacements for geometric verification. The approach provides a practical way to compare sensing and manipulation strategies before incurring the cost of full motion planning.

The results are particularly relevant to shelf retrieval, warehouse picking, household robotics, and other settings where occlusion and viewpoint constraints make repeated observation ineffective. The framework could reduce unnecessary sensing, manipulation, and replanning by identifying when an object should be exposed rather than merely searched for.

The method also has limitations. It relies on a single representative target particle and a limited set of imagined geometries and candidate skeletons. Approximate visibility and effort costs may not accurately predict execution cost, and performance depends on VLM judgments, scene reconstruction quality, cost-weight selection, and the underlying TAMP solver. The real-world evaluation contains only four trials, so its generality is not established. Future work identified by the authors includes maintaining uncertainty over multiple imagined geometries and adapting the generation and evaluation of task strategies more systematically.
