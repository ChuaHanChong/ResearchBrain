# MIRA: Memory-Integrated Reinforcement Learning Agent with Limited LLM Guidance

> [!summary] Summary
> MIRA, a memory-integrated reinforcement learning agent, effectively leverages LLM guidance by amortizing knowledge into an evolving memory graph, leading to faster learning and improved performance in sparse-reward environments. This approach significantly reduces the reliance on continuous LLM interaction while preserving the agent's autonomous policy learning and convergence guarantees.

## Problem

- Traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often suffer from high sample complexity and slow convergence, particularly in environments with sparse or delayed rewards.
- Existing methods for integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) into RL often incur prohibitive computational costs and latency due to frequent, per-step LLM supervision.
- Over-reliance on continuous LLM guidance can interfere with RL's fundamental learning principles, limit autonomous decision-making, and potentially introduce unreliability from LLM hallucinations.

## Method

- **MIRA** integrates an evolving ==memory graph== that dynamically stores and updates task-relevant information from offline LLM priors and the agent's high-return experiences.
- It employs an adaptive advantage shaping mechanism that modifies the standard PPO advantage term with a utility signal derived from the memory graph, providing a strong inductive bias early in training.
- The agent uses infrequent online LLM queries, filtered by a confidence-based screening unit, only when its internal memory lacks useful guidance, thus minimizing LLM dependency.

## Results

- MIRA achieves significantly faster early learning and higher asymptotic returns compared to PPO and hierarchical RL baselines across various sparse-reward, partially observable environments.
- It demonstrates high LLM query efficiency, performing comparably to more query-intensive methods while using substantially fewer online LLM calls (e.g., ~**30** total queries versus **500**+).
- The agent maintains stable performance even when exposed to unreliable LLM guidance in later training stages, indicating robustness, and its theoretical guarantees demonstrate non-vanishing policy updates and asymptotic convergence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLM-derived knowledge can be effectively amortized into a dynamic, compact memory structure, offering persistent guidance without requiring continuous online interaction.
> - Adaptive advantage shaping allows LLM priors to accelerate early-stage learning in sparse-reward settings while ensuring the agent's policy ultimately converges to the optimal behavior dictated by environmental rewards.
> - Combining offline LLM-generated priors with a carefully screened, sparse online query mechanism balances early learning acceleration with long-term autonomy and robustness to imperfect LLM outputs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge at the intersection of Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Large Language Models (LLMs): leveraging the rich knowledge of LLMs to enhance RL agents without incurring prohibitive costs or compromising RL's fundamental learning principles.

**Challenges in Reinforcement Learning:** Traditional RL agents often suffer from high sample complexity, particularly in environments with sparse or delayed rewards. This makes it difficult for agents to identify informative trajectories and credit successful actions, leading to slow convergence and inefficient exploration. Partial observability further exacerbates these issues, as agents must generalize from limited information.

**Emergence of LLM Guidance in RL:** LLMs have emerged as powerful tools capable of reasoning over abstract goals, interpreting high-level intent, and leveraging broad prior knowledge. This makes them natural candidates for providing structured guidance to RL agents, offering a complementary source of information to environment-driven feedback. The research landscape has explored various ways to integrate LLMs into RL:
- **Reward Models:** LLMs can act as implicit or explicit reward models, generating reward signals or defining reward functions.
- **Planning and Policy Guidance:** LLMs can generate high-level plans, policy sketches, or step-by-step guidance.
- **Task-Level Guidance:** LLMs can assist with subgoal decomposition, curriculum design, or goal interpretation.

**Limitations of Existing LLM-RL Integration:** Despite their promise, many existing approaches rely heavily on frequent (often per-step) LLM supervision. This introduces several significant problems:
- **Scalability and Cost:** Frequent LLM queries are computationally expensive and introduce latency, making real-time applications challenging.
- **Dependence and Autonomy:** Heavy reliance on LLMs can interfere with the RL learning signal, limiting the agent's autonomous decision-making and ability to generalize if the LLM is unavailable.
- **Reliability:** LLMs are prone to hallucinations, prompt sensitivity, and limited grounding in physical environments, making their outputs potentially unreliable.

MIRA directly tackles these limitations by proposing a method that *amortizes* LLM guidance into a persistent, evolving memory structure. This approach aims to strike a balance, leveraging LLM knowledge to accelerate early learning while preserving RL's core strengths—autonomous adaptation and optimization based on environmental feedback—and ensuring long-term convergence without LLM dependency. It pushes the frontier towards more efficient, robust, and scalable LLM-guided RL systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of MIRA are guided by two desiderata: (I) improve early learning by incorporating task-relevant priors from an LLM, and (II) minimize reliance on continuous real-time LLM supervision to ensure scalability and maintain autonomous policy learning.

The motivations for MIRA stem from the inherent challenges of traditional Reinforcement Learning and the shortcomings of existing LLM-RL integration strategies:

1. **Addressing High Sample Complexity in Sparse/Delayed Reward Settings:** In many real-world tasks, rewards are infrequent or appear long after the contributing actions, making it difficult for RL agents to learn effectively. MIRA aims to use LLM-derived knowledge to provide an initial "sense of direction," helping the agent discover rewarding trajectories much faster.
2. **Mitigating Over-reliance on Continuous LLM Supervision:** While LLMs offer valuable guidance, their computational cost, latency, and potential for generating unreliable outputs (hallucinations, prompt sensitivity) make continuous, real-time supervision impractical and risky. MIRA seeks to reduce this dependency by integrating LLM insights into a durable memory, reducing the need for repeated online queries.
3. **Preserving RL's Autonomous Learning and Convergence Guarantees:** A key concern with LLM-guided RL is that external guidance might distort the true reward function or prevent the agent from discovering optimal policies independently. MIRA's design ensures that LLM guidance acts as a temporary "scaffolding" that fades over time, allowing the agent's policy to eventually converge to the true optimal behavior dictated by environmental rewards.
4. **Improving Early-Stage Learning and Exploration:** During the initial phases of training, the RL critic is poorly calibrated, leading to weak or noisy advantage estimates. MIRA is motivated to provide a stable, informative signal derived from structured memory, thereby strengthening policy updates and enabling more effective exploration from the outset.
5. **Enhancing Generalization and Robustness:** By allowing the agent to validate and refine LLM-derived knowledge through its own experience, MIRA aims to create a more robust system that can filter out incorrect LLM suggestions and generalize beyond the initial priors.

In essence, MIRA's overarching goal is to enable RL agents to learn more efficiently and robustly in complex environments by judiciously integrating LLM intelligence, making them both smarter in early stages and more autonomous in the long run.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MIRA (Memory-Integrated Reinforcement Learning Agent) introduces a novel framework that uses a structured, evolving memory graph to integrate LLM guidance into Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The key components are:

#### 3.1. Memory Graph Design

- **Structure:** The core of MIRA is an evolving memory graph (G) that organizes task-relevant information. Nodes in this graph represent decision-relevant context, such as specific trajectory segments ($\tau_j$), and are associated with a goal term ($\zeta_j$ – either a final goal $g_j$ or an abstract subgoal $\kappa_j$). Each trajectory node also stores an estimated (sub)goal reward ($\hat{r}_j$) and a confidence score ($c_j$) derived from LLM statistics. Edges encode the hierarchical decomposition of goals into subgoals.
- **Co-construction:** The memory graph is dynamically built from two sources:
 1. **Offline LLM Priors:** Initialized with LLM-generated trajectory segments and subgoal decompositions based on the task description. These provide structured priors to accelerate early exploration.
 2. **Agent's High-Return Experiences:** The agent validates, revises, and extends the graph based on its own successful experiences. If the agent discovers a better trajectory for a (sub)goal or achieves a higher estimated return, the graph is updated.
- **Compactness and Adaptability:** The graph remains compact by pruning nodes that are unused for a fixed number of episodes, reflecting reduced relevance. This ensures that low-quality or misleading segments naturally fade over time as better agent-generated trajectories replace them.

#### 3.2. Offline and Online Guidance

- **Offline Guidance:** Pre-training LLM queries generate initial trajectory segments and subgoal decompositions, populating the memory graph with structured priors. This accelerates early exploration and provides a persistent baseline.
- **Infrequent Online Guidance:** Online LLM queries are triggered sparingly, only when the agent's current memory graph provides no useful guidance (e.g., utility remains near zero for several episodes). These queries are constrained by the agent's partial observability and may provide short plans or control signals.
- **Screening Unit:** All online LLM outputs are passed through a lightweight screening unit. This unit assesses confidence by either token-level likelihoods (geometric mean of per-token probabilities) or by measuring agreement across multiple independent completions (majority-consistency test). Low-confidence suggestions are discarded, reducing the risk of incorporating hallucinated or low-quality outputs. Accepted outputs are then grafted into the memory graph or used for soft logit injection.

#### 3.3. Utility Signal Computation

- **State-Action Utility:** A utility signal ($U_t$) is computed for each state-action pair in the agent's rollout, indicating how well it aligns with stored high-return trajectory segments in the memory graph.
- **Components of Utility ($U_t$):**
 - **Similarity ($\int(\cdot, \cdot)$):** Measures how closely the agent's behavior (observation, action) aligns with a stored trajectory segment, considering action agreement and spatial consistency (e.g., grid positions, directional alignment). Zero utility if no match.
 - **Goal Alignment ($\rho(\cdot, \cdot)$):** Scales the similarity based on the semantic relatedness between the subgoal of the matched memory segment and the implied subgoal of the agent's current transition. This ensures only semantically relevant memory entries influence utility.
 - **Confidence ($c_m$) and Estimated Reward ($\hat{r}_m$):** The utility is further modulated by the confidence score and estimated subgoal reward associated with the memory node. This downweights less reliable or less effective LLM-derived segments.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Advantage Shaping

- **Shaped Advantage ($\tilde{A}_t$):** MIRA modifies the standard advantage term ($A_t$) in PPO with a utility-based shaping term: $\tilde{A}_t = \eta_t A_t + \xi_t U_t$. Here, $\eta_t$ and $\xi_t$ are time-varying weights.
- **Cooperative Process:** This shaping creates a cooperative process where the critic's learned value estimates (via $A_t$) are refined by an inductive bias from language-guided priors (via $U_t$). Early in training, when the critic is unreliable, the utility term provides crucial directional guidance.
- **Convergence-Compatible Shaping:** As training progresses and the agent's policy improves (and the critic's $A_t$ becomes more reliable), the shaping weight $\xi_t$ is annealed to zero, and $\eta_t$ is ramped to one. This ensures that the shaping influence fades, preventing any inaccuracies from LLM priors from biasing the long-term optimal policy, thus preserving PPO's asymptotic convergence guarantees.
- **Theoretical Guarantee:** The paper theoretically demonstrates that this utility-based shaping improves early-stage learning in sparse-reward settings by ensuring non-vanishing policy updates even when reward-based advantages are weak (Theorem 1).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

MIRA's empirical evaluation across various sparse-reward, partially observable environments (Gymnasium ToyText, MiniGrid, BabyAI) highlights its effectiveness and efficiency. The findings are structured around several guiding questions:

**Q1: Early Learning Efficiency and Convergence (FROZENLAKE):**
- Both offline-only and online-only MIRA variants significantly improve early-stage learning speed compared to the PPO baseline.
- While PPO eventually catches up in asymptotic return in this simpler environment, MIRA achieves comparable final performance much faster.
- The adaptive shaping mechanism shows that the utility term's influence ($\delta_t = \xi_t/\eta_t$) decays over training, confirming that MIRA preserves long-term convergence properties.

**Q2: Performance in Long-Horizon Environments (MiniGrid/BabyAI):**
- MIRA consistently outperforms PPO and hierarchical RL (HRL) baselines in tasks like REDBALL, LAVACROSSING, DOORKEY, and REDBLUEDOOR.
- It achieves faster learning, higher asymptotic returns, and greater success rates. For instance, in REDBALL, MIRA reaches optimal returns in under half the training iterations compared to HRL. In LAVACROSSING, PPO fails, while MIRA converges significantly faster than HRL.
- These gains are achieved with a *limited* LLM query budget, combining a few offline prompts with infrequent online queries. MIRA avoids inefficiencies seen in HRL (e.g., exploring distractors) by focusing on goal-relevant behavior earlier.

**Q3: LLM Query Efficiency (DISTRACTED DOORKEY):**
- MIRA demonstrates significantly higher query efficiency compared to methods requiring continuous LLM supervision, such as LLM4Teach (which queries per state-action-reward triplet) and LLM-RS (which queries per layout).
- MIRA achieves comparable final performance to LLM4Teach but uses substantially fewer online LLM queries (e.g., ~30 total queries vs. 500+ for LLM4Teach).
- LLM-RS benefits early from reward shaping but plateaus lower than MIRA, further highlighting MIRA's balanced approach.

**Q4: Impact of Online Query Frequency (DOORKEY Ablation):**
- Increasing the frequency of online LLM queries accelerates learning and leads to faster achievement of optimal returns.
- Even an offline-only MIRA variant (zero online queries) provides a notable boost over baseline PPO, demonstrating the value of static memory. This highlights a practical trade-off: offline memory is useful when online access is restricted, but dynamic queries further accelerate learning.

**Q5: Robustness to Unreliable LLM Outputs (DISTRACTED DOORKEY Ablation):**
- MIRA maintains stable performance even when exposed to unreliable LLM guidance (e.g., switching to a less effective LLM with screening disabled) in later training stages.
- This indicates that once the agent's policy has matured and its memory graph is well-formed, its dependence on online LLM guidance diminishes, allowing it to tolerate imperfect suggestions without collapsing performance.

**Q6: Impact of LLM Reasoning Styles (Ablation with different LLMs):**
- The choice of LLM strongly influences the quality of the memory graph and downstream RL performance. Different LLMs (e.g., Gemma3, Claude, Gemini Pro, GPT-o4-mini) exhibit varied reasoning styles that lead to different learning curves and asymptotic returns.
- For instance, Gemma3's inefficient reasoning (checking doors after every pickup) hindered performance, while GPT-o4-mini and Gemini Pro generally enabled faster and higher-performing learning. This underscores the importance of high-quality LLM priors.

**Theoretical Findings:**
- **Non-Vanishing Updates:** MIRA's utility shaping ensures non-vanishing policy updates in sparse-reward regimes, providing a consistent gradient signal even when traditional reward-based advantages are weak (Theorem 1).
- **Stability and Asymptotic Equivalence:** The adaptive shaping mechanism preserves the stability and asymptotic convergence properties of PPO, ensuring that the shaped objective eventually converges to the standard PPO objective (Theorem 2 & 3).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MIRA represents a significant advancement in the field of LLM-guided reinforcement learning, offering a principled solution to several long-standing challenges.

**Key Contributions:**

1. **Reduced LLM Dependency and Scalability:** MIRA's most significant contribution is its ability to effectively integrate LLM guidance while drastically reducing the reliance on continuous, expensive LLM queries. By amortizing LLM outputs into an evolving memory graph, it addresses critical scalability and latency issues, making LLM-enhanced RL more practical for real-world applications.
2. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency in Sparse Reward Settings:** The utility-based advantage shaping provides a strong inductive bias that accelerates early learning, particularly in environments with sparse or delayed rewards where traditional RL struggles with credit assignment and exploration. This translates to faster convergence and higher final performance.
3. **Preservation of RL Autonomy and Convergence Guarantees:** Unlike methods that heavily rely on LLMs for direct policy generation or constant reward shaping, MIRA's adaptive advantage shaping decays over time. This crucial design choice ensures that the agent's policy ultimately optimizes against the true environment reward, maintaining RL's autonomous learning capabilities and preserving long-term convergence properties.
4. **Robustness to Imperfect LLM Outputs:** The inclusion of a screening unit for online LLM queries and the dynamic pruning mechanism for the memory graph make MIRA robust to potentially unreliable or outdated LLM suggestions. The agent can validate and refine its internal model based on its own experience, learning to discard unhelpful priors.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Broader Application of RL:** By overcoming the sample efficiency bottleneck and reducing LLM dependency, MIRA could enable RL to be successfully applied to a wider range of complex tasks in domains like robotics, autonomous systems, and advanced game AI, where designing dense reward functions is difficult and real-time LLM interaction is costly.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** MIRA's ability to leverage language for initial guidance can facilitate more intuitive human-robot interaction, allowing humans to provide high-level instructions that LLMs translate into actionable priors.
- **Foundation for Lifelong Learning:** The evolving memory graph, refined by agent experience, provides a foundation for more sophisticated lifelong learning agents that can continuously update their knowledge and filter out old or incorrect information.
- **Bridging Symbolic and Sub-symbolic AI:** MIRA effectively bridges the gap between the symbolic reasoning capabilities of LLMs (providing abstract plans and subgoals) and the sub-symbolic learning mechanisms of deep RL (optimizing policies through interaction), leading to more intelligent and adaptable agents.

**Future Directions:** The authors acknowledge limitations, such as the current focus on discrete action spaces. Extending MIRA to continuous control and vision-based domains, perhaps by integrating visual embeddings or learned affordances for similarity computation, is a natural and high-impact next step. Adapting MIRA to multi-goal domains, where reusable subgoal structures are prominent, also holds significant promise for further expanding its utility.
