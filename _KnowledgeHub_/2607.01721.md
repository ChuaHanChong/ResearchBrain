# CoRe: Combined Rewards with Vision-Language Model Feedback for Preference-Aligned Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Chinese Academy of Sciences developed CoRe, a hybrid framework that automatically constructs reinforcement learning reward functions by combining formal and residual components with Vision-Language Model feedback. It enables feedback-efficient and human preference-aligned policies, achieving a 99.0% average success rate across MetaWorld tasks with 3-40 times fewer labels than other methods.

## Problem

- Designing effective, hand-crafted reward functions for complex RL tasks is challenging, labor-intensive, and often leads to suboptimal or unintended behaviors.
- Learning reward functions from human preferences (PbRL) typically requires a substantial number of costly labels, leading to low feedback efficiency and poor generalization.
- Existing LLM/VLM-based reward generation methods often produce coarse or noisy signals, struggling to capture the fine-grained nuances required for intricate manipulation tasks.

## Method

- Propose **CoRe**, a hybrid framework decomposing reinforcement learning rewards into complementary ==Formal Rewards (FR)== and ==Residual Rewards (RR)== components.
- The ==Formal Reward Module (FRM)== iteratively optimizes code-based rewards using LLM-generated candidates evaluated via ==Reward-Preference Alignment== against a preference dataset.
- The ==Residual Reward Module (RRM)== learns implicit preferences from ==VLM-generated video-level feedback== and per-frame importance weights, enhancing credit assignment and feedback efficiency.

## Results

- CoRe achieved an average success rate of **99.0%** across seven MetaWorld tasks, closely matching human performance, and showed superior learning speed on SoftGym tasks compared to baselines.
- The framework demonstrated high sample efficiency, requiring **3 to 40 times fewer** preference labels than existing methods, significantly reducing human labeling burden.
- Policies trained with CoRe showed strong sim-to-real robustness, performing reliably on five real-world robotic manipulation tasks without requiring additional fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing rewards into explicit formal components and implicit residual components, guided by VLM feedback, enables a more robust and comprehensive reward signal aligned with human preferences.
> - Utilizing "Reward-Preference Alignment" with LLM-generated code and a preference dataset effectively optimizes formal rewards, preventing exploitation and ensuring alignment with human intent.
> - Leveraging VLM-generated video-level preferences and per-frame importance weights provides richer context and improves credit assignment for learning fine-grained residual rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in domains such as gaming, autonomous driving, and robotic manipulation. A persistent challenge in RL is the design of effective reward functions, which are critical for guiding policy optimization. Hand-crafted rewards are often difficult to specify and can lead to suboptimal or unsafe behaviors, requiring extensive domain expertise and engineering effort.

To address these limitations, a significant body of research has emerged, exploring methods to automatically derive rewards. These approaches broadly fall into categories such as:
- **Imitation Learning/Inverse Reinforcement Learning:** Leveraging human demonstrations to infer reward functions (e.g., Ho & Ermon, 2016; Christiano et al., 2017).
- **Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL):** Learning reward models from human comparisons of trajectory segments (e.g., Lee et al., 2021a; Liu et al., 2022). While eliminating the need for hand-crafted rewards and aligning with human preferences, this paradigm often demands a substantial number of costly human labels, limiting its scalability.
- **Leveraging Large Pre-trained Models:** More recently, large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) have been integrated into reward design:
 - **Automated Preference Labeling:** Using LLMs/VLMs to generate synthetic preference labels based on textual or visual goals, reducing the human labeling burden (e.g., Klissarov et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024).
 - **Code-based Reward Generation:** Exploiting LLMs' code-generation and reasoning capabilities to output executable reward function code, aiming to automate intricate reward engineering (e.g., Yu et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024).
 - **VLM-based State Scoring:** Employing VLMs like CLIP to score states based on visual or textual similarity to task objectives, providing per-step task rewards (e.g., Cui et al., 2022; Sontakke et al., 2023).

Despite these advancements, existing approaches face limitations. Learned reward models from human preferences often exhibit low feedback efficiency, unstable training, and poor generalization. LLM- or VLM-generated reward functions can be coarse or noisy, potentially failing to capture the fine-grained structure necessary for complex manipulation tasks. This leaves an open problem: how to obtain reward signals that are both feedback-efficient and reliably aligned with task-specific human intentions.

The current work, CoRe, attempts to address this gap by proposing a hybrid framework. It draws inspiration from cognitive science, which suggests that human learning combines prior knowledge with iterative refinement. CoRe decomposes rewards into two complementary components—Formal Rewards (FR) and Residual Rewards (RR)—and integrates them with VLM feedback. This approach aims to combine the structured, task-relevant features of explicit rewards with the flexible, nuanced alignment of preference-based learning, thereby enhancing both efficiency and alignment in reward design for RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that can automatically construct reliable reward functions for reinforcement learning, ensuring they are both efficient and aligned with human preferences, without requiring direct human involvement.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges inherent in conventional reward design for RL:
1. **Difficulty of Hand-crafted Rewards:** Manually designing reward functions for complex tasks is often a labor-intensive process that requires significant domain expertise. Poorly specified rewards can lead to unintended behaviors, known as "reward hacking," or result in suboptimal policies.
2. **Limitations of Learned Rewards:** Approaches that learn reward functions from human preferences, while valuable for aligning with human intent, typically suffer from low feedback efficiency. They often require a large number of preference labels, which are costly to obtain, and can lead to unstable training or poor generalization, particularly in complex or novel environments.
3. **Coarseness and Noise in LLM/VLM-generated Rewards:** While LLMs and VLMs offer promise in automating reward design by generating code or scoring states, their outputs can be coarse or noisy. These models may struggle to capture the fine-grained details and nuances required for intricate manipulation tasks, potentially leading to suboptimal policy learning.

To overcome these issues, the authors propose an approach inspired by cognitive science, specifically the dual nature of human learning. Human learning is influenced by existing prior knowledge (which aids in encoding, comprehension, and schema construction) and is refined through iterative interaction with the environment for specific dynamics, constraints, or feedback.

Translating this cognitive insight to RL, the paper motivates a decomposition of rewards into two complementary components:
- **Formal Rewards (FR):** These are explicitly designed and encoded based on prior task knowledge. They represent the aspects of a task that can be directly specified and provide a structured foundation for learning.
- **Residual Rewards (RR):** These capture task aspects that are difficult to hand-design and must be learned implicitly from observations. They enable adaptation to nuanced preferences that are not easily expressible through formal rules.

This decomposition is intended to combine the benefits of structured, task-relevant features (from FR) with flexible preference alignment (from RR), thereby reducing the overall difficulty of reward learning in preference-based RL. The key motivation is to leverage the common-sense knowledge and understanding capabilities of VLMs to facilitate the automatic construction of these combined rewards, ultimately leading to feedback-efficient and preference-aligned policies without requiring human intervention.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CoRe operates as a hybrid framework that decomposes the reinforcement learning reward into two complementary components: Formal Rewards (FR) and Residual Rewards (RR). These components are integrated with feedback from Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to achieve preference-aligned policies without human involvement. The overall architecture involves continuous interaction between policy learning, formal reward updating, and residual reward learning.

#### 3.1. Formal Reward Module (FRM)

The FRM is responsible for iteratively designing and optimizing code-based formal rewards.
- **Initial Approach and its Limitation:** Traditional methods (e.g., Eureka) leverage LLMs to generate and refine executable reward code based on task descriptions, environment knowledge, previous best rewards, and policy feedback. The selection of the "best" reward candidate typically relies on a task completion function ($F_c$), which evaluates the policy trained with that candidate. However, this task completion-based evaluation can lead to suboptimal behaviors or "reward exploitation" if the LLM-generated reward excessively focuses on superficial task completion rather than the intended human preference (as illustrated in Figure 2a, where striking a nail with a hammer handle might yield high "nail progress" but is not the preferred action).
- **CoRe's Solution: Reward-Preference Alignment:** To address this, CoRe replaces the task completion-based evaluation with a preference-guided reward search. This process, termed "Reward-Preference Alignment," involves:
 1. **Candidate Generation:** Given a task description, task-specific environment knowledge (e.g., state definitions), the previously identified best formal reward, and policy feedback, LLMs (specifically GPT-4.1 mini) generate a set of candidate reward functions.
 2. **Preference-based Evaluation:** Instead of training a policy for each candidate reward, the candidates are evaluated against a preference dataset ($D$). This dataset consists of triples $(\sigma_0, \sigma_1, y)$, where $\sigma_0$ and $\sigma_1$ are trajectory segments, and $y$ indicates human preference (e.g., $\sigma_1$ preferred, $\sigma_0$ preferred, or no preference).
 3. **Selection of Best Reward:** The best reward candidate ($\hat{r}^n_{best}$) is chosen as the one that maximizes its preference prediction accuracy rate ($F_p$) on the dataset $D$. This is calculated by comparing the sum of rewards ($\hat{R}^n_k(\sigma_i)$) for each segment with the preference label ($y$) (Equation 2 and 3).
- **Benefits:** This method ensures that formal rewards are continuously optimized and aligned with human preferences, guiding policy improvement while mitigating reward exploitation. It also accelerates the reward validation process by avoiding retraining policies from scratch for each candidate.

#### 3.2. Residual Reward Module (RRM)

The RRM is designed to learn residual rewards that capture implicit, nuanced preferences difficult to hand-design, complementing the FRM.
- **Challenges in Learning Residual Rewards:** Nonlinear reward models are expressive but often suffer from feedback inefficiency. This is because single state-based preferences offer limited information, and segment-based preferences make accurate state-wise credit assignment difficult.
- **CoRe's Solution: Video-level Preference and Importance Weights:** CoRe addresses this through video-level preference image-based reward learning, leveraging VLM feedback.
 1. **Video-level Preference Labeling:** Instead of relying on low-expression preferences (e.g., natural language, single-frame images), CoRe inputs the task description ($l_d$), start image ($I_s$), goal image ($I_d$), and video segments ($\{I^i_t\}$ for $i \in \{0,1\}$) into a VLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash). The VLM outputs a preference label ($y$) indicating which video segment is preferred (Equation 4). Segments deemed "incomparable" are excluded from training. Videos are downsampled to reduce computational cost.
 2. **Per-frame Importance Weight Assessment:** During preference labeling, the VLM also assesses task completion across the frames, generating per-frame importance weights ($W^i = \{w^i_t\}$). These weights serve as an approximate prior for state rewards, aiding in credit assignment. This is achieved through a two-stage multimodal prompting query, inspired by chain-of-thought reasoning. Importance weights are linearly interpolated and normalized using a softmax function.
 3. **Residual Reward Model Training:** The reward model ($\hat{r}_\psi$) is trained using a composite objective function (Equation 5):
 - **Cross-entropy Loss ($L_{Reward}$):** Standard loss to match the VLM-generated preference labels.
 - **Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence Loss ($L_{KL}$):** Enforces consistency between the predicted state reward distribution and the VLM-derived prior importance weights (Equation 6). This term regularizes the reward learning process.
- **Benefits:** Video-level preference provides richer information, and the importance weights facilitate accurate credit assignment, significantly accelerating reward learning and improving feedback efficiency for the residual reward model.

#### 3.3. Overall CoRe Framework (Algorithm 1)

CoRe integrates these two modules concurrently with policy learning:
1. **Policy Learning and Data Collection:** An off-policy RL algorithm (SAC) is used. The policy collects experience, including state, image, action, next state, next image, and the combined formal and residual rewards.
2. **Formal Reward Update:** Periodically (every $M$ time steps), the FRM updates the formal reward using the Reward-Preference Alignment process based on a preference dataset.
3. **Residual Reward Learning:** Periodically (every $N$ time steps), the RRM generates new preference labels and importance weights from sampled trajectory segments using the VLM, then updates the residual reward model.
4. **Reward Relabeling:** After any reward update (formal or residual), the entire replay buffer is relabeled with the latest combined reward function.

The synergy between FRM and RRM is critical: FRM provides structured guidance and facilitates RRM's exploration, while RRM captures the fine-grained nuances that FRM might miss, ensuring robust and preference-aligned learning.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Policy:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) with state observations.
- **Reward Models:** For MetaWorld, a 4-layer CNN; for SoftGym, ResNet-18. An ensemble of three reward models is used, with tanh activation.
- **Optimizers:** Adam for all models.
- **VLMs:** GPT-4.1 mini for FRM (code generation), Gemini 2.0 Flash for RRM (preference labeling and importance weighting).
- **Environment Settings:** Images are rendered with task-relevant objects only (robot arm transparent, camera focused) to mitigate VLM hallucinations. Reward scales are clipped to [-0.5, 0.5].

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive experiments across ten robotic manipulation tasks (seven from MetaWorld, three from SoftGym) and five real-world tasks to evaluate CoRe's performance.

#### 4.1. Overall Policy Learning Performance

- **Effectiveness and Efficiency:** CoRe generally achieves faster and more stable policy learning compared to baselines. On MetaWorld tasks, it frequently reaches or surpasses human-level performance (e.g., Sweep Into, Hammer, Soccer, Drawer Open). For SoftGym tasks, CoRe demonstrates rapid learning and high stability, often outperforming other methods (e.g., Straighten Rope).
- **Quantitative Comparison (Table 1, Figure 3):** CoRe achieves an average success rate of 99.0% across seven MetaWorld tasks, significantly outperforming baselines like Env Sparse (69.6%), CLIP Score (4.8%), Eureka (75.5%), Text2Reward (74.2%), RL-VLM-F (56.8%), PrefVLM (18.6%), and ERL-VLM (37.0%), while closely matching human performance (99.3%). In SoftGym, CoRe achieves the best episode rewards for Fold Cloth (-0.10), Pass Water (-30.0), and Straighten Rope (20.6).
- **Complex Task Handling:** CoRe exhibits strong performance on complex tasks requiring intricate actions (e.g., Hammer, Peg Insert), where sparse rewards are ineffective and other LLM/VLM-based methods struggle to specify suitable reward components or accurately assign credit. For example, in Hammer, CoRe achieves 98.0% success compared to Eureka's 33.3% and Text2Reward's 32.0%.

#### 4.2. Sample and Computational Efficiency

- **Feedback Efficiency (Table 3):** CoRe demonstrates superior sample efficiency, requiring 3 to 40 times fewer labels than other methods. For instance, in MetaWorld tasks, CoRe uses 0.5K labels compared to PrefVLM's 21K. This reduction is attributed to its structured reward design and efficient video-level preference learning.
- **Training Cost (Table 4):** CoRe maintains a favorable trade-off between performance, computational cost, and runtime efficiency, averaging 2.00M tokens, $0.37 API cost, and 2.15 hours per training run across ten tasks. This efficiency stems from the structured formal rewards and video-level residual reward learning.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Contribution of FRM and RRM (Table 2):**
 - FRM (code-based) performs better in rigid-object manipulation, demonstrating its effectiveness for tasks with explicit features.
 - RRM (vision-based) excels in deformable-object manipulation, showing its capability to handle complex visual dynamics.
 - The combined CoRe framework consistently yields performance gains across tasks, indicating a complementary synergy where RRM compensates for FRM's expressiveness limitations, and FRM facilitates RRM's reward learning.
- **Reward-Preference Alignment in FRM (Table 2):** Iterative application of Reward-Preference Alignment in FRM (FRM$_0$ to FRM$_4$) consistently improves policy performance. This mechanism enables rapid validation of reward candidates and ensures continuous alignment with human preferences.
- **VLM Preference for Images vs. Videos (Figure 4):** Video-level preference labels from VLMs consistently achieve higher accuracy (51.5% correct) and fewer "no preference" responses (33.5%) compared to image-level preferences (41.6% correct, 42.8% no preference). This suggests that video clips provide richer contextual information, leading to higher-quality VLM feedback.
- **Importance Weights:** VLM-derived importance weights show a moderate Spearman correlation (average $\rho=0.48$) with task progress, indicating a partial reflection of true state importance. RRM leverages these weights as an auxiliary constraint rather than direct rewards, enhancing accuracy and stability.

#### 4.4. Alignment of Rewards with Task Progress

- **Temporal and Spatial Quality (Figure 5, 6):** CoRe's learned rewards exhibit strong alignment with task progress along expert trajectories and smooth spatial distributions across object positions. The Spearman correlation between learned rewards and task progress averaged over ten tasks is 0.88 for CoRe, significantly higher than other baselines (e.g., PrefVLM: 0.09, RL-VLM-F: 0.44, ERL-VLM: 0.48). This indicates that CoRe produces accurate and stable reward models, facilitating stable policy optimization.

#### 4.5. Real-World Deployment

- **Sim-to-Real Robustness (Table 5):** Policies trained with CoRe demonstrate consistent superior performance across five real-world robotic manipulation tasks (Drawer Open, Dial Turn, Hammer, Fold Cloth, Straighten Rope) using UR5 robot arms. This indicates strong robustness to real-world noise and high-quality reward signals, enabling reliable deployment without fine-tuning.

#### 4.6. Additional Analyses

- **Stronger Models (Table 9):** Simply scaling to stronger proprietary LLM/VLM models (e.g., GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Flash) provides limited performance gains for FRM and RRM individually compared to CoRe's architectural improvements, despite significantly increased API costs.
- **Open-source VLMs (Table 10):** CoRe demonstrates compatibility with open-source VLMs. Using Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, it still achieves strong performance, whereas Video-LLaVA-7B struggled.
- **Robustness to Visual Disturbances (Table 11):** CoRe maintains stable performance with only minor degradation when a randomly moving red cube is introduced as a visual disturbance, indicating robustness to perceptual noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces CoRe, a reward learning framework that combines formal and residual reward components with VLM feedback, addressing critical challenges in reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation.

**Significance:**
- **Automated Reward Design:** CoRe offers a method for constructing reward functions without extensive manual engineering. This significantly reduces the expert burden typically associated with designing effective reward signals in complex RL tasks.
- **Preference Alignment:** By leveraging VLM-generated preference labels and importance weights, CoRe ensures that learned policies are aligned with nuanced human intentions, even for tasks that are difficult to define explicitly. This mitigates issues like reward hacking, where policies achieve high scores through undesirable behaviors.
- **Efficiency and Stability:** The decomposition into formal and residual rewards, combined with VLM-guided preference feedback, contributes to both feedback efficiency (requiring significantly fewer labels than traditional preference learning) and stable policy optimization. The iterative refinement of formal rewards and the regularization of residual rewards through importance weights enhance the robustness of the learning process.
- **Generalizability and Robustness:** Demonstrated performance across a diverse set of simulated tasks (rigid, articulated, and deformable objects) and successful deployment on real-world robotic platforms indicates the framework's generalizability and robustness to real-world complexities and noise.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Robotic Autonomy:** CoRe could enable robots to learn and perform complex manipulation tasks more autonomously and reliably, especially in environments where task specifications are ambiguous or human preferences are subtle.
- **Broader RL Applications:** By simplifying reward design, CoRe could broaden the applicability of RL to new domains where reward specification is a major bottleneck, potentially accelerating research and development in areas like human-robot interaction, manufacturing, and service robotics.
- **Advancement in VLM-RL Integration:** The work highlights the utility of advanced VLMs not just for state scoring or coarse goal interpretation, but for generating high-quality preference signals and importance weights that facilitate intricate reward learning. This could inspire further research into more sophisticated ways of integrating large foundation models into RL.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** Policies aligned with human preferences can lead to more intuitive, safer, and acceptable robot behaviors in shared spaces or collaborative tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
- **Computational and Financial Costs:** The reliance on iterative LLM/VLM querying for reward generation and preference feedback introduces computational overhead and financial costs associated with API usage. Future work aims to explore uncertainty-aware reward modeling and more efficient querying strategies to mitigate these aspects.
- **Potential Biases and Inaccuracies:** As with any system relying on large pre-trained models, CoRe is susceptible to potential biases or inaccuracies in VLM outputs, which could propagate into learned reward functions and policies.
- **Scalability to Diverse Environments:** While tested in real-world scenarios, evaluating CoRe in more diverse and unstructured real-world environments is an important direction for future research to further improve its generalizability across highly complex manipulation scenarios.
- **Safety Considerations:** The impact statement notes that the reliance on LLMs/VLMs raises robustness, scalability, and safety considerations that warrant further investigation for real-world robotic applications.
