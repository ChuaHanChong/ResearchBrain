# ARTiS: An Adaptive Robotic Gripper for Enhanced Tool Manipulation in Disassembly Applications

> [!summary] Summary
> ARTiS is a three-fingered robotic gripper for disassembly tool use, pairing an active granular-jamming palm that fixes the tool body with passively adaptive Fin-Ray fingertips and seven motorized degrees of freedom that reorient the tool tip. It grasps tools with 97% success, reorients and uses them at 85% and 68%, and reaches 95.27% grasping on YCB objects.

## Problem

- Existing grippers combine at most two innovations, such as adaptive fingers or dexterous fingers with an adaptive palm, so none holds tools securely while they are being used.
- Most three-finger designs lack a palm entirely, so tools spin under applied torque; fingers alone cannot resist the lever moment a screwdriver generates during unscrewing.
- The nearest jamming-palm hand targets household tasks: shallow round palm, deformable shell, and stiff fragile fingertips all fail under disassembly torques and loads.

## Method

- ==Palm-finger cooperative architecture==: an active pneumatic ==granular-jamming palm== using glass beads at -88 kPa fixes the tool body while three servo-driven fingers, seven degrees of freedom total, reorient the tool tip.
- A cup-shaped jamming palm with a perpendicular 60 mm cutout lets table-lying tools seat deeply for power wrap grasps, plus a granule filter and flat bearings for high-torque loading.
- Two pose-specific ==Fin-Ray fingertips==: fingertip 1 couples four Fin-Ray segments with a rubber ring for two-plane adaptation; fingertip 2 uses opposed Fin-Rays for semi-cylindrical wrap contact.

## Results

- Across 13 tools, jamming plus lifting succeeded **97%**, all three stages combined **85%**, and grasping with reorientation and use **68%**; the best screwdriver transmitted **4.79 N·m** peak torque.
- Against a LEAP Hand baseline, ARTiS resisted **5.4x** and **7.8x** more tool-tip force in two-finger sphere holding, and **20.4%** to **32.8%** more than LEAP's three-finger power wrap.
- Ablations attribute over **90%** of holding force to the palm; YCB grasping reached **95.27%** over 1290 trials, and robot fixation cut a three-screw human unscrewing task by **13.77%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robotic tool use is a fixation problem more than a grasping problem: an active palm should carry the load while fingers steer the tool tip.
> - Palm and finger contributions combine nonlinearly, so isolated component tests understate achievable holding force; ablate the combinations, not just the individual parts.
> - Granular jamming degrades under vibration because beads compact and contact is lost, so powered tools need vibration control, stiffer palm material, or active side stops.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The automation of assembly and disassembly processes represents a significant area of research in robotics, with a focus on improving efficiency and consistency through adaptive task performance. A persistent challenge in this field is the ability of robotic systems to securely grasp, hold, and actively utilize various hand tools during operation. Traditional approaches often rely on rigidly fixing tools to robot end-effectors or employing automated tool-change mechanisms, which limit versatility and adaptability to diverse tasks and tool geometries.

Current robotic grasping technologies include bionic, soft, reconfigurable, anthropomorphic, and three-fingered grippers, each offering specific advantages. Bionic and anthropomorphic designs aim for high dexterity and adaptability to complex object shapes. Soft grippers offer compliance and broad contact areas, while reconfigurable designs provide mechanical versatility. Many existing designs, however, frequently exhibit limitations in robustly fixing tools during active use, particularly when confronted with dynamic forces, torque transmission, or varied surface geometries. For instance, while some grippers can grasp tools, they may lack the necessary features to prevent slipping or maintain precise orientation during tasks like unscrewing or hammering. A comparative review of three- and four-fingered grippers in the paper highlights that many lack sufficient finger reorientation capabilities, fingertip adaptation, or robust palm-based tool fixation mechanisms to support sustained tool operation.

A notable gap exists in grippers that integrate sufficient dexterity for tool tip control, adaptive fingertips for reliable contact and robustness, and an active palm for versatile tool shaping and robust fixation. The ability to perform "power sphere" and "power wrap" grasps, analogous to human hand strategies for tool use, is identified as a key missing capability. This research addresses this gap by introducing a gripper design that combines elements from different gripper types—soft grippers (adaptability), anthropomorphic hands (dexterity), and rigid mechanisms (robustness)—to create a system specifically engineered for enhanced tool manipulation and fixation in disassembly applications. The work aims to advance robotic capabilities from mere tool grasping to integrated tool use, including reorientation and operational tasks, thereby contributing to more flexible and robust automation solutions in manufacturing and recycling.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an adaptive robotic gripper, named ARTiS (Adaptive Robotic Tool Gripper in Disassembly Systems), that not only grasps tools but also robustly fixes, reorients, and uses them during complex disassembly tasks. This objective is motivated by the recognized limitations of existing robotic grippers in handling and operating a variety of hand tools efficiently and consistently in dynamic environments.

The specific motivations and challenges addressed are:
- **Insufficient Tool Fixation and Adaptation:** Current robotic grasping technologies often lack the capability to securely manipulate tools while they are being actively used. Many grippers can pick up tools but fail to provide robust holding and adaptation to surfaces of different geometries during operations that involve significant forces or torques, leading to tool slippage or loss of control.
- **Need for Multi-faceted Adaptability:** Disassembly applications, such as the dismantling of automotive components, frequently involve a wide range of fasteners and tools that can vary significantly even within the same product line across different manufacturing years. This necessitates a gripper capable of rapid adaptation to diverse tool shapes, robust fixation, and enduring performance.
- **Mimicking Human Hand Capabilities:** The human hand demonstrates remarkable adaptability, robustness, and dexterity in tool manipulation, often utilizing combined palm and finger actions (e.g., "power wrap" and "power sphere" grasps) to fix tools and adjust their orientation. Existing robotic grippers often struggle to replicate these integrated capabilities, particularly the robust palm fixation combined with precise finger control for tool tip adjustment.
- **Integration of Multiple Innovations:** The paper highlights that many existing gripper designs are based on one or a limited combination of adaptive features (e.g., adaptive fingers or adaptive palm with dexterous fingers). There is a need for a unified architecture that integrates sufficient finger dexterity for tool tip control, adaptive fingertips for reliable contact and robustness, and an active palm for versatile tool body adaptation and fixation.
- **Addressing Specific Disassembly Tool Challenges:** The design of ARTiS was specifically motivated by representative tools used in automotive component disassembly, such as screwdrivers, hammers, and drills, provided by Denso Robotics. This provides a practical context for the gripper's development.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines several key contributions:
- **A Palm-Finger Cooperative Gripper Architecture:** This architecture combines an adaptive jamming palm with actively reconfigurable fingers, allowing for robust tool fixation by complementing the functions of the palm and fingers. The palm adapts to and fixes the tool body, while the fingers stabilize the grasp, adjust tool pose, and reorient.
- **Unique Jamming Palm Design:** A uniquely shaped palm jamming cup with a cutout enables perpendicular tool holding (power sphere grasp) and incorporates a filter for specific rigid glass granules to facilitate active jamming.
- **Novel 3D-Adaptive Pose-Specific Fingertips:** Two types of fingertips (one for sphere grasping, one for wrap grasping) are designed to improve contact adaptation with different tool handles and support stable tool interaction, drawing inspiration from Fin-Ray designs.
- **Experimental Validation of Tool Use:** The evaluation extends beyond simple tool grasping to comprehensive assessment of grasping, fixing, reorientation, and operational use of common disassembly tools (screwdrivers, hammers, drills).
- **Extended Grasping Generalization and Teaching Mode:** The gripper's generalization capability is assessed using YCB benchmark objects, and a "teaching mode" is developed to enable human operators to configure and record gripper states for collaborative tasks or imitation learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involved the design, fabrication, and comprehensive evaluation of the ARTiS gripper, focusing on its robustness, grasping versatility, and task versatility in disassembly and collaborative scenarios.

#### 3.1. Design Concept (ARTiS)

The ARTiS gripper is a three-fingered design incorporating a jamming palm and specialized fingertips, driven by a combination of electrical and pneumatic actuation.

- **Main Design:**
 - **Fingers:** The gripper features three fingers. One finger is fixed to the base, while the other two are driven by a Dynamixel MX-64R motor (J0) through a double gear transmission. This allows the two movable fingers to symmetrically change their orientation relative to the gripper axis from 0 to 70 degrees. Each finger is equipped with a four-bar mechanism, enabling a wide working range. A Dynamixel MX-28R motor (J4-J6) controls the closure of each finger. Additionally, each finger has an orientation motor (J1-J3, Dynamixel MX-28R) that rotates the finger around its own axis from -90 to 90 degrees, providing 3 degrees of freedom (DoF) per movable finger (2 DoF for the fixed finger). Kinematic equations (1) and (2) define the fingertip position based on link lengths and rotation angles.
 - **Jamming Palm:** A rubber-material jamming palm (100 mm diameter, 60 mm height) is situated atop the gripper in a cup-shaped cavity. It contains rigid glass beads (FGB-20) as jamming elements. A perpendicular cylindrical slot (60 mm diameter) in the cup allows for deep insertion of tools for power wrap grasping. The palm's stiffness is actively controlled by an in-line vacuum ejector (ZU05SA SMC), which creates negative pressure (-88 kPa) by sucking air through a filter, causing the granules to jam and fix the tool. Flat bearings (50 mm diameter) facilitate robust interaction between the base and the jamming palm.
 - **Control System:** A dedicated control box manages the gripper's operations. It accepts compressed air (0.1-0.9 MPa, typically 400 kPa), 12V 5A electrical power, and control signals via a USB-A serial port. An Arduino with DYNAMIXEL Shield and a U2D2 controller manage the motors (via TTL) and the pneumatic valve. The system provides force and position feedback from the motors, enabling both hard-programmed and adaptive control. A Python/ROS 2 compatible API provides a hardware abstraction layer for high-level commands.

- **Highly Adaptive Fingertips:** Two novel TPU-95A 3D-printed fingertips were developed, inspired by human finger adaptability and Fin-Ray designs.
 - **Fingertip 1 (Sphere Grasp):** Designed for power sphere grasping, this fingertip is symmetrical and consists of a rigid holder to which four TPU elements are attached. These elements are interconnected by a rubber ring, allowing the deformation of one element to influence the orientation of others, thus adapting to tool surfaces in two planes. A rubber friction surface (3M™ Gripping Material TB641) covers the outer elements.
 - **Fingertip 2 (Wrap Grasp):** Developed for power wrap grasping of semi-cylindrical objects, this fingertip features oppositely deployed small Fin-Rays with five elements, connected by a thin (0.3 mm) ellipse and covered with a friction surface. This design ensures maximum contact area and adaptability to object orientation with minimal force.

#### 3.2. Experimental Evaluation

The ARTiS gripper was mounted on a collaborative 6 DoF UR-5e robot arm, positioned at a 45-degree angle to a worktable, for all experiments.

- **Robustness Evaluation:**
 - **Fingertip Performance:** Hysteresis tests were conducted for both fingertips. An experimental setup measured force and displacement as objects were pressed against the fingertips at various orientations (0 to 50 degrees). Each experiment was repeated 30 times to assess dispersion and adaptability.
 - **Holding/Force Study:** The gripper's ability to hold screwdrivers against perpendicular forces was evaluated. Different holding configurations were tested: fingers-only, palm-only, palm+fingers. The LEAP Hand gripper was used as a baseline for comparison. A force sensor measured the force at which the tool slipped. Equations (3-5) were used to analyze translational and rotational slip based on tool length, finger position, palm radius, and friction. An ablation study (Appendix B) quantified palm and finger contributions to slip resistance.

- **Grasping Versatility Evaluation:**
 - **Tool Set:** Nine types of screwdrivers, two hammers, and two drills were used, representing common disassembly tools.
 - **Grasping Process:** Two primary methods were tested: "precise grasping" (tool handle fixed in the jamming palm, other end grasped by Fingertip 1, tool lifted from a holder) and "power grasping" (similar, but using Fingertip 2 with the tool resting on a table).
 - **Success Metrics:** For each tool, 30 trials were conducted to assess success rates for three stages: "Jamming + Lifting," "Grasping + Releasing," and "Grasping + Reorientation/Using + Releasing." Failure was defined as loss of fixation/contact.
 - **Torque Measurement:** Torque generated during screwdriver tightening (3 deg/s rotation) was measured for all nine screwdrivers using a 6 DoF force/torque sensor on the robot's wrist. Key metrics included maximum torque, torque at 10s, mean torque, RMS torque, torque-time integral (AUC), and time to maximum torque (Appendix D). Torque during unscrewing (5 deg/s) of two types of automotive screws was also measured.
 - **Drilling Performance:** Torque fluctuations during drilling plastic components were measured for power and precise holding methods.
 - **Generalization to YCB Objects:** ARTiS's general grasping capability was assessed using 43 objects from the YCB benchmark set (food, kitchen, and tool categories, excluding sharp objects). 30 grasping attempts were made per object, with success defined as securely grasping and lifting without dropping or unstable slipping.

- **Task Versatility Evaluation (Human-Robot Collaboration):**
 - **Workpiece Fixation:** A human study with 12 participants evaluated ARTiS's "teaching mode" for fixing a car air-conditioning unit (workpiece) on a table. The human operator manually configured the gripper's position, finger orientation, and activated palm/finger clamping. This configuration was recorded and then autonomously replayed.
 - **Task Performance:** Participants performed an unscrewing task (three screws) using two methods: M1 (human holds workpiece with one hand, uses screwdriver with the other) and M2 (robot fixes workpiece, human uses both hands for unscrewing). Task completion times were measured.
 - **Statistical Analysis:** Paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare task times between M1 and M2, including an 8s setup time for M2.
 - **Qualitative Demonstrations:** The paper qualitatively described the use of ARTiS for power wrap holding to perform tasks like hammering nails, hitting screwdrivers to wedge them, and using screwdrivers as levers to break fasteners.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ARTiS demonstrated its capabilities across robustness, grasping versatility, and task versatility.

#### 4.1. Robustness

- **Fingertip Adaptability:** Fingertip 1 showed consistent force absorption with minimal dispersion (below 0.5 N) across 180 tests, maintaining robust performance over repeated interactions. It exhibited a uniform decrease in maximum force (average 22% per 10-degree increment) as object orientation increased, indicating continuous reorientation and adaptability to varied surface angles. Fingertip 2 also showed low force dispersion. While experiencing a rapid force increase at small displacement for specific orientations (0 and 10 degrees) due to contact with the holder, it maintained significant adaptability, with sufficient force (18 N at 10 mm displacement) even at minimal contact areas.
- **Holding/Force Study:** The combination of the active jamming palm and fingers (Palm(on), Finger(on)) provided the highest robustness against perpendicular forces applied to tool tips. ARTiS demonstrated a 5.4 to 7.8 times greater force tolerance compared to the LEAP Hand in two-finger sphere holding. Even in power wrap (three-finger) holding, ARTiS achieved 20.4% to 32.8% more force than the LEAP Hand while retaining tool tip control. An ablation study indicated that the palm contributed over 90% of the resistance to screwdriver slipping, with translational slip being the predominant failure mode. This cooperative approach resulted in a synergistic effect, outperforming the linear sum of isolated palm-only and finger-only capabilities.

#### 4.2. Grasping Versatility

- **Tool-Specific Grasping and Use:**
 - **"Jamming + Lifting"**: Achieved a 97% overall success rate across all tools, with 100% success for most tools. Screwdriver №5, having a smooth, protrusion-free handle, showed a lower 57% success rate due to difficulty in establishing reliable friction with the jamming palm.
 - **"Grasping + Releasing"**: Maintained a 97% overall success rate, again with 100% for most tools and 0% for screwdriver №5, as it slipped from the palm after initial grasping due to insufficient friction.
 - **"Grasping + Reorientation/Using + Releasing"**: The most challenging operation, yielding an overall 68% success rate. Only 5 of 9 screwdrivers, 2 of 2 hammers, and 1 of 2 drills achieved 100% success. Screwdrivers №3, 5, and 8 slipped during reorientation due to smooth handles and insufficient frictional properties with the palm. Drill №1 failed (0% success) due to vibrations causing granule compaction in the palm and subsequent loss of contact, whereas Drill №2 was 100% successful due to lower vibration.
- **Torque Performance (Screwdrivers):**
 - **Tightening:** Screwdriver №9 consistently demonstrated the highest torque performance, with a maximum torque of 4.79 ± 0.13 Nm and a high sustained torque (4.77 ± 0.12 Nm at 10s). It also had the highest mean torque, RMS torque, and Area Under the Curve (AUC), indicating robust and sustained torque transmission. Screwdriver №4 was the second strongest (2.64 ± 0.09 Nm). The geometry and dimensions of the tool handle were observed to significantly affect the transmitted torque.
 - **Unscrewing:** Screw 2 required a higher peak torque (1.71 ± 0.15 Nm) and a longer time to reach it (2.36 ± 0.23 s) compared to Screw 1 (1.33 ± 0.22 Nm, 1.29 ± 0.20 s), indicating varying demands for different fasteners.
 - **Drilling:** Power wrap holding exhibited smaller torque fluctuations and better stability during drilling operations compared to precise holding, attributed to greater contact area with the palm and a shorter force lever.
- **Generalization to YCB Objects:** ARTiS achieved an overall grasping success rate of 95.27% across 43 diverse YCB objects (1290 attempts), demonstrating its broad applicability beyond specialized tools. Performance was highest for tool items (97.95%) and food items (97.41%). Kitchen items, which included objects with challenging geometries (thin, flat, or imbalanced), had a slightly lower success rate of 89.17%. 33 out of 43 objects were grasped with 100% success.

#### 4.3. Task Versatility

- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** A human study with 12 participants showed that using ARTiS for workpiece fixation (Method 2, M2) significantly reduced the total task completion time for unscrewing three screws by an average of 5.89 seconds (13.77%) compared to manual holding (Method 1, M1). This reduction was statistically significant (t(11) = 4.02, p = 0.0020, Cohen’s dz = 1.16). While M2 introduced an initial 8-second setup time, the break-even point for time savings was found to be approximately 1.73 screws, meaning M2 became advantageous for tasks involving two or more screws.
- **Advanced Tool Use:** ARTiS demonstrated its capability for diverse tool uses beyond simple manipulation, including power wrap holding for hammering nails, impacting screwdrivers to wedge them, and using screwdrivers as levers to break composite fasteners. This highlights the gripper's potential for various structural component destruction tasks common in disassembly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ARTiS gripper represents an advancement in robotic manipulation by addressing the multifaceted challenge of robustly grasping, fixing, reorienting, and actively using hand tools in dynamic environments, particularly within disassembly applications.

The key significance and potential impacts include:
- **Enhanced Robotic Tool Manipulation:** ARTiS's novel palm-finger cooperative architecture, combining an adaptive jamming palm, 3D-adaptive fingertips, and active finger reconfiguration, provides a mechanically simple yet functionally versatile approach to tool handling. This moves beyond basic pick-and-place operations to enable a wider range of functional tool use by robots.
- **Improved Robustness and Adaptability:** The design demonstrates high robustness through consistent force absorption and adaptability to varied tool shapes and orientations, which is crucial for unpredictable disassembly tasks. The ability to generate and sustain higher torques compared to baseline grippers highlights its potential for demanding applications.
- **Broadened Application Scope for Automation:** By successfully manipulating a range of common hand tools (screwdrivers, hammers, drills) and demonstrating generalization across diverse YCB objects, ARTiS shows potential for widespread application in automated industrial settings beyond its initial focus on disassembly. This includes manufacturing, maintenance, and recycling lines where object and tool variability is high.
- **Facilitating Human-Robot Collaboration:** The demonstrated "teaching mode" and the quantifiable time savings in human-robot collaborative tasks indicate significant potential for improving efficiency and safety in environments where humans and robots work together. Robots equipped with ARTiS could serve as reliable assistants, holding workpieces or tools, thereby freeing human hands for more dexterous or complex operations.
- **Foundation for Future Robotic Learning:** The teaching mode also provides a mechanism for collecting human demonstrations, which can serve as valuable data for imitation learning and further development of autonomous, adaptive tool-use behaviors in robots.
- **Addressing Key Disassembly Challenges:** The paper identifies and partially addresses critical issues in robotic disassembly, such as handling tools with varying geometries, maintaining contact during operation, and applying necessary forces/torques. The specific insights into failure modes (e.g., tool slippage due to handle geometry or vibrations from power tools) provide a clear roadmap for future improvements.

While the current design faces limitations (e.g., susceptibility to tool oscillations, physical dimensions), the research provides a robust foundation for next-generation adaptive grippers. Future work aims to mitigate these limitations through material innovation, structural optimization, and the integration of advanced control strategies like robot learning, paving the way for more capable and widely deployable robotic systems in complex manipulation tasks.
