# Playable Environments: Video Manipulation in Space and Time

> [!summary] Summary
> Playable Environments introduces a framework for interactively generating and manipulating 3D videos from a single image, enabling users to control objects, actions, and camera viewpoints. This system integrates 3D scene understanding with multi-object and deformable object handling, achieving improved action correlation and video quality over previous methods.

## Problem

- Prior novel view synthesis methods for dynamic scenes typically lack intuitive user interaction for manipulating scene elements or actions.
- Existing video synthesis approaches often lack explicit camera control, comprehensive multi-object support, and robust 3D environment understanding.
- Previous playable video generation methods were limited to single controllable objects, constrained camera movement, and uniform object appearances.

## Method

- A compositional Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) architecture is developed, extended to model multiple, deformable objects with varying appearances from monocular videos.
- An action module learns discrete actions in an unsupervised manner, and a dynamics network (LSTM) predicts future object states based on user-specified actions and current state.
- A two-phase training procedure optimizes the synthesis module for image reconstruction and the action module using a multi-component loss including a novel 
-MSE and a temporal discriminator to enhance dynamic realism.

## Results

- The method substantially improved action correlation on the Static Tennis dataset, achieving a 
-MSE of **32.8%** and a 
-Acc of **68.1%**, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines.
- Achieved superior video quality on challenging datasets (Tennis, Minecraft), demonstrating a significantly lower Missing Detection Rate (**4.84%** on Tennis) and improved LPIPS/FVD scores.
- Successfully synthesized scenes from novel camera perspectives (LPIPS 0.242, FID 29.2 on Minecraft Camera dataset) and maintained robustness against real-world input noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A compositional NeRF design, integrating object-specific MLPs, a ray bending network for deformation, and style embedding layers, effectively handles multi-object, deformable scenes with diverse appearances.
> - Learning camera-relative object movements within the action dynamics network provides intuitive user control over objects, allowing for flexible manipulation akin to video game mechanics.
> - A convolutional feature renderer processes NeRF-generated feature maps to reconstruct sharp images, enhancing robustness to inaccuracies in estimated camera parameters and object localizations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of several computer vision and graphics domains, primarily novel view synthesis and video generation. The broader objective in these fields is to reconstruct, manipulate, and generate visual content, often with a focus on realism and interactivity.

Prior work in **novel view synthesis**, particularly methods based on volumetric rendering like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), has demonstrated capability in rendering novel views of static scenes. Some extensions have addressed moving or articulated objects. However, these methods typically face challenges in handling dynamic environments and generally do not support intuitive user interaction for manipulating scene elements or actions.

**Video synthesis methods** aim to generate future frames, animate existing footage, or manipulate video content. These approaches often condition generation on previous frames, other videos, or explicit pose information. While some methods incorporate action labels for generation, they often require supervised action training and typically lack explicit camera control or comprehensive multi-object support. Consequently, the interactivity offered by these methods is often limited, as they may not fully account for the 3D nature of the environment.

A related area is **Playable Video Generation (PVG)**, which focuses on learning discrete action labels in an unsupervised manner to condition video generation. While methods like CADDY advanced this concept, they typically assume a single controllable object, limited camera movement, and uniform object appearances.

The presented work, "Playable Environments," aims to encapsulate and extend these existing representations by addressing several key limitations. It introduces a framework that integrates 3D scene understanding, multi-object manipulation, deformable object handling, varying appearances, camera control, and unsupervised action learning into a unified interactive system. This positions the work as an evolution from static or passively generated content towards dynamic, user-controlled 3D environments, akin to a video game experience derived from real-world footage.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce "Playable Environments" (PEs) – a novel representation for interactive video generation and manipulation across space and time. This framework aims to enable users to interactively generate and modify videos by controlling objects in 3D, specifying desired actions, and manipulating the camera viewpoint, all from a single initial image at inference time.

The motivation for developing Playable Environments stems from the aspiration to allow users to interact with observed video footage in a manner analogous to playing a video game. This involves addressing several detailed capabilities and challenges:

- **Interactive Manipulation:** The core motivation is to move beyond passive video consumption or limited editing by allowing users to change actions of subjects, alter environmental styles, or modify camera trajectories in real-time.
- **3D Reconstruction:** To facilitate such interaction, the environment's geometry and style, including that of players and the field, must be reconstructed in 3D.
- **Action Understanding and Anticipation:** The system needs to comprehend observed actions and predict the outcomes of future actions to enable meaningful user control.
- **Intuitive Interaction:** The framework must provide a simple and intuitive interface, offering an experience similar to video game play, where users specify discrete actions.
- **Overcoming Prior Limitations:** Existing methods typically fall short in several areas that Playable Environments aim to address:
 - **Lack of User Interaction:** Novel view synthesis methods, even those handling moving objects, generally do not allow active user manipulation.
 - **Limited 3D Awareness:** Many video synthesis methods lack explicit camera control and comprehensive multi-object support, limiting interactivity due to insufficient understanding of the 3D environment.
 - **Single Object Constraints:** Previous playable video generation approaches often assume a single controllable object.
 - **Static Camera/Appearance:** Prior methods may struggle with significant camera movements or diverse object appearances within a scene.

To achieve these goals, the proposed method is designed to exhibit six core characteristics:
1. **Playability (〈1〉):** Enabling user control over video generation via discrete actions.
2. **Camera control (〈2〉):** Explicit control over the camera pose at test time.
3. **Multi-object (〈3〉):** Explicit modeling of multiple objects within the scene.
4. **Deformable objects (〈4〉):** Capability to handle deformable objects, such as human bodies.
5. **Appearance changes (〈5〉):** Ability to manage objects whose appearance varies across the training set.
6. **Robustness (〈6〉):** Resilience to imprecise inputs, specifically estimated camera parameters and 2D object locations, without requiring ground truth.

Ultimately, the motivation is to unlock creative applications such as 3D- and action-aware video editing, camera trajectory manipulation, altering action sequences, modifying agents and their styles, and continuing videos beyond their original footage.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for Playable Environments employs an encoder-decoder architecture, designed to incorporate the six core characteristics outlined previously. It operates on monocular videos, requiring only estimated camera parameters and 2D object locations (obtained from an off-the-shelf object detector) as input during training. Actions are learned in an unsupervised manner.

The system comprises two main components: a **synthesis module** and an **action module**.

**A. Environment State Representation:**
The environment is compositionally decomposed into a predefined set of objects, categorized as static (e.g., background) or playable (e.g., humans). The environment state for each object *i* at time *t* is defined as `s_i_t = (x_i_t, w_i_t, π_i_t)`, where `x_i_t` is the object's 3D position, `w_i_t` is a style descriptor, and `π_i_t` is the object's non-rigid pose descriptor. An encoder network extracts `w_i_t` and `π_i_t` for each object from image crops defined by its bounding box. For static objects, `x_i_t` is assumed fixed; for playable objects, `x_i_t` is approximated by projecting the bounding box's lower midpoint onto a ground plane.

**B. Synthesis Module:**
The synthesis module is a decoder network responsible for reconstructing the input image from the camera pose and object states. It extends Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) with several modifications:

- **Camera Control (〈2〉):** A NeRF-based architecture serves as the foundation, allowing explicit camera manipulation. Instead of directly predicting pixel colors, the NeRF generates feature maps, which are then processed by a convolutional image generator.
- **Multi-object (〈3〉):** Each object (static or playable) is modeled with a separate, object-specific Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) feature field `V`. This field is bounded by a volume `β` centered at the object's `x_t`. Features and opacities are sampled along camera rays intersecting these volumes.
- **Deformable Objects (〈4〉):** For playable (deformable) objects, a ray bending network `B` (an MLP) is introduced. Given an object's pose descriptor `π_t` and a point `x_p` on a ray, `B` regresses a corresponding position `˜x_p` on a "bent" ray (`˜x_p = x_p + B(x_p, π_t)`). This allows `V` to encode a canonical representation while `B` handles transformations to the deformed space.
- **Appearance Changes (〈5〉):** To accommodate varying object appearances, a style embedding layer, inspired by AdaIN, is integrated into `V`. A style code `w_t` modulates hidden features `h_t` (`˜h_t = γ(w_t)h_t + β(w_t)`), specifically within the feature prediction branch of `V`, preserving geometry independence.
- **Robustness (〈6〉):** A convolutional network `F` acts as a feature renderer. It takes the NeRF-generated feature maps (which can be at a lower resolution than the target image) and reconstructs the final image. This enables `F` to model cross-pixel relationships, compensating for inaccuracies from input noise (e.g., estimated camera parameters, object localizations) and reducing blur, while also allowing for reduced memory consumption by sampling fewer rays.

**C. Action Module:**
The action module enables playability (〈1〉) by learning the action space for each playable object. It consists of an action network and a dynamics network.

- **Action Network (A):** For two consecutive environment states (`s_t`, `s_t+1`), `A` infers a discrete action label `a_t ∈ {1..., K}` (where K is a hyper-parameter) and an action variability embedding `v_t`. This learning is unsupervised.
- **Dynamics Network (R):** Implemented as an LSTM, `R` predicts the next environment state `ˆs_t+1` from the current state `s_t`, the action label `a_t`, and action variability `v_t`. Crucially, `R` predicts the object's movement (∆) relative to the camera coordinate system. This ensures that the learned actions are intuitive for a user, akin to video game controls where movement is relative to the viewpoint.

**D. Training Procedure:**
The model is trained in two phases:

1. **Synthesis Module Training:** The encoder and synthesis module are trained first using a combination of a perceptual loss (Johnson et al. using VGG features) and an L2 pixel reconstruction loss between ground truth and reconstructed image patches. To disentangle object style (`w`) from pose (`π`), the order of `w` codes is permuted along the temporal dimension during training, forcing the model to rely on `π` for pose changes.
2. **Action Module Training:** This phase optimizes the action module using a multi-component loss:
 - **Reconstruction Loss (`L_rec`):** An L2 loss between the ground truth state sequence (encoded from input images) and the sequence reconstructed by the action module.
 - **Action Learning Losses:**
 - **Information-theoretic action learning loss (`L_act`):** From, this loss maximizes the mutual information between action probability distributions from input and reconstructed states, encouraging action discovery and consistency.
 - **∆ Mean Squared Error (`L_∆`):** A novel soft version of ∆-MSE. It minimizes the squared distance between an object's actual motion (∆) and the mean motion for its assigned action category. This promotes consistency between discrete actions and their corresponding 3D movements.
 - **Temporal Discriminator (`D`):** A 1D ConvNet discriminator `D` is introduced to improve temporal dynamics. It classifies sequences of environment states as "real" (encoded from input images) or "fake" (reconstructed by the action module). A vanilla GAN loss (`L_G` for the action module, `L_D` for the discriminator) is used to train `D` to distinguish realistic motions and the action module to generate them.

**E. Inference:**
During inference, given an initial frame, the encoder extracts the first environment state. The user then specifies discrete actions for each playable object. The dynamics network `R` generates subsequent states autoregressively (with `v_t` set to 0), which are then rendered into images by the synthesis module, allowing interactive video generation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Playable Environments involved extensive experiments on both newly introduced datasets and existing benchmarks, assessing the six core characteristics (Playability, Camera control, Multi-object, Deformable objects, Appearance changes, Robustness).

**A. Datasets:**
The study introduced two large-scale datasets and utilized a third:
- **Minecraft dataset:** A synthetic video dataset (1 hour) featuring two sparring players with wide camera movements and diverse, deformable player appearances. Used to evaluate characteristics 〈1-5〉.
- **Minecraft Camera dataset:** Synthetic Minecraft sequences where the camera is moved around a starting position, providing ground truth for camera control evaluation 〈2〉.
- **Tennis dataset:** A large-scale real-world dataset (12 hours from 43 broadcast matches) with challenging player poses, high variability in fields and players, and noise in camera estimation and player localization. Used to evaluate all characteristics 〈1-6〉.
- **Static Tennis dataset:** An existing dataset with limited camera movement and a single player of uniform appearance. Used for comparison with previous playable video generation methods under their specific assumptions.

**B. Evaluation Protocol and Metrics:**
Evaluation was separated for the synthesis and action modules.
- **Synthesis Module (〈2-6〉):** Assessed by reconstructing test sequences by encoding and then rendering original frames. Metrics included LPIPS, FID, and FVD for video quality.
- **Action Module (〈1〉):** Assessed by extracting the first frame's state, inferring a sequence of discrete actions, and then reconstructing subsequent frames using these actions. Metrics included:
 - **∆ Mean Squared Error (∆-MSE):** Expected error in predicting object motion from a discrete action.
 - **∆-based Action Accuracy (∆-Acc):** Accuracy of regressing a discrete action from motion.
 - **Average Detection Distance (ADD):** Euclidean distance between bounding box centers of corresponding objects in test and reconstructed frames.
 - **Missing Detection Rate (MDR):** Percentage of ground truth object detections not matched in reconstructed sequences.
 - A user study was also conducted to measure user agreement on learned actions.

**C. Comparison on Playable Video Generation (Static Tennis Dataset):**
The method was compared against state-of-the-art PVG methods (MoCoGAN, SAVP, CADDY) on the Static Tennis dataset. The results indicated:
- **Action Quality:** The proposed method substantially improved ∆-MSE (32.8% vs. 72.2% for CADDY) and ∆-Acc (68.1% vs. 45.5% for CADDY), suggesting a stronger correlation between learned actions and actual player movements.
- **Video Quality:** Reduced LPIPS (0.089 vs. 0.102 for CADDY) and MDR (0.15% vs. 1.01% for CADDY) indicated improved reconstruction quality.
- **User Study:** A user study on action consistency yielded a Fleiss' kappa agreement of 0.444 for the proposed method, compared to 0.353 for the best baseline, indicating better consistency in learned actions.

**D. Comparison with Previous Methods (Tennis and Minecraft Camera Datasets):**
Baselines were constructed from modified versions of CADDY to support multiple objects and camera motion.
- **Playability Evaluation (Tennis Dataset) (〈1〉):**
 - The proposed method demonstrated substantial improvements across action space metrics (∆-MSE, ∆-Acc, ADD, MDR), LPIPS, and FVD.
 - MDR was notably lower (4.84% vs. 11.7% for the best CADDY variant), indicating a better capacity for the model to synthesize both players accurately.
 - Visualization of the learned action space showed diverse discrete actions corresponding to main movement directions.
- **Camera Control Evaluation (Minecraft Camera Dataset) (〈2〉):**
 - The method successfully synthesized scenes from novel camera perspectives (LPIPS 0.242, FID 29.2), outperforming baselines which struggled to generate correct views despite auxiliary inputs.
 - Qualitative results on the Tennis dataset also demonstrated the model's ability to synthesize scenes under novel views and alter the style of fields and players based on target images.

**E. Ablation Studies:**
- **Synthesis Module Ablation (Minecraft Dataset) (〈3-6〉):**
 - Multi-object modeling, deformation (π), and style modulation (w) were all shown to be necessary components for accurate scene synthesis.
 - Using style modulation layers for `w` was more effective than direct concatenation.
 - The Feature Renderer (F) was critical for recovering sharpness and reducing blur caused by calibration and localization errors. A simplified renderer that generated complete frames at a single resolution performed worse, attributed to sparse ray sampling.
- **Action Module Ablation (Minecraft Dataset):**
 - **Temporal Discriminator (D):** Its removal led to an increase in FVD, and qualitative analysis suggested that models without `D` produced less realistic limb movements in players.
 - **L_∆ Loss:** Positively impacted action space metrics, and allowed the model to learn an action space even without the `L_act` loss.
 - **Camera-relative Object Movement (Rel.):** Essential for producing intuitive object movements that are dependent on the current camera orientation. Without it, movements were unnatural.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work "Playable Environments" introduces a novel paradigm for interactive video generation and manipulation, addressing several fundamental limitations of prior research in video synthesis and novel view synthesis. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Truly Interactive Video Content:** The framework moves beyond passive viewing or limited video editing by allowing users to actively control scene elements, camera perspectives, and object behaviors in a 3D-aware manner. This transforms video from a fixed sequence into a dynamic, playable experience, similar to a video game but grounded in real-world footage.
- **Comprehensive Scene Understanding:** By integrating multi-object modeling, handling of deformable objects, and adaptation to diverse appearances, the method demonstrates a more comprehensive understanding and representation of complex, dynamic scenes compared to previous approaches that often focused on static scenes or single, non-deforming objects.
- **Robustness to Real-World Data Challenges:** The design incorporates mechanisms to achieve robustness against common inaccuracies in real-world data, such as estimated camera parameters and object localizations from monocular videos. This practical aspect is crucial for wider applicability.
- **Unsupervised Action Learning:** The ability to learn discrete actions in an unsupervised manner from raw video data reduces the need for costly manual annotations, making the system more scalable and applicable to diverse datasets. The learned actions are also shown to be more intuitive and consistent.
- **Novel Technical Contributions:** The research contributes a new compositional Neural Radiance Field architecture that supports deformable objects and style-based modulation, along with a sophisticated action module that learns camera-relative movements and leverages temporal discriminators for enhanced realism.
- **Catalyst for Future Research:** The introduction of two challenging, large-scale video datasets (Minecraft and Tennis) provides a new benchmark and resources to stimulate further research and development in interactive video generation, 3D scene understanding, and unsupervised learning for dynamic environments.
- **Broad Creative Applications:** The framework enables a range of creative applications, including:
 - **3D- and Action-Aware Video Editing:** Manipulating specific actions of subjects or changing scene geometry.
 - **Dynamic Camera Trajectory Manipulation:** Re-filming a scene from different viewpoints.
 - **Personalized Content Creation:** Changing the style of objects or characters in a video.
 - **Video Continuation:** Extending observed footage with user-defined actions.
 This could impact industries such as entertainment, sports analysis, virtual reality, and content creation tools.

In conclusion, "Playable Environments" represents a step towards creating digital experiences where users can intuitively interact with and re-imagine real-world video content, addressing complex challenges in 3D reconstruction, dynamic scene understanding, and interactive control.
