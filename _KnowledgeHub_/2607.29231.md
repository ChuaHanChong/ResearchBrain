# TacPrint: A Wearable Fingertip Tactile Sensor for Human-to-Robot Contact Reproduction

> [!summary] Summary
> TacPrint is a $50 wearable fingertip tactile sensor whose silicone skin carries 24 hemispherical protrusions aligned one-to-one with 24 capacitive taxels for localized transduction. A real-to-sim-to-real pipeline maps 9-frame capacitive windows to a dense 35x26 contact-depth map via an LSTM encoder and transposed-convolution decoder trained on simulation-generated labels. Depth error reaches 0.085 mm, and tactile compensation lifts human-to-robot replay success from 0% to about 90%.

## Problem

- Human-centric demonstration pipelines capture mostly vision and kinematics, missing the precise local fingertip contact states that govern contact-rich manipulation transfer.
- Existing wearable tactile fingertips fail to jointly deliver compact wearability, rich local contact representation, adaptable human and robot mounting, and low-cost scalable deployment.
- Sparse capacitive arrays yield only coarse contact signals, and vision-tracked fingertip poses omit contact deformation, so replayed grasps lose the needed normal contact.

## Method

- **TacPrint** hardware: ==Mold Star 20T silicone skin== with a 6x4 array of hemispherical protrusions aligned one-to-one to 24 capacitive taxels, Velcro strap mounting, and customizable robot connectors.
- ==Real-to-sim-to-real label generation== reproduces each CNC-guide contact configuration in ==TacFlex== on ==Isaac Gym/Flex== with a ==Neo-Hookean elastomer==, rasterizing deformation into 35x26 depth labels.
- Temporal learning maps a 9-frame 24-channel window through a ==256-unit LSTM encoder== and ==transposed-convolution spatial decoder==, trained with a ==foreground-weighted mixed L1/L2 depth loss==.

## Results

- Against simulation labels the model reached contact-region RMSE **0.223 mm**, weighted-centroid error **1.213 pixels**, and region IoU **0.829**; measured-input depth error was **0.085 mm**.
- Tactile-guided normal compensation raised replay success from **0%** to **95%**, **100%**, and **80%** for apple, mango, and peach (**91.67%** overall) and **90%** for whiteboard wiping.
- Closed-loop grasping succeeded in **87.5%** of trials overall and **85%** at edge contacts with dense-depth feedback, versus **67.5%** and **45%** for raw-taxel and **37.5%** and **20%** for contact-only.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aligning skin microstructure one-to-one with sparse taxels turns a 24-channel sensor into a dense-depth estimator, trading physical structure for missing electrode resolution.
> - Dense contact representations improve closed-loop reliability through better stopping decisions rather than fewer corrections, and the gain concentrates at sensing-boundary conditions.
> - Simulation labels validated only at configuration level (center depth, contact location) cannot certify pixel-level deformation fidelity, so boundary and fine shape claims stay unsupported.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The acquisition of high-quality data is fundamental for advancing intelligent robotic manipulation, particularly for learning and transferring skills. Current trends in data collection for robotics include teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching, motion-capture-based approaches, and human-centric paradigms. Human-centric data collection is gaining prominence for its capacity to capture natural and efficient manipulation behaviors, especially in dexterous, contact-rich tasks.

However, existing human-centric pipelines primarily rely on visual and kinematic information. They often lack the precise tactile feedback necessary to capture fine-grained local contact states, which are critical for robust manipulation. To address this, various tactile sensors, including vision-based and capacitive systems, have been developed to recover contact geometry and local interaction patterns. While these systems demonstrate the ability to capture detailed contact information like shape, deformation, and depth, integrating them into a wearable form factor suitable for natural human demonstrations remains a challenge.

Previous efforts in wearable tactile fingertips have explored devices such as ThimbleSense, FingerTac, and TacCap. These studies indicate the feasibility of acquiring rich contact information in a wearable manner. However, current solutions often face difficulties in simultaneously providing a compact and wearable design, an informative local contact representation, adaptable mounting for both human fingertips and robotic hands, and low-cost scalable deployment. The research presented in this paper, TacPrint, aims to bridge this gap by developing a wearable fingertip tactile sensor that balances sensing richness with practical usability and cost-effectiveness for human-to-robot skill transfer. It seeks to provide a dense contact-depth map from sparse capacitive measurements in a compact, adaptable, and low-cost design.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this work stems from the challenge of seamlessly integrating low-cost, scalable tactile sensing systems into human-centric data collection paradigms. Such integration is essential for capturing fine-grained fingertip interactions without disrupting natural human operation, thereby enhancing the reliability of human-to-robot skill transfer in contact-rich tasks. Current human-centric approaches often lack precise tactile acquisition, leading to inconsistencies when transferring skills to robots.

The key objectives of this study are structured into three main areas:

1. **Development of a Wearable Fingertip Tactile Sensor:** To design and construct a compact, low-cost wearable fingertip sensor, named TacPrint. This sensor is intended to feature adjustable mounting for human fingers, customizable connectors for various robotic hands, and a novel internal structure (taxel-aligned silicone protrusions) to enable localized and structured contact transduction. The goal is to provide a practical and accessible tool for human-centric data collection.

2. **Creation and Evaluation of a Tactile-to-Depth Learning Pipeline:** To develop a computational pipeline that can estimate a dense 35 × 26 contact-depth map from the sparse 24-channel capacitive signals generated by TacPrint. This pipeline is to be based on a real-to-sim-to-real approach, utilizing simulation-generated labels for training. A detailed evaluation of the pipeline's ability to consistently predict depth and contact location under controlled physical configurations is also a core objective.

3. **Demonstration of Task-Level Utility in Robotic Applications:** To validate the practical utility of the reconstructed contact information in two downstream robotic applications:
 - **Tactile-guided human-to-robot replay:** To demonstrate that tactile compensation, derived from TacPrint's output during human demonstrations, can improve the success rates of robot skill replay in contact-rich manipulation tasks (e.g., grasping and wiping), addressing the contact mismatch often encountered in purely vision-based replay.
 - **Closed-loop contact-position-aware grasping:** To evaluate whether the estimated dense-depth representation can provide more reliable contact-position feedback for robotic grasp adjustment compared to simpler contact-only sensing or direct raw-taxel localization, particularly for challenging edge-contact conditions.

Overall, the work aims to enhance the fidelity of human-centric data collection for robotic manipulation by providing a practical, high-resolution tactile sensing solution and demonstrating its efficacy in improving robot skill learning and execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves the design and fabrication of the TacPrint sensor, the development of a learning pipeline to interpret its signals, and a series of experimental evaluations to assess its performance and utility.

**A. Structure Design**
TacPrint is composed of three main parts: a silicone skin, a 24-channel capacitive sensing unit, and a 3D-printed support.
- **Silicone Skin:** Fabricated from Mold Star 20T silicone (Shore hardness 20A), the skin features a 6 × 4 array of 24 hemispherical protrusions on its inner surface. These protrusions, each with a radius of 0.7 mm and specific row/column spacings (1.9 mm and 2.5 mm, respectively), are aligned one-to-one with the capacitive taxels. The silicone was cast from a 3D-printed mold.
- **Capacitive Sensing Unit:** A standard 24-channel capacitive sensing unit (Model 2 from SNIOW Technology) is used to measure changes in capacitance corresponding to deformation of the silicone skin.
- **3D-Printed Support:** This component provides structural integrity and holds the sensing unit and silicone skin in place.
- **Wearability and Adaptability:** An adjustable Velcro strap allows the sensor to be worn on human fingertips (tested range: 14–18 mm width, 45–58 mm circumference). Customizable connectors enable installation on various robotic hands (e.g., LinkerHand O20, Tesollo DG-2F-M, Tesollo DG-5F-S), often with an additional rear Velcro strap for retention. The device adds 7.8 mm to fingertip thickness and has an approximate cost of $50.

**B. Tactile-to-Depth Learning Pipeline**
The pipeline is designed to map short temporal sequences of 24-channel capacitive measurements (X_t) to a 35 × 26 depth map (Y_hat_t). It consists of three main stages:
1. **Tactile Acquisition:** TacPrint is mounted on an XYZ Computer Numerical Control (CNC) linear guide. The sensor's active area is registered to the guide's coordinate frame. Rigid indenters of known geometries and orientations are used to apply controlled indentations. The guide's displacement defines the nominal indentation depth, and its in-plane coordinates define the nominal contact location. During each episode, the guide executes an approach, pressing, holding, and release sequence, and synchronized 24-channel capacitive measurements are recorded.
2. **Simulation-Generated Depth Labels:** To obtain ground-truth depth labels, the nominal contact configurations (indenter geometry, pose, contact location, and indentation depth) from physical experiments are reproduced in a physics simulation environment. This pipeline leverages TacFlex with Isaac Gym/Flex as the physics backend. The TacPrint elastomer is modeled as a Neo-Hookean material (E = 0.30 MPa, ν = 0.47) with an indenter–elastomer friction coefficient of 0.20. The simulated deformation field is then rasterized into a 35 × 26 depth map. The consistency of these simulation-generated labels was evaluated against controlled physical contacts, showing a mean contact-center depth error of 0.110 ± 0.016 mm and mean contact-position errors between 0.265 mm and 0.338 mm depending on the segmentation threshold (0.4 mm was chosen as representative). Before training, depth maps are clipped to a valid indentation range (0-3 mm) and normalized.
3. **Temporal Learning:** An LSTM-based encoder-decoder architecture processes the tactile sequences. A single-layer unidirectional LSTM (hidden dimension 256) encodes a fixed-length temporal window (T=9 frames) of 24-channel capacitive signals. The encoded feature is then fed into a spatial decoder, which comprises a linear projection, reshaping, two transposed-convolution layers, and convolutional refinement, to predict the 35 × 26 depth map. The network is trained using a foreground-weighted combination of squared and absolute errors, with specific parameters (λ=0.5, δ=0.3, γ=2). Training is performed for 50 epochs using the AdamW optimizer.

**C. Experiments**
Three experiments were conducted to evaluate TacPrint:
1. **Exp. 1: Contact Characterization:** This experiment evaluated the depth-map estimation performance. Predictions on a test set were compared against simulation-generated labels using metrics such as pixel-wise RMSE, peak-depth error, contact-region RMSE, depth-weighted centroid error, and Intersection over Union (IoU). A complementary physical evaluation compared network predictions (predicted depth at guide-calibrated contact center and centroid of the predicted region) with nominal indentation depths and guide-calibrated contact centers across 40 controlled indentation trials.
2. **Exp. 2: Human-Centric Data Collection and Robot Replay:** This experiment assessed the utility of tactile information in human-to-robot skill transfer. TacPrint was worn on an operator's thumb, index, and middle fingers to demonstrate grasping (apple, mango, peach) and wiping (eraser on a whiteboard). Visually tracked fingertip poses (using AprilTags) were used for direct robot replay. This was compared with tactile-compensated replay, where the robot's fingertip targets were adjusted along the normal direction based on TacPrint's estimated depth, using an empirically selected gain (α). Task success rate was the primary metric.
3. **Exp. 3: Contact-Position-Aware Grasping:** This experiment investigated the contribution of the estimated dense-depth representation to closed-loop robotic grasp adjustment. A robotic arm (RealMan RM65-B) equipped with a gripper (Tesollo DG-2F-M) and two TacPrint sensors attempted to grasp an orange model placed at eight different lateral positions. Three feedback strategies were compared: "Contact only" (binary contact detection, no adjustment), "Raw-taxel control" (adjusts based on the centroid of raw taxel responses), and "Dense-depth control" (adjusts based on the centroid of the thresholded estimated depth map). The number of lateral adjustments and grasp success rate were analyzed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded detailed findings across the three experimental categories, demonstrating TacPrint's capabilities in depth-map estimation and its utility in robotic manipulation tasks.

**A. Exp. 1: Depth-Map Estimation Performance**
- **Qualitative Agreement:** The predicted depth maps showed general agreement with simulation-generated labels in terms of contact position and depth distribution, though discrepancies were observed near contact boundaries and local peaks (Fig. 6). Continuous contact demonstrations on a water bottle also illustrated the sensor's ability to capture characteristic contact patterns (Fig. 7).
- **Quantitative Agreement (against simulation-generated labels, Table I):**
 - **Depth Errors:** Pixel-wise Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) was 0.101 ± 0.076 mm. The contact-region RMSE, focusing on areas where contact was simulated, was 0.223 ± 0.161 mm. Peak depth error, representing the maximum indentation, was 0.188 ± 0.258 mm.
 - **Localization Errors:** The depth-weighted centroid error between predicted and simulated contact centers was 1.213 ± 2.379 pixels.
 - **Contact Region Overlap:** The Intersection over Union (IoU) between predicted and simulated contact regions was 0.829 ± 0.169.
- **Quantitative Agreement (against physical measurements):**
 - For the 40 controlled indentation trials, the mean absolute error of the network-predicted depth evaluated at the guide-calibrated contact center was 0.085 ± 0.057 mm.
 - For 37 trials where the reference contact regions were not truncated by the sensing boundary, the mean contact-position error was 0.250 ± 0.208 mm. (Across all 40 trials, including truncated ones, the error was 0.285 ± 0.240 mm).

**B. Exp. 2: Tactile-Informed Robot Replay**
This experiment evaluated whether TacPrint-derived tactile compensation could improve robot replay in contact-rich tasks.
- **Fruit Grasping:** In grasping an apple, mango, and peach, direct replay (vision-only) resulted in a 0% success rate, failing to achieve sufficient normal contact for stable grasping. With tactile-guided normal compensation (gain vector α_grasp = [0.005, 0.005, 0.005] m/mm), success rates increased to 95% for the apple, 100% for the mango, and 80% for the peach, resulting in an overall success rate of 91.67% (Fig. 8).
- **Whiteboard Wiping:** For wiping an eraser, direct replay also yielded a 0% success rate, as the robot failed to maintain a stable grasp. Tactile compensation (gain vector α_wipe = [0.004, 0.010, 0.010] m/mm) increased the success rate to 90%, allowing the robot to stably grasp the eraser and complete two wiping passes (Fig. 9).

**C. Exp. 3: Contact-Position-Aware Grasping**
This experiment compared three feedback strategies for grasping an orange model over 40 trials (5 repetitions at 8 positions), including 20 edge-contact trials.
- **Overall Grasp Success Rates (40 trials):**
 - Contact-only feedback: 37.5% (15/40 successful trials).
 - Raw-taxel control: 67.5% (27/40 successful trials).
 - Dense-depth control: 87.5% (35/40 successful trials).
- **Edge-Contact Grasp Success Rates (20 trials):**
 - Contact-only feedback: 20% (4/20 successful trials).
 - Raw-taxel control: 45% (9/20 successful trials).
 - Dense-depth control: 85% (17/20 successful trials).
- **Lateral Adjustments (among successful trials):**
 - Raw-taxel feedback: 1.07 ± 1.36 adjustments.
 - Dense-depth feedback: 2.14 ± 1.24 adjustments.
 - Under edge-contact conditions: Raw-taxel required 2.56 ± 1.01 adjustments, while dense-depth required 2.71 ± 1.21 adjustments.
- **Analysis:** Dense-depth feedback significantly improved success rates, particularly for challenging edge-contact conditions, by enabling more reliable adjustment and stopping decisions, even if it sometimes required more corrective motions than raw-taxel feedback in successful trials (Fig. 10, Fig. 11). Raw-taxel feedback, despite its lower adjustment count, exhibited a lower success rate, suggesting its centroid estimation could be biased and lead to premature acceptance of unstable grasps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents TacPrint, a wearable fingertip tactile sensor, and an associated learning pipeline, which collectively offer several significant contributions and potential impacts on robotic manipulation and human-robot interaction.

The development of TacPrint addresses a recognized gap in human-centric data collection for robotics: the need for a low-cost, compact, and scalable tactile sensing system that provides fine-grained contact information without impeding natural human movements. Its design, incorporating taxel-aligned silicone protrusions, adjustable human mounting, and customizable robotic connectors, offers a practical solution for both human demonstrators and robotic platforms. The estimated cost of approximately $50 per sensor indicates potential for widespread adoption and scaling of data collection efforts.

The real-to-sim-to-real learning pipeline is a key innovation, enabling the conversion of sparse 24-channel capacitive signals into a dense 35 × 26 contact-depth map. The reported accuracy metrics, including a contact-region RMSE of 0.223 ± 0.161 mm and a mean contact-position error of 0.250 ± 0.208 mm against physical configurations, suggest that the system can reliably reconstruct local indentation and contact location. This detailed contact representation is valuable for understanding complex hand-object interactions.

The experimental results highlight the substantial impact of TacPrint's tactile feedback on robotic task performance. In human-to-robot replay, tactile-guided normal compensation increased grasping success rates from 0% to 91.67% and wiping success rates from 0% to 90%. This demonstrates that explicit tactile information can effectively mitigate contact mismatches inherent in purely vision-based robot teleoperation or imitation learning, leading to more robust and successful skill transfer.

Furthermore, the closed-loop contact-position-aware grasping experiments underscore the advantages of the dense-depth representation. Dense-depth feedback achieved significantly higher grasping success rates (87.5% overall and 85% for edge contacts) compared to raw-taxel feedback (67.5% overall and 45% for edge contacts). This indicates that the detailed spatial contact information derived from TacPrint facilitates more reliable contact-position estimation and more effective real-time grasp adjustments, particularly in challenging edge-contact scenarios where precise localization is critical for stability. The dense-depth map enables more informed decision-making regarding when to initiate or terminate corrective motions, ultimately leading to higher success rates.

Despite certain limitations, such as the 24-channel input limiting the detail of boundary and shape reconstruction and the validation focusing primarily on center depth and position rather than full pixel-level deformation, TacPrint represents a step towards integrating sophisticated tactile sensing into practical robotic applications. Its potential impact lies in enabling more capable and intelligent robotic manipulation systems through improved human-centric data collection and more reliable closed-loop control in contact-rich tasks. Future work aims to enhance prediction accuracy, robustness across various contact objects and wearing conditions, and overall deployment convenience.
