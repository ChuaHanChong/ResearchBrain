# TIES-Merging: Resolving Interference When Merging Models

> [!summary] Summary
> TIES-MERGING introduces a three-step approach to combine multiple fine-tuned models into a single multitask model by explicitly resolving parameter interference stemming from redundant changes and sign disagreements. This method consistently outperforms existing merging techniques across NLP and vision tasks, demonstrating average performance improvements up to 3.6% on T5-large models and superior out-of-domain generalization.

## Problem

- Existing model merging methods often suffer from performance degradation due to parameter interference between different fine-tuned models.
- Redundant, low-magnitude parameter changes introduced during fine-tuning interfere with more influential changes from other models when merged.
- Parameter values often have conflicting signs across different fine-tuned models, leading to cancellation or shrinkage when simply averaged.

## Method

- ==TIES-MERGING (TRIM, ELECT, MERGE)== is a three-step method that operates on =="task vectors"==, which are the differences between fine-tuned and initial pre-trained model parameters.
- ==TRIM== removes redundant parameter changes by setting low-magnitude values in each task vector to zero, retaining only the top-k% influential parameters.
- ==ELECT== resolves sign disagreements for each parameter by electing a dominant sign based on the greater total magnitude across all trimmed task vectors, and ==DISJOINT MERGE== then averages only the parameter values whose signs align with the elected sign.

## Results

- TIES-MERGING consistently outperformed baselines like Simple Averaging and Task Arithmetic across NLP (T5) and Vision (ViT) modalities, model sizes, and fine-tuning settings, with average improvements up to **3.6%** on T5-large and **2.5%** on PEFT models.
- It demonstrated superior out-of-domain generalization, outperforming the strongest baseline by **1.0%** (T5-base) and **4.4%** (T5-Large) on held-out tasks.
- The method exhibited more graceful performance degradation when merging a larger number of tasks, served as a better initialization strategy for subsequent fine-tuning, and improved robustness when merging multiple checkpoints from the same task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parameter interference in model merging primarily stems from redundant low-magnitude changes and sign disagreements across task-specific fine-tuned models.
> - Explicitly trimming redundant parameters and resolving sign conflicts can significantly improve merged model performance by preserving influential parameter magnitudes.
> - The correct sign direction for a parameter is crucial, as demonstrated by catastrophic performance drops when signs are flipped for high-magnitude parameters, and accurately estimating the optimal sign vector is key to bridging the performance gap with full multitask training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape: Model Merging and Transfer Learning

The work presented in "TIES-MERGING" is situated within the rapidly evolving landscape of **transfer learning** and **model merging** in deep learning. Transfer learning, particularly fine-tuning large pre-trained models (PTMs) like T5 for NLP or ViT for vision, has become the dominant paradigm for achieving state-of-the-art performance on various downstream tasks. This approach leverages the vast knowledge encoded in models trained on massive datasets, significantly improving performance, accelerating convergence, and enhancing sample efficiency on task-specific data.

However, the proliferation of fine-tuned models—often one per specific task—leads to practical drawbacks: increased storage and deployment costs, and the inability of these isolated models to benefit from shared knowledge or generalize across related tasks. **Multitask learning (MTL)** offers a solution by training a single model on multiple tasks simultaneously. While effective, MTL requires costly, resource-intensive training, simultaneous access to all task datasets, and complex strategies for dataset mixing, making it less flexible than fine-tuning.

Model merging emerges as an appealing alternative. It aims to combine the capabilities of multiple task-specific fine-tuned models into a single, compact multitask model *without requiring additional training*. This is achieved by manipulating the parameters (weights) of the fine-tuned models. Prior work in model merging has explored various techniques, including simple weight averaging (e.g., Model Soups), sophisticated weighted averaging based on parameter importance (e.g., Fisher Merging), or methods addressing permutation invariances (e.g., Git Re-basin). A particularly relevant line of work is **Task Arithmetic**, which frames merging as operations on "task vectors" – the differences between fine-tuned model weights and the initial pre-trained weights.

Despite these advancements, existing merging methods often face a critical challenge: **interference** between the parameters of different models. This interference, if unaddressed, can lead to significant performance degradation in the merged model. TIES-MERGING directly confronts this limitation, aiming to enhance the robustness and effectiveness of model merging by explicitly resolving these interference issues. It builds upon the concept of task vectors, pushing the boundaries of what can be achieved by intelligently combining already fine-tuned models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of TIES-MERGING is to develop a robust and effective method for combining multiple task-specific fine-tuned models into a single multitask model without requiring additional training, specifically by **resolving parameter interference that hinders the performance of existing merging techniques**.

The motivation stems from two critical observations regarding the shortcomings of prior model merging methods:

1. **Interference from Redundant Parameters:** During fine-tuning, many model parameters undergo changes, but a significant portion of these changes are "redundant" – meaning they have a small, non-influential impact on the task's performance. When multiple models are merged, these redundant parameter values can interfere with or obscure the truly "influential" parameters from other models, effectively diluting their impact and leading to performance drops. The authors empirically demonstrate that keeping only a small fraction (e.g., top-20%) of high-magnitude parameters can achieve comparable performance to using all parameters, highlighting the presence of redundancy.
2. **Interference from Sign Disagreement:** A given parameter might be updated in opposite directions (e.g., positive for one task, negative for another) across different fine-tuned models. Simple averaging of such conflicting values can lead to cancellation or shrinkage of the parameter's magnitude, compromising performance on both tasks. The paper shows that such sign conflicts are common, increasing with the number of models being merged, and occur even between different fine-tuned checkpoints for the *same* task.

By explicitly addressing these two sources of interference – redundancy and sign disagreement – TIES-MERGING aims to create a merged model that retains the benefits of all individual fine-tuned models more effectively, narrowing the performance gap between merged models and costly, simultaneously trained multitask models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TIES-MERGING (TRIM, ELECT SIGN & MERGE) is a three-step method designed to mitigate the aforementioned interference issues when merging models based on their task vectors.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Task Vectors

The method operates on **task vectors ($\tau_t$)**, which are defined as the element-wise difference between the parameters of a fine-tuned model ($\theta_{t}^{ft}$) and the initial pre-trained model ($\theta_{init}$). Formally, $\tau_t = \theta_{t}^{ft} - \theta_{init}$. This representation isolates the changes introduced during fine-tuning, allowing the merging process to focus on these task-specific modifications rather than the entire model weights. Each task vector $\tau_t$ can be decomposed into a sign vector ($\gamma_t$) and a magnitude vector ($\mu_t$), where $\tau_t = \gamma_t \odot \mu_t$.

#### 3.2. TRIM: Addressing Redundant Parameters

The first step, **TRIM**, focuses on eliminating the interference caused by redundant parameter changes. For each task vector $\tau_t$, the method identifies parameters with low magnitudes, as these are empirically shown to have minimal impact on performance. It sets the values of these "redundant" parameters to zero (or, equivalently, resets the fine-tuned parameter back to its pre-trained value). This is achieved by keeping only the top-k% of parameters based on their magnitude and setting the rest to zero, resulting in a "trimmed task vector" ($\hat{\tau}_t$). This step prevents small, noisy changes from one model from diluting the significant, influential changes from another when averaged.

#### 3.3. ELECT: Resolving Sign Disagreements

Even after trimming, influential parameters might still have conflicting signs across different task vectors. The second step, **ELECT**, addresses this sign interference. For each parameter across all trimmed task vectors, TIES-MERGING determines a single, aggregate "elected sign" ($\gamma_m$). This is done by summing the magnitudes of all positive changes for that parameter and all negative changes. The elected sign for that parameter is then assigned based on which direction (positive or negative) has the greater total magnitude (or "mass"). Formally, $\gamma_m = \text{sgn}(\sum_{t=1}^{n} \hat{\tau}_t)$. This ensures that parameters aligned with the overall dominant direction are prioritized.

#### 3.4. DISJOINT MERGE: Aggregating Aligned Values

The final step, **DISJOINT MERGE**, combines the parameter values to form the merged task vector ($\tau_m$). For each parameter, it only considers the values from the trimmed task vectors whose signs align with the previously "elected sign" for that parameter. It then computes the mean of these aligned values. Formally, for each parameter $p$, $\tau_p^m = \frac{1}{|A_p|} \sum_{t \in A_p} \hat{\tau}_p^t$, where $A_p$ is the set of tasks whose trimmed parameter $p$ has the same sign as the elected sign $\gamma_p^m$. Parameters from models that conflict with the elected sign or were trimmed to zero are effectively ignored in the averaging process for that specific parameter.

Finally, the merged model's parameters ($\theta_m$) are obtained by scaling the merged task vector ($\tau_m$) by a hyperparameter $\lambda$ and adding it back to the initial pre-trained model: $\theta_m = \theta_{init} + \lambda \ast \tau_m$. The paper highlights that TIES-MERGING can be applied effectively even without a validation set, using fixed hyperparameters (e.g., k=20%, λ=1), making it highly practical.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

TIES-MERGING demonstrates superior performance compared to several existing model merging techniques across a diverse range of experimental settings.

#### 4.1. Comprehensive Performance Evaluation

The method was evaluated on:
- **Modalities:** Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Vision.
- **Model Sizes & Architectures:** T5-base and T5-large (NLP), ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14 (Vision).
- **Fine-tuning Settings:** Full fine-tuning and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), specifically (IA)³ models.
- **Number of Tasks:** Merging multiple tasks into a single multitask model.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** Both with and without the availability of a validation set for tuning.

In **in-domain evaluation** (Table 1), TIES-MERGING consistently outperformed baselines like Simple Averaging, Task Arithmetic, Fisher Merging, and RegMean.
- For PEFT models ((IA)³ on T0-3B), TIES-MERGING showed an average enhancement of 2.5% over the top baseline across 11 tasks (with validation set).
- For fully fine-tuned ViT models (ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14), it achieved average improvements of 1.8% and 1.5% respectively over 8 tasks. Notably, without a validation set, TIES-MERGING still outperformed Task Arithmetic (even with validation) by 2.3% (ViT-B/32) and 1.5% (ViT-L/14).
- For fully fine-tuned NLP models (T5-base and T5-large), it showed improvements of 0.7% and 3.6% respectively over 7 tasks. For T5-large, TIES-MERGING without validation even surpassed all baselines with validation.

#### 4.2. Out-of-Domain Generalization

TIES-MERGING also proved effective for out-of-domain generalization. When T5-base and T5-large models (merged on seven in-domain datasets) were evaluated on six held-out tasks, TIES-MERGING outperformed the strongest baseline by 1.0% (T5-base) and 4.4% (T5-Large), indicating a better ability to generalize to unseen domains.

#### 4.3. Merging Varying Numbers of Tasks

As the number of merged tasks increased, all methods experienced a performance decline. However, TIES-MERGING's performance degraded more gracefully compared to Task Arithmetic and Simple Averaging, suggesting its superior ability to mitigate task interference when combining many models.

#### 4.4. Merging Checkpoints for Robustness and Initialization

- **Merging Checkpoints of the Same Task:** TIES-MERGING also improved robustness when merging multiple checkpoints trained on the *same* task (as in Model Soups). It outperformed ensembling, Fisher Merging, and simple averaging for RTE and MRPC tasks, demonstrating its utility beyond just combining different tasks.
- **Merging Models for Better Initialization:** The merged models produced by TIES-MERGING served as better initializations for subsequent fine-tuning on new tasks. When merging checkpoints from seven GLUE tasks and then fine-tuning on an eighth, TIES-MERGING consistently outperformed other merging methods as an initialization strategy.

#### 4.5. Analysis of Interference Types

The authors provide empirical evidence of the two identified interference types:
- **Redundant Parameter Interference:** When trimming redundant parameters, the magnitudes of influential parameters in the merged model were preserved or even increased, as their influence was no longer diluted by small, noisy values from other models.
- **Sign Interference:** The "ELECT" step, by resolving sign conflicts, significantly helped in preserving the overall parameter magnitudes, especially for parameters with low sign agreement across models.

#### 4.6. Importance of Sign and Oracle Sign

The study highlights the critical role of correct parameter signs. Flipping the signs of high-magnitude parameters (even just 20-30%) led to catastrophic performance drops, while flipping low-magnitude parameters had little impact. This underscores the necessity of the "ELECT" step.

Crucially, an "oracle sign" experiment demonstrated the potential of TIES-MERGING. When the method was provided with the "true" sign vector from a fully multitask-trained model, the performance of the TIES-merged model reached 72% compared to 73.1% for the actual multitask-trained model. This suggests that accurately estimating the optimal sign direction is a key factor in bridging the remaining gap between model merging and full multitask training.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Future Directions

#### 5.1. Significance and Impact

TIES-MERGING represents a significant advancement in the field of model merging, offering a practical and effective solution to a long-standing challenge of interference. Its key contributions and potential impacts include:

- **Enhanced Multitask Model Creation:** By providing a robust method for merging diverse fine-tuned models, TIES-MERGING enables the creation of single multitask models without the prohibitive costs and complexities of training from scratch or extensive multitask training. This facilitates more efficient storage and deployment of AI models in real-world applications.
- **Improved Performance and Generalization:** The consistent outperformance over state-of-the-art baselines across various modalities, model sizes, and fine-tuning settings demonstrates its practical utility. Its superior out-of-domain generalization further enhances the applicability of merged models in diverse and shifting environments.
- **Practicality and Accessibility:** The ability of TIES-MERGING to perform well even without a validation set, using fixed hyperparameters, makes it highly accessible and easy to implement in scenarios where hyperparameter tuning data is scarce.
- **Deeper Understanding of Model Merging:** The detailed analysis of redundant parameters and sign conflicts provides valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of why and how model merging works (or fails). This analytical contribution can guide future research in this area.
- **Bridging the Gap to Multitask Training:** The "oracle sign" experiment is particularly impactful. It suggests that if researchers can devise more effective ways to estimate the optimal sign vector for merged parameters without full multitask training, the performance of merged models could come very close to that of models explicitly trained on all tasks simultaneously, opening new avenues for efficient multitask learning.

#### 5.2. Limitations and Future Work

The authors acknowledge several limitations, which also serve as promising directions for future research:

- **Limited Theoretical Understanding:** The paper, while empirically strong, shares the general limitation of existing merging methods regarding a deep theoretical understanding of weight interpolation, mode connectivity, and the factors governing effective merging.
- **Dependence on Common Initialization and Architecture:** Current merging techniques, including TIES-MERGING, typically rely on all models sharing a common pre-trained initialization and architecture. Extending merging to models with different architectures or initializations remains a challenge.
- **Performance Gap to Simultaneous Multitask Training:** While TIES-MERGING significantly narrows the gap, merged models still generally lag behind models that undergo simultaneous multitask training.
- **Checkpoint Selection:** The paper does not delve into strategies for optimally selecting which checkpoints to merge for specific domain or task goals.
- **Estimating Oracle Sign Vector:** Given the dramatic performance improvement with an oracle sign vector, a crucial future direction is to develop efficient and reliable methods to estimate this vector without requiring full multitask training data. This could involve using limited validation data or novel architectural insights to infer the most beneficial parameter directions for a unified model.

In conclusion, TIES-MERGING offers a simple yet highly effective approach to address fundamental challenges in model merging, providing a significant step towards more efficient and powerful deployment of large pre-trained models.
