# DL-VINS-Factory: A Modular Framework for Learned Visual Front-Ends in Visual-Inertial SLAM

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces DL-VINS-Factory, a modular framework for visual-inertial SLAM that systematically integrates and evaluates diverse learned visual front-ends against handcrafted baselines. The study reveals that optimal front-end performance is conditional on environmental factors and demonstrates real-time viability on embedded hardware, offering insights into their practical utility and trade-offs.

## Problem

- Existing Visual-Inertial SLAM (VI-SLAM) systems primarily rely on handcrafted features, which often degrade under challenging visual conditions.
- The integration of emerging deep learning-based visual features into complete VI-SLAM systems remains fragmented, lacking systematic evaluation under consistent conditions.
- Traditional loop-closure mechanisms are often tightly coupled to descriptor-specific vocabularies, making it difficult to switch or benchmark different learned feature representations.

## Method

- Developed **DL-VINS-Factory**, a modular VI-SLAM framework based on ==VINS-Fusion==, enabling interchangeable integration and direct comparison of learned visual front-ends (e.g., ==ALIKED==, ==RaCo==, ==SuperPoint==, ==XFeat==) and matching paradigms (==Lucas-Kanade==, ==LightGlue==).
- Implemented a front-end-agnostic loop-closure mechanism using ==DINOv2== patch embeddings aggregated with a universal ==VLAD== codebook (==AnyLoc==) for image-level retrieval, decoupled from local feature descriptors.
- Conducted extensive cross-platform evaluation on four public datasets and an embedded NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, benchmarking performance, robustness, and real-time efficiency of various configurations against baselines.

## Results

- On the EuRoC dataset, ALIKED with LightGlue achieved a monocular ATE of **0.146m**, performing optimally among learned setups, while RaCo with LightGlue yielded the lowest monocular ATE (**0.877m**) for aggressive aerial motion in NTU-VIRAL.
- The AnyLoc loop closure module consistently identified **2-7 times** more potential loop candidates than traditional BRIEF, demonstrating its ability to expose more true revisits, though geometric verification remained critical.
- The system achieved real-time operation on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, with total module power consumption ranging from **11.5W** for GFTT to **22.7W** for RaCo with LightGlue, comfortably exceeding camera frame rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - No single learned visual front-end consistently outperforms others; optimal performance is conditional on scene structure, viewpoint variation, and ego-motion characteristics.
> - LightGlue-based matching generally excels in scenarios with significant inter-frame viewpoint changes (e.g., aggressive aerial motion), while optical flow remains competitive or superior in low-parallax or structured environments.
> - Learned visual front-ends can operate in real-time on resource-constrained embedded platforms like the Jetson AGX Orin, but they introduce increased GPU power and memory demands compared to handcrafted features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Visual-Inertial Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (VI-SLAM) systems are integral to mobile robotics due to their sensing capabilities, low cost, and rich information. Historically, VI-SLAM pipelines have relied on handcrafted visual features, such as ORB or BRISK, which offer efficiency but often experience performance degradation under challenging conditions like low texture, motion blur, or illumination variations.

Recent advancements in deep learning have introduced learned feature extractors (e.g., SuperPoint) and matchers (e.g., SuperGlue, LightGlue), which have demonstrated improved correspondence quality by leveraging descriptor similarity and spatial context. These learned features offer the potential for more robust visual front-ends in VI-SLAM. However, their practical integration into complete, tightly coupled VI-SLAM systems remains fragmented. Existing studies often evaluate different learned components in isolation or within varying back-end optimizers, loop-closure strategies, datasets, and hardware platforms. This variability makes it challenging to conduct fair comparisons and isolate the specific impact of the visual front-end on overall system performance. Furthermore, many conventional loop-closure systems are tightly coupled to descriptor-specific Bag-of-Words (BoW) vocabularies, necessitating significant re-engineering when the underlying local feature representation is changed.

This research addresses these challenges by providing a unified, modular framework. It aims to systematically evaluate various learned visual front-ends within a standardized VI-SLAM architecture, thereby contributing to a clearer understanding of their benefits and limitations in real-world applications. By decoupling the front-end from the back-end and introducing a front-end-agnostic loop-closure mechanism, the work facilitates more controlled benchmarking and promotes the practical adoption of emerging learned features in VI-SLAM. It positions itself as a comparative study under consistent conditions, moving beyond isolated evaluations to characterize the real-world utility and trade-offs of learned features for embedded robotics.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the identified fragmentation and lack of systematic evaluation regarding the integration of deep learning-based visual front-ends into complete visual-inertial SLAM (VI-SLAM) systems. While learned features show promise for improved visual matching, their comprehensive impact on tightly coupled VI-SLAM, particularly in diverse and challenging environments, remains insufficiently characterized.

The key objectives of the DL-VINS-Factory framework are:

1. **To develop a modular VI-SLAM framework for interchangeable front-ends:** The authors aimed to create a unified system that allows for the integration and direct comparison of various learned visual front-ends (specifically ALIKED, RaCo, SuperPoint, and XFeat). This framework supports two distinct feature tracking/matching paradigms: traditional Lucas–Kanade (LK) optical-flow tracking and modern LightGlue descriptor matching. All configurations are designed to operate within a common, tightly coupled sliding-window optimization back-end, adapted from VINS-Fusion.
2. **To integrate a front-end-agnostic loop-closure mechanism:** A significant objective was to overcome the limitation of descriptor-specific Bag-of-Words (BoW) vocabularies used in many traditional SLAM systems. The authors sought to implement a loop-closure module that performs image-level retrieval using DINOv2 patch embeddings aggregated with a universal VLAD codebook (AnyLoc). This approach eliminates the need to rebuild vocabularies when switching feature extractors, thereby facilitating easier benchmarking and adoption of new learned features. Geometric verification for loop closure was designed to reuse the active local feature descriptors through brute-force matching, fundamental-matrix RANSAC, and Perspective-n-Point RANSAC.
3. **To conduct a systematic and extensive evaluation:** A core objective was to rigorously benchmark the performance of the integrated learned front-ends against handcrafted baselines (GFTT+LK, SIFT+LK/LG) under identical experimental conditions. This evaluation spans four public datasets, encompassing a variety of challenging scenarios including indoor environments, unstructured outdoor settings, aggressive aerial motion, and visually degraded conditions (e.g., low light, smoke, over-exposure). The system was to be tested on both a high-performance workstation and a resource-constrained embedded platform (NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin) to assess real-time viability and power consumption.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aimed to provide isolated insights into the true impact of different learned visual front-ends on VI-SLAM performance, robustness, and real-time capability, thereby guiding future research and practical deployment decisions in mobile robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DL-VINS-Factory framework is primarily built upon VINS-Fusion, a widely used optimization-based visual-inertial state estimator. The authors re-implemented and updated parts of VINS-Fusion to support modern compiler threading, Ceres solver versions, numerical bounds, and ROS wrappers. The core of the methodology lies in its modular design, which allows for the interchangeable integration of learned visual front-ends and a unified loop closure pipeline.

**A. Learned Visual Front-end Integration:**
The framework supports four learned feature extractors:
- **ALIKED:** Processes 3-channel RGB input, extracting 64- or 128-dimensional descriptors. It uses a Sparse Deformable Descriptor Head (SDDH) and a Differentiable Keypoint Detection (DKD) module for subpixel keypoints. A custom CUDA kernel replaced ALIKED's DKD for static-shape TensorRT export and efficiency.
- **RaCo:** A keypoint detector for 3-channel RGB input, incorporating a multi-scale covariance estimator and a differentiable ranking module. As RaCo itself does not emit descriptors, ALIKED's dense encoder and SDDH descriptor head are conditionally used to extract descriptors over RaCo's keypoints.
- **SuperPoint:** Processes 1-channel grayscale images, outputting 256-dimensional descriptors and discrete keypoints via Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS).
- **XFeat:** A lightweight CNN for 1-channel grayscale input, generating compact 64-dimensional descriptors. Keypoints are filtered using a reliability heatmap.

These extractors conform to a unified feature set abstraction `(p_i, d_i, s_i)` (keypoint coordinates, L2-normalized descriptors, detection scores). For RaCo, the covariance head was re-enabled to predict a 2x2 spatial uncertainty matrix per keypoint, which forms an anisotropic square-root information matrix for the reprojection factor in the back-end, evaluated in an ablation study.

**B. Input Pre-processing:**
A fused CUDA kernel handles input pre-processing, including letterbox padding, color-format conversion, intensity normalization, and NCHW layout packing. This minimizes host-device transfer overhead. Frames are padded to the nearest valid resolution grid (multiples of 8 or 32) for static-shape TensorRT engine compatibility.

**C. Feature Matching and Tracking:**
Two paradigms for temporal tracking are supported:
- **Lucas–Kanade (LK) Optical Flow:** Utilized with classical features (Shi-Tomasi/GFTT, SIFT) and learned keypoints (ALIKED, RaCo, SuperPoint, XFeat) where only keypoint locations are needed.
- **LightGlue (LG):** Used for learned descriptor matching between frames. It processes the current frame's features against a cache of previous frame features. In stereo mode, both temporal and stereo pairs are processed efficiently in a single batch-2 LightGlue inference. Temporal correspondences undergo geometric verification via MAGSAC++ for essential matrix estimation. Stereo correspondences are validated through calibrated geometry, discarding matches with negligible disparity and triangulating remaining pairs to ensure positive depth and low reprojection error. The refined observations are then forwarded to the back-end.

**D. Back-end Optimization:**
A sliding-window estimator performs joint optimization over reprojection residuals and IMU preintegration terms, consistent with VINS-Fusion. The Ceres solver is capped at 40 ms per frame.

**E. Loop Closure:**
The loop closure module is designed to be front-end agnostic:
- **Retrieval (AnyLoc):** Each keyframe is encoded using a frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14 network. Its dense patch tokens are aggregated by a universal VLAD codebook (K=32 centers) into an L2-normalized global descriptor. Loop candidates are retrieved based on cosine similarity (`sim(g_t, g_k) > τ_sim = 0.60`). A fixed window of recent keyframes is excluded to prevent self-matches.
- **Geometric Verification:** Reuses the active local descriptors (from the selected front-end) for cosine nearest-neighbor matching with a Lowe ratio test. This is followed by 2D–2D fundamental-matrix RANSAC and 3D–2D PnP RANSAC. A minimum of 25 inliers is required to accept a loop constraint.
- **Pose-Graph Optimization (PGO):** Accepted loop constraints are incorporated into a 4-DoF pose graph for global trajectory consistency. Optical-flow variants, lacking learned descriptors, fall back to the standard DBoW2 BRIEF loop-closure path.

**F. Experimental Setup:**
- **Hardware:** Evaluations were conducted on an x86-64 workstation (Intel Core i7-12700H, NVIDIA RTX 3080 Ti) and an embedded NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin (Arm Cortex-A78AE, NVIDIA Ampere). Both platforms ran CUDA 12.6 and TensorRT 10.3, with FP16 precision for learned models.
- **Datasets:** Four public datasets were used: EuRoC (indoor, structured, MAV), NTU-VIRAL (indoor/outdoor, aggressive aerial motion, UAV), Botanic Garden (unstructured natural environments, wheeled robot, RGB/grayscale), and SubT-MRS (subterranean/urban, extreme degraded conditions, monocular fisheye).
- **Baselines:** Handcrafted baselines included Shi-Tomasi Corner (GFTT) with LK optical flow, and SIFT with both LK and LightGlue. System-level baselines included VINS-Fusion and OKVIS2.
- **Metrics:** Localization accuracy was measured using the root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) with the `evo` toolkit. Runtime performance and power consumption were monitored on the Jetson AGX Orin using `tegrastats`.
- **Configuration:** All learned models used original pre-trained weights without fine-tuning. A maximum of 256 keypoints was generally capped, except for specific ablations. Online camera-IMU extrinsic estimation was enabled for NTU-VIRAL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation yielded several findings regarding the performance, robustness, and efficiency of learned visual front-ends in VI-SLAM across diverse conditions.

**A. Accuracy Analysis:**
- **Overall Performance:** No single front-end consistently outperformed others across all environments. Performance was observed to be dependent on scene structure, viewpoint variation, and ego-motion characteristics.
- **EuRoC:** In this well-lit, structured indoor dataset, AL+LG (monocular ATE: 0.146 m) performed optimally among learned setups, but all learned configurations clustered closely (0.146–0.176 m). The classical GFTT+LK baseline achieved the second-lowest monocular (0.154 m) and stereo (0.122 m) ATE, suggesting that keypoint density and factor-graph formulation were significant drivers of performance here.
- **NTU-VIRAL:** For aggressive aerial motion and distant scenes, LightGlue matching generally outperformed optical flow. Ra+LG achieved the lowest monocular ATE (0.877 m), and AL+LG achieved the lowest stereo ATE (0.552 m). Optical flow variants had higher errors (1.06–1.40 m monocular). OKVIS2 failed in monocular mode but tracked successfully in stereo (1.452 m).
- **Botanic Garden:** This dataset, characterized by unstructured natural environments and low parallax, presented a contrast. Monocular tracking favored optical flow variants (SP+LK grayscale: 1.085 m; Ra+LK RGB: 0.767 m). Stereo performance degraded for most learned front-ends, often by a factor of two, attributed to low stereo match yield and weak effective disparity in dense vegetation. Minor translational offsets and spectral differences between grayscale and RGB cameras also influenced performance. XF+LG failed in all monocular configurations and most stereo configurations, indicating a limitation specific to its LightGlue matcher variant.
- **Stereo vs. Monocular:** Stereo configurations improved accuracy in environments with reliable metric depth (EuRoC, NTU-VIRAL). However, stereo provided no benefit and could degrade performance in scenes where disparity was poorly constrained (Botanic Garden).
- **LightGlue vs. Optical Flow:** LightGlue consistently outperformed optical flow when inter-frame viewpoint variations were significant (NTU-VIRAL). Conversely, optical flow was preferable for near-straight, low-parallax trajectories with minimal out-of-plane motion (Botanic Garden).

**B. Robustness Analysis (SubT-MRS):**
- **Visually Degraded Conditions:** SubT-MRS posed the most challenging conditions (low light, over-exposure, smoke, fisheye camera). No configuration completed all five sequences with a 160-keypoint cap.
- **Over-exposure/Flash:** XF+LK (0.638 m) and AL+LK (0.641 m) performed best in over-exposure. Ra+LG (0.150 m) was best in flash light.
- **Lowlight:** The illumination-trained LET-NET baseline led. SP+LG (1.061 m) and Ra+LG (1.661 m) were the strongest learned configurations in Lowlight 1 and 2, respectively. Some configurations exhibited severe drift (e.g., Ra+LK 16.4 m, AL+LG 16.0 m in Lowlight 1) due to sensitivity to dynamic objects rather than solely illumination.
- **Dense Smoke:** Under 160-feature cap, all learned front-ends failed to track due to obscured visual structure. Increasing the cap to 256 features partially recovered optical flow performance (AL+LK: 1.450 m; SP+LK: 2.792 m), suggesting feature starvation. However, LightGlue-based configurations did not reliably recover, indicating a fundamental breakdown of high-confidence descriptor matching in severe occlusion.

**C. Loop Closure Analysis:**
- **Impact:** Loop closure meaningfully improved ATE in 328 out of 764 successful runs, mostly on revisit-rich datasets (70% of NTU-VIRAL, 50% of SubT-MRS, 46% of EuRoC). Minimal impact (19–25% improvement) was observed in Botanic Garden due to fewer loop opportunities.
- **AnyLoc (DINOv2-VLAD) vs. BRIEF+DBoW2:** AnyLoc confirmed 2–7 times more valid loops per keyframe than BRIEF, demonstrating its ability to expose more true revisits.
- **Cosine Similarity Gate:** The DINOv2-VLAD cosine similarity score (threshold τ_sim=0.60) was found to have high recall but low precision, with most keyframes exceeding the threshold but only a small fraction (1.6% to 48%) passing geometric verification. Local descriptor matching and RANSAC were essential for rejecting false positives.
- **Regressions:** Loop closure regressions occurred in OKVIS2 (due to false relocalizations in outdoor vegetation), NTU-VIRAL (specific hotspot like `nya_02`), and EuRoC (isolated false constraints in already accurate odometry).

**D. Cross-platform Analysis:**
- **Reproducibility:** ATE values between x86-64 and Jetson AGX Orin showed high reproducibility, with a median relative difference of 6%. Absolute differences were small, often within ground truth uncertainty.
- **Numerical Perturbations:** Observed scatter, particularly in low-ATE EuRoC runs, was attributed to numerical perturbations from platform-specific TensorRT FP16 engines, floating-point arithmetic, and non-deterministic multi-threading. These small differences could amplify in weakly-constrained estimator windows, occasionally leading to divergent trajectories on one platform but not the other.

**E. Runtime Analysis:**
- **Bottlenecks:** Ceres optimization dominated back-end compute time. Feature extraction was the primary front-end bottleneck, with LightGlue adding substantial overhead compared to optical flow.
- **Monocular Mode:** The back-end determined overall latency for all configurations, as VINS-Fusion processes every other frame. Even the heaviest front-end (Ra+LG, 25 ms on Jetson mono) was back-end-bound.
- **Stereo Mode:** The back-end load increased significantly due to additional stereo residuals. Front-end computational increase for LightGlue was ~1.7x due to batch-2 inference. Ra+LG became the sole front-end-bound configuration on Jetson stereo.
- **Throughput:** Both platforms comfortably exceeded camera frame rates (29–47 FPS on Jetson, 41–79 FPS on x86-64).
- **Power Consumption (Jetson):** Total Jetson module power ranged from 11.5 W (GFTT) to 22.7 W (Ra+LG), with variations driven by GPU power for LightGlue and DINOv2.
- **Resolution Scaling:** Front-end computational cost scaled linearly with image resolution (e.g., 1.6x pixel count increase from EuRoC to Botanic led to 1.6x extraction time increase).

**F. Keypoint Count Ablation:**
- **Accuracy:** Trajectory accuracy was largely insensitive to keypoint limits (192, 256, 384) within this range. However, 192 keypoints sometimes induced tracking failures due to feature starvation, while 384 occasionally triggered new divergences without significant accuracy gains.
- **Runtime:** Learned front-ends (ALIKED, RaCo) showed a local minimum in runtime at 256 keypoints due to CUDA kernel efficiency. SP+LG's cost scaled linearly. The 256-keypoint cap was identified as the preferred choice for balancing efficiency and robustness.

**G. RaCo Covariance Weighting Ablation:**
- **Impact:** Covariance weighting (anisotropic Mahalanobis distance using RaCo's predicted pixel covariance) primarily helped in stereo mode (median ATE reduction of -14.4% at λ=1), where metric depth was observable and down-weighting poorly localized detections improved bundle adjustment. In monocular mode, it was marginally worse (+1.4%).
- **Gain Factor (λ):** Full reliance on RaCo covariance (λ=1) was generally most helpful. Intermediate weighting could sometimes ill-condition the optimizer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes several contributions to the field of VI-SLAM and its application in mobile robotics, primarily through the development and comprehensive evaluation of DL-VINS-Factory.

**A. Systematic Evaluation and Benchmarking:**
The modular framework allows for a standardized and controlled comparison of various learned visual front-ends (ALIKED, RaCo, SuperPoint, XFeat) and matching strategies (LK optical flow, LightGlue) within a consistent, tightly coupled VI-SLAM back-end. This addresses the previously fragmented nature of evaluations, providing isolated insights into the performance, robustness, and efficiency implications of different front-end designs. The inclusion of handcrafted baselines under identical conditions further contextualizes the findings. This systematic approach is significant for researchers seeking to understand the real-world trade-offs of integrating deep learning features into VI-SLAM.

**B. Characterization of Learned Feature Performance:**
The extensive evaluation across diverse datasets (EuRoC, NTU-VIRAL, Botanic Garden, SubT-MRS) reveals that the superiority of learned front-ends is not universal but conditional.
- **Conditional Advantage:** Learned matching (LightGlue) demonstrates benefits under large inter-frame viewpoint changes (e.g., aggressive aerial motion), while optical-flow tracking remains competitive or even superior in structured scenes, low-parallax environments, or visually degraded conditions where sparse, stable features are crucial.
- **Scene Dependence:** The findings highlight that VI-SLAM performance is not solely determined by feature representation but also by scene geometry, motion excitation, feature survival characteristics, and back-end conditioning. This underscores the need for careful selection of front-end components tailored to the intended deployment environment.

**C. Practicality for Embedded Deployment:**
The demonstration of real-time operation on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, coupled with TensorRT acceleration, indicates that learned front-ends are viable for resource-constrained embedded hardware. This is significant for the practical adoption of these technologies in mobile robots, drones, and other autonomous systems. However, the study also highlights trade-offs, noting that learned extraction, matching, and DINOv2-based retrieval increase GPU power and memory demands, which are critical considerations for embedded systems.

**D. Front-end-Agnostic Loop Closure:**
The integration of AnyLoc with DINOv2-VLAD for loop retrieval provides a front-end-agnostic mechanism, eliminating the need for descriptor-specific Bag-of-Words vocabulary reconstruction. This simplifies the development and evaluation of new visual front-ends. While the cosine similarity gate requires further refinement for precision, the underlying approach demonstrates a path towards more flexible and adaptable long-term localization systems.

**E. Identified Areas for Improvement and Future Work:**
The research identifies specific areas where current learned front-ends and integration strategies can be improved. These include the sensitivity of some learned matchers to severe visual occlusion, the high recall but low precision of the AnyLoc cosine similarity gate, and the need for more calibrated uncertainty estimation in learned features (as suggested by the RaCo covariance ablation). These insights can guide future research towards developing more robust, efficient, and context-aware learned perception components for VI-SLAM.
