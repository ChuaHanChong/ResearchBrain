# VerifyBench: Benchmarking Reference-based Reward Systems for Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed VerifyBench and VerifyBench-Hard, the first benchmarks for evaluating reference-based reward systems in Large Reasoning Models, demonstrating that while leading models achieve over 90% accuracy on standard tasks, performance drops by over 20% on challenging cases, with reference answers enhancing verification by 5-18%.

## Problem

- Existing reward benchmarks for Large Language Models (LLMs) primarily assess reward models through pairwise preference comparisons, which are insufficient for evaluating absolute correctness.
- Specialized Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) increasingly rely on "reference-based" reward systems, where rewards are assigned based on alignment with ground truth, necessitating a distinct evaluation methodology.
- There is an absence of dedicated, standardized benchmarks to systematically evaluate these reference-based reward systems, hindering objective assessment and improvement of verification methodologies.

## Method

- Constructed **VerifyBench**, a benchmark of 2,000 balanced question-answer-completion-correctness tuples across general, logical, and mathematical reasoning, curated from open-source problems and human-annotated.
- Developed VerifyBench-Hard, a more challenging dataset comprising 1,000 tuples derived from contentious cases where multiple top-performing LLMs produced conflicting correctness judgments.
- Evaluated various verification approaches, including rule-based baselines, LLM-as-a-judge models, and specialized model-based verifiers, using accuracy as the primary metric, and conducted ablation studies on the impact of reference answers.

## Results

- Top models like Qwen3-**32B** achieved **95.8%** accuracy on VerifyBench, but performance across all models dropped to a maximum of **72.4%** on VerifyBench-Hard, indicating substantial challenges.
- Smaller models (<**3B** parameters) exhibited notably poorer performance on VerifyBench, with Llama-3.2-**3B**-Instruct scoring only **60.95%**.
- Error analysis revealed common issues in verifying complex numeric values, algebraic expressions (e.g., unsimplified but equivalent forms), multiple-choice problems, and semantic consistency in string responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current state-of-the-art LLMs can act as reliable verifiers for standard reference-based tasks, but all evaluated systems, especially smaller models, struggle significantly on difficult instances.
> - Explicit ground truth reference answers play a crucial role in verification, demonstrably improving LLM performance by approximately 5% to 18% in reasoning-oriented tasks.
> - Verifier performance on VerifyBench positively correlates with greater improvements in downstream mathematical reasoning tasks when used in Reinforcement Learning (RL) training and Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advanced capabilities in various domains, with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) being a critical factor in their development. A central component of RLHF is the reward model, which evaluates and scores model-generated responses. Existing reward benchmarks primarily focus on evaluating these reward models through pairwise preference comparisons between responses. This approach assesses a model's ability to rank one response as superior to another.

However, the emergence of specialized Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1 has introduced a different training paradigm. These models often employ specialized reinforcement learning techniques that rely on "reference-based" reward systems. In this context, rewards are assigned based on the alignment of model-generated outputs with authoritative ground truth reference answers, rather than subjective human preferences or relative comparisons between multiple outputs. This shift necessitates a distinct evaluation methodology capable of assessing the absolute correctness of a single response against a reference.

The current research landscape is characterized by a significant gap: the absence of dedicated benchmarks for systematically evaluating these reference-based reward systems. This deficiency hinders researchers' ability to objectively assess, compare, and improve the verification methodologies crucial for training advanced reasoning models. This paper addresses this gap by introducing benchmarks specifically designed for reference-based verification, thereby aiming to provide a standardized framework for evaluating and improving the accuracy of such systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from a recognized gap in the evaluation of reward systems for Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly those specialized in reasoning tasks. While Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been instrumental in LLM development, existing reward benchmarks predominantly focus on preference-based evaluations, where reward models are assessed on their ability to rank competing responses. This approach involves comparing two or more outputs and determining which is "better" according to human preferences or a learned preference model.

For large reasoning models (LRMs) like OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1, however, training methodologies often incorporate reference-based reward systems. These systems evaluate model outputs by verifying their alignment with objective ground truth references. This paradigm shift means that rewards are assigned based on absolute correctness rather than relative preferences. The authors identify that current evaluation frameworks are inadequate for this specific use case because they do not directly assess a system's ability to verify a single response against an authoritative reference. This absence of dedicated benchmarks for reference-based reward systems limits the systematic assessment, comparison, and improvement of verification methodologies, potentially impeding progress in the development of more accurate and robust reasoning models.

To address this critical deficiency, the key objectives of this research are:

1. **To construct VerifyBench:** Develop a benchmark specifically designed for the objective evaluation of reference-based reward systems. This benchmark aims to reflect realistic reinforcement learning (RL) scenarios for reasoning models by focusing on absolute correctness judgments against ground truth references.
2. **To develop VerifyBench-Hard:** Create a more challenging variant of the benchmark. This dataset is curated from cases where leading models exhibit significant disagreement in their verification judgments, providing a stringent test for reward system accuracy and highlighting areas for substantial improvement.
3. **To conduct a comprehensive empirical analysis:** Perform a systematic evaluation of various model-based and rule-based verifiers on both VerifyBench and VerifyBench-Hard. This analysis aims to offer actionable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of current reference-based reward systems, thereby guiding future research and development in enhancing RL training for reasoning tasks.
4. **To establish a standardized framework:** Provide a publicly available and high-quality benchmark to foster consistent evaluation and accelerate the advancement of verification accuracy, ultimately contributing to the enhancement of reasoning capabilities in LLMs trained via RL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces two benchmarks, VerifyBench and VerifyBench-Hard, designed to evaluate reference-based reward systems. Both benchmarks are constructed through a multi-stage process involving data curation, completion generation, and human annotation.

#### 3.1. Construction of VerifyBench

1. **Query Curation:** To simulate realistic RL scenarios, a comprehensive collection of open-source reasoning problems with corresponding reference answers was curated. These problems span three main categories: general reasoning, logical reasoning, and mathematical reasoning, drawn from 41 distinct data sources.
2. **Answer Type Labeling:** To ensure diverse evaluation, four canonical answer types were defined: numerical values, algebraic expressions, multiple-choice selections, and free-form strings. A general-purpose LLM, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, was employed for automatic answer-type classification using a prompt-based judgment framework. Questions falling outside these categories (e.g., proof-based or open-ended) were excluded. Following classification, 2,000 instances were randomly sampled per answer type, resulting in a pool of 8,000 questions.
3. **Completion Generation and Pre-annotation:** Single-shot completions for the 8,000 curated questions were generated by 22 widely used open-source and proprietary LLMs, yielding a total of 176,000 completions. Initial correctness labels were assigned using Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct via a prompt-based judgment framework. For each question, four completions (two labeled correct, two labeled incorrect by the model) were selected for subsequent human annotation.
4. **Human Annotation:** Human annotators performed two primary tasks: (1) identifying the most appropriate answer type for each question, and (2) evaluating the correctness of each of the four generated completions. Each question was independently annotated by at least two annotators. Disagreements were resolved by a meta-annotator to ensure label consistency and reliability. Annotators received training and detailed instructions, with a web-based UI supporting LaTeX rendering.
5. **Benchmark Construction:** Following human annotation, biases in model predictions regarding answer types and completion correctness were observed. To mitigate these imbalances, controlled downsampling was performed. Specifically, 250 questions were retained per answer type, totaling 1,000 unique questions. Each question was then paired with exactly two completions: one correct and one incorrect. This process resulted in VerifyBench, comprising 2,000 well-balanced question-answer-completion-correctness tuples.

#### 3.2. Construction of VerifyBench-Hard

VerifyBench-Hard was developed to create a more challenging dataset by focusing on contentious cases where leading models produced conflicting judgments.

1. **Completion Generation:** Single-turn completions for the queries used in VerifyBench construction were generated using 18 open-source models, resulting in approximately 1.45 million completions. Closed-source models were excluded at this stage due to computational costs.
2. **Difficulty Filtering:** Five top-performing LLMs on VerifyBench (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Llama-4-Scout-17B-16E-Instruct, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and Qwen3-32B) were used to evaluate the correctness of the generated completions. Question-answer-completion tuples exhibiting significant model disagreement (where two models' assessments diverged from the other three) were identified. Stratified sampling across data domains and sources was applied to select 2,000 examples for human annotation.
3. **Human Annotation:** The selected samples underwent human annotation, involving answer type identification and completion correctness determination. Each instance was independently annotated by at least two annotators, with disagreements resolved by a meta-annotator, similar to the VerifyBench process.
4. **Benchmark Construction:** After human annotation and the exclusion of unsuitable samples, a final set of 1,000 question-answer-completion-correctness tuples was produced. Unlike VerifyBench, VerifyBench-Hard was derived through natural sampling, which resulted in a skewed distribution with a higher proportion of incorrect completions, reflecting the observed tendency of larger models to erroneously accept incorrect answers in challenging scenarios.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Approach

The evaluation involved assessing various verification approaches:
- **Rule-based baselines:** The `math-verify` method (v0.8.0) was used.
- **LLM-as-a-judge:** General LLMs were prompted to perform verification, with specific prompt templates used.
- **Model-based verifiers:** Existing verification-specialized models (e.g., xVerify, CompassVerifier, Tencent-Qwen2.5-7B-RLVR) were evaluated using their recommended prompt templates.

Accuracy was the primary metric, defined as the proportion of instances where the reward model correctly predicted the binary correctness label. An ablation study was also conducted for LLM-as-a-judge models to quantify the impact of providing reference answers. Additionally, reference-free reward models were assessed by reformulating VerifyBench instances into pairwise comparison tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VerifyBench and VerifyBench-Hard yielded several findings regarding the capabilities of various verification systems.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **VerifyBench Performance:** State-of-the-art LLMs demonstrated strong performance on VerifyBench. For example, GPT-4o-mini achieved an average accuracy of 92.85%, and Qwen3-32B reached 95.8%. This indicates that current LLMs can act as reliable verifiers for standard cases when provided with reference answers.
- **VerifyBench-Hard Challenge:** Performance on VerifyBench-Hard was observed to be significantly lower across all evaluated models compared to VerifyBench. The highest accuracy achieved was 72.4%, a decrease of over 20% from VerifyBench performance. This substantial performance gap highlights that VerifyBench-Hard effectively identifies challenging instances where current LLMs struggle to verify responses accurately, indicating considerable room for improvement in precise verification capabilities.
- **Small-scale Models:** Models with smaller parameter counts (less than 3 billion parameters) exhibited notably poorer performance on VerifyBench. For instance, Qwen3-1.7B achieved 81.10% accuracy, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct scored only 60.95%. This suggests that while larger models perform well, there is a need to enhance the verification capabilities of smaller models, which are important for inference efficiency in practical reinforcement learning scenarios.

#### 4.2. Reference Answers' Role in Verification

An ablation study demonstrated the impact of including reference answers in the verification process. When LLMs were evaluated without reference answers, a performance degradation of approximately 5% to 18% was observed across various models. This finding underscores the crucial role of explicit reference answers in reasoning-oriented reinforcement learning, suggesting they provide a reliable and informative supervision signal for reward modeling.

#### 4.3. Performance of Reference-free Reward Models

Reference-free reward models, when adapted to a pairwise comparison format using VerifyBench data (without explicit reference answers), achieved sub-80% accuracy. This performance level is comparable to existing preference-based reward benchmarks. The evaluation also showed that domain-specific reward models often performed less effectively on general reward benchmarks compared to VerifyBench, which aligns with the benchmark's design objectives of assessing specific verification tasks.

#### 4.4. Error Analysis

A fine-grained taxonomy of answer types revealed specific categories where models frequently made errors:

- **Numeric Values:** Errors were common in complex numbers and answers involving multiple numerical values.
- **Expressions:** Formulas and equations were particularly error-prone.
- **Multi-choice:** Instances involving multiple-choice problems presented challenges.
- **String:** String responses requiring semantic consistency verification also frequently led to incorrect judgments.

A common underlying issue identified was models' failure to fully comprehend the question or the intended objective. For example, in multi-value answers, models often incorrectly classified responses as erroneous if the value order differed from the reference, even when order was irrelevant. Similarly, with algebraic expressions, models might deem unsimplified but mathematically equivalent expressions as incorrect due to superficial textual discrepancies, rather than evaluating their mathematical equivalence.

#### 4.5. Correlation Analysis

To evaluate the practical utility of the benchmarks, a correlation analysis was performed by conducting Reinforcement Learning (RL) training on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, using various LLM judges (with differing VerifyBench performance) as reward systems. The results indicated a positive correlation: models performing well on VerifyBench, when used as verifiers in RL training, led to greater improvements on downstream mathematical reasoning tasks (such as MATH500, AMC23, and MinervaMath). Conversely, using verifiers with weaker VerifyBench performance sometimes resulted in degradation of the base model's capabilities, attributed to the introduction of substantial bias in reward assignment. Similar trends were observed in Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning (RFT) experiments, where verifiers with higher VerifyBench accuracy consistently yielded better performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH500, and SVAMP. These results suggest that VerifyBench provides effective guidance for improving RL training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work addresses a critical gap in the evaluation of reward systems for Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly within the domain of reasoning tasks. The introduction of **VerifyBench** and its challenging variant, **VerifyBench-Hard**, establishes the first dedicated benchmarks for assessing reference-based reward systems, a methodology central to training contemporary large reasoning models (LRMs).

The significance and potential impact of this research are multi-faceted:

- **Standardized Evaluation Framework:** By providing meticulously curated, high-quality benchmarks, the paper offers a standardized and objective framework for evaluating the accuracy of verification systems. This framework moves beyond traditional preference-based reward evaluations to directly assess absolute correctness against ground truth, which is essential for reasoning-focused RL.
- **Identification of Performance Gaps:** The findings clearly demonstrate that while larger model-based verifiers show promise on standard cases, all current systems, especially smaller models, face substantial challenges on more difficult instances (as highlighted by VerifyBench-Hard). This quantifies the current limitations and provides a clear target for future research and development in improving verifier accuracy.
- **Actionable Insights for Model Development:** The detailed error analysis, categorizing common failure modes across different answer types (numeric values, expressions, multi-choice, strings), offers specific insights into where current models struggle. This granular understanding can guide researchers in developing more robust verification architectures, training strategies, and data augmentation techniques to address these specific weaknesses, such as improving mathematical comprehension for unsimplified expressions or semantic reasoning for string comparisons.
- **Guidance for Reinforcement Learning Training:** The correlation analysis directly links verifier performance on VerifyBench to the efficacy of RL training. Demonstrating that high-performing verifiers lead to greater improvements in downstream reasoning tasks provides empirical evidence for the practical utility of these benchmarks. This impact extends to guiding the selection of reward models for RL, potentially preventing the introduction of harmful biases from less accurate verifiers and accelerating the development of more capable reasoning LLMs.
- **Advancing Reasoning Capabilities:** Ultimately, by enabling more accurate and reliable reference-based reward systems, the benchmarks are poised to enhance the overall reasoning capabilities of LLMs. As verification becomes more precise, RL agents can receive cleaner and more consistent feedback, leading to more robust learning and improved performance on complex reasoning problems. This work contributes to the foundational tools necessary for pushing the boundaries of AI in logical and mathematical reasoning.

In summary, this research provides essential infrastructure for the systematic advancement of reference-based reward systems, with significant implications for the training, evaluation, and ultimate capabilities of future large reasoning models.
