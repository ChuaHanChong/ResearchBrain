# TRACE: Learned Proprioceptive Odometry for Legged Robots under Unreliable Contact Conditions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KAIST developed TRACE, an end-to-end learned proprioceptive odometry system for legged robots that achieves robust state estimation under unreliable foot-ground contact conditions. The system leverages a foot-aware cross-attention module and physics-inspired auxiliary losses, demonstrating significant reductions in position error (e.g., up to 63.2% position ATE reduction on stairs) across diverse indoor and outdoor terrains compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Traditional exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR, GPS) for robot state estimation are prone to failure or degradation in challenging environments like poor lighting, textureless scenes, or GPS-denied areas.
- Proprioceptive odometry is challenged by inherent sensor noise and the unreliable nature of foot-ground contacts (e.g., slip, terrain deformation, foot rolling), leading to accumulating estimation errors.
- Existing filtering-based methods rely on fragile zero-velocity assumptions and heuristic contact thresholds, while purely learning-based approaches struggle with out-of-distribution motions and a significant sim-to-real gap without explicit physical constraints.

## Method

- **TRACE**, an end-to-end learned estimator, directly predicts relative robot motion and body-frame velocity from proprioceptive history (IMU, joint states) without requiring a filtering backend or manually tuned contact/slip thresholds.
- The architecture integrates a ==foot-aware cross-attention module== with ==physics-inspired auxiliary objectives== (kinematic model consistency and foot velocity loss) to adaptively downweight unreliable leg information.
- A two-stage training pipeline addresses the sim-to-real gap, combining ==policy-randomized simulation pretraining== to broaden motion distributions with partial real-world fine-tuning using a closed-loop relative-error objective.

## Results

- TRACE achieved consistently lower position and orientation errors across diverse indoor and outdoor terrains, including a **63.2%** reduction in position ATE on stairs and up to **53.8%** on soft indoor terrain compared to the strongest baselines.
- Attention analysis demonstrated that the learned foot-aware attention module effectively identified reliable contact, with contact feet receiving substantially higher attention scores (e.g., mean **0.2106** vs. **0.0060** for swing feet).
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the kinematic model consistency and foot velocity auxiliary losses were critical for performance, and the combined policy randomization and partial fine-tuning strategy significantly improved generalization and reduced the sim-to-real gap without overfitting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learned cross-attention mechanism, guided by a foot velocity loss, can effectively serve as a 'soft reliability measure' for foot contact, dynamically adjusting the influence of leg information based on contact quality without explicit thresholds.
> - Incorporating physics-inspired auxiliary losses like kinematic model consistency into an end-to-end learned odometry system significantly improves estimation accuracy and physical plausibility by enforcing structural constraints.
> - Policy randomization during simulation training, coupled with targeted partial fine-tuning on real-world data, is an effective strategy to mitigate the sim-to-real gap, enabling learned models to generalize robustly to diverse and unseen real-world conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reliable state estimation is a fundamental requirement for legged robots to effectively plan and navigate in complex environments. Traditional approaches often rely on exteroceptive sensors such as cameras, LiDAR, and GPS to acquire geometric or global environmental information. However, the performance of these sensors can be significantly degraded in challenging conditions, including poor illumination, motion blur, textureless scenes, adverse weather, or in indoor and occluded environments where GPS signals are unavailable.

Proprioceptive odometry offers an alternative by estimating ego-motion using only onboard inertial measurement units (IMUs) and joint sensors. This makes it a valuable approach when exteroceptive sensing is unreliable or unavailable. Despite its advantages, accurate proprioceptive odometry presents significant challenges. These include inherent measurement noise from onboard sensors and the complex, often unreliable, nature of foot-ground contacts. Factors such as slip, compliant or deformable terrain, and foot rolling can introduce persistent estimation errors that accumulate into substantial long-term drift.

Existing proprioceptive state estimation methods can be broadly categorized into classical filtering-based, hybrid learning-based, and purely end-to-end learning-based approaches.

- **Classical filtering-based estimators**, particularly those based on the Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (IEKF), commonly leverage contact-aided principles. These methods typically assume that stance feet maintain zero velocity relative to the ground. However, inferring the true contact state is not directly observable and usually necessitates heuristic thresholds for ground reaction forces or gait phase information. These manually tuned thresholds often prove inadequate for complex terrains or dynamic locomotion, and the zero-velocity assumption can be violated by unmodeled effects like slip, impact, terrain deformation, and foot rolling. Furthermore, their reliance on manually configured covariance settings complicates robust performance across diverse conditions.

- **Learning-based approaches** have emerged to address the limitations of classical filters.
 - **Hybrid methods** augment model-based filters with neural networks. Examples include learning contact estimates for an IEKF, predicting foot-contact probabilities and body-frame velocities as IEKF measurements, using CNNs for slip detection, or employing attention mechanisms to compensate for slip-induced errors or predict velocity and uncertainty for IEKF updates (e.g., NMN-IEKF, GAIT). While these methods improve robustness, they remain coupled to filter backends and may still require explicit contact or slip estimates and their associated thresholds.
 - **Purely learning-based odometry methods**, such as Legolas, directly predict robot odometry from IMU and leg histories, foregoing analytical kinematic models or filter-based physical constraints. This absence of explicit constraints can make them more vulnerable to out-of-distribution motions and contact conditions, potentially leading to increased drift. A significant challenge for these purely learned models is the "sim-to-real gap," where performance degrades when models trained primarily in simulation are deployed on real robots due to discrepancies in sensor and motion distributions.

The current work, TRACE (Tokenized Robust Attention for Contact-Aware Estimation), addresses these limitations by proposing an end-to-end learned proprioceptive odometry estimator. It aims to improve robustness under unreliable contact conditions and mitigate the sim-to-real gap without relying on filtering backends or manually defined thresholds. This places TRACE within the purely learning-based paradigm but with specific architectural and training innovations designed to overcome common pitfalls of such approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing TRACE stems from the inherent difficulties and limitations observed in existing proprioceptive odometry methods for legged robots, particularly under challenging real-world operating conditions.

The specific motivations are:
- **Limitations of Exteroceptive Sensors**: While often used for state estimation, exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR, GPS) are prone to failure or degradation in various environments, such as those with poor lighting, uniform textures, adverse weather, or GPS-denied indoor spaces. Proprioceptive sensing offers an alternative that is less susceptible to these environmental factors.
- **Challenges of Proprioceptive Odometry**: Despite its potential, accurate proprioceptive odometry is challenging due to inherent sensor noise from IMUs and joint encoders. More critically, the reliability of foot-ground contacts is often compromised by phenomena such as ground slip, deformation of compliant terrain, or the rolling motion of the robot's feet. These factors invalidate common assumptions in model-based estimators and lead to significant, accumulating errors.
- **Deficiencies of Classical Filtering Methods**: Traditional approaches, particularly IEKF-based methods, depend heavily on the zero-velocity assumption for stance feet. However, determining true contact state accurately is difficult and typically involves heuristic thresholds that are fragile to diverse terrain and dynamic gaits. Furthermore, unmodeled contact effects (slip, impact, foot rolling) fundamentally violate the core assumptions, leading to performance degradation. The manual tuning of filter covariances for various conditions is also a practical hurdle.
- **Incompleteness of Hybrid Learning Methods**: While hybrid approaches integrate neural networks to enhance filter performance, they remain coupled to the filter's underlying physical models. This can limit their ability to fully adapt to unmodeled complexities and may still require explicit contact or slip estimates, inheriting some of the threshold-related issues of classical filters.
- **Vulnerability and Sim-to-Real Gap in Purely Learning-Based Methods**: End-to-end learned odometry methods offer direct prediction without explicit models, potentially making them more flexible. However, this lack of explicit physical constraints can render them susceptible to out-of-distribution motions or contact conditions, leading to large estimation errors. A major practical challenge is the "sim-to-real gap," where models trained predominantly in simulation perform poorly on real hardware due to discrepancies between simulated and real-world sensor data and robot dynamics.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the TRACE research are:
1. **End-to-End Learning Formulation**: To formulate quadruped proprioceptive odometry as a direct end-to-end learning problem, predicting relative motion and body-frame velocity directly from proprioceptive history, without a filtering backend or reliance on hand-tuned contact/slip thresholds.
2. **Contact-Aware Estimation**: To develop an estimator architecture that can inherently handle unreliable foot-ground interactions. This involves coupling a "foot-aware cross-attention module" with "physics-inspired auxiliary objectives" to adaptively downweight unreliable leg information without requiring explicit contact or slip thresholds.
3. **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer**: To introduce a two-stage training pipeline designed to mitigate the sim-to-real gap. This pipeline integrates policy-randomized simulation pretraining to broaden the observed motion distribution, followed by partial real-world fine-tuning using a closed-loop relative-error objective to adapt to real-world characteristics while preserving generalization.
4. **Performance Validation**: To demonstrate empirically that the proposed method consistently reduces position drift compared to classical filtering-based, hybrid, and purely learning-based baselines across a diverse range of challenging indoor and outdoor terrains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TRACE estimator is an end-to-end learned system designed to predict relative robot motion and body-frame velocity from a history of proprioceptive measurements. Its methodology encompasses a specific input-output definition, a novel network architecture, a comprehensive set of training losses, and a two-stage sim-to-real training pipeline.

**A. Estimator Input and Output**
The estimator receives a temporal window of $T=30$ historical observations. Each observation $o_t$ at time $t$ is a 47-dimensional vector comprising:
- IMU measurements: angular velocity ($\omega_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$) and linear acceleration ($a_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$).
- Joint states: joint positions ($q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$), joint velocities ($\dot{q}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$), and target joint torques ($\tau_t \in \mathbb{R}^{12}$).
- Previous estimates: roll and pitch ($\hat{\theta}_{r,t-1}, \hat{\theta}_{p,t-1}$), and horizontal body velocity ($\hat{v}_{x,t-1}, \hat{v}_{y,t-1}$). During initial training, these are noisy ground-truth values; during fine-tuning and deployment, they are the estimator's previous outputs.
- Sampling interval ($\Delta t_t$).

The network outputs a 9-dimensional vector $\hat{y}_t$, consisting of:
- Body-frame relative displacement ($\Delta\hat{p}_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$).
- Relative rotation in Lie algebra representation ($\Delta\hat{\theta}_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$).
- Body-frame linear velocity ($\hat{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^3$).

Ground-truth labels for training are derived from consecutive simulated robot states. Relative displacement is computed in the previous body frame, relative rotation is represented in Lie algebra from the previous to the current body frame, and body-frame velocity is obtained by rotating the world-frame velocity into the current body frame.

**B. Network Architecture**
The TRACE architecture comprises four main components:
1. **Proprioceptive Query CNN**: A temporal CNN processes the recent history of IMU signals and previous state estimates (specifically $\omega, a, \hat{\theta}_r, \hat{\theta}_p, \hat{v}_x, \hat{v}_y, \Delta t$) over the $T=30$ sample window. This CNN uses two 1D convolutional layers, temporal pooling, a linear projection, and layer normalization to generate a compact, $d$-dimensional "query feature" $Q_t$ that summarizes recent body motion.
2. **Foot-Aware Cross-Attention Module**: This module processes six "sensor tokens" derived from current proprioceptive measurements. These tokens include linearly embedded IMU acceleration and gyroscope readings, and four distinct leg tokens. Each leg token is a 13-dimensional feature ($\{q_i, \dot{q}_i, \tau_i, ID_i\}$) embedded via a small MLP. The CNN-generated query $Q_t$ then attends to these six sensor tokens using a two-head cross-attention mechanism with embedding dimension $d=64$. This mechanism adaptively weights different IMU and leg features based on the current locomotion state. The attention weights $\alpha_{i,t}$ are averaged across heads and used for subsequent loss calculations and analysis.
3. **GRU Temporal Encoder**: The context vector $c_t$ produced by the attention module is passed to a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) with a hidden dimension of 128. This GRU further encodes the temporal dynamics.
4. **MLP Prediction Head**: An MLP with hidden dimensions takes the attention context, the GRU hidden state, and the sampling interval as input to predict the final 9-dimensional output.

For robustness during deployment, a ZUPT-inspired (Zero-velocity UPdaTe) output clamp is applied. This mechanism sets the predicted motion to zero when angular and joint velocities are minimal and measured acceleration is close to gravity, effectively suppressing drift during stationary periods.

**C. Training Losses**
The total training objective ($L$) is a weighted sum of three components:
$L = w_{est}L_{est} + w_{model}L_{model} + w_{foot}L_{foot}$

1. **Estimation Loss ($L_{est}$)**: This directly supervises the network's output using a Smooth L1 loss ($\rho$) for relative displacement, relative rotation, and velocity predictions against their respective ground-truth labels.
 $L_{est} = w_p\rho(\Delta\hat{p}_t - \Delta p_t) + w_R\rho(\Delta\hat{\theta}_t - \Delta\theta_t) + w_v \rho(\hat{v}_t - v_t)$

2. **Kinematic Model Consistency Loss ($L_{model}$)**: This auxiliary loss promotes kinematic consistency between the predicted displacement and a displacement reconstructed from the predicted velocity. The velocity-based displacement ($\Delta p_{kin,t}$) is approximated using trapezoidal integration, incorporating the previous ground-truth velocity (or previously predicted velocity during fine-tuning) and the predicted current velocity and rotation.
 $L_{model} = \rho(\Delta\hat{p}_t - \Delta p_{kin,t})$

3. **Foot Velocity Loss ($L_{foot}$)**: This loss encourages physically consistent contact behavior, addressing the limitations of the fixed-foot-point assumption (e.g., due to foot rolling). For each leg, the contact-point velocity ($v_{contact,i,t}$) is computed using forward kinematics, incorporating the predicted body velocity, angular velocity, and joint states, as well as an offset to the contact point based on ground normals from simulation. This velocity is then penalized, weighted by the corresponding leg's attention score ($\bar{\alpha}_{leg,i,t}$). A stop-gradient operation is applied to the total leg attention to prevent the network from trivially reducing the loss by collapsing attention. This loss helps the network identify and downweight unreliable leg information.

**D. Simulation Training Details**
The estimator is trained in the RaiSim simulator using trajectories generated by a pretrained locomotion policy. 400 parallel environments run 4-second rollouts, with data recorded at 500 Hz (policy at 100 Hz, simulator at 4 kHz). Training uses Adam optimization with truncated backpropagation through time (sequence length 100).

Extensive **domain randomization** is applied during simulation training to improve generalization:
- **Locomotion**: Random commands and occasional standing commands.
- **Terrain**: Flat ground, Perlin and discrete height maps, random and inclined steps, stairs.
- **Contact**: Ground friction sampled from $U(0.4, 1.2)$, with sudden slip events simulated by reducing friction to $U(0.3, 0.4)$ for contacted feet.
- **Dynamics**: Perturbations to inertial properties, joint friction, and motor saturation.
- **Sensors and Timing**: Variations in sensor measurements and sampling intervals.
- **External Disturbances**: Torso disturbances applied to expose the estimator to transient motions beyond nominal gaits.

**E. Policy Randomization (PR)**
To counter overfitting to a single locomotion policy's specific gait timing, action distribution, and contact patterns, policy randomization is introduced. This broadens the range of behaviors encountered in simulation without requiring multiple policies. Specifically, the action scale is randomized (0.1 × $U(0.7, 1.2)$ rad), a Gaussian bias is added to the action mean ($N(0, (0.006 \text{ rad})^2)$), and target actions are low-pass filtered with a random coefficient ($\alpha \sim U(0, 0.6)$). This approach diversifies foot-contact timing, step length, impact profiles, and body-motion distributions, encouraging the estimator to learn more robust proprioceptive patterns.

**F. Real-World Fine-Tuning (FT)**
After simulation pretraining, the estimator undergoes real-world fine-tuning to reduce the sim-to-real gap. This involves:
- **Data**: Six 150-second outdoor trajectories (grass, flat ground) collected using a Raibo2 robot under random velocity commands.
- **Partial Fine-Tuning**: Only the GRU temporal encoder and the MLP prediction head are updated, while the CNN query encoder and the attention module are frozen. This strategy aims to adapt to real-world characteristics without overfitting to specific real-world dynamics, preserving simulation-learned representations.
- **Loss Function**: A 1-second-window relative error objective ($L_{RE}$) is used, comparing predicted and reference relative motions in the anchor body frame. To improve velocity estimation, the model consistency loss ($L_{model}$) is retained, using the previously predicted velocity. An L2-SP regularization term ($L_{SP}$) penalizes deviations from simulation-pretrained weights, preventing catastrophic forgetting. Direct velocity supervision is excluded due to inherent noise in real-world velocity labels.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TRACE involved extensive testing across diverse indoor and outdoor terrains, comparing its performance against classical, hybrid, and purely learning-based baselines. Ablation studies further clarified the contribution of specific architectural and training components.

**A. Attention Analysis**
Analysis of the learned attention weights in simulation revealed that the foot-aware cross-attention module effectively learns to identify reliable foot contact information.
- **Contact Alignment**: The foot with the highest attention score was in contact in 96.21% of samples. Contact feet consistently received substantially higher mean (0.2106) and median (0.1984) attention compared to swing feet (mean 0.0060, median 0.0000).
- **Reliability Measure**: The attention score discriminated between contact and swing states with an AUC of 0.9637. Critically, attention decreased rapidly as contact-point velocity increased. This suggests that the foot velocity loss successfully shaped attention into a "soft reliability measure" that adaptively downweights leg information when contaminated by slip, terrain deformation, or transient contact dynamics, without explicit thresholds.

**B. Indoor Terrain Experiments**
Evaluations on four indoor terrains (flat, rough, slippery, soft) demonstrated TRACE's superior performance in position and orientation estimation.
- **Overall Performance**: TRACE achieved the lowest position and orientation errors across all tested conditions, indicating robustness under nominal and unreliable contact scenarios.
- **Position ATE Reductions**: Compared to the strongest baseline for each terrain, TRACE reduced position ATE by 50.7% on flat, 39.2% on rough, 44.1% on slippery, and 53.8% on soft terrains. Similar reductions were observed for the 10-second position Relative Error (RE).
- **Soft Terrain Performance**: The gains were particularly significant on soft terrain, where unmodeled foot sinkage and terrain deformation often violate rigid-contact assumptions, demonstrating TRACE's ability to handle novel contact conditions.
- **Velocity Estimation**: While position improvements were more pronounced, TRACE generally achieved lower velocity errors on most terrains, though the coupling between velocity and position errors differs from filter-based methods.

**C. Outdoor Terrain Experiments**
Testing on various outdoor conditions (grass, hard ground, stairs, full course) confirmed TRACE's generalization capabilities in real-world environments.
- **Long-Horizon Accuracy**: TRACE accurately reconstructed complex trajectories over long outdoor sequences (e.g., a 72.22m full course). On the full course, it achieved the lowest mean 10-s position RE (37.4% reduction) and P90 (30.7% reduction) compared to the strongest baseline, alongside competitive ATE.
- **Terrain-Specific Robustness**: Position ATE was reduced by 17.0% on grass, 43.3% on hard ground, and 63.2% on stairs. Mean 10-s position RE also saw reductions of 51.9% on grass, 33.1% on hard ground, and 3.2% on stairs relative to baselines.
- **Challenging Contacts**: Significant RE improvement on grass indicated robustness to compliant and deformable contacts, while large ATE reduction on stairs showed improved consistency under repeated impacts and rapid elevation changes. Lower P90 values highlighted improved robustness to large estimation errors.

**D. Ablation Study I: Impact of Auxiliary Losses**
This study confirmed the importance of the physics-inspired auxiliary objectives:
- **Kinematic Consistency ($L_{model}$)**: Removing $L_{model}$ led to a 28.4% increase in mean position ATE and 28.8% in mean velocity ATE. This validated its role in enforcing consistency among predicted rotation, velocity, and displacement.
- **Foot Velocity Loss ($L_{foot}$)**: Without both $L_{model}$ and $L_{foot}$, performance degraded substantially. Adding back $L_{foot}$ (i.e., comparing "w/o both" to "w/o $L_{model}$") reduced mean position ATE by 83.8% and RE by 87.0%, with similar improvements in velocity errors. This was correlated with a reduction in mean swing-foot attention (from 0.0452 to 0.0060), demonstrating its role in selectively suppressing unreliable leg information.

**E. Ablation Study II: Policy Randomization (PR)**
PR was shown to improve generalization to unseen policies and real-world conditions:
- **Generalization to Unseen Policies**: In simulation, the model with PR achieved lower mean error and standard deviation across three independently trained unseen policies, reducing 10-s position RE by 30.7% compared to the model without PR.
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real**: In a zero-shot indoor evaluation (without real-world fine-tuning), PR reduced mean 10-s position RE from 0.1098 m to 0.0907 m, indicating better generalization.

**F. Ablation Study III: Real-World Fine-Tuning (FT)**
This study evaluated the proposed partial fine-tuning strategy:
- **Partial vs. No FT**: The proposed partial FT strategy yielded lower position ATE across all indoor terrains (which were disjoint from the fine-tuning data collected on grass and hard ground) compared to the model without FT.
- **Partial vs. Full FT**: Crucially, partial FT significantly reduced position ATE on slippery (48.7%) and soft (43.6%) terrains compared to full-network fine-tuning. This suggested that full-network fine-tuning overfit to the training distribution, whereas partial fine-tuning preserved generalizable simulation-learned representations.
- **Comparison with Legolas-FT**: TRACE, despite having significantly fewer parameters (0.2M vs. Legolas's 1.4M), achieved higher estimation accuracy, reducing position ATE by 73.8% and RE by 61.8% on average across indoor terrains compared to Legolas-FT. This demonstrated the effectiveness of TRACE's compact, foot-aware architecture.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TRACE research introduces several advancements in proprioceptive odometry for legged robots, with notable significance and potential impact:

**A. Enhanced Robustness under Unreliable Contact Conditions:**
The primary significance lies in TRACE's demonstrated ability to maintain accurate state estimation even under challenging and unreliable foot-ground contact conditions. This includes terrains causing slip, deformation (e.g., soft ground), and impacts (e.g., rough terrain, stairs), which often lead to substantial errors in traditional and even some learning-based methods. By adaptively downweighting unreliable leg information through a learned attention mechanism guided by physics-inspired losses, TRACE provides a more resilient solution. This robustness is critical for legged robots operating in unstructured and dynamic real-world environments.

**B. Reduction of Reliance on Manual Tuning and External Sensors:**
As an end-to-end learned estimator, TRACE removes the need for manually defined contact or slip thresholds, which are difficult to tune and generalize. Furthermore, its proprioceptive-only nature makes it independent of exteroceptive sensors (cameras, LiDAR, GPS), which are prone to failure in adverse conditions (poor lighting, occlusions, textureless scenes, GPS-denied areas). This independence expands the operational envelope for legged robots, enabling them to navigate autonomously in a wider range of challenging scenarios.

**C. Methodological Advancements in Learning-Based Odometry:**
The paper contributes novel methodological aspects to the field of learning-based state estimation:
- **Contact-Aware Attention**: The foot-aware cross-attention module represents a significant step towards enabling learned systems to reason about sensor reliability dynamically. This adaptive weighting of IMU and leg-wise kinematic tokens based on inferred contact reliability offers a more flexible approach than hard-coded logic.
- **Physics-Inspired Auxiliary Losses**: The integration of kinematic model consistency and foot velocity losses into the training objective demonstrates an effective way to embed physical principles into a purely data-driven model. This grounds the learned mapping in physical reality, preventing non-physical estimations and improving consistency and accuracy without explicitly modeling contact dynamics.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer Pipeline**: The two-stage training approach combining policy randomization and partial real-world fine-tuning addresses a major hurdle in robotics research. Policy randomization improves generalization in simulation to unseen locomotion behaviors, while partial fine-tuning effectively reduces the sim-to-real gap without causing catastrophic overfitting to limited real-world data.

**D. Superior Performance Across Diverse Environments:**
The empirical results, demonstrating consistent reductions in position drift across a wide array of indoor and outdoor terrains, including those with compliant, slippery, or uneven surfaces, underscore TRACE's practical effectiveness. Outperforming filtering-based, hybrid, and other purely learning-based baselines positions TRACE as a leading approach for proprioceptive odometry. The competitive performance even with fewer parameters than some baselines (e.g., Legolas-FT) suggests an efficient and effective architecture.

**E. Facilitating Autonomous Legged Robot Deployment:**
The ability to perform accurate and robust state estimation is foundational for higher-level robotic tasks such as locomotion control, path planning, and mapping. By providing a more reliable ego-motion estimate, TRACE can significantly contribute to the safe and effective deployment of legged robots in real-world applications, including inspection, exploration, logistics, and disaster response, where dependable autonomy in varied and unpredictable environments is paramount.

**F. Future Research Directions:**
The work also opens avenues for future research, such as investigating explicit IMU bias estimation and uncertainty prediction within the learning framework, which could further improve long-horizon accuracy. Extending the framework to other legged platforms, like humanoid robots with different foot geometries and contact dynamics, represents a natural progression of this research.
