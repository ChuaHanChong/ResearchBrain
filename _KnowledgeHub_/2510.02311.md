# Inferring Dynamic Physical Properties from Video Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces PhysVid, a new dataset with quantitative ground-truth annotations for elasticity, viscosity, and dynamic friction across synthetic and real-world videos. The study assesses whether pre-trained video foundation models inherently understand these dynamic physical properties, revealing that while models show promising capabilities on synthetic data, their performance often lags behind physics-informed oracle methods, especially for absolute value prediction and generalization to real-world scenarios without domain adaptation.

## Problem

- Inferring dynamic physical properties like elasticity, viscosity, and friction from visual observations has been a long-standing challenge in computer vision.
- Existing physics prediction methods often rely on simulation supervision or produce implicit representations that lack interpretability in terms of concrete physical quantities.
- There is a scarcity of suitable datasets providing quantitative ground-truth annotations for dynamic physical properties to rigorously evaluate AI models.

## Method

- The authors developed **PhysVid**, a new dataset with quantitative ground-truth annotations for elasticity, viscosity, and friction, across both synthetic and real-world videos.
- They propose oracle methods that leverage domain-specific visual cues and classical computer vision to establish an upper bound for inferable information.
- Pre-trained video foundation models (generative, self-supervised) are probed using a lightweight visual prompting mechanism, and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) are evaluated with various text prompting strategies for property inference.

## Results

- Oracle methods achieved near-perfect performance on synthetic data (ROC AUC **0.99**-**1.00**, Pearson Correlation **0.98**-**1.00**) and strong performance on real-world data (ROC AUC **0.87**-**1.00**, Pearson Correlation **0.80**-**0.87**), setting a high benchmark.
- DynamiCrafter and V-JEPA-2 showed strong performance on synthetic datasets (e.g., ROC AUC **1.00**, Pearson Correlation **0.89**-**0.99** for elasticity/viscosity on test-1), but performance for friction and real-world generalization was more challenging, with domain adaptation improving friction performance on real-world test-3 significantly.
- MLLMs generally showed lower performance on synthetic data (Pearson Correlations often near zero) but occasionally performed better on real-world test-3, with GPT-4o and Gemini-**2.5**-pro achieving high ROC AUCs (e.g., **0.99** for viscosity) for relative comparisons.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Video generative (DynamiCrafter) and self-supervised (V-JEPA-2) models can learn useful representations for inferring dynamic physical properties, achieving strong performance on synthetic data.
> - Current video foundation models exhibit a noticeable performance gap compared to physics-informed oracle methods, especially for absolute value prediction and generalizing to real-world scenarios without specific domain adaptation.
> - MLLMs generally perform worse than dedicated video models on quantitative physical tasks but can benefit from tailored prompting strategies, sometimes relying more on semantic cues than direct kinematic motion, particularly in real-world contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The ability to infer physical properties from visual observations is a long-standing challenge in computer vision and a critical component for developing more intelligent artificial systems, particularly in robotics and embodied AI. Historically, approaches to physics prediction from images and videos have explored several avenues. Early methods often relied on differentiable physics engines or learned simulators to estimate latent physical parameters such as mass, friction, or stiffness. Subsequent works focused on inferring specific attributes like viscosity or elasticity by leveraging task-specific visual cues, often heavily depending on simulation supervision, domain priors, or hand-crafted heuristics. More recently, the field has seen the emergence of unsupervised learning for intuitive physics, primarily through next-frame prediction tasks on large-scale everyday scenes. While these methods can capture latent dynamics, the resulting representations are typically implicit and may lack interpretability in terms of concrete physical quantities.

This current work positions itself at the intersection of these traditional physics prediction efforts and the advancements in large-scale video foundation models. Recent progress in video generative models (e.g., DynamiCrafter), self-supervised models (e.g., V-JEPA-2), and multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) has demonstrated capabilities in synthesizing realistic dynamics, learning general-purpose video representations, and performing semantic understanding tasks. However, the extent to which these powerful models acquire an explicit understanding of dynamic physical properties remains largely unexplored.

Existing datasets for physics-related tasks often provide ground-truth annotations for material properties, shadows, support relations, mass, or volume. Some benchmarks assess physical understanding qualitatively through video-image-text question-answering tasks. More recent efforts evaluate the physical plausibility of video generative models by analyzing object trajectories in generated videos. In contrast, this research addresses a specific gap by focusing on the *quantitative inference* of dynamic physical properties from videos, which necessitate temporal reasoning and sensitivity to subtle visual cues. The authors' contribution of a new dataset with quantitative ground-truth annotations for elasticity, viscosity, and friction, across synthetic and real-world videos, directly addresses the scarcity of suitable benchmarks for this specific problem. By directly probing pre-trained video foundation models without extensive task-specific heuristics or end-to-end pipelines trained from scratch, the study aims to assess their inherent physical comprehension.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether contemporary video foundation models possess an inherent understanding of dynamic physical properties, particularly those that manifest through temporal dynamics rather than static appearance. The authors specifically focus on three representative properties:

1. **Elasticity** of a bouncing object.
2. **Viscosity** of a flowing liquid.
3. **Dynamic friction** of an object sliding on a surface.

These properties were selected because their inference requires temporal information and sensitivity to subtle visual cues, such as object deformation, deceleration, liquid spreading, or oscillatory motion.

The motivation for this investigation is multi-faceted:

- **Human Intuitive Physics**: Humans exhibit a remarkable capacity to intuitively estimate physical properties from visual observations without direct interaction. For instance, individuals can approximate a ball's bounciness, a liquid's thickness, or a surface's slipperiness simply by observing motion. Endowing machines with similar capabilities is considered a critical step towards building more general, robust, and physically grounded artificial intelligence systems.
- **Enhancing AI Capabilities**: Visual systems capable of inferring dynamic physical properties from raw video could significantly enhance various AI applications. This includes improving robotic manipulation by allowing robots to adapt to different material properties, enabling more sophisticated embodied agents to navigate and interact with diverse physical environments, and deepening video understanding tasks beyond traditional recognition, detection, and segmentation to include predictive and explanatory capabilities.
- **Assessing Foundation Model Understanding**: Despite significant advancements in video foundation models—enabling realistic video synthesis, learning general-purpose video representations, and tackling semantic tasks—it remains unclear whether these models genuinely acquire an understanding of underlying physical principles. This research aims to assess their "physical common sense" beyond superficial pattern recognition or semantic labeling.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity**: A significant challenge in this domain is the lack of existing datasets that provide ground-truth quantitative annotations for dynamic physical properties in videos. To support their investigation, the authors are motivated to collect and introduce a new dataset, "PhysVid," specifically designed to facilitate the evaluation of these properties across both synthetic and real-world scenarios. This dataset is intended to enable studies on out-of-domain generalization.
- **Establishing Baselines and Upper Bounds**: By developing oracle methods that leverage privileged access to relevant visual cues and classical computer vision techniques, the research aims to establish an upper bound on what is inferable from visual input alone for these tasks, providing a benchmark against which foundation model performance can be measured.

In essence, the research seeks to gauge the depth of physical understanding embedded within modern video foundation models and identify current limitations, paving the way for future advancements in physically-informed AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors address the problem of estimating dynamic physical properties from videos using a structured approach involving a newly developed dataset, multiple model categories, and diverse inference techniques.

**Problem Formulation:**
The task is approached in two distinct formulations:
1. **Absolute Value Prediction:** Given a single video, the model predicts the numerical value of a physical property (e.g., elasticity coefficient, viscosity value).
2. **Relative Value Comparison:** Given a pair of videos from the same viewpoint, the model determines which video exhibits a higher value of the target physical property (binary classification).

In both scenarios, models are expected to be sensitive to changes in the physical property while remaining robust to nuisance parameters such as camera viewpoint, object appearance, and lighting conditions.

**PhysVid Dataset Collection:**
To facilitate the study, a new dataset, PhysVid, is introduced for elasticity, viscosity, and friction, comprising both synthetic and real-world videos.
- **Synthetic Data Generation:** Videos are generated using the Genesis physics simulator.
 - **Splits:** A training split (10,000 videos) and two test splits (1,000 videos each, test-1 and test-2) are created.
 - **Nuisance Parameters:** Training and test-1 use nuisance parameters sampled from domain A1 (e.g., drop height, camera viewpoint, object appearance, lighting). Test-2 introduces a distribution shift by sampling nuisance parameters from domain A2, differing from A1 (e.g., different camera viewpoints, lighting conditions, initial velocities).
 - **Ground Truth:** Property values are directly obtained from the simulator.
- **Real-World Data Collection (test-3):**
 - **Elasticity:** Videos are collected from YouTube ("ball bouncing experiments"). Ground truth elasticity values are estimated from `sqrt(h_bounce / h_drop)` using manual annotations of ball heights.
 - **Viscosity:** Videos are collected in-house under controlled conditions (fixed funnel nozzle, fixed pouring height) using various liquids. Ground truth viscosity values are sourced from standard physics reference tables.
 - **Friction:** Videos are collected in-house using different objects and surface materials. Objects are given an initial velocity by sliding them down a slope. Ground truth dynamic friction coefficients are measured using a spring dynamometer (`μ_k = F/G`). For friction, an additional real-world training split with disjoint objects and surfaces is introduced for domain adaptation.
- **Scale:** Each property dataset consists of 10,000 training videos, 1,000 videos for each synthetic test set (test-1, test-2), and 100 videos for the real-world test set (test-3).

**Inference Methods:**
Three main categories of methods are explored for inferring physical properties:

1. **Oracle Estimation:** These methods serve as an upper bound by leveraging domain-specific visual cues and classical computer vision techniques.
 - **Elasticity:** Ball trajectory (y-coordinates of segmented ball centroids) is normalized. A GRU network regresses elasticity from the sequence of normalized height ratios, approximating `sqrt(h_bounce / h_drop)`.
 - **Viscosity:** Segmentation masks track liquid spreading area over time. The area sequence is normalized by the initial contact area. The slope of the normalized area growth is inversely proportional to viscosity, approximated as `1 / (dA(t)/dt)`.
 - **Friction:** Object motion is mapped to a bird's-eye view using homography from object corner points. A parabola `x = αt^2 + βt + c` is fitted to the transformed trajectory. The acceleration `a` is derived from `2α`, and the dynamic friction coefficient `μ_k` is computed as `a/g`. For relative comparison, a sigmoid function is applied to the log ratio of estimated property values.

2. **Visual Prompting for Video Generative and Self-Supervised Models:** Pre-trained, frozen video foundation models are used as backbones.
 - **Feature Extraction:**
 - **Generative Model (DynamiCrafter):** Multi-scale features are extracted from U-Net layers at a specific diffusion time step (50).
 - **Self-Supervised Model (V-JEPA-2):** Feature tokens are extracted from all layers of the ViT encoder.
 - **Readout Mechanism:** A lightweight "visual prompt" architecture is trained. This involves a learnable query vector `q` that performs cross-attention with the backbone's internal feature representations `r`. The resulting context vector `p` from different layers are mapped via MLPs and averaged to produce the final video feature representation `P`.
 - **Property Prediction:** A separate MLP network `γ` is trained for each property to predict:
 - **Absolute Value:** `χ = γ(P)`. Supervised with L1 loss (elasticity, friction) or Log L1 loss (viscosity).
 - **Relative Value:** `ξ = γ([P1, P2])` for paired videos. Supervised with binary cross-entropy loss.

3. **Prompting Strategies for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs):** Off-the-shelf MLLMs (Qwen2.5-VL-Max, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro) are evaluated directly using various prompting techniques.
 - **Input:** Video frames are provided as visual input, accompanied by text prompts.
 - **Prompting Strategies:**
 - **Baseline Prompt:** Basic description of the task and query.
 - **Oracle Estimation Teaching:** Provides step-by-step guidance based on the oracle's visual cues and reasoning steps.
 - **Few-Shot Examples:** Includes video input and ground truth examples from the synthetic training split.
 - **Frame Index Provided:** Explicitly labels frames with their indices.
 - **Black Frames in Between:** (For relative tasks only) Inserts black frames to visually separate the two videos.
 - An ablation study on the elasticity task determined the most effective prompting strategies for absolute (Few-Shot Examples) and relative (Oracle Estimation Teaching) settings.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Relative Value Comparison:** ROC AUC score.
- **Absolute Value Prediction:** Pearson Correlation Coefficient between predictions and ground truth.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide insights into the capabilities of different model types in inferring dynamic physical properties across synthetic and real-world datasets.

**Oracle Estimator Performance:**
- The oracle methods demonstrated near-perfect performance on the synthetic test sets (test-1 and test-2) for all three properties (ROC AUC of 0.99-1.00, Pearson Correlation of 0.98-1.00).
- On the real-world test set (test-3), the oracle maintained strong performance (ROC AUC of 0.87-1.00, Pearson Correlation of 0.80-0.87). These results confirm that the tasks are largely solvable from visual cues by applying geometric and physical principles, establishing a robust upper bound for performance.

**Video Generative (DynamiCrafter) and Self-Supervised (V-JEPA-2) Models:**
- **Comparable Performance:** Both DynamiCrafter and V-JEPA-2 achieved strong results on synthetic data, with generally comparable performance across properties. This suggests that both generative and self-supervised training paradigms can lead to representations useful for physical property inference.
 - For elasticity, both models achieved ROC AUCs of 1.00 and Pearson Correlations of 0.89-0.92 on test-1, with slight drops on test-2.
 - For viscosity, both models achieved ROC AUCs of 1.00 and Pearson Correlations of 0.98-0.99 on test-1, maintaining high performance on test-2.
- **Generalization to Real-World Data (test-3):**
 - **Elasticity and Viscosity:** Models showed reasonable generalization, with V-JEPA-2 performing particularly well for elasticity (ROC AUC 0.99, Pearson 0.37) and DynamiCrafter for viscosity (ROC AUC 0.93, Pearson 0.44).
 - **Friction Challenge:** Friction proved more challenging for generalization to real-world data (test-3). V-JEPA-2's ROC AUC dropped to 0.48 and Pearson to 0.21. DynamiCrafter also saw reduced performance (ROC AUC 0.97, Pearson 0.25). This is attributed to the synthetic data's reliance on clear visual references (e.g., ground plane grids) that are often absent in real videos, and the inherent complexity of inferring friction from higher-order motion and projective geometry.
- **Domain Adaptation:** For friction, fine-tuning the visual prompting architecture with an additional real-world training split significantly improved performance on the real test-3 set (ROC AUC 0.87* for V-JEPA-2 and 0.97* for DynamiCrafter; Pearson Correlation 0.73* for V-JEPA-2 and 0.80* for DynamiCrafter). This indicates that while the foundation models can learn relevant representations, explicit exposure to real-world variations for complex properties is beneficial.
- **Performance Gap to Oracle:** A notable performance gap exists between these foundation models and the oracle, especially for absolute value prediction, suggesting that precisely regressing quantitative physical values remains a significant challenge.

**Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs):**
- **Overall Inferior Performance:** MLLMs generally exhibited lower performance compared to the generative and self-supervised video models on most synthetic test splits, particularly for absolute value prediction (Pearson Correlations often close to zero or negative on synthetic data).
- **Impact of Prompting Strategies:** Performance improved with suitable prompting strategies. The ablation study indicated Few-Shot Examples were best for absolute prediction, and Oracle Estimation Teaching for relative comparison.
- **Better on Real Data:** MLLMs often performed better on the real-world test set (test-3) than on the synthetic test sets. This might be due to their familiarity with real-world visual semantics, potentially leveraging semantic cues (e.g., liquid type, object category) rather than purely kinematic visual motion.
 - For viscosity, GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-pro achieved strong ROC AUCs (0.99 and 0.95 respectively) on test-3.
 - For friction, Gemini-2.5-pro achieved a high ROC AUC of 0.97 on test-3.
- **Largest Gap to Oracle:** MLLMs showed the largest performance gap compared to the oracle, suggesting limitations in their quantitative physical reasoning from visual input.

**Qualitative Observations:**
- Scatter plots illustrated performance degradation from in-distribution synthetic (test-1) to out-of-distribution synthetic (test-2) to real-world (test-3) evaluations for V-JEPA-2's elasticity predictions.
- Qualitative examples indicated that while generative models and MLLMs could sometimes make reasonable predictions, self-supervised models occasionally made confident but incorrect predictions, especially in relative comparisons for friction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of physical reasoning in AI and has several potential impacts:

- **Benchmark for Dynamic Physical Properties:** The introduction of the PhysVid dataset, with its quantitative ground-truth annotations for elasticity, viscosity, and dynamic friction across synthetic and real-world videos, fills a notable gap in existing benchmarks. This dataset facilitates rigorous evaluation of physical understanding in video models, including out-of-domain generalization capabilities, and will likely serve as a valuable resource for future research in this area.
- **Assessment of Video Foundation Models' Physical Understanding:** The study provides an initial, systematic assessment of whether state-of-the-art video generative, self-supervised, and multi-modal large language models inherently acquire an understanding of dynamic physical properties. It demonstrates that while these models show promising capabilities on synthetic data, their performance often lags behind physics-informed oracle methods, particularly for absolute value prediction and generalizing to complex real-world scenarios without domain adaptation.
- **Methodological Insights for Probing Foundation Models:** The proposed lightweight visual prompting mechanism for frozen video generative and self-supervised models offers an efficient way to extract physical cues from pre-trained representations. Furthermore, the exploration of various prompting strategies for MLLMs highlights the importance of crafting appropriate language prompts to elicit more accurate physical reasoning, even if MLLMs still struggle with precise quantitative tasks.
- **Identification of Current Limitations:** The findings underscore that current video foundation models, despite their impressive abilities in video generation and representation learning, often lack a deep, quantitative physical understanding necessary for robust real-world applications. The particular challenges observed with friction inference and the general performance gap in absolute value prediction indicate that models tend to fall short when precise physical values need to be regressed. The observation that MLLMs sometimes leverage semantic cues over direct visual motion for physical inference also points to a limitation in their integration of visual information.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** This work identifies critical directions for future research. Enhancing physical reasoning in video models is a key area, suggesting the need for architectures that better integrate physical laws, develop more robust representations of material properties, or are trained with more physically diverse and annotated datasets. The performance gains observed with domain adaptation for friction further indicate that incorporating more real-world physical variation during training could be beneficial.
- **Implications for Downstream Applications:** Improved physical reasoning capabilities in AI models would have a significant impact on various applications. This includes:
 - **Robotics:** Robots could perform more dexterous manipulation tasks by understanding object material properties (e.g., how to grasp a fragile object, how much force to apply to a bouncy surface).
 - **Embodied AI:** Intelligent agents could better navigate and interact with complex physical environments by anticipating object behaviors (e.g., predicting how a liquid will flow, how an object will slide).
 - **Video Understanding:** Advanced video analytics could move beyond semantic labeling to predict future physical states or explain observed phenomena, leading to more profound video content understanding.

In conclusion, this research establishes a foundational benchmark and provides valuable insights into the current state of physical understanding in video foundation models, highlighting both their potential and the significant challenges that remain in building truly physically intelligent AI systems.
