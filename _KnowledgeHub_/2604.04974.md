# From Video to Control: A Survey of Learning Manipulation Interfaces from Temporal Visual Data

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey provides a structured taxonomy for methods that learn robot control interfaces from large-scale, non-action-annotated video, categorizing them into three families and analyzing their control-integration properties. It identifies critical challenges in effectively connecting video-derived predictions to dependable robot behavior.

## Problem

- High cost and difficulty of collecting large-scale, action-labeled robot demonstration data, which limits progress in robotic manipulation.
- Vast quantities of unannotated video data exist, depicting diverse physical interactions, but critically lack synchronized robot action labels and often exhibit domain shifts.
- Existing literature reviews primarily focus on foundation models or general world models, rather than the specific design of interfaces that translate video-derived dynamics into robot actions.

## Method

- Introduces an ==interface-centric taxonomy== that classifies methods based on where and how video-derived temporal structure enters the robot's control stack and what ==control properties== it enables.
- Categorizes approaches into three recurring interface families: ==Direct Video-Action Policies==, ==Latent-Action Methods==, and ==Explicit Visual Interfaces==.
- Analyzes ==control-integration properties== for each family, including ==control-loop closure==, opportunities for ==pre-execution verification==, and likely sources of physical inconsistencies or ==grounding failures==.

## Results

- The survey systematically classifies methods into three families: Direct Video–Action Policies (implicit video-action link), Latent-Action Methods (compact learned intermediate "latent actions"), and Explicit Visual Interfaces (interpretable visual targets like subgoals or trajectories).
- It details control integration challenges unique to each family, such as limited pre-execution verification for direct policies, issues with identifiability and physical consistency for latent actions, and tracking errors and hallucinated physics for explicit visual interfaces.
- A cross-cutting finding is the lack of standardized, comparable evaluation protocols across the field, which hinders robust cross-method comparisons and progress toward deployable systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The most critical unresolved gaps reside in the "robotics integration layer," which involves reliably connecting video-derived predictions to dependable robot behavior, encompassing grounding, loop closure, and physical feasibility.
> - Different interface designs—implicit (direct policies), abstract (latent actions), or explicit (visual targets)—present distinct trade-offs in transparency, physical consistency, and robustness to perception errors.
> - Action-free video provides a scalable prior over dynamics, but not direct executability, often necessitating a two-stage approach (video pretraining followed by robot-specific grounding) for many methods.
