# From Video to Control: A Survey of Learning Manipulation Interfaces from Temporal Visual Data

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey provides a structured taxonomy for methods that learn robot control interfaces from large-scale, non-action-annotated video, categorizing them into three families and analyzing their control-integration properties. It identifies critical challenges in effectively connecting video-derived predictions to dependable robot behavior.

## Problem

- High cost and difficulty of collecting large-scale, action-labeled robot demonstration data, which limits progress in robotic manipulation.
- Vast quantities of unannotated video data exist, depicting diverse physical interactions, but critically lack synchronized robot action labels and often exhibit domain shifts.
- Existing literature reviews primarily focus on foundation models or general world models, rather than the specific design of interfaces that translate video-derived dynamics into robot actions.

## Method

- Introduces an ==interface-centric taxonomy== that classifies methods based on where and how video-derived temporal structure enters the robot's control stack and what ==control properties== it enables.
- Categorizes approaches into three recurring interface families: ==Direct Video-Action Policies==, ==Latent-Action Methods==, and ==Explicit Visual Interfaces==.
- Analyzes ==control-integration properties== for each family, including ==control-loop closure==, opportunities for ==pre-execution verification==, and likely sources of physical inconsistencies or ==grounding failures==.

## Results

- The survey systematically classifies methods into three families: Direct Video–Action Policies (implicit video-action link), Latent-Action Methods (compact learned intermediate "latent actions"), and Explicit Visual Interfaces (interpretable visual targets like subgoals or trajectories).
- It details control integration challenges unique to each family, such as limited pre-execution verification for direct policies, issues with identifiability and physical consistency for latent actions, and tracking errors and hallucinated physics for explicit visual interfaces.
- A cross-cutting finding is the lack of standardized, comparable evaluation protocols across the field, which hinders robust cross-method comparisons and progress toward deployable systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The most critical unresolved gaps reside in the "robotics integration layer," which involves reliably connecting video-derived predictions to dependable robot behavior, encompassing grounding, loop closure, and physical feasibility.
> - Different interface designs—implicit (direct policies), abstract (latent actions), or explicit (visual targets)—present distinct trade-offs in transparency, physical consistency, and robustness to perception errors.
> - Action-free video provides a scalable prior over dynamics, but not direct executability, often necessitating a two-stage approach (video pretraining followed by robot-specific grounding) for many methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation is a foundational capability for embodied artificial intelligence, with applications spanning household assistance, logistics, and industrial automation. Recent progress in large-scale learning-based policies has enabled generalist manipulation systems, but these approaches are significantly constrained by the high cost and difficulty of collecting extensive robot trajectories with synchronized actions, proprioception, and rewards. In contrast, vast quantities of non-action-annotated video exist on the web and personal devices, capturing diverse physical interactions and temporal dynamics without requiring explicit robot action labels. The challenge lies in translating this rich temporal visual structure into reliable robotic control, given the absence of action supervision and the domain gaps related to embodiment, viewpoint, and physical constraints.

This survey distinguishes itself from prior reviews by adopting an "interface-centric" perspective. Existing surveys primarily focus on:
- **Foundation models and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems**: These often organize literature by model families, system modules, training pipelines, or action-conditioned policies, largely relying on action-labeled robot trajectories or robot interaction data. While they discuss the use of internet-scale video for signals like trajectories and affordances, their primary grounding signal remains robot action data.
- **Video-based and dynamics-centered surveys**: Earlier works on vision-based manipulation frequently focused on sensing and control pipelines, such as visual servoing or 3D perception. More recent surveys emphasize predictive modeling and world models, but are typically grounded in robot interaction data or action-labeled demonstrations.
- **Learning from Video (LfV) surveys**: These explicitly discuss leveraging large-scale internet or in-the-wild video despite the lack of action labels and domain shift.

In contrast to these approaches, the present survey specifically centers on **non-action-annotated temporal video** as a primary supervision source. It analyzes how video-derived temporal structure is explicitly connected to robot control, focusing on the construction of the video-to-control interface and its implications for control-loop closure, pre-execution verification, embodiment transfer, and potential failure modes. This unique focus leads to a distinct interface-centric taxonomy that structures the literature based on the mechanism linking video dynamics to control, rather than merely by model architecture or generative technique.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation behind this survey stems from a fundamental tension in contemporary robot learning: the widespread availability of action-free video data versus the scarcity and expense of robot data with synchronized action labels. While robot demonstrations have driven significant progress in generalist manipulation, their collection is a bottleneck for scalability. Conversely, diverse video sources (e.g., egocentric human videos, web videos of robots) offer abundant information about object motion, contact dynamics, and scene evolution, but lack direct action supervision and present significant domain shifts from a target robot's embodiment and sensing modalities.

The **unifying question** that guides this survey is:
"How can large-scale, non-action-annotated video—viewed as a scalable observation of world dynamics—be used to learn control interfaces that support reliable robotic manipulation?"

To address this question, the survey outlines three primary objectives:

1. **To establish an interface-centric taxonomy**: This objective aims to organize the existing literature by systematically categorizing where and how video-derived temporal structure is integrated into a robot's control stack. The taxonomy is developed along two design axes: the explicitness of the interface (from implicit to explicit) and its distance from low-level robot actions (from direct motor commands to abstract targets like subgoals). This framework seeks to clarify recurring design patterns that cut across varied model architectures.
2. **To provide a per-family control-integration analysis**: This objective involves a detailed examination of each taxonomic family to understand how its interface design influences critical control properties. Specifically, it analyzes methods based on how they achieve control-loop closure, what opportunities they offer for verification or inspection prior to execution, and where potential physical inconsistencies or grounding failures are most likely to arise.
3. **To present a cross-family synthesis and identify future research directions**: This objective integrates findings from the individual family analyses to identify overarching challenges in the field. The survey introduces the concept of the "robotics integration layer" as the primary bottleneck, encompassing issues related to grounding video-derived predictions to dependable robot behavior, maintaining adequate control rates, ensuring physical feasibility, and enabling robust failure recovery. Based on this synthesis, it outlines consolidated research themes to guide future work.

In essence, the survey is motivated by the need to effectively leverage scalable, action-free visual data for robotic manipulation, moving beyond mere visual understanding to actual, reliable control, while explicitly addressing the unique challenges posed by the interface between learned temporal dynamics and real-world robot execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey employs an interface-centric approach to analyze methods that utilize non-action-annotated temporal video for robotic manipulation. Its methodology is structured around rigorous inclusion/exclusion criteria, a novel taxonomy, and a systematic analysis of control integration.

**Paper Selection Criteria:**
The survey includes methods that satisfy all three of the following conditions:
- **Temporal video supervision:** The method must leverage temporal continuity in video as a core training signal (e.g., video prediction, trajectory forecasting, learning temporally predictive latents from frame transitions), excluding methods relying solely on static images or non-temporal contrastive objectives.
- **Interface learned from non-action video:** The primary video-to-control interface must be learned or pretrained using video data for which aligned robot actions are unavailable or unused in the learning objective. This covers human videos, robot videos without actions, or mixed sources, though auxiliary annotations like captions or masks are permitted.
- **Grounding to manipulation:** The learned interface must be connected to robotic manipulation through robot data (which can be limited) and/or applied within a policy, planner, or control loop.

**Exclusion Criteria:**
The survey explicitly excludes methods such as those relying only on static visual cues, methods using temporal objectives primarily for visual state representation where policies are learned mainly from action-labeled robot data, approaches learning rewards/values without predictive models, general video world models focused on action-conditioned simulation, large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models trained primarily on action-labeled robot demonstrations, single-demonstration imitation systems, and latent-action methods where embodiment-specific action supervision is involved in *learning* the latent-action interface itself. This focused scope ensures the survey adheres to its core theme of leveraging non-action-annotated temporal video.

**Taxonomy Design:**
The core of the methodology is an "interface-centric taxonomy" organized along two key design axes:
1. **Interface Explicitness (x-axis):** Measures how directly the connection between visual change and control is externalized, ranging from implicit (inside a shared representation) to explicit (interpretable targets).
2. **Distance from Robot Actions (y-axis):** Quantifies the degree of control abstraction, from low-level motor commands to high-level abstract targets (e.g., subgoals, pose plans).

Based on these axes, the survey identifies and structures the literature into three main families (illustrated in Figures 2 and 3):
- **Direct Video–Action Policies:** Keep the video-to-control interface implicit, where temporal prediction shapes internal representations from which actions are directly decoded.
- **Latent-Action Methods:** Introduce compact, learned action-like intermediates that route temporal structure through an abstract, internal representation.
- **Explicit Visual Interfaces:** Predict structured, human-interpretable targets (e.g., subgoal images, video plans, trajectories, pose sequences) for downstream control.

Each method is assigned to a family based on the signal that carries the *primary control burden* at the final grounding stage, ensuring a consistent classification.

**Analysis Structure:**
For each of the three families, the survey provides a detailed analysis covering:
- **Architectural design**: How temporal prediction is coupled with action generation.
- **Training workflow**: Data sources for both video-derived temporal learning and robot-specific action grounding.
- **Deployment mechanisms**: How the learned interface is integrated into the robot’s control loop, including execution modes (e.g., stepwise, chunked, receding-horizon) and real-robot evaluation status.
- **Control-integration properties**: A dedicated analysis of how interface design impacts control-loop closure, opportunities for pre-execution verification, and where control failures are likely to enter.
- **Quantitative snapshots**: Reported results from original papers are included (with caveats about comparability across different benchmarks and settings) to illustrate performance and maturity.

**Literature Search and Preprints:**
The authors conducted a comprehensive literature search across Google Scholar, arXiv, and Semantic Scholar, supplemented by conference proceedings and reference chasing, up to May 25, 2026. Preprints are included selectively to represent emerging design directions, with their quantitative results treated as indicative rather than a basis for comparative ranking. The use of generative AI for language polishing is also disclosed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's primary findings stem from its comprehensive analysis and synthesis of the literature, organized by its novel interface-centric taxonomy. These findings characterize the current state of video-based robot manipulation, highlight common challenges, and identify critical areas for future development.

**A. Taxonomy and Design Patterns:**
The proposed interface-centric taxonomy successfully categorizes diverse methods into three families: Direct Video–Action Policies, Latent-Action Methods, and Explicit Visual Interfaces. This framework reveals recurring design patterns across methods, clarifying the trade-offs between interface explicitness, distance from robot actions, and data requirements for grounding.

**B. Family-Specific Insights:**

1. **Direct Video–Action Policies:**
 - **Functionality:** These methods aim for tight coupling, using temporal video prediction mainly to shape dynamics-aware internal representations during training. At deployment, they often bypass explicit video generation for efficiency, directly outputting robot actions.
 - **Coupling Mechanisms:** Employ shared autoregressive token spaces (e.g., GR-1, GR-2), joint diffusion processes (e.g., PAD, UWM), or shared latent encoders with decoupled video and action heads (e.g., UVA, Fast-WAM). Cosmos Policy uses latent-frame interfaces for policy and planning by treating actions/states/values as additional latent frames.
 - **Control Integration:** Execution modes vary from stepwise to chunked or receding-horizon control. The implicit nature of the video-to-action link makes these policies difficult to inspect, debug, or verify before execution, with physical inconsistencies often appearing directly as failed actions.

2. **Latent-Action Methods:**
 - **Functionality:** These methods learn compact, action-like intermediate variables from observation transitions (action-free video) to support planning or policy learning, subsequently grounding them to robot actions with limited labeled data. This modularizes discovery from grounding.
 - **Latent Space Design:** Focus on learning latent actions that capture controllable, task-relevant change (e.g., via disentanglement, contrastive objectives like ConLA, or compositional structures like RotVLA, ALAM). HiLAM introduces hierarchical latent actions for long-horizon skill learning.
 - **Control Integration:** Latent actions can serve as standalone control interfaces (e.g., CLASP via MPC-style planning), or more commonly, as pretraining targets or conditioning signals within instruction-conditioned policies (e.g., LAPA, Moto, UniVLA). Key challenges include ensuring latent identifiability, maintaining physical consistency of latent forward models, and mitigating grounding brittleness due to the action-space gap between abstract latents and specific robot controls.

3. **Explicit Visual Interfaces:**
 - **Functionality:** These methods predict human-interpretable visual signals (e.g., subgoal images, video plans, 2D/3D trajectories, 6D poses) as explicit targets for a downstream robot controller. This offers transparency and can facilitate cross-embodiment transfer.
 - **Interface Types:** Include dense video plans (e.g., UniPi, Gen2Act), subgoal images (e.g., SuSIE, CLOVER), and various trajectory-based interfaces (e.g., 2D pixel tracks in ATM, 3D object points in GeneralFlow, 6D poses in ZeroMimic). Many methods involve an "interface transfer" step, converting visual predictions into geometric targets (e.g., video to 6D object poses in AVDC, RIGVid).
 - **Control Integration:** Controllers track these explicit targets, operating in open-loop, with periodic replanning, or in closed-loop fashion. Challenges include the "tracking-error problem" (kinematic infeasibility, out-of-workspace targets), fragility of multi-stage interface-transfer pipelines (compounding perception errors), and "hallucinated physics" where generated plans are visually plausible but dynamically inconsistent. Pre-execution verification opportunities are higher in this family due to the interpretable nature of the interface.

**C. Cross-Family Synthesis and The Robotics Integration Layer:**
The survey's cross-family analysis (Table 11 and 12) reveals that the choice of interface design dictates how robot control loops are closed, where physical inconsistency arises, and what opportunities exist for verification. A central finding is the identification of the **"robotics integration layer"** as the most critical bottleneck. This layer encompasses the mechanisms that translate video-derived predictions into dependable robot behavior, including grounding interfaces to executable actions, closing control loops at adequate rates, verifying physical feasibility, and robustly recovering from errors, all within a specific robot’s embodiment and sensing constraints.

**D. Empirical Evidence and Evaluation:**
While all families demonstrate progress in leveraging action-free video, direct quantitative comparisons across methods remain challenging due to fragmented evaluation protocols, varying robot embodiments, diverse pretraining corpora, and inconsistent data budgets. Most methods report improvements over baselines within their specific settings, but robust cross-method benchmarks are still evolving. Real-robot deployment is a crucial validation, and many methods report such evaluations, though often on custom task suites.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey makes several significant contributions to the field of robotic manipulation and has substantial potential impact:

**A. Conceptual Clarity and Structured Understanding:**
The primary significance lies in providing an **interface-centric taxonomy** that offers a novel and clarifying framework for understanding the rapidly expanding literature on learning manipulation interfaces from temporal visual data. By organizing methods around *where and how video-derived temporal structure connects to robot control*, the survey moves beyond mere architectural or generative technique classifications. This structured approach helps researchers identify commonalities, differentiate distinct design philosophies, and systematically compare the inherent trade-offs (e.g., explicitness, transferability, robustness) across diverse approaches. This structured understanding is crucial for navigating a complex and fast-evolving domain.

**B. Highlighting the "Robotics Integration Layer" as the Critical Bottleneck:**
A key finding is the identification of the **"robotics integration layer"** as the most pressing unresolved challenge. This shifts the focus from solely optimizing visual prediction or representation learning to the practical and often overlooked complexities of translating high-level visual understanding into reliable, physically grounded robot actions. This emphasizes issues such as:
- **Robust Grounding:** Reliably mapping abstract video-derived signals to a robot's specific action space.
- **Control-Loop Closure:** Achieving adequate control rates given the computational demands of generative models.
- **Physical Feasibility and Consistency:** Ensuring that predicted behaviors are not just visually plausible but kinematically reachable, collision-free, and dynamically valid.
- **Failure Detection and Recovery:** Developing mechanisms to verify planned actions before execution and to gracefully handle unexpected events or errors.
By spotlighting this integration layer, the survey directs future research towards crucial engineering and fundamental challenges that determine real-world deployability.

**C. Guiding Future Research Directions:**
The report consolidates open problems into four actionable research themes:
1. **Execution-aware and physically grounded learning:** Encouraging the development of models that explicitly incorporate physical constraints and robot capabilities during video-based learning, moving beyond visual plausibility to physically actionable predictions.
2. **Robust grounding and cross-embodiment transfer:** Focusing on mechanisms to disentangle controllable aspects from exogenous dynamics in diverse video data and developing efficient, embodiment-agnostic adaptation strategies.
3. **Multimodal sensing and contact-rich manipulation:** Advocating for the integration of tactile, force, and proprioceptive sensing to enable learning for more complex tasks involving deformable objects or intricate contact dynamics.
4. **Evaluation, verification, and safe deployment:** Calling for standardized benchmarks, robust monitoring tools, and explicit verification modules to assess robustness, ensure safety, and facilitate real-world deployment.

**D. Potential Impact on Robot Learning and Generalization:**
The successful addressing of these challenges, informed by the survey's insights, could significantly accelerate progress in robot learning. By enabling robots to effectively learn from the massive quantities of existing action-free video—human, web, or unlabelled robot footage—the field can overcome the prohibitive data bottleneck currently limiting generalist robot capabilities. This could lead to:
- **More Generalist Robots:** Robots capable of performing a wider array of tasks in diverse environments with less robot-specific training data.
- **Enhanced Autonomy:** Systems that can adapt to novel scenarios and recover from unexpected situations more gracefully.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** Interfaces that are more transparent and interpretable, fostering trust and easier human supervision.

In conclusion, this survey serves as a critical reference point for researchers and practitioners, providing a structured map of the landscape, identifying core challenges, and charting a clear course for future innovations to transform scalable video observations into dependable robotic control.
