# Towards a Sharp Analysis of Offline Policy Learning for $f$-Divergence-Regularized Contextual Bandits

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UCLA and UIUC provided a sharp theoretical analysis for offline policy learning in f-divergence-regularized contextual bandits, demonstrating an eΘ(ϵ⁻¹) sample complexity for reverse KL regularization under single-policy concentrability and for strongly convex f-divergences without any data coverage dependency. This work established the sufficiency and necessity of single-policy concentrability for KL-regularization and showed that strongly convex f-divergences inherently mitigate data distribution shift challenges.

## Problem

- Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) struggles to achieve optimal policies from static datasets due to data hunger and instability, often relying on policy regularization.
- Prior theoretical analyses for f-divergence-regularized offline RL yielded suboptimal eO(ϵ⁻²) sample complexity bounds or required strong "all-policy concentrability" for tighter eO(ϵ⁻¹) bounds.
- A critical open problem was determining the weakest data coverage conditions necessary for near-optimal offline learning with f-divergence-regularized objectives, particularly for reverse KL divergence.

## Method

- Proposed **KL-PCB**, an algorithm for KL-regularized contextual bandits, which integrates ==pessimistic reward estimation== using a confidence bonus to address uncertainty.
- Introduced f-CB, an algorithm for f-divergence-regularized contextual bandits with strongly convex f, which directly computes the policy from a least-squares reward estimate.
- Utilized novel analytical techniques, including a moment-based analysis for KL-regularization and exploiting the dual-Bregman perspective for strongly convex f-divergences, to derive sharper sample complexity bounds.

## Results

- KL-PCB achieved an eO(η D²π* ϵ⁻¹ log N_G(ϵ)) sample complexity for KL-regularized contextual bandits, confirming the eΘ(ϵ⁻¹) rate under "single-policy concentrability" and matching information-theoretic lower bounds.
- The f-CB algorithm for strongly convex f-divergences attained an eΘ(α⁻¹ηϵ⁻¹ log N_G(ϵ)) sample complexity, a key finding demonstrating independence from data coverage conditions.
- Empirical results across various bandit environments validated the n⁻¹ rate for sub-optimality and consistently supported the theoretical predictions on concentrability dependency for KL-regularization and coverage independence for strongly convex f-divergences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reverse KL-regularization can achieve an eΘ(ϵ⁻¹) sample complexity under a weaker "single-policy concentrability" condition, which is shown to be both sufficient and necessary.
> - Strongly convex f-divergences inherently offer a stronger regularization effect, enabling eΘ(ϵ⁻¹) sample complexity without any dependency on data coverage conditions.
> - Novel analytical techniques, including a moment-based lemma, effectively transform risk bounds from all-policy to single-policy concentrability dependencies for KL-regularization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of offline reinforcement learning (RL), specifically focusing on the theoretical understanding of algorithms that incorporate f-divergence regularization. Offline RL aims to learn optimal policies from pre-collected, static datasets without further environment interaction, addressing challenges such as data hunger and instability often encountered in online RL. A common strategy in this domain is to regularize the policy objective using divergences, with Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence being a prominent choice due to its theoretical properties as both an f-divergence and a Bregman divergence, as well as its practical prevalence in methods like entropy-regularized RL and fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs) with human feedback (RLHF).

Prior theoretical work in RL, particularly concerning KL-regularization, often analyzes the unregularized reward maximization objective, which typically leads to an Ω(ϵ⁻²) sample complexity for finding an ϵ-optimal policy. More recent research has shifted focus to analyzing sub-optimality with respect to the *regularized* objective itself, where an Ω(ϵ⁻¹) sample complexity is theoretically possible. However, existing analyses in this direction have limitations: some still yield 𝑒O(ϵ⁻²) bounds, while others achieve 𝑒O(ϵ⁻¹) but impose stringent "all-policy concentrability" conditions, which require the behavior policy to cover a broad range of possible actions uniformly. This highlights a gap in the literature regarding tight sample complexity analyses for f-divergence-regularized objectives, particularly under realistic or weaker data coverage conditions.

A central issue in offline RL is "data coverage" or "concentrability," which quantifies the distributional shift between the data-collecting (behavior) policy and the target policies. The current work aims to address the question of the weakest coverage conditions necessary for near-optimal offline learning with f-divergence-regularized objectives, distinguishing between reverse KL divergence and f-divergences induced by strongly convex functions. Furthermore, the paper relates to existing pessimism principles in offline RL, noting that traditional pessimism analyses for unregularized objectives do not fully exploit the strong concavity properties of KL-regularized objectives, which could lead to suboptimal sample complexity bounds. The research also extends its analysis to contextual dueling bandits (CDBs), a variant relevant for preference-based RL, thereby engaging with a growing area in RLHF.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to provide a "sharp analysis" of offline policy learning for f-divergence-regularized contextual bandits, aiming to precisely define the sample complexity and the necessary data coverage conditions. This is motivated by a significant open problem in the field: "What is the weakest coverage condition required for offline learning to be near-optimal with respect to f-divergence-regularized objectives?"

More specifically, the authors articulate several key objectives:
1. **For Reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence Regularization**: To establish near-optimal sample complexity guarantees (specifically, 𝑒Θ(ϵ⁻¹)) under the weakest possible data coverage conditions. This involves demonstrating that "single-policy concentrability" is both sufficient and necessary, thereby improving upon prior work that either yielded looser bounds (𝑒O(ϵ⁻²)) or required stronger "all-policy concentrability" for 𝑒O(ϵ⁻¹) bounds. The motivation here stems from the widespread practical use of KL regularization in various RL applications, including recent advancements in LLM fine-tuning, and the existing lack of a tight theoretical understanding under realistic data constraints.
2. **For f-Divergences with Strongly Convex f**: To investigate whether f-divergence regularizers induced by strongly convex functions (e.g., χ²-divergence) can offer advantages in terms of data coverage requirements. The underlying hypothesis is that the "nicer" (stronger) curvature property of such functions might lead to improved coverage dependencies, or even eliminate them, for the desired 𝑒Θ(ϵ⁻¹) sample complexity. This is motivated by observations in other literature suggesting that strongly convex f-divergences can relax data coverage requirements or prevent reward hacking when performance is measured against unregularized objectives.
3. **Development of Novel Analytical Techniques**: To devise new theoretical tools and algorithms that can leverage the specific curvature properties of f-divergence-regularized objectives. This includes refining existing pessimism-based analytical pipelines to be more effective for regularized objectives.
4. **Empirical Verification and Generalization**: To corroborate theoretical findings through numerical experiments and to extend the analysis to related problem settings, specifically contextual dueling bandits, to demonstrate the versatility of the proposed methods.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for a precise theoretical foundation for f-divergence-regularized offline RL, particularly regarding the crucial role of data coverage, and aims to develop both algorithms and analytical techniques to achieve sharp sample complexity bounds under minimal assumptions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research primarily employs a theoretical approach, developing novel algorithms and analytical techniques to derive sample complexity bounds, complemented by empirical validations.

**Problem Setup:**
The study focuses on offline contextual bandits, where the agent learns from a pre-collected, static dataset $D = \{(s_i, a_i, r_i)\}_{i=1}^n$. Contexts $s_i$ are sampled from a distribution ρ, and actions $a_i$ from a reference policy $π_{ref}$. Rewards $r_i$ are noisy observations of a true reward function $r(s,a)$. The reward function $r$ is assumed to belong to a known function class $\mathcal{G}$ (Assumption 2.1) with a polynomial covering number (Assumption 2.3), enabling general function approximation.
The objective function for policy $π$ is defined as $J(π) = E_{(s,a)∼ρ×π}[r(s,a)] - \eta^{-1}D_f(π(\cdot|s)∥π_{ref}(\cdot|s))$, where $D_f$ is an f-divergence and $\eta$ is an inverse temperature parameter. The goal is to find an ϵ-optimal policy relative to this regularized objective.

**Concentrability Conditions:**
Two types of concentrability conditions are utilized to characterize data quality:
- **Density-ratio-based concentrability ($C_{π^*}$)**: $C_{π^*} := \sup_{s∈S, a∈A} π^*(a|s)/π_{ref}(a|s)$, which directly bounds the density ratio of the optimal policy $π^*$ relative to the reference policy.
- **D²-based concentrability ($D^2_{π^*}$)**: $D^2_{π^*} := E_{(s,a)∼ρ×π^*} [D^2_\mathcal{G}((s,a); π_{ref})]$, which measures how well errors in the function class, estimated under $π_{ref}$, generalize to state-action pairs sampled from $π^*$. This is a weaker "single-policy" concentrability condition compared to "all-policy" versions that apply over all $(s,a)$ pairs. For contextual dueling bandits, an adapted $D^2_\mathcal{G}$ definition is used, accounting for the indistinguishability of absolute reward values from pairwise comparisons.

**Algorithms:**
1. **For KL-Regularized Contextual Bandits (Algorithm 1: KL-PCB)**:
 - **Least Squares Estimation**: Computes an initial reward function estimate $\bar{g}$ by minimizing the squared error on the offline dataset.
 - **Pessimistic Estimation**: Constructs a pessimistic reward estimator $b_g = \bar{g} - \Gamma_n$, where $\Gamma_n$ is a bonus term proportional to $D^2_\mathcal{G}$ and a confidence radius $\beta$. This implements the "pessimism in the face of uncertainty" principle.
 - **Policy Output**: Derives the final policy $b_π$ by maximizing the KL-regularized objective with the pessimistic reward estimator $b_g$. The maximizer has a closed-form solution: $b_π(a|s) \propto π_{ref}(a|s) \exp(\eta \cdot b_g(s,a))$.

2. **For f-Divergence-Regularized Contextual Bandits with Strongly Convex f (Algorithm 2: f-CB)**:
 - **Least Squares Estimation**: Similar to KL-PCB, it obtains $\bar{g}$ from the offline dataset.
 - **Direct Policy Computation**: It directly computes the policy $b_π$ by maximizing the f-divergence regularized objective using $\bar{g}$, without an additional pessimistic step.

**Analytical Techniques:**
- **Novel Pessimism-Based Analysis (for KL-Regularization)**: The proof for KL-PCB leverages algorithmic pessimism combined with a specific curvature property of the KL-regularized objective. A key technical contribution is a "moment-based analysis" (Lemma 2.15). This lemma states that if a random variable $X \le 0$ with probability 1, then $E[X^3] - E[X^2]E[X] \le 0$. This property is applied to the difference between the pessimistic estimator and the true reward, allowing the transformation of a "mean-value-type" risk upper bound (Lemma 2.14, involving a "mid-point" policy $π_γ$) into a bound that depends only on the optimal policy $π^*$. This effectively converts the dependency from all-policy to single-policy concentrability.
- **Dual-Bregman Perspective (for Strongly Convex f-Divergence)**: For f-divergences with strongly convex $f$, the analysis exploits the strong convexity of the regularization term $H_s(π) = \eta^{-1}D_f(π(\cdot|s)∥π_{ref}(\cdot|s))$. The sub-optimality gap is expressed using the Bregman divergence of the convex conjugate $H_s^*(r)$. Crucially, the strong convexity of $f$ implies that the Hessian of $H_s^*(r)$ can be bounded, leading to an error term that depends only on the mismatch between $\bar{g}$ and $g^*$ under the reference policy $π_{ref}$, effectively removing any dependency on specific data coverage conditions.
- **Lower Bounds**: The authors establish theoretical lower bounds on sample complexity using information-theoretic tools like Fano's and Assouad's lemmas. These bounds are constructed by designing "hard instances" of contextual bandit problems to demonstrate the necessity of certain sample sizes and coverage conditions.

**Experimental Validation:**
Simulations are conducted across three settings:
- **Multi-armed bandits**: Simple two-armed bandit instances, specifically designed to test the ϵ⁻¹ rate and the impact of $π_{ref}$ coverage on KL-regularization, as well as the scaling with α for strongly convex f-divergences.
- **Linear bandits**: More complex linear bandit setups with varying behavior policies to empirically demonstrate the relationship between concentrability coefficients ($C_{π^*}$ and $D^2_{π^*}$) and sub-optimality gaps for both KL and χ² (a strongly convex f-divergence) regularizations.
- **Real-world dataset (MNIST)**: Contextual bandits derived from the MNIST dataset, using uniform and "spiky" reference policies to observe coverage effects in a practical setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents distinct theoretical findings for two classes of f-divergence regularizers, along with empirical support.

**For Reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence Regularization:**
- **Sharp Sample Complexity Under Single-Policy Concentrability**: The proposed algorithm, KL-PCB (Algorithm 1), achieves an 𝑒O($\eta D^2_{π^*} \epsilon^{-1} \log N_G(\epsilon)$) sample complexity for finding an $\epsilon$-optimal policy. This is a primary result, demonstrating that the fast $e\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$ rate can be obtained under "single-policy concentrability" ($D^2_{π^*}$) (Theorem 2.10), which is a weaker condition than the "all-policy concentrability" previously required for similar rates.
- **Necessity of Single-Policy Concentrability**: A corresponding lower bound of $\Omega(\min\{\eta\epsilon^{-1}, \epsilon^{-2}\} C_{π^*} \log N_G(\epsilon))$ is established (Theorem 2.11). This result indicates that a multiplicative dependency on density-ratio-based single-policy concentrability ($C_{π^*}$) is necessary, thereby demonstrating that single-policy concentrability is both sufficient (via $D^2_{π^*}$) and necessary (via $C_{π^*}$) for achieving the $e\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$ sample complexity. The paper notes that $D^2_{π^*}$ and $C_{π^*}$ are not directly inter-convertible in general, but for certain tabular settings, $D^2 \approx C$.
- **Technical Contributions**: The improvement in coverage conditions stems from a novel pessimism-based analysis combined with a newly identified moment-based inequality (Lemma 2.15). This technical machinery refines a mean-value-type risk bound, allowing the analysis to shift its dependency from a "mid-point" policy to the optimal policy, which can then be bounded by single-policy concentrability.

**For f-Divergences with $\alpha$-Strongly Convex f (e.g., $\chi^2$-divergence):**
- **Coverage-Independent Sample Complexity**: The proposed algorithm, f-CB (Algorithm 2), achieves a sharp sample complexity of $e\Theta(\alpha^{-1}\eta\epsilon^{-1} \log N_G(\epsilon))$ (Theorem 3.2).
- **Elimination of Coverage Dependency**: A significant finding is that this rate is achieved *without any dependency on data coverage conditions* (e.g., $D^2_{π^*}$ or $C_{π^*}$). This implies that the strong convexity of $f$ in the f-divergence regularizer inherently provides a stronger regularization effect, keeping the optimal and estimated policies close to the reference policy, thus rendering specific coverage assumptions unnecessary for statistical efficiency. The algorithm itself is simpler, not requiring pessimistic estimation.
- **Matching Lower Bound**: A corresponding lower bound of $\Omega(\alpha^{-1}\eta\epsilon^{-1} \log N_G(\epsilon))$ confirms the optimality of this rate and the absence of coverage dependency (Theorem 3.4).

**Experimental Results (Figures 1, 2, 3):**
- **Statistical Rate Verification**: Experiments on multi-armed bandits, linear bandits, and the MNIST dataset consistently show an empirical rate of approximately $n^{-1}$ for the sub-optimality gap (i.e., $e\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$ sample complexity), matching the theoretical predictions for both KL and strongly convex f-divergence regularizations.
- **Concentrability Impact**:
 - For KL-regularization, the sub-optimality gap exhibits a clear dependency on concentrability coefficients ($C_{π^*}$ and $D^2_{π^*}$), with instances having poorer coverage showing larger gaps.
 - For $\chi^2$-divergence regularization (an example of strongly convex f), the sub-optimality gaps are observed to be very similar across instances with varying coverage conditions, particularly for sufficiently large sample sizes, corroborating the theoretical finding of coverage independence.
- **$\alpha$ Scaling**: Empirical results confirm that the sub-optimality for strongly convex f-divergences is inversely proportional to $\alpha$, aligning with the theoretical bounds.

**Generalization to Contextual Dueling Bandits (CDBs):**
- The framework extends to CDBs with similar $e\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$ sample complexity bounds.
- For KL-regularized CDBs (Algorithm 3: KL-PCDB), the upper bound is $eO(\eta(D^2_{π^*} \land C_{π^*})\epsilon^{-1})$ (Theorem F.4), with a near-matching lower bound depending on $C_{π^*}$ (Theorem F.6).
- For strongly convex f-divergence regularized CDBs (Algorithm 4: f-CDB), the $e\Theta(\alpha^{-1}\eta\epsilon^{-1})$ bounds are maintained without coverage dependency (Theorems F.8 and F.9).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to a deeper theoretical understanding of offline policy learning with f-divergence regularization, which has both theoretical and practical significance.

**Theoretical Significance:**
- **Refined Understanding of KL-Regularization**: The work clarifies the specific data coverage requirements for achieving optimal statistical efficiency (an $e\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$ sample complexity) with reverse KL-regularization. By demonstrating that "single-policy concentrability" is both sufficient and necessary, it significantly relaxes the stringent "all-policy concentrability" assumption found in previous work, making the theoretical guarantees more applicable to real-world offline RL scenarios where comprehensive data coverage is often unrealistic.
- **Novel Analytical Framework**: The introduction of a novel pessimism-based analysis, synergistically combined with a moment-based lemma (Lemma 2.15), provides a powerful new tool for analyzing offline RL algorithms. This technical machinery exploits the strong concavity properties of regularized objectives in a way that previous general pessimism pipelines did not, enabling sharper bounds under weaker assumptions. This method might be of independent interest for other complex optimization problems in machine learning.
- **Identification of Coverage-Agnostic Regularization**: A notable finding is that for f-divergences with strongly convex $f$, $e\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})$ sample complexity can be achieved *without any dependency on data coverage conditions*. This suggests that choosing specific types of regularization can fundamentally mitigate the distributional shift challenge, potentially enabling efficient learning even in severely data-limited or poorly covered environments. This insight offers a new perspective on designing robust offline RL algorithms.

**Practical Significance:**
- **Improved Algorithm Design**: The sharp theoretical analysis can guide the design of more statistically efficient offline RL algorithms by informing the choice of regularization terms based on the expected data coverage. For instance, in contexts where data coverage is inherently limited, employing strongly convex f-divergence regularization might be a more robust approach than traditional KL regularization.
- **Relevance to RLHF and LLMs**: Given the widespread use of KL-regularization in areas like LLM fine-tuning with human feedback (RLHF), the precise characterization of its data requirements is highly relevant. Understanding that single-policy concentrability is sufficient provides clearer guidelines for data collection and algorithm deployment in such critical applications. The extension to contextual dueling bandits further strengthens this relevance, as preference feedback is central to RLHF.
- **Empirical Validation**: The consistent empirical verification across multi-armed bandits, linear bandits, and real-world datasets (MNIST) reinforces the practical applicability and correctness of the theoretical insights.

**Potential Impact and Future Work:**
The results take a significant step towards a comprehensive understanding of f-divergence-regularized objectives in offline policy learning. Future research directions include:
- **Closing Remaining Gaps**: Further efforts could focus on fully bridging the gap between the $D^2_{π^*}$ (upper bound) and $C_{π^*}$ (lower bound) metrics for single-policy concentrability in reverse KL-regularization to obtain an even tighter characterization.
- **Generalizing f-Divergence Analysis**: Extending the analysis to more general f-divergences beyond reverse KL and those with twice-continuously differentiable, strongly convex $f$, potentially relaxing current assumptions.
- **Algorithm-Specific Enhancements**: Leveraging these theoretical insights to develop new, practically optimized algorithms that specifically exploit the identified coverage properties of different f-divergences.

Overall, this paper provides foundational insights into the statistical properties of f-divergence-regularized offline RL, with direct implications for both theoretical development and practical algorithm design in a critical area of machine learning.
