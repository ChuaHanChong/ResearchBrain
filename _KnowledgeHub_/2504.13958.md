# ToolRL: Reward is All Tool Learning Needs

> [!summary] Summary
> This research presents ToolRL, a novel reinforcement learning framework that utilizes a meticulously designed reward mechanism to enhance tool-use capabilities in Large Language Models. The approach achieves up to a 17% performance improvement over raw base models and 15% over Supervised Fine-Tuning models on the Berkeley Function Call Leaderboard, while also demonstrating strong generalization to unseen scenarios and fostering emergent agentic behaviors.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) inherently possess limitations such as outdated knowledge, calculation inaccuracies, and shallow reasoning, which external tools can help address.
- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), the prevalent method for training LLMs in Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR), struggles with generalization to complex or unseen scenarios and lacks exploratory capabilities, often leading to superficial imitation.
- Designing effective and generalizable reward signals for multi-step, multi-tool invocation tasks within a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework for TIR has been largely unexplored.

## Method

- Introduces ==ToolRL==, a novel Reinforcement Learning (RL) approach for ==Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR)== in LLMs, centered on a principled, rule-based reward design.
- Deploys a ==fine-grained correctness reward== that assesses tool invocation accuracy across tool names, parameter names, and parameter content, providing dense intermediate feedback.
- Utilizes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for stable training, with a custom advantage normalization and an omission of the KL penalty to encourage adaptation to the structured reward signals.

## Results

- ToolRL, using GRPO with cold start, consistently outperformed raw base models by approximately **17%** and SFT models by **15%** on the BFCL benchmark (e.g., Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct improved from **41.97%** to **58.38%** on BFCL).
- GRPO-trained models demonstrated strong generalization performance on unfamiliar programming languages and novel task objectives like detecting irrelevant tools.
- Models exhibited emergent proactiveness (e.g., clarifying ambiguous intent) and metacognitive reasoning (e.g., opting to answer directly when tools are irrelevant), enhancing efficiency and reducing hallucinations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Longer reasoning (measured by the length of the field) is not consistently beneficial for tool-use tasks and can sometimes degrade performance.
> - Fine-grained reward decomposition, which separately evaluates tool name, parameter names, and parameter values, provides richer and more effective learning signals than coarser granularities.
> - Training GRPO from a 'cold start' (raw instruct model) often outperforms models initialized with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), suggesting SFT can hinder generalization by promoting memorization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has witnessed remarkable progress in complex reasoning tasks, with Reinforcement Learning (RL) emerging as a powerful paradigm enabling emergent capabilities like self-reflection and long-horizon planning, as seen in models such as o1 and R1. However, LLMs inherently possess limitations, including outdated knowledge, calculation inaccuracies, and shallow reasoning. **Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR)** has emerged as a critical solution to address these shortcomings by allowing LLMs to interact with external tools (e.g., search engines, calculators, code interpreters) in a multi-step, feedback-driven loop. This capability is foundational for transforming LLMs into versatile and grounded "LLMs-as-agents," enabling diverse applications from scientific discovery to embodied task completion.

Historically, training LLMs for TIR tasks has predominantly relied on **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**. In SFT, models are trained on carefully curated, offline-generated trajectories of integrated reasoning steps. While effective to some extent, SFT suffers from significant limitations: it struggles with generalization to unfamiliar or complex scenarios, lacks exploratory capabilities, and often leads to models that merely imitate superficial cues without true deep thinking or strategic flexibility. This "overthinking" behavior, as illustrated in the paper, highlights SFT's inability to adapt to novel tool use contexts.

Recent efforts have begun to explore RL-based training for TIR, such as Search-R1 for search tools and TORL for code tools. However, these works are narrowly focused on specific tool types or task settings. The broader challenge of designing effective reward signals for general-purpose tool selection and application within an RL framework remains largely unexplored. Tool use tasks introduce unique complexities compared to traditional reasoning or question-answering: they involve multi-step interactions, multiple tool invocations with diverse parameters, and require fine-grained feedback that simple answer-matching rewards cannot provide.

This paper distinguishes itself by presenting the **first comprehensive study on reward design for general-purpose tool selection and application tasks within the RL paradigm.** It systematically explores various dimensions of reward strategies—type, scale, granularity, and temporal dynamics—to identify principles for optimal RL design for TIR. By focusing on general tool use across diverse and complex tool sets, this work aims to provide a fundamental understanding and an empirical roadmap for advancing LLM-agent capabilities, moving beyond the limitations of SFT and the narrow scope of previous RL for TIR attempts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for equipping Large Language Models (LLMs) with robust and generalizable tool-use capabilities. While SFT can teach LLMs to mimic tool-using behaviors on seen data, it fails to promote true understanding, strategic flexibility, and generalization to unseen or complex scenarios. This often leads to "overthinking" or inappropriate tool invocation, where the model follows superficial patterns rather than genuine reasoning.

To address these shortcomings, the authors are motivated to explore Reinforcement Learning (RL) as a more promising paradigm for fostering agentic tool-using capabilities in LLMs. However, they identify a critical bottleneck for effective RL in Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR): the inherent complexity of designing appropriate reward signals. Unlike simpler tasks with clear, singular correct answers (e.g., in mathematics), TIR involves multi-step interactions, potential invocation of multiple tools with precise parameters, and dynamic feedback loops. Coarse-grained rewards (e.g., simple answer matching) are insufficient to provide the nuanced feedback required for effective learning in such complex environments.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of this research are multi-faceted:

1. **To conduct the first comprehensive study on reward design for general-purpose tool selection and application tasks within the RL paradigm.** This involves moving beyond specific tool types or narrow task settings to understand how rewards can guide LLMs in broad tool-use scenarios.
2. **To systematically explore a wide range of reward strategies:** analyzing their types (what aspects to reward), scales (how much to reward), granularity (how detailed the signal is), and temporal dynamics (how rewards evolve over time). The goal is to uncover insights into what constitutes a "useful" reward for tool-invoking LLMs.
3. **To propose a principled and generalizable reward design tailored for tool use tasks.** This design should overcome the limitations of simplified or binary reward signals by capturing the nuanced correctness of tool invocations.
4. **To apply this principled reward design to train LLMs using a robust RL algorithm, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).**
5. **To empirically evaluate the proposed approach across diverse benchmarks,** demonstrating its ability to yield robust, scalable, and stable training, and to achieve significant performance gains over existing base and SFT models.
6. **To showcase the generalization performance and emergent behaviors** (e.g., proactiveness, metacognitive reasoning) of the RL-trained models, highlighting their enhanced agentic intelligence.
7. **To distill actionable insights and provide an empirical roadmap for future research** on RL-based training for LLM-agents, facilitating progress in the field by open-sourcing their code and data.

In essence, the paper seeks to answer the fundamental question: "Can RL-based training methods better equip LLMs with agentic tool-using capabilities, and if so, what is the optimal RL design for TIR?" by meticulously investigating the central role of reward design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a novel reinforcement learning (RL) approach, dubbed **ToolRL**, to enhance Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) in Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically focusing on a principled reward design. This methodology is built upon a well-defined task, a custom rollout strategy, a detailed reward formulation, and leverages the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm for training.

#### 3.1. Task Definition

TIR is formalized as a sequential decision-making process where an LLM interacts with a given tool set $\mathcal{T} = \{t_1, \dots, t_n\}$ to solve a user query $Q$. At each step $k$, the model generates natural language reasoning $r_k$, selects a set of tools $T_k \subseteq \mathcal{T}$, and formulates grounded tool calls. Following execution, an observation $o_k$ (tool output, environment feedback) is received. The model's policy aims to maximize the immediate reward $R(s_k, T_{k+1}, o_{k+1})$ at each step, while implicitly optimizing for the cumulative reward over the entire trajectory. This approach is suited for the fine-grained, step-wise feedback available in tool selection and application tasks, differing from end-to-end QA tasks that only reward the final answer.

#### 3.2. TIR Rollout

To facilitate autonomous generation of reasoning traces and tool calls, a structured "system prompt" is employed during rollout. This prompt specifies available tools and guides the LLM to use special tokens: `<think>` for reasoning, `<tool_call>` for tool invocations, and `<response>` for final answers. When `<tool_call>` is generated, the system parses the model's predicted parameters, executes the tools, and inserts the outputs into an `<obs>` field within the dialogue history. This dynamic feedback loop allows the model to refine its subsequent actions based on real-time tool execution results. The system supports simultaneous multiple tool calls and manages the dialogue history for contextual awareness.

#### 3.3. Reward Design

The cornerstone of ToolRL is its meticulously designed rule-based reward mechanism, which combines structural adherence with correctness evaluation. The final reward $R_{\text{final}}$ is a sum of two components: $R_{\text{format}} + R_{\text{correct}}$.

- **Format Reward ($R_{\text{format}}$):** This is a binary (0 or 1) reward that assesses whether the model's output adheres to the specified structural format (i.e., inclusion and correct ordering of `<think>`, `<tool_call>`, and `<response>` tokens). This ensures syntactic validity.

- **Correctness Reward ($R_{\text{correct}}$):** This is a continuous reward in the range $[-3, 3]$ that evaluates the accuracy of the predicted tool calls against ground-truth calls. It is decomposed into three fine-grained sub-components, reflecting the nuanced nature of tool use:
 - **Tool Name Matching ($r_{\text{name}}$):** Measures the similarity between the sets of predicted and ground-truth tool names using the Jaccard index.
 - **Parameter Name Matching ($r_{\text{param}}$):** For each matched ground-truth tool, it sums the Jaccard indices of parameter names between the predicted and ground-truth tool calls.
 - **Parameter Content Matching ($r_{\text{value}}$):** For each matched ground-truth tool and parameter, it sums binary indicators (1 if values match, 0 otherwise).
 The total match score is computed by finding the optimal matching between predicted and ground-truth tool calls to maximize the sum of $r_{\text{name}}, r_{\text{param}}, r_{\text{value}}$. This total score is then normalized to the $[-3, 3]$ range. This fine-grained decomposition provides dense intermediate feedback, capturing partial correctness and guiding the model towards accurate tool invocation beyond just the final outcome.

#### 3.4. RL Training with GRPO

To optimize the LLM policy with these structured rewards, the authors employ **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, a variant of PPO. GRPO enhances training stability by normalizing the advantage function within groups of responses originating from the same input query.
For each query $Q$, multiple responses are rolled out, forming a group $G_Q$. Rewards $r_i$ for each response $s_i$ are computed as $R_{\text{format}}(s_i, A) + R_{\text{correct}}(s_i, A)$, where $A$ is the ground-truth annotation. The mean ($\mu_Q$) and standard deviation ($\sigma_Q$) of rewards within the group are calculated. The normalized advantage for each sample is then $A_i(s_i|Q) = \frac{r_i - \mu_Q}{\sigma_Q + \eta}$.
The policy is optimized using a standard clipped PPO objective, adapted with these group-wise normalized advantages. Notably, unlike original GRPO, the KL penalty term against a reference model is *omitted*. This design choice encourages the model to more freely adapt to the custom response format and structured reward signals, leading to faster convergence and comparable performance.

#### 3.5. Training Dataset and Settings

The training dataset is a mixed compilation of 4K examples, drawing 2K from **ToolACE** (general tool use), 1K from **Hammer (Masked)** (randomized names for generalization), and 1K from **xLAM** (compositional multi-tool calls). Multi-step trajectories are broken down into single-step instances, with dialogue history preserved in the prompt to maintain context.
Experiments are conducted using the veRL framework, initializing models with Qwen-2.5-Instruct and Llama-3.2-Instruct series. Key hyperparameters include a batch size of 512, 4 responses per query, 15 epochs, and a generation temperature of 1.0 to encourage exploration.
**Evaluation** is performed on three diverse benchmarks: **Berkeley Function Call Leaderboard (BFCL)** (comprehensive tool use, including single-step, multi-step, real-time execution, relevance detection), **API-Bank** (multi-turn API calls across difficulty levels), and **Bamboogle** (QA requiring free-form web search tool use). Accuracy is the primary metric.
**Baselines** include: Raw Instruct Model, SFT on the same 400/4K RL data, GRPO and PPO initialized with SFT, and PPO Cold Start, providing a comprehensive comparison to isolate the impact of the proposed reward design and GRPO.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of ToolRL across diverse benchmarks yielded significant insights into the effectiveness of the proposed reward design and the GRPO training paradigm.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Comparison to Baselines

The "GRPO Cold Start" approach, trained from scratch with the proposed reward design, consistently demonstrated superior performance on core tool-use benchmarks:
- On **BFCL**, GRPO Cold Start achieved robust gains, showing approximately a **17% improvement over raw base models** and a **15% gain over SFT models** trained on similar data volumes. For instance, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct improved from 19.41% (Raw) and 40.67% (SFT4k) to 46.20% (Ours, GRPO Cold Start). Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct showed even more significant improvement from 41.97% (Raw) to 58.38% (Ours, GRPO Cold Start).
- On **API-Bank**, similar trends were observed, with GRPO Cold Start models generally leading in overall accuracy across difficulty levels.
- The **LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct** series showed less improvement compared to Qwen models but remained competitive and often outperformed other baselines, suggesting model architecture might play a role in adaptability to GRPO.

#### 4.2. Impact of SFT Initialization

A counter-intuitive but crucial finding was that initializing GRPO with an SFT-tuned model often underperformed compared to training GRPO from a "cold start" (i.e., directly from the raw instruct model). While SFT-initialized models sometimes surpassed SFT-only baselines (even those with 10x more data), their performance was generally inferior to cold-start GRPO. The authors hypothesize that SFT initialization can lead to memorization and overfitting to the SFT data distribution, which subsequently restricts GRPO's ability to learn generalizable strategies and explore effectively. This is further supported by the observation that SFT-initialized models achieved higher *training* rewards, but this did not translate to better *generalization* on unseen benchmarks.

#### 4.3. Reward Design on PPO

The effectiveness of the proposed reward design was also tested with standard PPO. While PPO with a cold start could outperform SFT in some cases, it exhibited less stability across different model settings compared to GRPO. GRPO consistently achieved higher and more stable rewards, indicating that the principled reward design is effective for PPO but synergizes optimally within the GRPO framework. GRPO also showed more rapid improvement in both format and correctness rewards during training.

#### 4.4. Generalization Studies

A key strength of ToolRL is its generalization ability.
- The GRPO-trained models (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) maintained high performance when evaluated on **unfamiliar programming languages** and in **novel task objectives** such as detecting irrelevant tools. These scenarios were not explicitly part of the RL training data.
- Qualitatively, the models exhibited **emergent proactiveness** (e.g., clarifying ambiguous user intent) and **metacognitive behaviors** (e.g., opting to answer directly when tools are irrelevant), enhancing efficiency and reducing hallucinations—crucial traits for agentic intelligence.

#### 4.5. Free-form Inference Effectiveness

When evaluated on **Bamboogle**, a multi-hop QA benchmark requiring free-form web search tool use (a "goal-oriented" rather than "process-oriented" task, with no constraints on tool call parameters), the GRPO Cold Start models achieved the highest accuracy. Importantly, they did so without relying on an excessive number of tool calls, suggesting that the model learned to flexibly and efficiently invoke tools when necessary, demonstrating wise resource utilization.

#### 4.6. Ablation Studies on Reward Design (Key Insights)

The paper conducted systematic ablations to understand the impact of various reward components:
- **Effect of Length Reward:** Adding a reward proportional to the length of the `<think>` field (to encourage deeper reasoning) surprisingly did not consistently improve task performance and could even degrade it for smaller models. This suggests that **longer reasoning is not inherently better** and may introduce unnecessary complexity or "overthinking" in tool-use tasks. Dynamic length rewards (gradually increasing target length) also failed to yield performance improvements.
- **Effect of Reward Scale:** Equalizing the maximum correctness and format rewards (e.g., both in $[-1, 1]$) led to a slight drop in overall accuracy. This highlights the importance of assigning a **greater weight to correctness reward** (original range $[-3, 3]$) to properly steer the model towards mastering core reasoning and tool use. Dynamic reward scaling, which smoothly shifts emphasis from format adherence to correctness throughout training, showed slight performance improvements over fixed scales, suggesting that a **gradual transition is beneficial** while abrupt shifts are detrimental.
- **Effect of Reward Granularity:** The original fine-grained decomposition of the correctness reward (matching tool name, parameter names, and parameter values separately) performed best. Progressively coarser granularity (e.g., exact match on entire parameter dictionary or entire tool set) led to sparser learning signals and degraded performance. This strongly indicates that **fine-grained reward decomposition provides richer and more effective learning signals** for complex tool-use tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "ToolRL: Reward is All Tool Learning Needs" paper marks a significant advancement in the training of Large Language Models (LLMs) for complex, real-world tasks involving external tools. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Pioneering Research in General-Purpose ToolRL:** This work is the first comprehensive study to systematically investigate reward design for **general-purpose tool selection and application** using reinforcement learning (RL). Prior RL efforts for tool use were often confined to specific tool types or narrow task domains. By addressing the fundamental challenges of reward engineering for versatile tool integration, ToolRL provides a much-needed foundation for future research in this area.

2. **Empirical Roadmap for Reward Design:** The systematic ablation studies on reward type, scale, granularity, and temporal dynamics offer invaluable insights into what makes a reward "useful" for LLM-agent training. The distilled takeaways—that longer reasoning isn't inherently better, dynamic reward scaling is beneficial, and fine-grained reward decomposition is crucial—constitute an **empirical roadmap** that can guide researchers in designing more effective reward functions for diverse LLM agent applications. This moves beyond intuitive reward formulations to evidence-based design principles.

3. **Enhanced LLM Agentic Capabilities:** The demonstrated improvements in performance (17% over base models, 15% over SFT models) and, crucially, the strong generalization to unfamiliar scenarios (e.g., unseen programming languages, irrelevant tool detection) and novel task goals (e.g., free-form QA) are highly impactful. This suggests that the proposed RL approach, powered by the principled reward design, can instill genuine **agentic intelligence** in LLMs.

4. **Emergent Behaviors for Practical Agents:** The observation of emergent behaviors such as **proactiveness** (e.g., clarifying ambiguous user intent) and **metacognitive reasoning** (e.g., deciding when *not* to use a tool, but to answer directly) is a testament to the sophistication of the learned policies. These behaviors are critical for developing robust, efficient, and user-friendly AI agents that can navigate complex, ambiguous real-world environments without hallucinating or misusing tools. Such agents would require less human supervision and intervention.

5. **Robust and Stable Training Paradigm:** The consistent performance gains and stable training observed with GRPO, even from a cold start, underscore the robustness and scalability of the proposed method. This indicates a viable and efficient path for training LLMs to acquire complex tool-use skills, reducing the reliance on extensive and often brittle supervised datasets.

6. **Catalyst for Future Research and Applications:** By providing a clear methodology and empirical insights, ToolRL is poised to accelerate research into more capable and autonomous LLM agents. This includes further exploration of complex tool interaction, multi-modal applications, and real-world deployment of LLM-powered agents in domains like scientific discovery, research automation, and embodied AI. The open-sourcing of code and data further facilitates community engagement and reproducibility.

In conclusion, ToolRL effectively demonstrates that carefully crafted reward signals are paramount in unlocking generalizable and sophisticated tool-using abilities in LLMs. Its findings challenge conventional assumptions (e.g., regarding reasoning length) and provide a principled foundation for engineering intelligent agents that can reliably interact with the world through tools, truly embodying the vision that "reward is all tool learning needs."
