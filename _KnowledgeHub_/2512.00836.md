# Assessing model error in counterfactual worlds

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from Princeton University and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill presents a framework to assess model error in unobserved counterfactual scenarios, differentiating between scenario deviation and model miscalibration. The paper introduces three approaches, with simulation experiments demonstrating that statistically inferring error distributions or estimating counterfactual observations provides more accurate and less biased error assessments than direct comparisons.

## Problem

- Retrospective evaluation of counterfactual scenario projections is challenging because the assumed conditions for these scenarios are rarely observed in reality.
- Differences between a model's counterfactual projection and observed reality can stem from both the real world not matching the scenario (scenario deviation) and the model being inaccurate for that scenario (model miscalibration).
- A lack of robust methods for systematically evaluating scenario projections hinders model improvement and reduces trust among end-users.

## Method

- A conceptual framework was established to clearly separate ==scenario deviation error== from ==model miscalibration error==.
- Three distinct approaches were proposed to estimate model miscalibration error in unobserved ==counterfactual worlds==: evaluating only plausible scenarios, inferring the error distribution directly, and estimating observations in modeled scenarios.
- A simulation experiment using an ==SIR epidemiological model== was conducted to test the performance, benefits, and limitations of each proposed evaluation approach under controlled conditions where true counterfactual outcomes were known.

## Results

- The 'evaluate only plausible scenarios' approach (Approach 1) consistently showed systematic bias, often misattributing errors or appearing accurate due to canceling effects between scenario deviation and model miscalibration.
- Approaches 2 and 3, which involve statistical modeling of errors or observations, yielded more accurate mean error estimates and captured the overall error distribution better than Approach 1.
- Approach 2 (inferring error distribution directly) slightly outperformed Approach 3 (estimating observations in modeled scenarios) in capturing the full error distribution across various model-scenario comparisons, and both benefited significantly from the inclusion of covariates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Systematically distinguishing between scenario deviation and model miscalibration is fundamental for a meaningful evaluation of scenario projections, as these error sources can confound or even cancel each other out.
> - Directly comparing model projections to observed realities, as in the 'evaluate only plausible scenarios' approach, leads to biased and misleading error estimates because it fails to adequately separate scenario deviation from model miscalibration.
> - Approaches that statistically infer error distributions (Approach 2) or estimate counterfactual observations (Approach 3) provide more accurate assessments of intrinsic model miscalibration, especially when incorporating relevant covariates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Predictive models are fundamental tools across numerous scientific and applied fields, serving to both forecast future events and assess hypothetical scenarios ("what would happen if?"). While extensive theoretical and methodological frameworks exist for evaluating forecasts, which compare model predictions against observed realities, the retrospective evaluation of *counterfactual scenario projections* presents a distinct challenge. This challenge arises because the specific conditions assumed in a counterfactual scenario are rarely, if ever, precisely observed in the real world. Consequently, such scenario projections are infrequently evaluated in practice.

The absence of systematic evaluation impedes model improvement and can erode trust among end-users. Fields such as climate modeling have long confronted this issue, developing alternative metrics like a model's ability to recreate past conditions or its consistency with physical laws. More recently, some attempts have been made to directly evaluate scenario projections in climate and infectious disease modeling, though these approaches possess identified limitations. This paper contributes to the broader research landscape by proposing a conceptual framework to systematically address the evaluation of counterfactual scenario projections, differentiating between sources of error and presenting three novel approaches to estimate model error in unobserved counterfactual worlds. By doing so, it aims to establish a more rigorous foundation for evaluating models used in planning and decision-making under uncertainty.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate methodologies for retrospectively assessing model error in counterfactual scenario projections. The authors identify a fundamental challenge in the evaluation of "what would happen if?" scenarios: differences between projected outcomes and observed realities can stem from two distinct sources. These are:
1. **Scenario deviation:** The real world did not unfold precisely according to the assumed conditions of any modeled scenario.
2. **Model miscalibration:** Even if the real-world conditions perfectly matched a modeled scenario, the model's projection for that scenario might still deviate from what would have actually occurred.

The authors argue that, for the purpose of assessing the intrinsic value and reliability of models in informing decisions, model miscalibration is the more critical source of error. This is because decisions are made based on the projections generated *for the modeled counterfactual scenarios*. Therefore, understanding how well a model performs within those hypothetical conditions is paramount. However, directly measuring model miscalibration in counterfactual worlds is inherently difficult because the "observed" outcome for a hypothetical scenario (i.e., what *would have happened* if a specific scenario *had* been realized) is never directly available.

Given this challenge, the key motivations for this work are:
- To present a conceptual framework that clarifies the two sources of error in scenario projection evaluation.
- To propose and describe three distinct approaches designed to estimate model error specifically due to miscalibration within counterfactual scenarios.
- To demonstrate the feasibility, benefits, and limitations of each proposed approach through a controlled simulation experiment where the "true" counterfactual outcomes are known.
- To provide practical recommendations for the estimation of counterfactual error.
- To discuss the necessary components of scenario design that facilitate the evaluability of scenario projections.

Ultimately, the motivation is to bridge the gap between the widespread use of scenario projections in planning and decision-making and the current lack of robust, interpretable methods for their retrospective evaluation, thereby fostering model improvement and enhancing confidence in their application.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research consists of a conceptual framework, the proposal of three distinct evaluation approaches, and a simulation experiment to test these approaches.

**Conceptual Framework for Error Decomposition:**
The authors begin by establishing a conceptual framework that formalizes the two sources of error. They define a scenario projection as $P_m(y|x_i)$, representing the projected outcome $y$ from model $m$ under scenario conditions $x_i$. The observed reality is given by $(x^*, P_*(y|x^*))$, where $x^*$ is the realized scenario condition and $P_*(y|x^*)$ is the actual observed outcome. The core problem is that comparing $P_m(y|x_i)$ directly to $P_*(y|x^*)$ conflates two error sources:
- **Scenario deviation:** The difference between $x_i$ (modeled) and $x^*$ (realized).
- **Model miscalibration:** The difference between $P_m(y|x_i)$ and what the true outcome *would have been* if scenario $x_i$ had been realized, denoted $P_*(y|x_i)$. This is the target error for assessment: $e_m(x_i) = P_m(y|x_i) - P_*(y|x_i)$.

The critical challenge is that $P_*(y|x_i)$ is unobservable for counterfactual scenarios $x_i \neq x^*$.

**Three Approaches for Estimating Counterfactual Error:**

The paper introduces three distinct approaches to estimate $P_*(y|x_i)$ or $e_m(x_i)$:

1. **Approach 1: Evaluate only plausible scenarios.**
 - **Concept:** This approach treats scenario projections as conditional forecasts. It evaluates only a subset of projections where the assumed scenario $x_i$ is retrospectively deemed "plausible," meaning it is sufficiently close to the realized scenario $x^*$. For these plausible scenarios, a direct comparison between $P_m(y|x_i)$ and $P_*(y|x^*)$ is made.
 - **Requirements/Assumptions:** Some modeled scenarios must be sufficiently close to reality. It assumes that any remaining scenario deviation in these "plausible" cases will not significantly affect the projected outcomes.
 - **Limitations:** Defining "plausible" is subjective and can lead to misattribution of error. It can only evaluate a subset of projections and provides no insight into error in less plausible, but still relevant, counterfactual scenarios.

2. **Approach 2: Infer the error distribution directly.**
 - **Concept:** This approach infers the distribution of errors across a set of projections by leveraging naturally occurring variation in realized scenarios and outcomes (e.g., across different locations or time periods).
 - **Steps:**
 - Rerun each model $m$ to obtain $P_m(y|x^*)$, the projection the model *would have made* given the realized scenario $x^*$.
 - Calculate the error in the realized scenario: $e_m(x^*) = P_m(y|x^*) - P_*(y|x^*)$.
 - Fit a statistical model (e.g., $e_m(x) = g(x) + \epsilon$) relating these realized errors to $x^*$.
 - Use this fitted relationship to estimate the distribution of errors $e_m(x_i)$ for each of the originally modeled counterfactual scenarios $x_i$.
 - **Requirements/Assumptions:** Requires multiple projections with natural variation in realized $x^*$. Assumes realized $x^*$ is sampled independently of model error and that models capture important system properties. Honest retrospective reprojection is crucial.
 - **Limitations:** Statistical modeling of complex error distributions can be challenging. Each model might imply a different distribution of "observations" in counterfactual worlds if errors are fitted independently.

3. **Approach 3: Estimate observations in modeled scenarios.**
 - **Concept:** This approach directly estimates what the observations *would have been* in the modeled counterfactual scenarios, $P_*(y|x_i)$. It also leverages naturally occurring variation across realized worlds.
 - **Steps:**
 - Fit a statistical model (e.g., $P_*(y|x) = f(x) + \epsilon$) to the observed data $(x^*, P_*(y|x^*))$, modeling the relationship between the scenario variable and the observed outcome.
 - Use this fitted model to infer $P_*(y|x_i)$ for each of the modeled scenarios $x_i$.
 - Calculate the model miscalibration error by comparing the original projection $P_m(y|x_i)$ with the inferred counterfactual observation $P_*(y|x_i)$.
 - **Requirements/Assumptions:** Requires multiple projections with natural variation in realized $x^*$. Strong assumptions are needed about the "true" relationship between $x$ and $y$, and the role of covariates, often drawing on causal inference methods.
 - **Limitations:** The necessary assumptions might be too strong or miss key system properties. Biases in the estimated observations can directly bias the error estimates.

**Simulation Experiment:**
To test these approaches, a simulation experiment was designed using a Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) epidemiological model.
- **"True" Model:** A "true" SIR model was defined for 50 locations, with varying basic reproduction numbers ($R_0$) and heterogeneity constants ($\alpha$). This allowed the calculation of "true" epidemic sizes under any given vaccination coverage.
- **"Projection" Models:** Ten "projection" SIR models were created, each with systematic and location-specific biases in their estimations of $R_0$ and $\alpha$ compared to the "true" model.
- **Scenario Projections:** Each projection model generated epidemic size projections for two fixed vaccination coverage scenarios (30% and 50%) for all 50 locations.
- **"Observed" and Counterfactual Data:** Realized vaccination coverages ($x^*$) were randomly generated for each location, distinct from the modeled scenarios. "Observed" epidemic sizes ($P_*(y|x^*)$) were then calculated using the "true" model and $x^*$. Crucially, the "true" counterfactual epidemic sizes ($P_*(y|x_i)$) for the 30% and 50% vaccination scenarios were also calculated using the "true" model, allowing for direct assessment of miscalibration error.
- **Reprojection:** For Approach 2, the projection models were "rerun" at the realized vaccination coverage ($x^*$) to obtain $P_m(y|x^*)$, assuming honest and exact reprojection.
- **Implementation of Approaches:** Each of the three proposed approaches was applied to the simulated data. For Approaches 2 and 3, Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) were used to model error/observations, both with and without location-specific $R_0$ as a covariate.
- **Evaluation:** The accuracy of each approach was assessed by comparing its estimated error distributions (mean and full distribution) against the "true" model miscalibration errors. Metrics included mean absolute error and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test statistic.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation experiment provided empirical insights into the performance of the three proposed approaches for assessing model error in counterfactual worlds.

**Approach 1: Evaluate only plausible scenarios**
- **Systematic Bias:** This approach demonstrated systematic bias in estimating model miscalibration error for both low and high vaccination scenarios. This bias primarily stemmed from its incomplete accounting for scenario deviation.
- **Misleading Results:** The method produced misleading results where scenario specification error (the difference between what would have happened in the modeled scenario and what actually happened in the realized scenario) sometimes offset model miscalibration error. This led to estimated errors near zero when actual errors were substantial. Conversely, cases existed where projections were accurate, but scenario deviation artificially inflated the estimated error.
- **Impact of Scenario Deviation:** Locations with larger differences between scenario-assumed and realized vaccination uptake exhibited more biased error estimates under Approach 1.

**Approaches 2 and 3: Inferring error distributions directly and Estimating observations in modeled scenarios**
- **Improved Accuracy:** Both Approach 2 and Approach 3 generated more accurate estimates of the error distribution compared to Approach 1, particularly when covariates were incorporated into the estimation process.
- **Comparable Mean Accuracy:** These two approaches were found to be comparably accurate in their mean error estimates.
- **Distribution Capture:** In terms of capturing the full error distribution (assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), Approach 2 outperformed Approach 3, with 14 out of 20 model-scenario comparisons showing no significant difference from the true distribution, compared to 10 out of 20 for Approach 3.
- **Specific Biases and Strengths:**
 - **Approach 3:** Showed a slight bias in the low vaccination scenario, specifically due to an overestimation of observations for locations with a high basic reproduction number ($R_0$). A notable characteristic was that any bias in estimating observations affected the error estimates for all models equally.
 - **Approach 2:** Did not exhibit this problem as consistently, likely due to the projection models' ability to capture the non-linear dynamics of the SIR model better than the statistical model used to infer observations in Approach 3. However, since errors were fitted independently for each model, the implied observation distributions could vary across models, making results less consistent across models compared to Approach 3.
- **Role of Covariates:**
 - **Approach 3:** The inclusion of covariates (location-specific $R_0$) was essential for Approach 3's performance. Without covariates, the inferred error distribution's variance was too large.
 - **Approach 2:** Covariates did not significantly improve the overall distribution of errors for Approach 2 but were necessary to generate location-specific error estimates.
 - **Location-Specific Errors:** When assessing the ability to capture location-specific errors, Approaches 2 and 3 were comparable. Approach 2 showed better performance in capturing errors for locations with higher $R_0$, where non-linear effects are more pronounced.

**Error Decomposition:**
The research highlights the utility of accurately estimating model miscalibration error, as it enables the decomposition of total observed deviation into its two components: model calibration error and scenario specification error. The study illustrates that these errors can be additive or can act in opposing directions. The latter case can lead to situations where a model appears to "get the right answer" (small observed deviation) but "for the wrong reason" (significant, but canceling, model miscalibration and scenario deviation errors). This reinforces the inadequacy of direct comparison (as in Approach 1) for a robust model evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a critical gap in the robust evaluation of models used for future planning and decision-making. By clearly distinguishing between scenario deviation and model miscalibration, and by proposing and testing concrete methodologies to isolate model miscalibration error, the paper significantly advances the theory and practice of scenario projection evaluation.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**
- **Enhanced Model Reliability:** The ability to retrospectively evaluate model miscalibration in counterfactual worlds provides a crucial feedback mechanism for model developers. This will facilitate continuous improvement of predictive models, leading to more reliable projections.
- **Informed Decision-Making:** For decision-makers, understanding the intrinsic calibration error of a model, independent of whether the assumed scenario materialized, offers a clearer picture of the model's trustworthiness and limitations. This knowledge can improve the confidence and effectiveness of decisions based on scenario analyses.
- **Transparency and Trust:** The framework enables a more transparent communication of model performance. By decomposing observed deviations into model calibration error and scenario specification error, evaluators can explain *why* projections differed from reality, even in complex cases where errors might cancel out, thus building greater trust with end-users.
- **Guidance for Methodology Selection:** The comparative analysis of the three approaches, particularly highlighting the strengths of Approach 3 (estimating observations) and Approach 2 (inferring errors directly) when certain conditions are met (e.g., strong statistical models of observations or important non-linear dynamics), provides practical guidance for researchers on selecting the most appropriate evaluation method for their specific context. It also underscores the limitations of simplistic direct comparisons (Approach 1).
- **Implications for Scenario Design:** The research indirectly impacts scenario design practices by emphasizing the need for measurable, continuous scenario axes and sufficient diversity in realized values across groups to facilitate robust statistical evaluation. This encourages a forward-looking perspective on evaluation during the initial stages of scenario development.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations, including the use of a relatively simple simulation framework, the assumption of underlying model structure matching the true data-generating process reasonably well, and the focus on point predictions rather than probabilistic forecasts. These areas represent avenues for future research to extend the applicability and robustness of the proposed methods in more complex, real-world settings with observation error and probabilistic outputs.

In conclusion, this work provides a rigorous and actionable framework for evaluating scenario projections, moving beyond the current limitations to offer a path towards more accountable and trustworthy model-informed decision-making in diverse fields.
