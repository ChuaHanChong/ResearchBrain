# SKATER: Synthesized Kinematics for Advanced Traversing Efficiency on a Humanoid Robot via Roller Skate Swizzles

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Science and Technology of China developed SKATER, a humanoid robot capable of roller skating using a deep reinforcement learning control framework for the swizzle gait. This system achieved a 75.86% reduction in ground reaction force impact intensity and a 63.34% reduction in Cost of Transport compared to traditional walking.

## Problem

- Traditional bipedal locomotion (walking, running) on humanoid robots incurs high-impact contact forces, leading to accelerated joint wear and poor energy utilization.
- Existing roller skating robots are either simplified bipedal platforms with low robustness or quadrupedal systems, leaving a gap for efficient and robust bipedal humanoid roller skating.
- Controlling continuous foot sliding with nonholonomic constraints on a humanoid robot for dynamic gaits is challenging with traditional model-based or explicit trajectory planning methods.

## Method

- Designed a custom 33-DoF humanoid robot, **SKATER**, with specialized feet featuring four inline passive wheels, optimized for ankle joint load reduction during skating.
- Developed a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) control framework using ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== in parallel simulation, employing a meticulously designed reward function that implicitly generates the swizzle gait without explicit kinematic trajectories.
- Implemented robust sim-to-real transfer strategies, including system identification and extensive domain randomization, to deploy the DRL policy successfully on the physical robot across varied friction surfaces.

## Results

- The swizzle gait reduced Impact Intensity by **75.86%** (**2469.44** N/s) compared to walking (**10231.42** N/s) in simulation, indicating significantly lower joint impact loads.
- SKATER achieved a **63.34%** reduction in Cost of Transport (CoT) compared to bipedal walking, with specific joint energy consumption reductions of approximately **95.39%** for Hip Pitch and **92.29%** for Ankle Pitch.
- The physical SKATER robot demonstrated stable omnidirectional locomotion with a **100%** success rate across varied friction surfaces (tile, rubber, gravel) and showed 65-**75%** reductions in peak lower-limb joint torques compared to walking.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Roller skating offers substantial biomechanical advantages for robots, leveraging continuous sliding and inertia to significantly reduce joint impact forces and improve energy efficiency compared to discrete walking.
> - Deep reinforcement learning, guided by an implicitly designed reward function, can effectively learn and generate complex, dynamic, and stable nonholonomic gaits like the roller skate swizzle on a humanoid robot.
> - Careful hardware design, particularly the wheel configuration and ankle joint strength, is critical for enabling robust roller skating performance on bipedal platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has seen remarkable advancements in recent years, with robots demonstrating increasingly agile and robust locomotion capabilities, including dynamic walking, running, jumping, and even dancing. However, a significant challenge inherent in these traditional locomotion modes is the repeated, high-impact contact forces between the robot's feet and the ground. These impacts lead to accelerated joint wear, increased noise, and substantial energy dissipation, limiting the operational endurance and longevity of humanoid robots.

This paper positions itself within a burgeoning area of robotics research that seeks to overcome these limitations by exploring alternative, more efficient, and lower-impact locomotion modalities. Humanoid roller skating is identified as a particularly promising avenue due to its biomechanical advantages observed in humans: smooth, continuous sliding motions that leverage body inertia to minimize kinetic energy loss and reduce joint impact forces. Previous studies, for instance, have shown roller skating to generate approximately 50% of the joint impact forces compared to running in humans.

Prior work on roller skating robots has predominantly focused on either highly simplified bipedal platforms with limited robustness or more recently, quadrupedal robots. Early bipedal roller skating efforts, such as those by Itabashi et al. and Hashimoto et al., often relied on model-based control or trajectory planning, which suffered from low robustness, poor environmental adaptability, and limited skating speeds. Iverach-Brereton et al.'s "Jennifer" robot demonstrated model-based control for ice skating but also faced speed and success rate limitations. More recently, quadrupedal platforms, notably the ETH research team's modified ANYmal and Chen et al.'s 4-DoF legged robot, have achieved significant success in roller skating, reporting substantial reductions in Cost of Transport (CoT) and demonstrating robust control frameworks. These quadrupedal advances, while impressive, do not fully address the unique challenges of bipedal humanoid roller skating, which include complex balance control with continuous foot sliding and intricate nonholonomic constraints.

This work explicitly addresses the gap in robust and efficient roller skating for *humanoid* robots. It moves beyond traditional model-based and explicit trajectory planning approaches by adopting Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). DRL has gained significant traction in high-dimensional robot control, particularly for gait generation. While some DRL approaches still integrate model-based priors (e.g., MIT's work combining LIP models with DRL), this paper aligns with a growing trend of "end-to-end" DRL methods that can spontaneously generate stable periodic gaits through implicitly designed reward functions, as exemplified by Peng et al.'s work on natural walking. Furthermore, the use of large-scale parallel simulation environments like Isaac Gym/IsaacLab is a critical enabling technology for training complex DRL policies and achieving successful sim-to-real transfer, a methodology widely adopted in advanced legged robot locomotion. By combining novel hardware design with an advanced, data-driven control strategy, SKATER directly contributes to expanding the locomotion repertoire of humanoid robots towards more efficient and durable operation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind the SKATER project stems from the inherent drawbacks of conventional bipedal locomotion methods like walking and running when applied to humanoid robots. These methods, despite their advancements, impose significant instantaneous impact forces at each foot strike, leading to:
- **Exacerbated Joint Wear:** Frequent high impacts accelerate the degradation of joint actuators and mechanical components.
- **Poor Energy Utilization:** The intermittent nature of ground contact and the need to absorb/re-generate kinetic energy with each step results in substantial energy loss.
- **High Demands on Actuators:** Requires high torque and velocity from joint actuators, limiting performance and potentially increasing system cost.

Inspired by the biomechanical efficiency of human roller skating, the authors identify it as a superior locomotion modality for robots. Roller skating allows for continuous sliding, rational utilization of body inertia, and minimal kinetic energy loss, significantly reducing joint impact and energy dissipation.

Given this motivation, the key objectives of this research are:
1. **Hardware Development:** To design and construct a novel humanoid robot, SKATER, equipped with specialized roller skate foot mechanisms compatible with its bipedal structure.
2. **Control Framework Development:** To develop a robust and adaptive deep reinforcement learning (DRL) control framework specifically for the roller skate swizzle gait on a humanoid platform. This framework aims to overcome challenges posed by continuous foot sliding, nonholonomic constraints, and the need for precise whole-body coordination, without relying on explicit kinematic trajectories or gait timing.
3. **Performance Validation:** To thoroughly evaluate the SKATER system in both simulation and physical experiments, demonstrating the advantages of roller skating (specifically the swizzle gait) over traditional bipedal walking in terms of:
 - Reducing joint impact loads (Impact Intensity).
 - Improving energy efficiency (Cost of Transport, CoT).
 - Ensuring motion smoothness and stability.
 - Achieving adaptability to various ground surfaces.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study combines novel hardware design with an advanced deep reinforcement learning (DRL) control strategy, specifically tailored for the complexities of bipedal roller skating.

**A. Hardware Design (SKATER Robot)**
The core of the hardware innovation is the SKATER robot itself, a 33-degree-of-freedom (DoF) humanoid, with 25 actuated DoFs (6 per leg, 5 per arm, 2 waist, 1 neck). It has a mass of 38 kg and a standing height of 140 cm. The critical feature is its specially designed foot mechanism: each foot is equipped with four passive inline polyurethane wheels arranged linearly. These wheels have a smaller diameter (62 mm) compared to standard roller skate wheels (72-80 mm). This design choice was made to lower the foot's center of mass (CoM) and reduce the moment load on the ankle roll axis, which is crucial during the lateral propulsion phases of roller skating. The authors explicitly avoided using off-the-shelf roller skate shoes due to incompatibility with the robot's rigid foot structure (compromising force transmission) and interference with the ankle joint's parallel-link transmission mechanism. An ASUS NUC mini-PC handles onboard computation, communicating with motors via EtherCAT-CAN and receiving state feedback from an Xsens MTi-630R IMU.

**B. Reinforcement Learning Control Framework**
The roller skating locomotion problem is formulated as a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP). The control policy $\pi_\theta$ is learned using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, a widely used policy gradient method, within the IsaacLab simulator, leveraging 4096 parallel simulation environments for efficient training.

- **Observation Space:** The Actor network receives a comprehensive state vector, including current joint positions and velocities, robot body angular velocity, gravity vector, user command velocity, and the action from the previous time step. Notably, a historical observation window of 4 past time steps is included to implicitly model dynamics and inertia. Wheel joint states are excluded as they are passively determined. The Critic network has access to additional "privileged information" like actual base linear velocity, inter-ankle distance, and ankle orientation angles.
- **Action Space:** A torque-based PD controller is used for joint actuation. The DRL policy outputs a residual joint position command, which is added to the current joint position to calculate the desired position for the PD controller. The action scaling coefficient $\beta$ implicitly regulates motion velocity.
- **Reward Function:** A multi-objective reward function comprising 22 terms is meticulously designed. Crucially, it **does not explicitly impose kinematic trajectories or gait timing constraints** for the swizzle gait. Instead, it guides the agent through:
 - **Task Rewards:** Penalizing deviations from desired linear and angular velocities.
 - **Base Penalties:** For high angular velocity, joint velocity/acceleration, action rate, joint position limits, and energy consumption.
 - **Joint Penalties:** For deviations in arm, waist, head, and ankle roll joints from neutral postures.
 - **Posture Penalties:** For maintaining a flat base orientation and target base height.
 - **Foot Constraints:** Penalties for feet being too near (min 0.2m) or too far (max 0.5m), guiding the characteristic swizzle motion.
 - **Wheel Penalties:** For wheel axial slip and air time (encouraging continuous ground contact).
 - **Symmetry Rewards:** Promoting bilateral symmetry in leg and arm joint positions and velocities.
 - **Contact Penalties:** For undesired body contacts. This implicit design strategy allows for the natural emergence of the swizzle gait and enhances adaptability.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer**
To bridge the simulation-reality gap, two key strategies are employed:
1. **System Identification:** Dynamic parameters of the physical robot (damping, moments of inertia) are carefully calibrated.
2. **Domain Randomization:** During training, various physical parameters (static/dynamic friction, link mass, CoM offset, actuator gains, wheel joint damping) are randomized within specified ranges. This forces the policy to be robust to parameter variations, facilitating successful transfer to the physical robot.

For comparison, bipedal walking gaits are also tested on SKATER, with its roller skate feet replaced by rigid flat feet, utilizing an open-source Unitree RL lab framework. The learned policy is validated through sim-to-sim tests in MuJoCo (known for high-precision contact simulation) before deployment on the physical SKATER robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of SKATER's roller skating capabilities, particularly the swizzle gait, demonstrated significant advantages over traditional bipedal walking.

**A. Simulation Results (MuJoCo)**
1. **Contact Force Analysis:** Comparing vertical Ground Reaction Forces (GRF) at 1 m/s, the swizzle gait exhibited smooth, periodic force profiles, with peak forces reaching 1.1-1.3 times body weight. In contrast, walking generated sharp impact peaks of 1.5-2.0 times body weight. Quantitatively, the Impact Intensity metric showed a **75.86% reduction** for the swizzle gait (2469.44 N/s) compared to walking (10231.42 N/s), highlighting significantly lower joint impact loads.
2. **Velocity Tracking Performance:** SKATER showed reasonable velocity tracking for variable forward speeds (0.6, 1.2, 1.8 m/s). However, it displayed consistent steady-state errors where actual velocity exceeded desired, and sluggish deceleration responses. This is attributed to the continuous sliding nature of roller skating, which limits rapid velocity adjustments compared to discrete foot braking in walking.
3. **Gait Pattern Visualization:** The DRL framework successfully generated the characteristic "swizzle-shaped" periodic foot trajectories in the horizontal plane, along with bounded inter-foot distance variations (between 0.2m and 0.5m) and coordinated ankle joint angles. This validated that the implicitly designed reward function, without explicit kinematic guidance, could lead to the emergence of the desired swizzle gait.

**B. Hardware Results (Physical SKATER Robot)**
The learned control policy was successfully transferred to the physical SKATER robot, with inference running at 50 Hz on the onboard mini-PC.
1. **Locomotion Performance Testing:** SKATER demonstrated stable and maneuverable omnidirectional locomotion, including forward sliding, backward sliding, and forward obstacle-crossing sliding. The robot initiated motion, accelerated, and braked stably using a pigeon-toed stance.
2. **Multi-Friction Terrain Testing:** The robot was tested on tile floor (low friction), rubber floor (medium friction), and gravel pavement (high friction). SKATER achieved a **100% success rate** across 10 trials for each surface type, demonstrating excellent robustness and adaptability to varying ground friction coefficients, largely due to the domain randomization strategy employed during training.
3. **Joint Impact Load Evaluation (Peak Torques):** A comparison of peak joint torques between roller skating and walking (at 1 m/s) showed substantial reductions for the swizzle gait, especially in the lower-limb joints. Notably, the **Hip Pitch joints experienced reductions of approximately 74-75%**, and the **Ankle Pitch joints saw reductions of 65-75%**. This significant decrease in peak torques is a direct benefit of the continuous sliding motion, which avoids the high-impact forces associated with discrete foot touchdowns in walking.
4. **Cost of Transport (CoT) Comparison:** The swizzle gait achieved a remarkable **63.34% reduction in CoT** compared to traditional bipedal walking. Joint-level analysis revealed that the Hip Pitch joint, typically a major energy consumer in walking, saw an **energy consumption reduction of about 95.39%** during swizzling. Similarly, the Ankle Pitch joint's energy consumption was reduced by **92.29%**. These results indicate that roller skating effectively leverages passive dynamics and momentum conservation, transforming discrete locomotion into continuous sliding, thereby significantly reducing actuation demands and improving energy efficiency for long-distance tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SKATER project represents a significant step forward in humanoid robot locomotion, offering a compelling alternative to traditional walking and running gaits with substantial practical implications.

**A. Scientific and Technical Contributions:**
- **Novel Locomotion Paradigm:** It successfully demonstrates the feasibility and advantages of roller skating as a high-efficiency, low-impact locomotion mode for humanoid robots, an area previously underexplored, especially for bipedal platforms.
- **Advanced DRL for Non-Holonomic Systems:** The research validates an innovative DRL control framework that can generate complex, dynamic gaits (swizzle) in systems with nonholonomic constraints without explicit kinematic modeling or trajectory planning. The implicit reward function design represents a more flexible and adaptable approach than traditional model-based methods.
- **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The effective use of system identification and domain randomization techniques showcases a robust methodology for deploying DRL-trained policies on complex physical humanoid robots, bridging a critical gap in robotics research.
- **Specialized Hardware Design:** The custom roller skate foot mechanism highlights thoughtful engineering to integrate roller skating capabilities within a humanoid robot's kinematic and dynamic constraints.

**B. Practical Significance and Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Robot Longevity and Reduced Maintenance:** The demonstrated **75.86% reduction in impact intensity** and **70-75% reduction in peak hip/ankle torques** directly translates to significantly less wear and tear on robot joints and actuators. This could drastically extend the operational lifespan of humanoid robots and substantially lower maintenance costs, making them more economical for long-term deployment.
- **Increased Energy Efficiency and Endurance:** A **63.34% reduction in Cost of Transport** is a groundbreaking achievement. This means SKATER can operate for much longer periods on a single charge, greatly enhancing its utility in applications requiring extended endurance, such as logistics, surveillance, large-scale facility management, or exploration over relatively flat terrains.
- **Expanded Operational Domains:** The demonstrated robustness across varied friction surfaces (tile, rubber, gravel) indicates that roller skating robots could operate reliably in a wider range of real-world environments, from indoor industrial settings to outdoor paved areas.
- **Foundation for Future Robot Capabilities:** This work lays the groundwork for more advanced roller skating gaits (e.g., push steps, single-leg support) and integration with perception for autonomous navigation in complex, dynamic environments. The concept of actively switchable passive wheel-foot mechanisms could lead to hybrid robots capable of both efficient skating and versatile walking/climbing as needed.

In conclusion, SKATER opens up exciting new possibilities for humanoid robot mobility. By enabling robots to emulate the elegant and efficient motion of roller skating, this research contributes significantly to the development of more durable, energy-efficient, and adaptable humanoid robots that can perform complex tasks in diverse environments.
