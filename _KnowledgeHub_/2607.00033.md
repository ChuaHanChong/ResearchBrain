# Learning Dexterous Manipulation Using Contact Wrench Guidance From Human Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> NVIDIA researchers developed CHORD, a framework that enables robots to learn dexterous manipulation from human demonstrations by leveraging an object-centric contact wrench space for guidance. This approach allows robots to acquire long-horizon, contact-rich skills for rigid and articulated objects, achieving an average 82.12% success rate across 1,831 benchmark tasks and outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Bridging the morphological and kinematic gap between human and robot hands for effective transfer of complex, contact-rich manipulation skills from human demonstrations.
- Existing learning from demonstration methods often lack scalability and generalizability, requiring task-specific robot demonstrations or highly aligned human-robot data.
- Current contact guidance methods, primarily relying on contact locations, are insufficient to capture the full physical effect of contacts (force direction, normal) essential for dynamic, long-horizon tasks.

## Method

- **CHORD** introduces an ==object-centric contact wrench space== as an ==embodiment-agnostic representation== to functionally compare human and robot contact behaviors.
- The framework utilizes ==reinforcement learning== with a ==composite reward function== that includes a novel ==contact wrench space (CWS) reward==, task-specific rewards, and imitation rewards.
- A large-scale benchmark of 4,739 bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks, converted into simulatable ==Isaac Lab== environments, was curated to support robust evaluation and training.

## Results

- CHORD achieved an average success rate of **82.12%** across **1,831** diverse manipulation tasks, demonstrating scalability and robustness for long-horizon, contact-rich scenarios.
- The method consistently matched or surpassed state-of-the-art baselines like ManipTrans, DexMachina, and SPIDER on their respective task suites, with broader applicability across rigid, articulated, and multi-object manipulation.
- Ablation studies confirmed that contact wrench guidance significantly outperformed position-only and no-contact guidance, and learned policies were successfully deployed on a real Dexmate robot for diverse manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representing contacts in an object-centric wrench space provides a robust and functional method for transferring human manipulation intent, effectively bridging the human-robot morphology gap.
> - Contact wrench guidance is significantly more effective than kinematic-only or contact location-based supervision for learning complex, long-horizon, and contact-rich behaviors.
> - A strong positive correlation between the contact wrench support reward and task success indicates its reliability as a training signal and a useful proxy metric for manipulation performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic dexterous manipulation has increasingly explored the use of human demonstrations to mitigate the challenges associated with policy optimization, particularly in complex, contact-rich tasks. Prior work has leveraged human demonstrations for both policy optimization and learning transferable human-robot representations. However, several limitations restrict the scalability and applicability of existing methods.

Current approaches that transfer human demonstrations often rely on assumptions regarding the transfer process, which can be brittle. Representation-learning methods typically require aligned human and robot data specific to each task and object, limiting their generalization beyond curated environments. A fundamental challenge arises from the morphological and kinematic differences between human and robot hands; direct replay of human hand motions by a robot does not typically reproduce the intended manipulation behavior.

Reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation has been applied to dexterous manipulation. These methods often use task-level imitation objectives, such as object and hand-reference tracking. More recent advancements integrate contact guidance to enhance exploration. For instance, ManipTrans incorporates rewards based on contact forces near demonstrated hand-object interactions. DexMachina and SPIDER utilize demonstrated contact locations, often coupled with curricula like a Virtual Object Controller (VOC) that applies auxiliary wrenches to smooth the learning process by guiding object movement along reference trajectories.

A limitation of contact location-based rewards is their insufficiency for capturing the full physical effect of a contact. The outcome of a contact depends not only on its location but also on the contact surface normal and force direction. While wrench space supervision has been explored in prior RL research, these applications typically focus on static stability for grasping, which is too rigid for general, dynamic robot manipulation that involves transient, non-force-closure phases such as pushing, levering, and sliding.

This work positions itself to address these gaps by proposing an object-centric contact wrench space as a shared, embodiment-agnostic representation for comparing human and robot contact behaviors. This approach aims to provide a more functional and scalable guidance mechanism for RL, particularly for long-horizon, contact-rich tasks involving both rigid and articulated objects, thereby overcoming the limitations of kinematic-only or location-based contact supervision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, named Contact Wrench Guidance from Human Demonstration in Robotic Dexterous Manipulation (CHORD), for enabling robots to learn long-horizon, dexterous manipulation skills for rigid and articulated objects from human demonstrations using reinforcement learning.

The motivations for this work stem from several challenges inherent in transferring human manipulation skills to robotic platforms:

1. **Bridging the Human-Robot Morphology Gap**: Human and robotic hands possess distinct morphologies, kinematics, and geometries. A robot cannot directly mimic human hand movements to achieve the same functional outcome on an object. This necessitates a method that can abstract the functional intent of human manipulation, rather than just replicating kinematics.
2. **Scalability of Learning from Demonstration**: Existing methods for learning from human demonstrations often face scalability issues. They may require specific robot demonstrations for each task or object, or they depend on precisely aligned human-robot data, limiting their application to a broad range of tasks and environments. A key motivation is to enable scalable knowledge transfer without such demanding data requirements.
3. **Limitations of Current Contact Guidance**: Previous attempts to incorporate contact guidance in RL, such as rewarding based on demonstrated contact locations, have proven insufficient. The physical effect of a contact (e.g., the resulting object motion) is determined by more than just its spatial position; factors like contact normal and force direction are crucial. The research is motivated by the need for a more physically aligned and robust representation of contact behavior.
4. **Enabling Long-Horizon, Contact-Rich Manipulation**: Dexterous manipulation often involves complex, non-smooth contact dynamics and extends over long durations, encompassing phases like pushing, levering, and sliding that do not always involve stable, force-closure grasps. Current RL methods often struggle with the exploration challenges posed by these intricate contact sequences and long-term objectives.
5. **Generalizability across Embodiments and Demonstration Types**: The ideal framework would be generalizable across different robot embodiments (e.g., hand-only to whole-body robots) and robust to various types of human demonstrations, including those with noisy contact information (e.g., from video reconstructions).

The central motivation for CHORD is the insight that contact provides a natural and universal bridge between human demonstrations and robot actions. By representing contacts in an object-centric wrench space—which captures the forces and torques that contacts can induce on an object—the functional equivalence of human and robot manipulation can be measured. This approach, which focuses on the motion-inducing capability of contacts rather than their precise locations or kinematic trajectories, aims to provide a scalable and robust guidance signal for reinforcement learning in diverse and contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CHORD (Contact Wrench Guidance from Human Demonstration in Robotic Dexterous Manipulation) is a framework designed for learning long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation from human demonstrations. The approach integrates reinforcement learning with a novel contact wrench space guidance mechanism and is supported by a large-scale simulation benchmark.

**Input and Initial Processing:**
The framework takes a human reference trajectory $\tau_{ref} = \{x_{human_t}, x_{object_t}\}_{t=1}^H$ as input, comprising 3D human hand keypoints and object-part poses. Initially, the human hand motion is retargeted to robot configurations ($x_{robot_t}$) using inverse kinematics. The objective for the robot's policy is to ensure its object pose rollouts ($\{s_{object_t}\}_{t=1}^H$) track the reference trajectory ($\{x_{object_t}\}_{t=1}^H$).

**Reinforcement Learning (RL) Formulation:**
The robot policy $\pi(a_t | o_{robot_t}, o_{object_t}; x_{robot_t}, x_{object_t})$ predicts actions based on current robot and object observations, as well as the reference motion. The training incorporates a composite reward function: $r = r_{task} + r_{imit} + r_{contact}$.

1. **Task Reward ($r_{task}$)**: This reward encourages the robot's object movements to follow the demonstrated reference trajectory. It accounts for both individual object-part poses and, for multi-object interactions, the relative poses between parts. An exponential kernel measures the squared Euclidean distance for pose differences. For tasks requiring precise object-object geometry (e.g., insertion), a relative reward term, $r_{relative}$, is added, which activates only during demonstrated object-object interaction phases.
2. **Imitation Reward ($r_{imit}$)**: This reward regularizes the robot's motion towards the retargeted human motion, promoting kinematic similarity between the robot's hand trajectory and the transformed human demonstration. It uses an exponential kernel on the squared difference between robot and retargeted human configurations.
3. **Contact Guidance Reward ($r_{contact}$)**: This is the core novelty of CHORD. Instead of relying on contact locations, it leverages an object-centric wrench space to compare human and robot contacts.
 - **Wrench Matrix Extraction**: For each object part $k$ and human demonstration, contact positions ($p_{h,k}$) and contact normals ($n_{h,k}$) are extracted in the object frame. The Coulomb friction cone at each contact is approximated by a polyhedral cone with $d$ unit-magnitude edge forces. Each edge force induces a primitive wrench $w_{i,j_{h,k}} = [f_{j_{h,k}}, p_{i_{h,k}} \times f_{j_{h,k}}]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^6$. All primitive wrenches for a hand-part pair are collected into a wrench matrix $\mathcal{W}_{h,k}$.
 - **Support Function for Comparison**: To compare wrench matrices with potentially different dimensions, a support function is introduced. Given $b$ pre-sampled unit directions $\mathcal{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times b}$, the support function $\sigma_{h,k}$ is defined as the maximum projection of the primitive wrenches onto each basis direction: $\sigma_{h,k} = \max_{col}(\mathcal{B}^\top \mathcal{W}_{h,k}) \in \mathbb{R}^b$. A corresponding robot support function $\sigma_{r,k}$ is computed.
 - **Contact Wrench Space (CWS) Reward ($r_k^{cws}$)**: This reward compares $\sigma_{r,k}$ against $\sigma_{h,k}$ with a relative tolerance $\beta$. It rewards robot contacts that generate support within a tolerable range of the human reference. Additionally, the policy is penalized for generating unintended contacts (robot contact present when human contact is absent) and for missing required contacts (human contact present when robot contact is absent).

**Training Efficiency and Robustness Mechanisms:**
CHORD incorporates several mechanisms to enhance training:
- **Arbitrary State Resets**: The simulator resets to random states along the reference trajectory.
- **Virtual Object Controller (VOC)**: A VOC is used to assist object movement in early training and is fully active for a stabilization window after resets, before annealing through a curriculum.
- **Task-Relevant Disturbances**: Object parts are perturbed with wrenches sampled from the human contact wrench matrix ($\mathcal{W}_{h,k}$), providing disturbances aligned with demonstrated contact mechanics.
- **Residual Action Space**: The policy operates in a residual action space, using the retargeted robot motion as a prior.

**Handling Noisy Demonstrations and Generalization:**
- **Force-Closure Objective for Noise**: When contact estimates from human demonstrations are noisy (e.g., from RGB videos), direct wrench matching can be unreliable. In such cases, a reduced contact wrench guidance objective is used, $r_k^{fc} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B 1[\sigma_{r,k,b} > \epsilon]$, which rewards the robot for generating positive support along wrench-space basis directions, effectively promoting force closure.
- **Whole-Body Manipulation Extension**:
 - For **hand-only references**, an inpainting module is trained to predict full-body motion from end-effector trajectories, and the standard CWS RL formulation is applied.
 - For **whole-body references** (e.g., from third-person-view videos), finger reconstruction can be inaccurate. In these scenarios, the more robust force-closure objective ($r_k^{fc}$) is utilized.

**Dexterous Manipulation Benchmark:**
To support large-scale evaluation, the authors curated a benchmark of 4,739 bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks. These tasks are constructed from open-source motion-capture datasets and reconstructed in-house videos, converted into simulatable environments in Isaac Lab. The benchmark includes manipulation of single and multiple rigid objects, as well as articulated objects. It features longer horizons and denser contact events compared to prior benchmarks, requiring complex dexterous strategies. A post-processing pipeline converts human MANO hand fits and object trajectories to robot-specific trajectories using differential inverse kinematics, including quality checks for hand-object penetration and simulator replay validity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate CHORD's scalability, its advantages over existing methods, the efficacy of wrench space guidance, and its generalization capabilities.

**Scalability and Performance on Large-Scale Tasks:**
CHORD was evaluated on 1,831 tasks sampled from its benchmark, using a consistent set of hyperparameters. It achieved an average success rate of 82.12%. Qualitatively, the learned policies were observed to solve diverse contact-rich tasks, including pick-and-place, handover, coordinated manipulation, articulated-object manipulation, and tool use, while closely following human demonstrations. The framework demonstrated robustness to retargeting noise and potential hand-object interpenetration through its wrench-space guidance. Stable training required the contact wrench reward to converge before the Virtual Object Controller (VOC) assistance was annealed.

**Baselines Comparison:**
CHORD was compared against state-of-the-art dexterous manipulation methods: ManipTrans, DexMachina, and SPIDER. The evaluation utilized each baseline's original task suite and metrics (e.g., area under the object-tracking-error curve (AUC) for DexMachina, success rate for SPIDER and ManipTrans). CHORD consistently matched or surpassed the performance of these baselines across their respective metrics. For instance, on DexMachina's task suite, CHORD achieved an AUC of 0.687 ± 0.358 compared to DexMachina's 0.232 ± 0.214. Notably, CHORD demonstrated broader applicability, succeeding across rigid, articulated, and multi-object manipulation categories, unlike prior methods that often specialized in subsets.

**Examination of Contact Wrench Guidance:**
An ablation study compared CHORD (using contact wrench support reward) against "Position Only" (using contact position reward, similar to DexMachina) and "No Contact" (using only task and imitation rewards). On representative long-horizon articulated object tasks (box grab, mixer use), CHORD achieved significantly higher success rates (box grab: 0.702 ± 0.257; mixer use: 0.894 ± 0.023) compared to "Position Only" (box grab: 0.334 ± 0.141; mixer use: 0.624 ± 0.166) and "No Contact" (box grab: 0.384 ± 0.206; mixer use: 0.423 ± 0.273). This indicates that rich contact guidance is crucial, and wrench-based supervision is more effective than location-based rewards for reproducing contact-rich behaviors.

**Correlation between Contact Wrench Reward and Task Success:**
Across 1,831 benchmark runs, a strong positive association (Pearson $r \approx 0.80$) was observed between the normalized Contact Wrench Support (CWS) reward and task success. This correlation was consistent within individual datasets ($r=0.76-0.89$). The relationship exhibited a monotonic, saturating trend, where success increased steeply with CWS reward and then plateaued. This finding suggests that the CWS reward serves as a reliable training signal and a useful proxy metric for manipulation success.

**Diverse Capabilities and Long-Horizon Manipulation:**
CHORD maintained high object-tracking accuracy (ADD-AUC $\approx 0.85-0.98$) across a wide range of interaction horizons, including tasks lasting nearly a minute (40-48 seconds). In contrast, baseline methods showed substantial performance degradation as the task horizon increased. This outcome supports the claim that contact wrench space serves as a unified abstraction, enabling CHORD to compose a broad repertoire of manipulation behaviors for long-horizon task execution.

**Generalization to Whole-Body Manipulation:**
CHORD was extended to whole-body manipulation. For hand-only references, where full-body motion was predicted by an inpainting module, CHORD achieved a 92.5% overall success rate on 12 tasks (rigid, articulated, multi-object) on a G1 robot with Dex3 three-fingered hands. This demonstrated the efficacy of wrench-space alignment in learning contact behaviors despite morphological differences. An ablation showed CHORD significantly outperforming the "Position Only" approach (overall 0.925 vs 0.217 success rate) in this cross-embodiment transfer scenario. For whole-body references from third-person-view videos (where hand-object reconstruction noise is higher), the reduced force-closure objective (Section 3.2) was successfully employed, achieving consistent success on 5 loco-manipulation tasks.

**Real-World Evaluation:**
The learned policies were deployed on a Dexmate robot equipped with two Sharpa dexterous hands, with robot and object poses tracked by a motion-capture system. Both open-loop action-chunk execution and closed-loop inference were tested. The policies successfully manipulated both rigid and articulated objects with bimanual coordination, indicating successful transferability to real hardware and accurate contact control.

**Teleoperation Comparison (Pilot Study):**
A qualitative pilot study compared CHORD's performance to human teleoperation for contact-rich tasks. Teleoperation proved difficult due to issues such as inverse kinematics not preserving intended finger configurations, lack of haptic feedback, and limited direct control over joint-level torques. Operators could achieve approximate object trajectories, but the resulting grasps were often fragile and susceptible to minor errors, leading to unstable manipulation or task failure. This comparison highlights the potential value of RL-based approaches like CHORD for learning robust and precise contact interactions.

**Robustness on Human Demonstration Noise:**
An ablation study on noise levels (Appendix A.1) showed that CHORD remained robust to moderate hand-pose noise with gradual performance degradation. When noise was introduced into contact guidance, performance decreased more noticeably. At very high noise levels, force-closure guidance could outperform noisy wrench-space guidance, though it reduced dexterity by prioritizing stable grasps over faithful imitation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper offers several significant contributions and has the potential to impact the field of robotic manipulation in multiple ways:

1. **Advancement in Dexterous Manipulation from Demonstration**: CHORD addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: effectively transferring complex, contact-rich manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robots. By moving beyond direct kinematic imitation or simple contact location matching, it provides a more robust and functionally aligned approach to human-to-robot skill transfer.
2. **Scalability and Generalizability**: The framework's demonstrated scalability across a large and diverse benchmark of 1,831 tasks (from a total of 4,739) represents a notable advancement. This reduces the reliance on task-specific robot demonstrations, which is a significant bottleneck in developing broad manipulation capabilities. Its ability to generalize across different robot embodiments (hand-only to whole-body) and adapt to varied demonstration qualities (clean motion capture to noisy video reconstructions) broadens the applicability of learning from demonstration.
3. **Novelty of Contact Wrench Guidance**: The introduction of an object-centric contact wrench space as a unified, embodiment-agnostic representation for contact guidance is a key innovation. This allows for a functional comparison of how human and robot contacts influence object motion, circumventing the limitations posed by morphological differences between human and robot hands. This approach is particularly impactful for dynamic, long-horizon manipulation tasks that involve transient, non-force-closure interactions.
4. **Enabling Long-Horizon Tasks**: By successfully tackling tasks with extended temporal horizons and dense contact events, CHORD contributes to the development of robots capable of performing more complex, multi-step manipulation sequences that are common in real-world scenarios.
5. **New Benchmark for Research**: The creation and release of a large-scale simulation benchmark of bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks, comprising 4,739 tasks, is a valuable resource for the robotics community. This benchmark provides a standardized platform for evaluating and comparing future research in learning from demonstration and dexterous manipulation.
6. **Real-World Applicability**: The successful deployment of policies trained with CHORD on real robot hardware, in both open-loop and closed-loop settings, demonstrates the practical feasibility and potential for real-world application of the learned skills. This bridges the gap between simulation-based learning and physical robot execution.
7. **Addressing Teleoperation Limitations**: The pilot study comparing CHORD to teleoperation highlights the inherent difficulties in manually controlling contact-rich tasks. This suggests that learned policies, such as those derived from CHORD, can offer a more robust and precise alternative for executing such intricate maneuvers.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
Despite its strengths, the paper identifies several limitations. Real-world deployment currently relies on state-based observations, indicating a need for future work in vision-based deployment. Effective contact guidance requires relatively clean human demonstrations, as noisy contacts can degrade reward quality and necessitate a switch to a less dexterous force-closure assumption. Lastly, object pose error, while useful, is acknowledged as an imperfect evaluation metric; future research could explore more task-aware success metrics. Addressing these limitations could further enhance CHORD's applicability and robustness.
