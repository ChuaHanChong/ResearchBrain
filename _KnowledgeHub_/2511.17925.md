# Switch-JustDance: Benchmarking Whole Body Motion Tracking Controllers Using a Commercial Console Game

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce Switch-JustDance, a low-cost framework for benchmarking whole-body humanoid robot controllers using a commercial console game. The system demonstrated that state-of-the-art robot controllers achieve roughly one-third of average human performance in dynamic dance routines, while validating the in-game score as a reliable performance metric.

## Problem

- Lack of standardized, reproducible, and physically grounded benchmarks for whole-body robot control that allow for direct human-robot performance comparison.
- Existing evaluation methods (pre-collected datasets, simulations, costly MoCap setups) have limitations in accessibility, scope, or capturing real-world hardware factors.
- Absence of quantitative, real-world criteria to assess how close robot athletic performance is to human capabilities.

## Method

![[2511.17925-method.png]]
*The Switch-JustDance pipeline captures game footage, reconstructs human motion in real-time, retargets it to the robot, and uses the in-game score to evaluate the quality of the movement.*

- Developed **Switch-JustDance**, a framework integrating the Nintendo Switch and Just Dance game with humanoid robot control via a ==real-time pipeline==.
- Utilized a custom ==motion capture module (GVHMR)== for 3D human pose reconstruction and a ==retargeting module (GMR)== to map motions to the robot.
- Leveraged the game's built-in scoring system as a low-cost, objective, and ==physically grounded performance metric== for ==hardware-in-the-loop evaluation==.

## Results

- System validation showed JDS inversely correlated with PA-MPJPE (**r from -0.76 to -0.42**) and high repeatability (**ICC 0.735**, **CV 10.0%**), confirming its utility as a benchmark metric.
- The best-performing robot controller achieved approximately **one-third** of the average human baseline JDS (**2,900 vs. 8,538**), highlighting current limitations in robot agility and coordination.
- Robots exhibited significantly higher joint jerk and acceleration compared to humans, especially with dynamic inputs, and struggled on 'Hard' routines, leading to a **573-point** average JDS decrease for controllers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Just Dance Score (JDS) is a valid, discriminative, and repeatable metric for evaluating whole-body motion tracking quality, inversely correlating with kinematic errors and capable of differentiating performance levels.
> - A substantial performance gap exists between current humanoid controllers and human capabilities in dynamic dance, particularly concerning smoothness and sustained agile movements.
> - Different input motion characteristics (smoothed vs. dynamic) and evaluation environments (simulation vs. real-world) significantly impact controller performance, revealing trade-offs between tracking accuracy, stability, and smoothness.
