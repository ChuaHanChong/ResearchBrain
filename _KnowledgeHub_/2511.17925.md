# Switch-JustDance: Benchmarking Whole Body Motion Tracking Controllers Using a Commercial Console Game

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduce Switch-JustDance, a low-cost framework for benchmarking whole-body humanoid robot controllers using a commercial console game. The system demonstrated that state-of-the-art robot controllers achieve roughly one-third of average human performance in dynamic dance routines, while validating the in-game score as a reliable performance metric.

## Problem

- Lack of standardized, reproducible, and physically grounded benchmarks for whole-body robot control that allow for direct human-robot performance comparison.
- Existing evaluation methods (pre-collected datasets, simulations, costly MoCap setups) have limitations in accessibility, scope, or capturing real-world hardware factors.
- Absence of quantitative, real-world criteria to assess how close robot athletic performance is to human capabilities.

## Method

![[2511.17925-method.png]]
*The Switch-JustDance pipeline captures game footage, reconstructs human motion in real-time, retargets it to the robot, and uses the in-game score to evaluate the quality of the movement.*

- Developed **Switch-JustDance**, a framework integrating the Nintendo Switch and Just Dance game with humanoid robot control via a ==real-time pipeline==.
- Utilized a custom ==motion capture module (GVHMR)== for 3D human pose reconstruction and a ==retargeting module (GMR)== to map motions to the robot.
- Leveraged the game's built-in scoring system as a low-cost, objective, and ==physically grounded performance metric== for ==hardware-in-the-loop evaluation==.

## Results

- System validation showed JDS inversely correlated with PA-MPJPE (**r from -0.76 to -0.42**) and high repeatability (**ICC 0.735**, **CV 10.0%**), confirming its utility as a benchmark metric.
- The best-performing robot controller achieved approximately **one-third** of the average human baseline JDS (**2,900 vs. 8,538**), highlighting current limitations in robot agility and coordination.
- Robots exhibited significantly higher joint jerk and acceleration compared to humans, especially with dynamic inputs, and struggled on 'Hard' routines, leading to a **573-point** average JDS decrease for controllers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Just Dance Score (JDS) is a valid, discriminative, and repeatable metric for evaluating whole-body motion tracking quality, inversely correlating with kinematic errors and capable of differentiating performance levels.
> - A substantial performance gap exists between current humanoid controllers and human capabilities in dynamic dance, particularly concerning smoothness and sustained agile movements.
> - Different input motion characteristics (smoothed vs. dynamic) and evaluation environments (simulation vs. real-world) significantly impact controller performance, revealing trade-offs between tracking accuracy, stability, and smoothness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics, particularly whole-body control for humanoid and legged robots, has seen advancements, enabling robots to execute agile and coordinated movements. These developments span both model-based whole-body controllers and reinforcement learning approaches, leading to capabilities such as gymnastic motions, dynamic dancing, and real-time teleoperation. However, a persistent challenge in this domain is the absence of standardized, reproducible, and physically grounded benchmarks that allow for direct and fair comparison of robot athletic performance with human capabilities in real-world settings.

Current evaluation practices often rely on several methods, each with limitations. Pre-collected human motion datasets, such as AMASS and LaFAN1, are used for training and evaluation but are infrequently updated, and their varied evaluation protocols hinder consistent cross-method comparisons. Simulation benchmarks, like HumanoidBench, offer standardization but are restricted to specific robot models and simulated environments, failing to capture real-world hardware factors. Real-world benchmarking, particularly with motion-capture (MoCap) systems, is costly, requires specialized equipment and controlled environments, making large-scale adoption impractical. Furthermore, direct, quantitative comparisons between robot and human performance under identical criteria are rare, leaving a gap in understanding how close robots are to achieving human-level athletic skill.

This work positions itself within the context of leveraging commercial games for AI benchmarking, a strategy that has previously catalyzed progress in various AI domains. Examples include the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) for Atari games, VizDoom and StarCraft II for complex tasks, Minecraft-based ecosystems for embodied agents, and Grand Theft Auto V for synthetic data generation in driving perception. These platforms offer standardized interfaces, abundant content, and community baselines.

The paper also builds upon the use of commodity gaming hardware for embodied AI. Previous efforts utilized Microsoft Kinect for low-cost full-body motion capture in human-robot interaction and rehabilitation, and Nintendo Wii devices for motion and balance signal capture. More recently, VR-based platforms (e.g., Open-TeleVision, H2O, OmniH2O, Open-WBT) have emerged as alternatives to optical MoCap for humanoid learning and teleoperation, providing immersive experiences and scalable data collection, but often requiring custom infrastructure.

Switch-JustDance contributes to this landscape by proposing a novel, low-cost, and accessible benchmarking framework that integrates a commercial motion-sensing console game (Just Dance on the Nintendo Switch) with humanoid robot control. It addresses the identified gaps by providing a standardized platform for physically grounded, hardware-in-the-loop evaluation, with a built-in scoring system that enables direct, quantitative human-robot performance comparisons. The framework's ability to offer continuous motion updates and a continually expanding content library further distinguishes it from static datasets or specialized MoCap setups.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing methods for evaluating whole-body robot control and the scarcity of standardized benchmarks that allow for direct comparison with human performance in real-world scenarios. While humanoid and legged robots demonstrate increasingly agile and coordinated movements, the community lacks a fair, reproducible, and physically grounded method to assess their athletic capabilities.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **To propose Switch-JustDance as a low-cost and accessible framework for evaluating whole-body humanoid controllers:** The researchers aim to leverage the Nintendo Switch console and the Just Dance game as a standardized, real-world benchmark platform. The motivation for this is the low cost (approximately $400 for a Nintendo Switch), plug-and-play setup, robustness for repeated use, and a large, continually updated library of full-body motions offered by the game. The game's built-in scoring system, designed for human players of different body sizes, is identified as a suitable mechanism for evaluating robot performance.

2. **To systematically validate the Just Dance platform’s scoring mechanism:** Before utilizing Just Dance as a benchmarking tool, the researchers recognized the critical need to assess whether its built-in scoring provides consistent and meaningful feedback that correlates with controller quality. This objective involves a systematic validation study focused on three aspects:
 - **Validity:** To determine if the Just Dance Score (JDS) is a valid indicator of humanoid controller performance, specifically by correlating it with a standard kinematic-based metric (Procrustes-Aligned Mean Per-Joint Position Error, PA-MPJPE).
 - **Discriminability:** To ascertain if the JDS is sensitive enough to differentiate between performances of varying skill levels (e.g., static baseline, average human players, expert human players).
 - **Repeatability:** To evaluate the consistency of the JDS across repeated trials of the same dance motion, ensuring reliable and stable performance measurements.

3. **To benchmark state-of-the-art humanoid whole-body controllers on hardware and provide insights into their capabilities and limitations:** Building on the validated platform, the final objective is to apply Switch-JustDance to evaluate representative state-of-the-art controllers (GMT, TWIST, Any2Track). This involves comparing their performance against human baselines and analyzing how different input motion characteristics (smoothed vs. dynamic) and evaluation environments (simulation vs. real-world hardware) affect controller behavior. The goal is to identify existing performance gaps between robots and humans, uncover specific challenges in dynamic whole-body dancing, and reveal differences in robustness and tracking accuracy among the evaluated control policies.

These objectives collectively aim to establish Switch-JustDance as a practical and physically grounded benchmark to advance athletic whole-body robot control and embodied AI research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the development and validation of the Switch-JustDance benchmarking pipeline, followed by its application to benchmark state-of-the-art humanoid controllers.

#### 3.1. Switch-JustDance Pipeline Overview

The Switch-JustDance system enables humanoid motion controllers to play the Just Dance game on the Nintendo Switch, utilizing the in-game score as an objective performance metric. The pipeline consists of four main modules:

1. **Streaming Module:** Captures real-time Nintendo Switch gameplay video via an HDMI capture card, streaming frames to the motion capture system with minimal latency.
2. **MoCap Module:** Reconstructs 3D human poses from the monocular RGB video using GVHMR, outputting SMPL parameterizations. GVHMR is modified for real-time operation using a sliding-window inference scheme, providing stable pose estimates at approximately 200 ms per frame.
3. **Retargeting Module:** Maps the reconstructed SMPL motions to the target robot morphology using GMR. This module runs at roughly 50 ms per frame, generating robot-space keyframe trajectories synchronized with MoCap updates. An asynchronous interpolation thread uses linear interpolation (LERP) for positions and spherical interpolation (SLERP) for rotations to increase the output frequency to a smooth 10–15 Hz reference stream for the robot controller.
4. **Motion Tracking Controller:** This represents the humanoid controller under evaluation. It generates control commands based on the retargeted reference motion and the robot’s proprioceptive feedback.

A Joy-Con controller is securely mounted to the robot’s hand, allowing the Nintendo Switch to track the robot's movements and compute in-game scores identically to a human participant. The entire pipeline is implemented on a workstation with an RTX 4090 GPU and an Intel Core i9-13900K CPU, with GVHMR inference being the dominant factor in end-to-end runtime.

#### 3.2. System Validation

A systematic validation study was conducted to evaluate the properties of Switch-JustDance, addressing validity, discriminability, and repeatability.

- **User Study:** Ten human participants (ages 20–34) with diverse demographics performed five songs of varying difficulty, each repeated four times, yielding 200 dance trials.
- **Validity:** The Just Dance Score (JDS) was compared against Procrustes-Aligned Mean Per-Joint Position Error (PA-MPJPE), a standard kinematic metric. PA-MPJPE was computed by aligning each participant’s motion with the reference choreography and calculating the mean joint position error.
- **Discriminability:** JDS values were compared across three performance levels: a static baseline (Joy-Con stationary, yielding scores near 0), average scores of study participants (intermediate range), and world leaderboard scores (expert human players, maximum 13,333).
- **Repeatability:** Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) and Coefficient of Variation (CV) were calculated for JDS to assess consistency across repeated trials. Kendall’s Coefficient of Concordance (KCC) was used to evaluate ranking consistency among participants across different songs and repetitions.
- **Potential Shortcoming:** The reliance of the Just Dance scoring system primarily on single Joy-Con motion was acknowledged as a limitation, though it was posited that high scores implicitly require full-body coordination.

#### 3.3. Benchmark Metrics

Four complementary metrics were used for controller evaluation:
- **Just Dance Score (JDS):** The in-game score (max 13,333).
- **Mean Per Joint Position Error (MPJPE, mm):** Average joint position error between robot state estimation and retargeted reference, measured in the robot's torso frame. Reported as "Active" (before fall) and "All" (entire sequence).
- **Success Rate (SR, %):** Percentage of trials completed without falling or overheating.
- **Smoothness:** Quantified by average joint jerk (rad/s³) and acceleration (rad/s²) using finite difference.

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

Controllers were evaluated across different input fidelity levels and environments:
- **Input Motion Types:**
 - **Streaming Smoothed Gameplay (Smo):** Real-time, temporally filtered poses (low-pass smoothing, outlier rejection, velocity clamps) to reduce jitter and emphasize stability, at the cost of high-frequency details and small phase lag.
 - **Offline Dynamic Reference (Dyn):** Preprocessed trajectories from unfiltered streams, refined offline for temporal smoothness, kinematic continuity, and coordinate-frame consistency using batch GVHMR and non-causal smoothing. This preserves dynamics but is more challenging.
- **Environments:** Simulated (Sim) and Real-world (Real) environments. JDS was not applicable in simulation.
- **Dance Routines:** Five distinct songs (three "easy," two "hard") as detailed in Appendix C, selected to avoid hand-floor interaction and excessive in-place turning.
- **Trials:** Three repeated trials per routine per setting, totaling 60 trials per controller (4 environments/input types × 5 songs × 3 repetitions). A gantry was used for robot safety during real-world experiments.

#### 3.5. Candidate Controllers

Three state-of-the-art general-purpose whole-body tracking humanoid controllers were benchmarked:
- **GMT:** A unified motion-tracking policy using adaptive sampling and a mixture-of-experts architecture. Different configurations were used for Smo (no future frames) and Dyn (future frames available).
- **TWIST:** A general robot motion controller trained using human motion capture for teleoperation.
- **Any2Track:** A tracking policy using a two-stage RL framework for online disturbance adaptation.

Human baselines were also reported, including world leaderboard JDS entries and averaged JDS and kinematic metrics from the user study. Human motion sequences were retargeted to the robot's joint space for kinematic metric comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the Switch-JustDance system and the performance of benchmarked humanoid controllers.

#### 4.1. System Validation Results

- **Validity:** A consistent inverse correlation was found between JDS and PA-MPJPE, with correlation coefficients (r) ranging from [-0.76, -0.42] and significance levels (p) below 0.05. Higher JDS correlated with lower tracking errors, validating JDS as an indicator of motion-tracking quality. The JDS was also suggested to capture additional performance aspects beyond pure kinematic accuracy, such as joint acceleration and rhythmic alignment.
- **Discriminability:** JDS demonstrated a wide dynamic range. Static Joy-Con baselines yielded scores near 0, study participants achieved scores between 3,460 and 12,952, and world leaderboard experts reached the maximum of 13,333. This range indicated sufficient discriminative capability for varying performance levels.
- **Repeatability:** The JDS showed an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.735 and a Coefficient of Variation (CV) of 10.0%, indicating low trial-to-trial variation. Kendall’s Coefficient of Concordance (KCC) of 0.678 further suggested consistent rank ordering among participants across different dances and repetitions. These results supported the repeatability and robustness of the benchmark.

#### 4.2. Controller Benchmark Results

- **Human-Robot Performance Gap:** Current humanoid controllers exhibited a substantial performance gap compared to humans. The best-performing robot controller achieved approximately one-third of the average human baseline JDS (2,900 vs. 8,538). While some controllers achieved lower MPJPE than human players (e.g., GMT-Real-Dyn at 88.8 mm vs. human at 111.9 mm), this was often at the cost of stability, resulting in lower success rates. Human players consistently produced smoother movements, characterized by lower jerk and acceleration compared to all robot controllers.
- **Controller Specifics:**
 - **Any2Track:** Achieved the highest overall JDS in the Real-Smo setting (2,900) and demonstrated comparatively smoother motions. It was the only controller capable of completing an entire dance without significant gantry support.
 - **GMT:** Showed relatively high JDS and success rates in Real-Dyn settings, indicating robustness to rapid movement changes.
 - **TWIST:** Demonstrated strong performance in MPJPE, success rate, and smoothness under the Real-Smo setting, though its JDS did not match GMT's, suggesting JDS involves factors beyond pure pose tracking or smoothness.
- **Effect of Song Difficulty:** All controllers performed better on "Easy" routines (higher JDS, more stable tracking), with a significant average JDS decrease of 573 points on "Hard" routines. This indicates that dynamic changes and complex coordination patterns present substantial challenges for current control policies.
- **Effect of Input Motion (Real-Smo vs. Real-Dyn):**
 - **Real-Dyn inputs:** Led to significantly higher joint jerk and acceleration across controllers, as robots had to reproduce rapid transitions. This also increased instability; for example, TWIST's success rate dropped by 46.7%, MPJPE increased by 23.9 mm, and JDS decreased by 16.7% in Real-Dyn compared to Real-Smo.
 - **Real-Smo inputs:** Resulted in gentler and more predictable motions, leading to smoother trajectories (average jerk reduction of 1,268.7).
 - **Controller Robustness:** GMT maintained a success rate above 70% in both settings, showing robustness to rapid movements, and surprisingly achieved higher JDS in Real-Dyn due to reduced mismatch with original choreography. Any2Track's stability was also less affected by dynamic inputs.
- **Sim-to-Real Performance:**
 - **Smoothness Differences:** Simulated motions exhibited lower jerk and acceleration compared to hardware executions, which showed larger spikes. This difference was attributed to real-world factors like joint backlash, torque limits, friction, and timing delays.
 - **MPJPE vs. Smoothness:** MPJPE differences between simulation and hardware were smaller than those in smoothness metrics, suggesting that low MPJPE in simulation does not guarantee stable, hardware-friendly motions.
 - **Success Rate Discrepancy:** Counterintuitively, success rates were sometimes lower in simulation than on hardware. This was primarily attributed to the safety gantry providing slight support to the physical robot, preventing falls that would occur in simulation, particularly for controllers like GMT that are more sensitive to disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Switch-JustDance framework introduces a significant contribution to the field of whole-body robot control and embodied AI by addressing several limitations of existing evaluation paradigms.

**Primary Significance:**

- **Low-Cost and Accessible Benchmarking:** By leveraging a commercial gaming console (Nintendo Switch, ~$400), the system offers a significantly more affordable entry point for real-world hardware benchmarking compared to traditional motion-capture setups (often >$50,000). This accessibility can foster wider adoption and community participation in robot control research.
- **Reproducible and Standardized Evaluation:** The global availability and consistent standards of the Nintendo Switch platform ensure high reproducibility of experiments across different research groups. The game's built-in, objective scoring system provides a standardized metric for performance comparison.
- **Direct Human-Robot Comparability:** Unlike most existing benchmarks, Switch-JustDance is designed for human players, naturally enabling direct and quantitative comparisons between robot and human athletic performance under identical evaluation criteria. This provides a clear measure of how close robots are to human-level skill.
- **Physically Grounded and Dynamic Evaluation:** The system provides real-time assessment of whole-body tracking, balance, and agility in dynamic, long-horizon (over 3 minutes) dance motions. It captures real-world hardware factors, which are often overlooked in simulation-only evaluations.
- **Support for Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Motions:** The continually updated content library of Just Dance offers a diverse and expanding set of OOD motions that controllers have not been specifically trained on, promoting research into more generalized and robust control policies.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Advancement of Dynamic Humanoid Control:** The benchmark clearly highlights the performance gap between current robot controllers and humans, particularly in dynamic, high-frequency motions. This will motivate researchers to develop more robust, agile, and smoother whole-body control policies capable of handling complex choreographies and real-world disturbances.
- **Insights into Controller Limitations:** The detailed metrics (JDS, MPJPE, SR, Smoothness) and comparative analysis across different input types and environments provide critical insights into the strengths and limitations of existing controllers. For instance, the findings regarding the trade-off between tracking accuracy and stability, and the discrepancies between simulation and hardware performance, can guide future controller design and evaluation strategies.
- **Guidance for Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines promising avenues for future work, including:
 - **Improving Control Architectures:** Encouraging the development of learning-based or hybrid control systems that can handle smooth transitions, noise, partial observations, and long, dynamic sequences more effectively.
 - **Enhancing Motion Reconstruction and Retargeting:** Pointing towards research into faster, low-latency motion tracking, and dynamically consistent retargeting methods that are robust across various robot morphologies.
 - **Expanding Console-Based Benchmarks:** Suggesting the broader use of commercial game platforms as rich, physically interactive environments not only for evaluation but also for training embodied AI agents, potentially opening new research frontiers beyond motion imitation.

Overall, Switch-JustDance offers a practical, comprehensive, and accessible framework that is expected to facilitate progress in athletic whole-body robot control and embodied AI by providing a standardized means for evaluating and comparing robot performance against human benchmarks in physically meaningful ways.
