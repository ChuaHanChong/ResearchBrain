# Evolution Fine-Tuning: Learning to Discover Across 371 Optimization Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Minnesota, Carnegie Mellon University, and collaborators developed Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT), a method that trains large language models to internalize discovery capabilities for optimization tasks. EFT converts evolutionary search trajectories into supervised learning signals, enabling smaller open-source models to demonstrate improved cross-task generalization and surpass base models or human performance on various complex problems.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) in evolutionary search primarily function as mutation operators, with the strategic 'discovery capability' residing externally in the search scaffold.
- Existing LLM-driven optimization methods struggle with cross-task generalization, often discarding learned search experience and requiring models to approach new problems from scratch.
- Optimization tasks frequently lack ground truth optimal solutions, making it challenging to apply standard supervised learning paradigms for teaching discovery skills to LLMs.

## Method

- Introduces **Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT)**, a ==mid-training paradigm== that transforms observable ==evolutionary search trajectories== into a structured supervised learning signal for LLMs.
- Constructed the `Finch Collection`, a large-scale dataset comprising approximately 156,`Finch Collection``Finch Collection``Finch Collection` evolutionary search trajectories collected from **371** diverse optimization tasks across 1`Finch Collection` domains.
- Fine-tuned open-source LLMs (Qwen3.5 2B-9B series) on the `Finch Collection` using ==Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)== and further enhanced them with ==preference learning (KTO)== to teach self-judgment.

## Results

- `Finch` models demonstrated a consistent average performance improvement of 1`Finch`.22% over their base LLM counterparts across **22 held-out** optimization tasks.
- Smaller `Finch` models achieved performance comparable to or exceeding base models **twice their size**, democratizing access to LLM-driven discovery.
- `Finch-8B + KTO` surpassed the **best human scores** on the AC1 and AC2 mathematical discovery tasks, showcasing advanced intrinsic discovery skills.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evolutionary search trajectories, even without definitive ground truth optima, can be effectively converted into a supervised learning signal to instill discovery capabilities into LLMs.
> - Internalizing discovery capability through EFT enables LLMs to generalize optimization strategies and leverage acquired domain knowledge across a diverse range of unseen tasks.
> - Preference learning, by training on both improving and regressing trajectories, significantly enhances an LLM's intrinsic ability to differentiate between beneficial and detrimental mutations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses advancements in applying Large Language Models (LLMs) to complex optimization tasks, which are prevalent in mathematics, algorithm engineering, and natural sciences. These tasks are characterized by the ability to score candidate solutions against an objective, but the optimal solution is not directly computable, making them computationally challenging (e.g., NP-hard).

Prior research has integrated LLMs into evolutionary search processes, where the LLM proposes new candidate solutions that are then evaluated and integrated into a population by an external "scaffold." This field broadly divides into two methodological branches:
1. **Test-time search methods:** These approaches utilize a fixed LLM as a mutation operator, relying on the scaffold's logic for parent selection and prompting to drive improvements. These methods often require large, proprietary LLMs due to the demand for consistent, high-quality proposals.
2. **Test-time learning methods:** These methods additionally update the LLM’s weights during the search process, allowing the model to adapt to the specific target task. While successful in finding novel solutions, the acquired strategies are typically not consolidated for reuse across different tasks.

A common limitation across both branches is that the "discovery capability"—the skill of iteratively improving solutions, identifying effective mutations, retaining strong candidates, and knowing when to backtrack—remains largely external to the LLM itself. The model does not inherently acquire or generalize this capability, meaning each new problem is approached from a relatively nascent state, and accumulated search experience is often discarded. This necessitates a new problem being solved from scratch or relying heavily on the external scaffold.

This paper positions itself by aiming to enable LLMs to *meta-learn* this discovery capability, thereby internalizing it into the model's parameters rather than leaving it solely within the external scaffold. This is challenging because optimization tasks frequently lack ground truth optimal solutions, preventing standard supervised learning paradigms. The work aims to bridge this gap by transforming evolutionary search trajectories into a supervised learning signal, allowing smaller, open-source LLMs to acquire and transfer discovery capabilities across diverse optimization problems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable Large Language Models (LLMs) to internalize the "discovery capability," which refers to the skill of iteratively improving solutions, understanding what parts of a solution to mutate, what to preserve, and when to adjust search strategies (e.g., backtrack). In existing LLM-driven evolutionary search paradigms, this capability primarily resides in the external search scaffold rather than in the LLM itself.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several limitations observed in current practices:
1. **Externalized Discovery Capability:** In both test-time search and test-time learning methods, the core capability to guide an evolutionary search is typically embedded within the external scaffold. For test-time search, the LLM acts as a mutation operator with fixed weights, and all strategic decisions are handled by the scaffold. For test-time learning, while LLM weights are updated, these updates are tailored to a single search loop and a single task, and the learned strategies are not consolidated for reusable cross-task application. This design prevents the LLM from independently acquiring and retaining generalizable discovery skills.
2. **Reliance on Proprietary Models:** Test-time search methods often necessitate large, proprietary LLMs to consistently generate high-quality proposals. Smaller open-source models (e.g., less than 9B parameters) have been observed to perform significantly weaker in such scaffolds, limiting the accessibility and scalability of LLM-driven discovery.
3. **Lack of Cross-Task Generalization:** When models are trained or adapted for one task, the experience gained is frequently discarded. Consequently, they cannot compose or transfer strategies from previously encountered tasks to new, unseen problems. This results in an inefficient "from scratch" approach for every new optimization challenge.
4. **Challenges of Supervised Learning for Optimization:** Optimization tasks are often NP-hard and lack definitive ground truth optimal solutions, making it difficult to apply standard supervised learning techniques that rely on (problem, answer) pairs. A novel approach is required to generate effective supervision signals.

To address these limitations, the paper introduces **Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT)**. The core motivation is to develop a mid-training paradigm that converts the observable trajectories of evolutionary search runs into a structured supervision signal. By fine-tuning LLMs on this signal, the researchers aim to:
- Internalize the discovery capability directly into the LLM's parameters, allowing the model to become a more intelligent and autonomous mutation operator.
- Enable cross-task generalization, where the LLM can leverage learned evolutionary patterns and domain knowledge across a diverse set of optimization problems.
- Reduce the reliance on large, proprietary models by empowering smaller, open-source LLMs with enhanced discovery abilities, thereby democratizing access to LLM-driven scientific and algorithmic discovery.
- Allow LLMs to serve as "general-purpose discovery agents" that do not begin solving every new problem without prior learned experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT)**, a mid-training paradigm designed to internalize discovery capabilities into LLMs. This is achieved by converting evolutionary search trajectories into supervised training data. The methodology involves the construction of a large-scale dataset, `Finch Collection`, followed by the fine-tuning of open-source LLMs.

#### 3.1. Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT) Concept

EFT operates as a "mid-training" step, situated between pre-training and test-time deployment. Its goal is to teach LLMs to act as more effective mutation operators *before* they are deployed within search scaffolds. The paradigm transforms the sequence of parent-to-child solution transitions observed in evolutionary search runs into supervised learning instances.

#### 3.2. Finch Collection Construction

The `Finch Collection` is a dataset of approximately 156,000 evolutionary search trajectories collected across 371 optimization tasks from 10 diverse domains. The construction process involves three main steps:

- **Step 1: Seed Optimization Task Collection:**
 - **Source and Criteria:** Tasks were sourced from existing, externally validated optimization benchmarks. The selection criteria mandated tasks that: (i) require non-trivial search, (ii) do not reduce to matching a known ground-truth answer, and (iii) provide a deterministic evaluator yielding continuous or comparable scores.
 - **Diversity:** A total of 371 tasks were collected from 10 benchmarks, spanning domains such as mathematical discovery, competitive programming, heuristic optimization, numerical algorithm optimization, symbolic regression (physics, biology, chemistry), GPU kernel optimization, constructive search, and biological denoising. Examples include the Erdős minimum-overlap problem and GPU kernel design tasks.

- **Step 2: Evolutionary Trajectory Collection:**
 - **Scaffold and Teacher Model:** An evolutionary scaffold, OpenEvolve, was used to generate trajectories. A large proprietary model, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, acted as the "teacher mutation operator" within this scaffold.
 - **Process:** For each seed task, the scaffold ran for a fixed budget of iterations. At each iteration, a prompt was constructed from the parent solution, task instruction, search history, and evaluator artifacts. The teacher LLM then generated a new candidate solution, which was evaluated.
 - **Mutation Strategies:** To encourage both local refinement and broader exploration, trajectories were collected using two mutation strategies: diff-based editing (emphasizing exploitation) and full rewriting (encouraging exploration). Stochastic decoding (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95) was employed for diversity.
 - **Data Captured:** Each trajectory instance recorded the instruction, parent solution, search history, generated candidate, evaluator output, score change, and execution artifacts (logs, error traces). A total of 172,997 raw trajectories were initially collected.

- **Step 3: Trajectory Filtering:**
 - Raw trajectories were filtered to remove unreliable or misleading data, retaining 156,731 (90.6%) instances. The filtering criteria included:
 - **Systematic Errors:** Trajectories where evaluator output was unreliable or score delta undefined (e.g., missing parent scores, timeouts, syntax errors, evaluator crashes). 6,321 trajectories (3.7%) were removed.
 - **Unrecoverable & Breakage Cases:** Trajectories where both parent and child solutions were erroneous (unrecoverable) or an error-free parent led to an erroneous child (breakage). These were considered hard negatives that could destabilize training. 1,575 trajectories (0.9%) were removed.
 - **Length Constraints:** Trajectories with excessively long inputs or outputs (exceeding 16,384 tokens for responses or 32,768 tokens for total input-output length) to maintain training stability. 8,370 trajectories (5.0%) were removed.
 - **Trajectory Classification:** Retained trajectories were classified based on score change: Improvement (Imp, Δ > 0), No Change (NC, Δ = 0), and Regression (Reg, Δ < 0). The dataset contained 39.4% Imp, 19.2% NC, and 41.3% Reg trajectories. For initial EFT, primarily Imp trajectories were used to avoid imitating non-improving behaviors. Preference learning (KTO) was later used to leverage both Imp and Reg trajectories to teach the model to self-judge solutions.

#### 3.3. Analysis of Finch Collection

The dataset exhibits characteristics indicating its suitability for training diverse discovery capabilities:
- **Task Distribution:** Competitive Programming accounts for the largest share of tasks (172 tasks), followed by Numerical Algorithm Optimization and Symbolic Regression.
- **Language and Mutation:** 68.5% of trajectories are in Python, and 31.5% in C++. Mutation strategies are balanced between diff-based edit (50.3%) and full rewrite (49.7%).
- **Trajectory Length:** Input lengths average 8,902 tokens, while output lengths average 6,865 tokens, suggesting models learn to synthesize solutions from extensive context.
- **Package Usage:** Frequent use of `numpy` and `bits/stdc++` indicates coverage of numerical computing and competitive programming.

#### 3.4. Finch: Evolution Fine-Tuned Language Model

- **Base Models:** Open-source LLMs from the Qwen3.5 series (2B, 4B, 9B) and Qwen3-8B were used.
- **Training Strategy:** Models were fine-tuned using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on only `Imp` trajectories from 355 training tasks (excluding 16 held-out tasks). To mitigate class imbalance, only one trajectory per task was used during SFT, resulting in 30,445 training trajectories.
- **Preference Learning:** Further training was conducted using preference learning (KTO) on a combination of `Imp` and `Reg` trajectories to teach the model to distinguish beneficial from detrimental mutations.
- **Technical Details:** Training was performed for one epoch with a global batch size of 128 and a learning rate of 1e-5 using the LLaMA-Factory framework on NVIDIA H200 GPUs.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Evaluation Tasks:** 22 held-out optimization tasks, distinct from the training set, were selected across five domains: Mathematical Discovery (5 tasks), Algorithm Engineering (2 tasks), System Performance (4 tasks), Competitive Programming (6 tasks), and Algorithmic Heuristics (5 tasks). Each task used a specific, continuous, task-specific metric (e.g., c5_bound for Erdős problem).
- **Baselines:**
 - Base LLMs integrated into the OpenEvolve search scaffold.
 - Base LLMs combined with a test-time learning scaffold, specifically `nanodiscover` (an open-source reproduction of TTT-Discover).
- **Inference:** For search-based scaffolds, `T`=100 iterations, parallel evaluation, temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95, and max 30K tokens were used. Learning-based scaffolds followed TTT-Discover configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the effectiveness of Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT) by comparing `Finch` models (EFT-trained) against their base LLM counterparts across 22 held-out optimization tasks.

- **Improved Cross-Task Discovery Generalization:**
 - `Finch` models consistently surpassed their base counterparts across the 22 held-out tasks. The average performance improvement for `Finch` over base models of the same size was 10.22%.
 - Notable improvements were observed on tasks like `ahc058` (+290.59%) and `Transaction` (+74.30%).
 - EFT was effective across various model scales, with larger models (e.g., `Finch-9B`) generally benefiting more (average gain +10.24%) than smaller ones (e.g., `Finch-2B` with +1.56%).
 - EFT enabled smaller `Finch` models to achieve performance comparable to or exceeding base models twice their size. For example, `Finch-4B` achieved a score of 0.386460 on the Erdős task, which was competitive with `Qwen3-8B`'s score of 0.403585.

- **Effectiveness on Complex Optimization Tasks:**
 - `Finch` demonstrated efficacy on NP-hard competitive programming and algorithmic heuristics tasks. For instance, `Finch-9B` achieved an average score of 46.01 on competitive programming tasks, significantly higher than `Qwen3.5-9B`'s 32.46.
 - In a case study on the `Convolve2D` task, `Finch-9B` exhibited cross-domain knowledge transfer, modifying the implementation from `scipy` to `jax` for improved computational efficiency, suggesting it internalized optimization patterns from `jax`-heavy trajectories in its training data.

- **Enhanced Discovery Capability with Preference Learning:**
 - Further training `Finch` with preference learning (KTO) using both `Imp` (improvement) and `Reg` (regression) trajectories consistently improved its discovery capability.
 - `Finch-8B + KTO` surpassed the best human scores on both the AC1 and AC2 mathematical discovery tasks, achieving 1.5089 (lower is better) and 0.9146 (higher is better) respectively, compared to human scores of 1.5097 and 0.9015. This indicates that teaching the model to differentiate between good and bad solutions directly enhances its intrinsic discovery skills.

- **EFT as Mid-Training for Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - When `Finch` models were combined with a test-time RL scaffold (`nanodiscover`), they achieved state-of-the-art performance on two circle-packing tasks and improved performance on the Erdős Minimum Overlap Problem (+3.2%) compared to base models.
 - However, `Finch-8B` with `nanodiscover` still showed lower performance compared to the original TTT-Discover using a larger `GPT-OSS-120B` model, suggesting that while EFT strengthens test-time RL for smaller models, reaching the absolute frontier may still require significantly larger models or further advancements.

- **Scalability of Training Data:**
 - Performance on tasks like AC2, CP (n=26), and PRISM showed a positive scaling trend as the number of training tasks in `Finch Collection` increased from 15 to 355. This suggests that the dataset provides a scalable training signal, and further improvements could be realized with larger collections and models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Evolution Fine-Tuning (EFT) and the `Finch Collection` dataset as a method to advance LLM-driven discovery. The work addresses a limitation in current approaches where LLMs primarily act as mutation operators without internalizing the strategic aspects of evolutionary search.

The **significance** of this work lies in several areas:

- **Internalization of Discovery Capability:** The introduction of EFT demonstrates a method for training LLMs to internalize the complex skill of evolving solutions across various optimization tasks. This shifts the "discovery capability" from being solely external (in the search scaffold) to being an inherent part of the LLM's parameters.
- **Cross-Task Generalization:** The empirical results across 22 held-out tasks indicate that EFT confers cross-task generalization. `Finch` models can apply learned optimization patterns and domain knowledge across diverse problems, rather than solving each new problem from scratch. This implies a step towards more general-purpose discovery agents.
- **Democratization of LLM-Driven Discovery:** By enabling smaller, open-source LLMs (2B-9B parameters) to achieve substantial performance gains and even match or exceed larger base models, EFT contributes to democratizing access to powerful LLM-driven discovery. This can reduce computational costs and enable more local and accessible discovery pipelines.
- **Synergy with Test-Time Reinforcement Learning:** The finding that EFT acts as an effective "mid-training" for test-time RL methods suggests a promising path for hybrid approaches, where a generally capable `Finch` model can be further specialized and optimized during deployment.
- **Resource Contribution:** The `Finch Collection`, a large-scale dataset of evolutionary trajectories across diverse domains, is a valuable public resource for future research in LLM-driven optimization and meta-learning.

The **potential impact** of this research is broad:

- **Accelerated Scientific Discovery:** By making LLMs more adept at generating and refining solutions for optimization problems, the approach could accelerate discovery in fields like mathematics (e.g., conjectures), algorithm engineering (e.g., efficient GPU kernels), and the natural sciences (e.g., scientific law discovery, biological denoising).
- **Improved Algorithmic Development:** The ability of `Finch` to transfer domain knowledge and adapt optimization patterns could lead to more efficient and novel algorithms for competitive programming, heuristic optimization, and numerical analysis.
- **Foundation for Future AI Agents:** The internalization of discovery capability lays groundwork for developing more autonomous and intelligent AI agents that can not only generate solutions but also strategically evolve them over extended horizons.
- **Advancements in LLM Training:** The methodology of converting complex, iterative processes like evolutionary search into a supervised learning signal offers a novel paradigm for LLM training beyond traditional problem-solution pairs, potentially inspiring similar approaches in other challenging domains.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations, including the current reliance on a single test-time search scaffold (`OpenEvolve`) for data collection and evaluation. Future work includes collecting trajectories from mixed scaffolds to improve cross-scaffold generalization. Expanding test-time RL experiments to a broader range of practical tasks (beyond mathematical problems) is also a stated goal. Furthermore, extending the framework to multimodal reasoning and multi-turn interactions (rather than single-turn solution generation) is identified as an important future direction, recognizing that scientific discovery often involves interpreting visual observations and continuous iterative dialogue.

Ethical considerations are also addressed, noting potential misuse for harmful optimization objectives or over-reliance on automated discoveries. The authors suggest mitigation through training on public benchmarks, preserving base model safety, and recommending human oversight and verification in downstream deployments.
