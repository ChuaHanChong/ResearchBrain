# EgoHumanoid-V2: Human-to-Humanoid Transfer of Coordinated Whole-Body Skills for Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> EgoHumanoid-V2 transfers coordinated whole-body loco-manipulation skills from egocentric human demonstrations to a Unitree G1 humanoid. Offline coarse-to-fine action alignment (IK reference correction plus learned-response token refinement under a fixed SONIC tracker) and robot-arm rendering with image augmentation produce paired data for pi0.5 post-training. Without target-task robot demos, policies reach a 51.67% mean score on four real tasks, near teleoperation-trained policies at 76% lower collection labor.

## Problem

- Prior egocentric human-to-robot transfer via UMI interfaces, VR capture and relative end-effector actions mainly targets bimanual manipulation and navigation, not coordinated arm-torso-leg loco-manipulation skills.
- Decoupled humanoid controllers pair arm IK with separate balance and locomotion control, so earlier egocentric humanoid transfer could not convey coordinated whole-body skills directly.
- Whole-body trackers fed raw human references leave large end-effector errors, and online IK corrections override part of the coordinated output, destabilizing balance and causing falls.

## Method

- Kinematic reference correction (coarse stage): ==constrained offline IK== pulls the initial SONIC rollout toward human palm and sole targets with temporal continuity, then encodes it into 64-D ==SONIC motion tokens==.
- Dynamics-aware refinement (fine stage): an ==MLP response model== predicts executed end-effector change from decoder joint-command changes, and tokens are iteratively optimized against fresh rollout residuals, with gradients bypassing the simulator.
- Visual alignment: ==SAM2== segments human forearms, ==DiffuEraser== inpaints them, rendered G1 arms are composited in, plus crop, rotation and photometric augmentation; the paired data post-train one pi0.5 VLA per task.

## Results

- With 200 human demos per task, zero-shot real-robot task scores span **38.33%** to **61.67%**, a four-task mean of **51.67%**, up from **38.33%** with 25 demos.
- Alignment cuts mean end-effector error from **11.21 cm** / **21.22 degrees** to **3.41 cm** / **6.71 degrees**, lifting policy score from **21.25%** to **51.67%**; all online-IK variants produced unstable rollouts with falls.
- Human data reach **53.33%** versus 56.67-**63.33%** from 50 teleoperation demos at **76.36%** less labor per episode; the learned MLP response cuts Close Drawer foot error from 9.18 to **5.80 cm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Correct supervision offline under a fixed whole-body tracker rather than with online IK overrides: changing only part of a coordinated controller's output silently breaks balance.
> - Kinematic and dynamic corrections are complementary: one IK pass fixes reference geometry, repeated execution-feedback rounds fix tracking error, while repeating IK alone plateaus.
> - Cheap egocentric human demos can replace teleoperation as direct action supervision but need roughly 2-4x more episodes, so compare collection labor rather than episode counts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

EgoHumanoid-V2 asks whether a robot can learn coordinated household loco-manipulation from a person’s egocentric demonstrations, without collecting robot demonstrations of those tasks. The difficulty is that human movement is plentiful but does not directly match a humanoid’s body, controller, or camera view. The authors adjust the demonstrated motion *offline*. They first make a geometrically corrected robot reference, then refine it against the controller’s observed execution. The authors argue that this enables direct supervision of coordinated skills. The demonstrations and tests support that interpretation on the evaluated tasks. They do not establish general transfer to unseen tasks.

### 2. How a human motion becomes robot supervision

The authors’ earlier [[2602.10106|EgoHumanoid (Shi et al., 2026)]] studied egocentric transfer for loco-manipulation under decoupled control. V2 instead targets coordinated whole-body skills. Its controller jointly generates commands using full-body feedback, and the authors refine the motion supervision offline under the fixed SONIC tracker.

Action alignment has three stages:

- **H** is an initial robot motion obtained by executing the human body motion through SONIC in simulation.
- **K** is constrained offline inverse kinematics (IK). It moves the robot reference toward fixed human hand or foot targets while limiting changes to the original motion and maintaining temporal continuity.
- **D** is the fine stage. An execution rollout measures the remaining endpoint error. Motion tokens are then adjusted using a learned local response model, and a fresh rollout measures the next residual.

The response model is an MLP trained on paired perturbed and unperturbed rollouts. It predicts how a kinematic change affects execution. Optimization gradients go through the fixed decoder and response model, not through the simulator. A validated candidate from the optimization history supplies the training labels. It need not be the last refinement round.

Visual alignment is a separate step that addresses appearance and viewpoint mismatch. It masks human forearms and wrist trackers, inpaints the background, and composites G1 arms rendered from aligned robot poses. Training images are also augmented with crops, small rotations, and photometric jitter. At deployment, the policy receives robot images.

### 3. Does refinement improve execution?

The key comparison isolates action labels under the same controller and visual preprocessing. From **H** to **H + K + Dₛ**, task-averaged simulated position error falls from 11.21 to 3.41 cm. Hand-task orientation error falls from 21.22° to 6.71°. With 200 human demonstrations per task, mean real-robot task score rises from 21.25% to 51.67%, and the full pipeline improves policy scores over H + K on every task. Position averages palm endpoints for three hand tasks and sole endpoints for foot-operated Close Drawer. Orientation averages only the three hand tasks. The selected Dₛ candidate comes from the optimization history and may be an earlier candidate, including H or H + K. The reported error comparison does not use validation replay or acceptance filtering.

Adding more K rounds is not the source of the gain. The refinement-depth comparison favors one K round followed by repeated D refinement over repeated K alone.

Position error (cm, all four tasks) and orientation error (degrees, three hand tasks) against iteration depth for Dᵏ, Kᵐ, KDᵏ, and KᵐD⁸. K¹ to K⁸ changes position error from 4.86 to 4.77 cm. K¹D⁸ gives 3.97 cm and 8.08°, and D⁸ alone gives 8.40 cm and 16.40°. These are raw outputs at each depth, without historical candidate selection.

### 4. What the real-robot test establishes

The four tasks are Close Laptop (hand press), Open Fridge (hook and pull), Pull Curtain (grasp and pull), and Close Drawer (toe push). Each task has its own vision-language-action policy, post-trained on aligned human data but without target-task robot demonstrations. This is the narrow sense of *zero-shot* used here.

With 200 human demonstrations per task, the mean task score is 51.67%. Each of 20 real-robot trials has three ordered subtasks scored pass/fail. Evaluation stops at the first failure, and later subtasks score zero. The score therefore measures partial completion. It is not the share of trials completing the entire task.

In the two-task collection comparison, human collection took 76.36% less labor per episode than teleoperation: 33.30 versus 140.85 person-seconds. Human data used more episodes in the score comparisons. Their scores approached, but did not always match, those from teleoperation data.

The results support human motion as direct supervision for coordinated humanoid skills. In this method, the robot reference is corrected and then refined against the controller’s execution. The evidence covers four task-specific policies, not unseen-task generalization by a single policy.
