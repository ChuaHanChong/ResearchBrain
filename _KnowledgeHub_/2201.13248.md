# SafeAPT: Safe Simulation-to-Real Robot Learning using Diverse Policies Learned in Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The SafeAPT framework enables robots to safely and data-efficiently adapt to new tasks in the real world. It generates a diverse policy repertoire in simulation under varying dynamics and employs online learned reward and safety transformation models to minimize safety violations during real-world interaction.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) on physical robots is data-inefficient, requiring many costly and time-consuming real-world interactions.
- The 'reality gap' causes policies learned in simulation to behave unsafely on physical robots, potentially leading to damage during adaptation.
- Existing sim-to-real methods often lack simultaneous consideration for multi-goal learning, safety, and robustness to diverse unknown dynamics.

## Method

- A diverse policy repertoire is generated in simulation using ==MAP-Elites==, ensuring each policy is maximally safe across a range of potential real-world dynamics conditions.
- ==Gaussian Process (GP) models== are used for online learning of reward and safety transformation functions from simulation to the real world, utilizing simulated data as strong priors.
- Policy selection employs an ==Expected Safe Improvement (ESI)== acquisition function, a ==constrained Bayesian optimization== method that prioritizes high-reward policies while maintaining a probabilistic safety limit based on learned GP models.

## Results

- **SafeAPT** demonstrated significantly fewer safety violations across all simulated tasks (e.g., **0.0 ± 0.0** in Asteroid landing) and **zero** safety violations in a real Kuka-arm hockey task (**0.0 ± 0.0** over 8 replicates) compared to baselines.
- The framework consistently found high-rewarding policies within minutes of interaction, often achieving superior rewards to baselines while maintaining strict safety, highlighting its data-efficiency.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the diversity of simulated dynamics used for repertoire generation and the online learning of a real-world safety model are essential for robust safety and high reward achievement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generating a policy repertoire under a distribution of diverse simulated dynamics significantly enhances safety and robustness when adapting to unknown real-world dynamics.
> - Online learning of real-world specific reward and safety transformation models via Gaussian Processes, initialized with simulated priors, is crucial for data-efficient and safe adaptation.
> - A safety-aware Bayesian Optimization acquisition function (ESI), incorporating a Lower Confidence Bound on predicted safety, effectively guides policy selection to minimize safety violations during real-world learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The application of Reinforcement Learning (RL) to physical robots faces significant challenges, primarily due to the data-inefficiency of RL algorithms. While RL has shown impressive results in simulated environments, transferring these successes to the real world is often hindered by the high number of interactions required, which can be time-consuming and costly, particularly for physical robots.

Simulation-to-real (sim-to-real) policy adaptation has emerged as a strategy to address this data-efficiency problem. In this paradigm, policies are initially learned in a simulator and then adapted in the real world to bridge the "reality gap"—the discrepancies between the simulated and physical environments. To further enhance data-efficiency, repertoire-based learning approaches have been developed. These methods involve evolving a large collection (repertoire) of diverse, high-rewarding policies in simulation, each associated with a unique outcome in a lower-dimensional outcome-space. Bayesian optimization (BO) is then typically used to select an optimal policy from this repertoire in the real world. The underlying hypothesis is that the diversity within the repertoire ensures some policies remain effective even when a significant reality gap exists, or when the robot's physical capabilities are altered (e.g., a damaged leg).

Despite its advantages, the sim-to-real adaptation approach, especially repertoire-based methods, possesses a critical limitation: safety. A policy that performs well and is considered safe in simulation may behave unsafely on a physical robot due to the reality gap. Such unsafe behavior during real-world adaptation can lead to damage to the robot or its surroundings. Prior work has addressed safety in RL through various means, including probabilistic dynamical models, shielding-based methods, and constrained Bayesian optimization. Some repertoire-based extensions, like sIT&E, incorporate safety constraints as additional dimensions in the policy repertoire. However, these methods often face limitations such as computational expense when the task goal changes, increased dimensionality with more constraints, or a lack of simultaneous consideration for multi-goal learning and safety under diverse dynamics.

This work, SafeAPT, positions itself within this landscape by seeking to provide a data-efficient, multi-goal, and safety-aware sim-to-real learning algorithm. It aims to overcome the identified limitations by explicitly considering diverse dynamics conditions during repertoire generation and by learning probabilistic reward and safety models online, using simulated data as priors, to enable safe Bayesian optimization in the real world. This approach attempts to integrate safety, data-efficiency, and multi-goal learning capabilities, addressing a notable gap in existing sim-to-real robot learning methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind SafeAPT is to enable robots to learn new skills safely and data-efficiently in the real world, leveraging insights gained from simulation. A central challenge in sim-to-real learning is the "reality gap," which can cause policies learned in simulation to exhibit unsafe behaviors when transferred to a physical robot. This can result in damage to the robot or its environment during the adaptation process. SafeAPT aims to mitigate this risk.

The key objectives of this research are:

- **Ensuring Safety during Real-world Interaction:** The paramount objective is to minimize safety violations when a robot executes policies in the real world during the learning and adaptation phases. This is achieved by actively monitoring and predicting the safety of policies before execution.
- **Achieving Data-Efficiency:** The algorithm aims to enable robots to adapt to real-world conditions and find high-performance policies with a minimal number of real-world interactions. This is critical for practical robotic applications where real-world trials are costly and time-consuming.
- **Supporting Multi-Goal Learning:** SafeAPT seeks to allow a single, pre-computed repertoire of policies to be adapted for a wide range of real-world goals without requiring a new, computationally expensive simulation-based optimization for each new goal or safety limit. This provides flexibility and scalability.
- **Robustness to Unknown Dynamics:** The approach is motivated by the need to handle unknown or unmodeled variations in real-world dynamics (e.g., friction, gravity, component damage). The method addresses this by generating a diverse policy repertoire that is maximally safe across a distribution of simulated dynamics conditions, anticipating the variations a robot might encounter.

In essence, SafeAPT is motivated by the need for a robot learning framework that is practical, robust, and safe, allowing for rapid deployment and adaptation of learned skills to dynamic, unpredictable real-world environments while protecting the robot and its surroundings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SafeAPT framework consists of three sequential and iterative steps: generation of a diverse policy repertoire in simulation, online learning of reward and safety transformation models in the real world, and safe policy selection through constrained Bayesian optimization.

**A. Generating the Policy Repertoire in Simulation**
The initial phase involves creating a repertoire, or archive, of policies in a simulated environment. The objective is to generate a diverse set of policies that not only cover the task's goal-space broadly but also exhibit maximal safety across a range of potential real-world dynamics conditions.
1. **Dynamics Condition Sampling:** A set of *N* different dynamics conditions ($\psi_{i=0...,N-1}$) are uniformly sampled from a predefined set of feasible real-world dynamics ($\Psi$). These conditions represent variations such as object mass, joint friction, or link lengths.
2. **Quality-Diversity Algorithm:** The MAP-Elites algorithm is employed to construct the policy repertoire. MAP-Elites discretizes the task's goal-space (*G*) into *K* cells.
3. **Policy Evolution:**
 - An initial set of *m* policies ($\theta_{i=1:m}$) are randomly generated and evaluated across all *N* sampled dynamics conditions in the simulator.
 - For each policy, a tuple $\langle\theta, \tau, x, c\rangle$ is created. $\theta$ is the policy parameter, $\tau$ is the resulting trajectory, *x* is the mean goal-space descriptor achieved across dynamics conditions, and *c* is the *minimum* safety score observed across all dynamics conditions for that policy. This minimum safety score promotes robustness.
 - These tuples are then inserted into the repertoire cells based on their goal-space outcome. If multiple policies map to the same cell, the one with the highest minimum safety score is retained.
 - MAP-Elites then iteratively mutates existing policies from the repertoire, evaluates the mutated policies across all dynamics conditions, and attempts to insert new tuples into the repertoire, replacing existing ones if the new policy yields a higher minimum safety score for that cell.
This process continues until a specified evaluation budget is reached, resulting in a repertoire where each policy is associated with a unique goal-space outcome and is as safe as possible across diverse simulated dynamics.

**B. Learning of the Reward and the Safety Model**
Once the repertoire is generated, SafeAPT learns how the reward and safety associated with these policies transform from simulation to the real world.
1. **Reward Assignment:** Given a specific real-world goal *g*, each policy in the repertoire is assigned a real-world reward $r_i = R(\tau_i, g)$, where $R(\cdot, g)$ is the trajectory reward function. This updates the repertoire tuples to $\langle\theta_i, \tau_i, x_i, c_i, r_i\rangle$.
2. **Gaussian Process (GP) Models:** Two GP regression models are initialized: $T_c: G \to \mathbb{R}$ for safety transformation and $T_r: G \to \mathbb{R}$ for reward transformation.
 - **Priors from Simulation:** The simulated safety scores ($c_i$) and the computed rewards ($r_i$) for each policy's associated goal-space outcome ($x_i$) in the repertoire are used as *prior mean functions* ($M_c(x_i) = c_i$ and $M_r(x_i) = r_i$) for the respective GP models. This significantly reduces the amount of real-world data needed for learning.
 - **Online Learning:** As real-world interaction occurs, actual observed safety scores and rewards are used to iteratively update the GP models, calculating the posterior mean ($\mu_c(x), \mu_r(x)$) and variance ($\sigma^2_c(x), \sigma^2_r(x)$) for any goal-space outcome *x*. These models quantify the reality gap for both reward and safety.

**C. Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer using Bayesian Optimization**
With the repertoire and the learned GP models, SafeAPT iteratively selects and executes policies on the physical robot.
1. **Expected Safe Improvement (ESI) Acquisition Function:** A modified Bayesian optimization acquisition function, ESI, is introduced to select policies. ESI combines the standard Expected Improvement (EI) with a safety constraint:
 $ESI(x) = EI(x) \times \mathbf{1}_\lambda(x)$
 where $\mathbf{1}_\lambda(x)$ is an indicator function.
2. **Safety Filtering:** The indicator function $\mathbf{1}_\lambda(x)$ is 0 if the Lower Confidence Bound (LCB) on the predicted safety for outcome *x* is below a specified safety limit $\lambda$; otherwise, it is 1. The LCB is defined as $\mu_c(x) - \kappa\sigma_c(x)$, where $\kappa$ is a positive hyperparameter that controls the trade-off between exploration/exploitation and safety. A higher $\kappa$ leads to more cautious policy selection.
3. **Policy Selection and Iteration:** In each episode, the policy $\theta^+$ (associated with goal-space outcome $x^+$) that maximizes ESI(x) across the entire repertoire is selected. This policy is executed on the real robot, and the observed real-world reward and safety score are used to update the GP models. This iterative process continues for a fixed number of trials.

**D. Probability of Safety Violation**
The paper provides a mathematical derivation showing that the choice of the $\kappa$ parameter in the LCB calculation directly influences an upper bound on the probability of violating the safety limit. Specifically, a higher $\kappa$ value results in a lower upper bound on the probability of safety violation, assuming the GP models accurately represent the safety distribution. This provides a mechanism to control the risk taken during policy evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SafeAPT was conducted on three simulated robotic tasks and one real-world task, with comparisons against three baseline methods. The performance metrics focused on data-efficiency, the rate of safety violations, and the final policy's reward.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Simulated Tasks:**
 - **Asteroid Landing:** A lander policy (8D PID controller + velocity set-points) must hover at a target altitude (100m) without falling below a safe altitude (40m), with varying unknown gravity. Goal-space: 1D altitude.
 - **Planar-arm Goal Reaching:** A 4-DoF arm (204-parameter neural network policy) must reach a target 2D location while avoiding 4 unsafe regions, maintaining a minimum distance of 1 unit. Link lengths vary. Goal-space: 2D coordinates.
 - **Kuka-arm Hockey:** A Kuka LWR 4+ robot arm (119D open-loop policy) hits a puck to a target position. The puck must stay at least 0.1m from the table edge. Friction varies. Goal-space: 2D puck landing coordinates.
- **Real-world Task:** A physical Kuka LWR4+ arm hockey-puck setup, mirroring the simulated task, using a Kinect camera for puck tracking.
- **Baselines:**
 - **CBO (Constrained Bayesian Optimization):** Optimizes directly in the high-dimensional policy parameter space using learned reward and safety models. Expected to be less data-efficient.
 - **SafeAPT (no GP-safety):** An ablation of SafeAPT where the real-world safety transformation function is not learned; instead, simulated safety priors are assumed to be valid. This tests the importance of learning real-world safety.
 - **SafeAPT (single dynamics):** An ablation where the policy repertoire is generated using only one randomly sampled dynamics condition, instead of a diverse distribution. This evaluates the importance of diverse simulation conditions.

#### 4.2. Main Findings

- **Safety Performance:**
 - Across all simulated experiments (Asteroid, Planar-arm, Kuka Hockey), SafeAPT demonstrated a significantly lower number of safety violations compared to all baselines (Table I). For instance, in Asteroid landing, SafeAPT had 0.0 ± 0.0 violations, while SafeAPT (no GP-safety) had 2.20 ± 3.06 and CBO had 1.13 ± 2.36.
 - The real-world Kuka-arm hockey task further confirmed this, with SafeAPT achieving 0.0 ± 0.0 safety violations over 8 replicates (Table II), whereas SafeAPT (no GP-safety) had 4.4 ± 4.4 violations.
 - The "no GP-safety" baseline consistently showed frequent safety violations, emphasizing the critical role of learning a real-world safety transformation model using GP priors.
 - The "single dynamics" baseline also incurred more safety violations than full SafeAPT, demonstrating the benefit of evolving the repertoire under diverse simulated dynamics conditions for robust safety in the real world.

- **Reward Performance and Data-Efficiency:**
 - SafeAPT consistently found high-rewarding policies within a few minutes of interaction, often achieving comparable or superior rewards to the baselines while maintaining safety (Figure 5).
 - In the physical Kuka hockey task, SafeAPT achieved a maximum reward of 0.97 ± 0.04 before any safety violation, outperforming all baselines (Table II).
 - CBO, which optimizes directly in high-dimensional policy space, generally failed to compete with the repertoire-based methods in terms of reward maximization due to its lower data-efficiency in such spaces.

- **Role of GP Models and Diverse Priors:**
 - Visualizations of the GP updates (Figure 3, Asteroid landing) illustrate how SafeAPT cautiously improves reward by selecting policies with high predicted reward and acceptable safety LCBs. The GP models, informed by simulated priors, adapt rapidly with few real-world observations.
 - The results from the ablation studies underscore that both the diversity of simulated dynamics used for repertoire generation and the online learning of a real-world safety model (via GPs with simulated priors) are crucial for achieving both high reward and safety. The "single dynamics" baseline showed improved safety over "no GP-safety" due to the learned safety model but suffered in reward due to insufficient repertoire diversity.

In summary, the experiments confirm that SafeAPT effectively achieves high-performance policies while minimizing safety violations in a data-efficient manner, by combining a diversely generated policy repertoire, online learned reward and safety transformation models informed by simulated priors, and a safety-aware Bayesian optimization acquisition function.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SafeAPT framework presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning, particularly in addressing the critical challenges of safety, data-efficiency, and multi-goal adaptation in sim-to-real transfer.

- **Enhanced Safety in Robot Learning:** The primary impact of SafeAPT is its ability to minimize safety violations during real-world robot interactions. This is crucial for deploying learning algorithms on physical robots, where unsafe behaviors can lead to costly damage or harm. By proactively predicting and constraining against unsafe policies through the Expected Safe Improvement (ESI) acquisition function and Lower Confidence Bounds (LCB), SafeAPT allows for cautious exploration, a vital requirement in many robotic applications.
- **Improved Data-Efficiency for Real-world Deployment:** SafeAPT enables robots to adapt to real-world dynamics and achieve high-performance policies with a limited number of real-world trials. This is achieved by leveraging a diverse policy repertoire pre-trained in simulation and using simulated data as strong priors for online learning of reward and safety transformation models. This data efficiency makes complex skill acquisition more feasible and economical for physical robots.
- **Flexible Multi-Goal Adaptation:** The design of SafeAPT allows a robot to adapt to a wide range of task goals in the real world using the *same* pre-computed policy repertoire. This eliminates the need for computationally expensive re-optimization in simulation every time the task's objective or safety limit changes, significantly increasing the flexibility and scalability of the learning system.
- **Robustness to Reality Gap and Unknown Dynamics:** By generating the policy repertoire under a distribution of diverse dynamics conditions in simulation, SafeAPT intrinsically prepares the robot for various real-world scenarios, including unmodeled variations or even minor damage. The online learning of transformation models further refines this robustness to the specific, unknown dynamics encountered in the real world.
- **Applications in Safety-Critical Domains:** The capability for safe and data-efficient learning has particular significance for safety-critical applications where mistakes are costly or mission-critical, such as space robotics, deep-sea exploration, or hazardous material handling. In these contexts, SafeAPT could provide a method for robots to acquire new skills or adapt to unforeseen circumstances with a controlled risk profile.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as potential sub-optimality if the goal-space discretization in the repertoire is not dense enough (suggesting further parameter space refinement using methods like Safe-Opt), the overall impact of SafeAPT lies in providing a comprehensive and practical framework for advancing safe, data-efficient, and adaptable robot learning from simulation to reality.
