# Computational Design of a Low-Visibility UAV Using a Human-Aligned Perceptual Metric

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northwestern University computationally designed a single-propeller UAV, named Phantom Twist, engineered for low visual perceptibility by actively exploiting motion blur from high-speed spinning. Its two-stage optimization pipeline, which integrated a human-aligned perceptual metric (LPIPS) and strict physical constraints, produced stable flying prototypes that were significantly less visible than conventional UAVs.

## Problem

- Robots can negatively impact their effectiveness, data validity, and ability to operate in sensitive environments due to their perceptible presence.
- Prior methods for reducing robot perceptibility (e.g., camouflage, transparency, biomimicry) have not fully explored exploiting high-speed periodic motion to reduce visual presence through motion blur.
- Existing spinning platforms exhibited incidental blurring but were not explicitly designed or optimized for low visibility, resulting in inadequate spin speeds or high-contrast, opaque parts.

## Method

- A two-stage computational design pipeline was developed for a single-propeller UAV (**Phantom Twist**) to minimize visual perceptibility by leveraging ==motion blur== from high-speed rotation.
- The ==Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)== metric was integrated as the primary visibility objective due to its strong correlation with human perception of image differences.
- The optimization process incorporated ==ten comprehensive inertial, aerodynamic, and geometric constraints== to ensure physical realizability and stable, controllable flight for the designed UAVs.

## Results

- The optimization pipeline reduced the average visibility metric by **12%** for the top 500 feasible designs and decreased the minimum visibility metric from **0.0134** to **0.0104**.
- Fabricated prototypes of optimized designs achieved stable hover flight with approximately **3 cm** RMS position error, validating the physical realizability and the accuracy of the simulated motion-blurred appearance.
- The optimized Phantom Twist designs were significantly less visible in flight than human-designed baselines or conventional quadcopters in both simulated and real-world outdoor conditions, especially with an optimized launch interface.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-speed periodic motion can be actively exploited as a core design principle to manipulate human visual perception, creating a motion-blurred, translucent appearance for low-visibility robots.
> - An automated computational design framework can effectively optimize complex robotic systems for non-traditional, human-aligned perceptual objectives like low visibility, even with counter-intuitive component arrangements.
> - Effective low-visibility designs require careful component placement that avoids high-contrast features near the rotation axis and distributes mass and volume to create a diffuse rotational envelope.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The broad problem addressed by this research is the negative impact a robot's perceptible presence can have on its effectiveness, data validity, and ability to operate in sensitive environments. Robots intended for tasks like inspection, environmental monitoring, or human-robot interaction may alter the behavior of nearby subjects simply by being sensed. Minimizing this influence is crucial for unbiased observations, reliable interactions, and expanded applicability of robotic systems.

Prior research has explored several strategies to reduce robot perceptibility:
- **Visual Salience Reduction:**
 - **Static or Active Coloration:** Drawing inspiration from biological camouflage, some systems adapt their surface colors and patterns to blend into the environment. This includes active color emitters and counter-illumination techniques, such as the "Yehudi light" developed in the 1940s.
 - **Material Transparency:** More recent efforts have focused on making robot bodies optically permeable using clear actuators and sensors.
- **Biomimicry:** Some robots are designed to mimic benign natural objects, such as ornithopters or insect-scale flyers that visually resemble birds or insects, allowing them to "hide in plain sight."
- **Motion Camouflage:** This approach involves designing robots to move in ways that obscure their apparent motion to an observer, thereby reducing their chance of detection.

In contrast to these established methods, the potential of exploiting high-speed periodic motion to reduce robot visibility has remained largely unexplored. The human visual system's persistence of vision causes fast-moving objects to appear blurred, translucent, or spatially diffuse rather than sharply defined. This temporal integration effect suggests an alternative path: shaping perception through rapid motion and rotation.

Previous attempts to leverage this concept, such as the Phantom Sentinel surveillance drone, were limited by insufficient rotational speeds, rendering the vehicle largely visible. Other spinning platforms, including the Piccolissimo series drones, the Monospinner, and various plant seed-inspired flyers, inadvertently exhibit some blurring due to their flight dynamics. However, their designs were not explicitly optimized for low visibility, resulting in either inadequate spin speeds for significant persistence effects or high-contrast, opaque parts that prevent effective visual reduction.

This work addresses this identified gap by introducing a specific UAV design (Phantom Twist) that actively exploits motion blur, and critically, develops a computational design framework that integrates a human-aligned perceptual metric directly into the optimization loop. This allows for explicit optimization of component arrangement to minimize visual footprint during high-speed spinning, moving beyond incidental blurring to intentional low-visibility design.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to computationally design and physically validate a novel type of single-propeller Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV), named Phantom Twist, engineered to achieve low visual visibility through the active exploitation of motion blur generated by high-speed spinning.

The key motivations and more detailed objectives are:
- **Exploiting Motion Blur for Low Visibility:** The central motivation is to leverage the phenomenon of persistence of vision, where objects undergoing rapid periodic motion appear blurred and diffuse to the human eye. This contrasts with traditional approaches to stealth or camouflage, which typically focus on static appearance or motion masking. The objective is to harness this perceptual effect as a core design principle for minimizing visual perceptibility.
- **Developing an Automated Design Pipeline:** A significant objective is to create a robust, two-stage computational design pipeline. This pipeline is intended to automate the process of optimizing the spatial arrangement of functional UAV components (batteries, control PCB, motor-propeller assembly, and counterweights). The goal is to move beyond heuristic or human-intuitive designs, which have proven insufficient for effective visibility reduction in this context.
- **Integrating a Human-Aligned Perceptual Metric:** A critical objective is to quantify visibility using a metric that correlates well with human perception. The research specifically aims to integrate the Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) metric into the design loop. This ensures that the optimization process directly targets the visual features most salient to human observers, rather than relying on simpler, less perceptually relevant metrics.
- **Satisfying Strict Inertial and Aerodynamic Constraints:** Alongside minimizing visibility, a crucial objective is to ensure that the designed UAVs are physically realizable and capable of stable, controllable flight. This requires strictly satisfying a comprehensive set of inertial, aerodynamic, and geometric constraints within the optimization framework. The motivation here is to produce designs that are not only theoretically low-visibility but also practical and functional aerial vehicles.
- **Validating Computational Designs through Fabrication and Flight Testing:** A final objective is to physically fabricate multiple prototypes based on the computational designs and conduct flight tests. This validation step is essential to confirm that the automated design pipeline produces stable, controllable UAVs and, most importantly, that the optimized designs exhibit a tangible and significant reduction in visual perceptibility compared to conventional or unoptimized counterparts in real-world conditions. This empirical validation bridges the gap between simulation and practical application.

Overall, the motivation stems from the desire to create robotic systems that can operate more discreetly in environments where their presence might be disruptive or bias observations, by pioneering a novel design approach that actively manipulates human visual perception through motion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on a computational design pipeline for a single-propeller UAV, Phantom Twist, aiming to minimize visual perceptibility while adhering to flight stability constraints.

**UAV Architecture:**
Phantom Twist utilizes a minimalist single-propeller UAV architecture, similar to the Maneuverable Piccolissimo series. A single motor, offset from the center of mass, drives a propeller. The main body of the UAV spins rapidly in the opposite direction due to reaction torque, maintaining passive stability through gyroscopic effects. Active control is achieved by modulating motor thrust throughout each rotation.
The core functional components include a brushless DC motor with a propeller, two batteries, a main control PCB, and optionally, one or two counterweights. These components are connected by thin carbon fiber rods, which are considered to have negligible mass and visual impact and are thus excluded from the design model for computational efficiency.

**Design Constraints:** Ten specific constraints, categorized into mass and inertia, control and aerodynamics, and geometric, are established to ensure physical realizability and stable flight:
- **Mass and Inertia Constraints:**
 1. **Center of Mass (CoM):** Set at the origin (⃗rCoM = O).
 2. **Principal Axis Alignment:** The UAV must rotate about the Z-axis, which aligns with an axis of maximum inertia (Ixz = Iyz = 0).
 3. **Rotational Stability:** The ratio η = I2/I1 between the first and second principal moments must be between 0.5 and 0.75 (η ≤ η0 = 0.75) to ensure stability without becoming too flat or overly sensitive to perturbations.
 4. **Total Mass:** Limited to 40 g to allow for sufficient thrust and control authority from the selected GEPRC GR1102 motor.
 5. **Counterweight Feasibility:** All added counterweights must have non-negative mass.
- **Control and Aerodynamic Constraints:**
 6. **Control Authority:** The thrust arm of the motor (∥r motor,xy ∥) must be sufficiently large (≥ 15 mm) to provide adequate control.
 7. **Spin Rate Range:** The equilibrium spin rate must be maintained between 15 and 25 rotations per second (rps). Below 15 rps, the rigid-body assumption for stability fails, and motion blur is reduced. Above 25 rps, motor control becomes unstable. This constraint is simplified to a constraint on the drag torque coefficient (CM) within g·mm².
 8. **Wake Clearance:** No components may be placed in the immediate vicinity of the motor-propeller assembly or its downstream wake to prevent thrust loss.
- **Geometric Constraints:**
 9. **Compact Volume:** All components must be within a cylindrical boundary (radius ≤ 70 mm, height z ∈ [−55, 55] mm).
 10. **Component Non-intersection:** Physical components must not overlap.

**Spin Rate Calculation:** The equilibrium spin rate (ω) is determined by balancing the propeller drag torque (Mprop, treated as constant) and the aerodynamic drag torque (Mdrag) of the other components. Mdrag is modeled as the sum of contributions from the motor (approximated as a cylinder), counterweights (spheres), and PCB/batteries (cuboids). Drag torques for spheres/cylinders are calculated using standard aerodynamic drag equations (CD,sph = 0.47), while cuboid drag is based on CFD simulations providing a constant C1 = 1.3. Mdrag is found to be proportional to ω², leading to ω = √(Mprop / CM), where CM is the drag torque coefficient dependent on geometric configuration. This allows constraint 7 to be expressed purely geometrically.

**Optimization Objective: Visibility Metric:**
The visibility metric quantifies how perceptible the UAV is to a human observer, accounting for the effects of high-speed rotation.
- **Visual Appearance Model:** Given the miniature size and high spin rate (15-25 rps, comparable to human persistence of vision time), the UAV's appearance is modeled as a rotational average of its orthographic projection across all yaw angles. This synthesizes a motion-blurred, semi-transparent image. Gamma correction (γ=2.2) is applied to simulate realistic brightness perception.
- **Perceptual Metric:** The Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) metric, utilizing deep features from a pre-trained AlexNet CNN, is employed to measure the perceptual difference between images. LPIPS is selected for its superior correlation with human similarity judgments compared to simpler metrics.
- **Visibility Score Calculation:** The motion-blurred UAV image is superimposed onto 100 diverse background scenes from the MiniPlaces dataset. The LPIPS distance is computed between each original background and its superimposed version. A higher LPIPS score indicates higher visibility.
- **Overall Visibility Metric:** To account for unpredictable observer positions, the final visibility metric is the expected visibility score, calculated as a weighted average over 10 distinct pitch angles (weighted by solid angle) and an average over the 100 background images.

**Optimization Pipeline:**
The design pipeline is two-staged:
1. **Rapid Feasibility Filtering:**
 - Randomly samples poses for the motor, batteries, and PCB.
 - Applies a preliminary filter to discard designs violating constraints 6, 8, 9, 10.
 - For passing samples, a numerical solver (Mathematica's NMinimize) determines counterweight configurations (mass and position) that minimize total weight while satisfying inertial (1-5) and volume (9) constraints.
 - Finally, verifies complete designs against remaining constraints (6, 7, 8, 10) to ensure full feasibility.
2. **Gradient-Based Refinement:**
 - Starts with feasible solutions identified in Stage 1.
 - Utilizes the Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) optimizer (SciPy) with a maximum of 1000 iterations and a custom gradient computed via central finite difference.
 - Iteratively adjusts component poses and counterweight parameters (positions and masses) to minimize the visibility metric (LPIPS) while strictly adhering to all 10 constraints.

**Fabrication and Control:**
- **Fabrication:** Three distinct designs (human-designed baseline, low-visibility sampled, low-visibility optimized) were selected for physical prototyping. A truss-based architecture using 0.8 mm carbon fiber rods and 3D-printed miniature mounting interfaces connect the core components. Counterweights are steel blocks encapsulated in 3D-printed PLA spherical shells. The launch interface, initially monolithic, was redesigned for the optimized prototype into a distributed truss structure to prevent high-contrast visual artifacts near the rotation axis.
- **Control:** An on-board Espressif ESP32-S3-PICO controller executes a single-actuator control law, similar to prior work, to regulate vertical thrust and generate horizontal acceleration. An external OptiTrack system provides state estimation. A custom protocol synchronizes the UAV's internal clock with the OptiTrack system to within 0.1ms, crucial for precise timing of motor commands in the fast-spinning system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**Simulation and Optimization Analysis:**
- **Feasible Designs:** The feasibility filtering stage generated approximately 20,000 valid Phantom Twist configurations. From these, the 500 lowest visibility designs were selected for further gradient-based optimization.
- **Visibility Comparison to Conventional UAVs:** Conventional quadcopters and helicopters were found to have visibility metrics an order of magnitude higher than the best Phantom Twist designs, primarily due to their large, opaque areas and sharp edges.
- **Impact of Optimization on Visual Appearance:** As the LPIPS score decreased through optimization, the simulated motion-blurred image of the Phantom Twist transformed from appearing as a more solid object into a faint, translucent volume with significantly reduced high-contrast features.
- **Analysis of Design Characteristics:**
 - High-visibility designs (even within the feasible set) often suffered from non-transparent areas caused by components intersecting the rotation axis or dark areas from overlapping rotational envelopes of multiple components.
 - The human-designed baseline exhibited a visibility metric of 0.0207, confirming that intuitive component placement is insufficient for minimal visibility.
 - The low-visibility sampled design, achieved after feasibility filtering, showed a much lower metric of 0.0135, primarily due to components being placed further from the center of rotation and distributed across different heights.
 - The low-visibility optimized design, resulting from the full two-stage pipeline, achieved the lowest visibility metric of 0.0104. This improvement was attributed to fine-tuning component positions and orientations, which effectively reduced features like high-contrast rings and sharp edges.
- **Quantified Optimization Benefit:** For the 500 best samples that underwent gradient-based optimization, the average visibility metric was reduced by 12% (from 0.0161 before optimization to 0.0142 after). The minimum visibility metric decreased from 0.0134 to 0.0104.

**Physical Validation:**
- **Flight Stability:** All three fabricated prototypes (human-designed baseline, low-visibility sampled, and low-visibility optimized) demonstrated stable hover capability for approximately 10 minutes. They achieved an RMS position error of around 3 cm and a maximum position error of less than 10 cm, with flight trajectories consistent with related work. This physically validates that the established constraints successfully define sufficient conditions for stable flight.
- **Simulation-Reality Correspondence:** Long-exposure photographs (1/4 s shutter speed) of the flying prototypes closely matched their simulated motion-blurred appearances. The primary discrepancies were due to unmodeled elements such as connecting rods, wires, and the original launch interface. This strong correspondence confirms the accuracy of the simulation model in predicting the UAV's visual characteristics during flight.
- **Visual Perceptibility Comparison:**
 - The human-designed baseline prototype produced distinct, high-contrast concentric rings in flight.
 - The low-visibility sampled and optimized designs were significantly less visible.
 - The optimized design exhibited distinct structural improvements: reduced dark bands at a 0° pitch angle and a smaller overall projected area at a 90° pitch angle.
 - Crucially, the structurally optimized launch interface in the low-visibility optimized design successfully eliminated the dark, non-blurred feature near the rotation axis that was present in the baseline and sampled designs.
- **Real-world Comparison:** In a realistic outdoor setting, the dynamic, motion-blurred optimized Phantom Twist was significantly less visible than a static optimized Phantom Twist or a static conventional quadcopter against the same background, underscoring the practical effectiveness of the design approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

**Significance:**
This work introduces a novel paradigm for designing low-visibility UAVs by actively exploiting the motion blur effect caused by high-speed rotation, leveraging the persistence of vision inherent in human perception. This departs from traditional camouflage strategies that rely on static appearance, active coloration, material transparency, or motion masking.
The research demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of an automated, two-stage computational design pipeline. This pipeline uniquely integrates a human-aligned perceptual metric (LPIPS) with a comprehensive set of inertial, aerodynamic, and geometric constraints, enabling the generation of physically realizable and optimally camouflaged designs. The successful fabrication and flight validation of counter-intuitively configured, yet stable, prototypes confirm the practical applicability of the computational framework. The findings establish that explicitly optimizing for motion blur significantly reduces visual perceptibility compared to conventional UAVs, even those with incidentally spinning components.

**Potential Impact:**
The development of the Phantom Twist and its underlying design methodology has several potential impacts:
- **Enabling Less Intrusive Robotic Operations:** By significantly reducing visual perceptibility, Phantom Twist-like UAVs could be deployed in sensitive environments for tasks such as inspection, environmental monitoring, or human-robot interaction with minimal disturbance or distraction. This could lead to more accurate data collection in natural settings (e.g., animal behavior observation) and more reliable interactions by reducing the "observer effect."
- **Advancement in Automated Design for Robotics:** The two-stage computational design pipeline, which balances complex physics constraints with a sophisticated perceptual objective, represents an advance in automated robot design. This framework could be adapted for optimizing other robot characteristics (e.g., acoustics, thermal signature) or for different robotic platforms, offering a blueprint for integrating multi-modal performance metrics into design processes.
- **Future Research Directions:**
 - **Dynamic Perceptual Modeling:** Future work could enhance the visibility metric to account for dynamic effects such as flicker perception (e.g., using the Ferry-Porter law under varying luminance) and spatial drift or oscillation due to imperfect control, which could affect real-world visibility. Exploring new hardware allowing faster rotations might further reduce flicker.
 - **Comprehensive Component Integration:** The current model excludes minor components like connecting rods and wires. Future iterations could incorporate these into the optimization pipeline or explore the use of optically transparent materials for these elements to further minimize residual visual artifacts.
 - **Multimodal Low-Observability:** Recognizing that acoustic emissions are also critical for UAV perceptibility, future efforts could integrate visual minimization with strategies for quiet propulsion, such as optimized propeller designs or alternative propulsion concepts like electrohydrodynamic thrust.
 - **Exploiting Rotation for Enhanced Functionality:** The continuous rotation of Phantom Twist could be leveraged as an advantage. Similar platforms have demonstrated 360° communication and relative localization. Future designs could integrate LEDs, time-of-flight (ToF) sensors, or line-scan cameras to enable tasks like 360° imaging or in-air displays, opening new avenues for functionality in a low-visibility platform.
