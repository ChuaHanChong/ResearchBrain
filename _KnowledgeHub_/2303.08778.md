# Fully neuromorphic vision and control for autonomous drone flight

> [!summary] Summary
> A fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline flies a real quadrotor: a five-layer spiking network (28.8k neurons) maps raw event-camera data from four 16x16 image corners to optical flow, trained self-supervised by contrast maximization, while a linear spiking control layer is evolved in a drone simulator. Running on Intel Loihi at 200 Hz for 27 microjoules per inference, it hovers, lands, and maneuvers sideways while yawing.

## Problem

- Event-based vision is usually processed by accumulating events into frames for conventional artificial networks, discarding the sparse asynchronous structure that makes neuromorphic sensing low-latency and cheap.
- Spiking networks accurate enough for optical flow still need millions of neurons, far beyond embedded neuromorphic chips that hold only hundreds of thousands.
- Prior neuromorphic robot demos used low-dimensional inputs or kept conventional vision and external motion capture in the loop, so no fully learned vision-to-control flight existed.

## Method

- Corner-wise ==spiking vision network==: one weight-shared five-layer ==CUBA-LIF== network (three ==self-recurrent encoders== plus spiking pooling, 7.2k neurons) runs on each 16x16 downsampled image corner, emitting per-corner optical flow.
- ==Self-supervised contrast maximization==: the four corner flows parameterize a four-point planar ==homography== giving dense flow, whose event-deblurring loss backpropagates through time with ==surrogate gradients== and ==Loihi quantization-aware training==.
- Evolved linear control decoder: a ==genetic algorithm== in an event-free drone simulator maps visual observables (scaled velocity, yaw rate), roll, pitch and setpoint to thrust and attitude commands.

## Results

- On Loihi the pipeline runs onboard at **200 Hz** using **27 microjoules** per inference, three to four orders of magnitude below a Jetson Nano running **14-26** inferences per second.
- Real flights tracked **16** setpoints (hover, landing at **0.5 1/s** divergence, sideways flight, yawing at **0.2 rad/s**) and survived flickering and dim light, failing only in near-darkness.
- Ablations: the artificial baseline hit best **0.056** endpoint error; the self-recurrent spiking net fell from **+94%** to **+39%** via corner cropping and **90-event** limits, then **+50%** after quantization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Splitting perception from control lets each half train where its reality gap is smallest: vision on real events, control in an event-free simulator.
> - Task priors such as a static planar scene and corner-only processing shrink the network over 100x, making capable perception fit current neuromorphic chips.
> - Neuromorphic energy and speed gains scale with input sparsity, so quiet scenes run fastest, while chip I/O bandwidth, not neuron count, caps richer controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself at the intersection of deep learning, robotics, and neuromorphic computing, addressing a critical bottleneck in the deployment of advanced AI on resource-constrained platforms. Over the past decade, deep Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have revolutionized computer vision, enabling state-of-the-art performance in tasks like object detection, segmentation, and optical flow estimation. However, the computational demands and power consumption of these large ANNs limit their application, particularly for small robots like drones that operate under severe SWaP constraints.

Neuromorphic computing offers a promising alternative by mimicking the sparse, asynchronous, and energy-efficient processing found in biological brains. Event-based cameras, which only report brightness changes, provide a paradigm shift from traditional frame-based vision by generating sparse data streams with microsecond temporal resolution. The ideal companion to such sensors is Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) running on neuromorphic processors, which can process these events asynchronously and with significantly lower power consumption due to their binary spike-based communication.

Despite this promise, the field has faced significant challenges:
1. **SNN Training Difficulty:** Training SNNs is inherently harder than ANNs due to their sparse, binary, and asynchronous nature, the non-differentiability of the spiking activation function (often addressed by surrogate gradients), and complexities in encoding/decoding continuous values.
2. **Hardware Limitations:** Current embedded neuromorphic processors (e.g., Intel Loihi, ROLLS, SpiNNaker) have limited neuron and synapse counts, making it difficult to deploy large, complex SNNs that can handle high-dimensional sensory inputs and complex tasks. Previous work in neuromorphic robotics has largely been confined to simple tasks, low-dimensional inputs, or hybrid approaches where only parts of the pipeline are neuromorphic (e.g., SNNs for feature detection combined with traditional controllers). For instance, prior work by Dupeyroux et al. (2021) used Loihi for drone landing, but relied on conventional vision for ego-motion estimation and controlled only vertical motion.

This paper directly tackles these limitations by presenting the **first fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline for a freely flying drone**. It bridges the gap between the theoretical potential of neuromorphic systems and their practical application in complex, real-world robotic tasks. By demonstrating the end-to-end processing of high-dimensional raw event data to generate low-level control commands on an embedded neuromorphic chip, the authors advance the state of the art significantly. This work showcases that neuromorphic systems can move beyond simple demonstrations and perform sophisticated, real-time control, opening avenues for future generations of autonomous robots that are smaller, more agile, and significantly more energy-efficient than their conventional counterparts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop and demonstrate the first fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline for autonomous flight of a freely flying drone.** This overarching goal encompasses several specific aims:

- **End-to-End Neuromorphic Processing:** To create a spiking neural network that can directly process high-dimensional, raw event-based camera data (from a DAVIS240C event camera) and output low-level control actions (thrust and attitude commands) for a drone.
- **Embedded Deployment and Real-time Operation:** To implement this entire pipeline on an embedded neuromorphic processor (Intel Loihi) and achieve real-time autonomous flight, specifically targeting an execution frequency of approximately 200 Hz.
- **Energy Efficiency:** To validate the inherent energy-saving potential of neuromorphic hardware by demonstrating significantly lower power consumption per inference compared to conventional embedded GPU solutions.
- **Overcoming SNN Training Challenges:** To devise a robust learning framework that allows for the effective training of complex SNNs despite their inherent difficulties (e.g., non-differentiability, sparse communication).
- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To ensure that controllers trained in simulation can be reliably transferred and perform effectively on a real physical drone, addressing the notorious "reality gap" challenge in robotics.
- **Autonomous Flight Capabilities:** To enable the drone to perform various vision-based autonomous flight tasks, including hovering, landing, and maneuvering sideways, even under challenging conditions such as simultaneous yawing and varying lighting.

The core motivation driving these objectives stems from the fundamental limitations of traditional deep learning approaches for small, agile robots. Conventional Deep ANNs, while powerful, demand substantial computational resources (heavy CPUs/GPUs) and power, making them impractical for deployment on SWaP-constrained platforms like micro air vehicles. The authors are motivated by the biological paradigm of sparse, asynchronous processing observed in animal brains, which enables low-latency and energy-efficient perception-action loops crucial for agile behaviors (e.g., flying insects). By leveraging event-based vision and SNNs on neuromorphic hardware, the research seeks to unlock similar capabilities for artificial systems, paving the way for smaller, lighter, and more intelligent autonomous robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research is characterized by a novel split-training strategy and careful architectural design tailored for embedded neuromorphic hardware.

#### 3.1. System Architecture

The proposed system is a fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline. It comprises a Spiking Neural Network (SNN) that takes raw event-camera data as input and produces low-level drone control commands. The pipeline is designed to run entirely on Intel's Loihi neuromorphic processor.

#### 3.2. Neuromorphic Vision Network Design

- **SNN Model:** The SNN utilizes a current-based leaky-integrate-and-fire (CUBA-LIF) neuron model, explicitly configured to match the integer quantization and arithmetic operations of the Loihi chip for accurate hardware simulation. Learned parameters include membrane potential and synaptic input current decays (τU, τI) and a firing threshold (θ).
- **Input Processing:** To address computational resource limitations, the high-dimensional (240x180 pixels) raw event stream from the DAVIS240C camera is spatially reduced. The SNN only processes information from the four 16x16 pixel corners of the image (after nearest-neighbor downsampling). This design decision leverages the assumption of a static, flat ground surface, which simplifies ego-motion estimation from optical flow, as the apparent motion at these corners encodes non-metric velocity and rotational rates linearly.
- **Network Structure:** Each of the four corner processing units consists of a small, identical SNN with five layers (one input layer, three self-recurrent encoders, and a pooling layer), totaling 7.2k neurons and 506.4k synapses per corner. The output of each corner network is a 2D optical flow vector (pixels per millisecond).
- **Spike-to-Flow Decoding:** Spikes from the vision network are decoded into two floats representing optical flow for each corner.

#### 3.3. Control Network Design

- **Linear Control Layer:** The control part consists of a single linear decoding layer. It maps the estimated optical flow from the four corners (transformed into visual observables like scaled velocities and yaw rate), absolute roll and pitch from the drone's IMU, and desired visual observable setpoints, directly to collective thrust and attitude control commands (desired thrust, roll, pitch, and yaw rate). This linear transformation is designed to be merged with the vision network's linear spike decoding, allowing for a single matrix multiplication from vision network spikes to control commands, minimizing off-chip data transfer.

#### 3.4. Training Strategy (Split Learning)

The core innovation in training is a split approach to circumvent the difficulty of simulating event-based vision for control policy learning.
- **Vision Network Training (Self-supervised):**
 - **Framework:** The vision SNN (mapping events to optical flow) is trained in a self-supervised fashion using the contrast maximization framework (Gallego et al., 2018). This approach aims to "deblur" accumulated events by compensating for spatiotemporal misalignments, thereby retrieving accurate optical flow.
 - **Loss Function:** A reformulation of the focus objective function based on the per-pixel and per-polarity average timestamp of the "image of warped events" (IWE) is used. A Charbonnier smoothness prior is also applied.
 - **Techniques:** Surrogate gradients (Neftci et al., 2019) are used to handle the non-differentiability of spiking neurons during backpropagation through time. Quantization-aware training ensures compatibility with Loihi's integer-based arithmetic. Training is performed on *real-world* event sequences (a custom 40-minute dataset) to prevent sim-to-real gap issues for the vision component.
- **Control Network Training (Evolutionary Algorithm in Simulation):**
 - **Simulator:** A modified version of the Flightmare drone simulator (Song et al., 2020) is used.
 - **Input Simulation:** To avoid simulating events, the ground-truth state of the simulated drone is used to generate the *expected* corner optical flows via a continuous homography transform (Ma et al., 2004). This allows the controller to be trained on realistic optical flow inputs without the computational cost of event simulation.
 - **Algorithm:** A mutation-only genetic algorithm with a population size of 100 evolves the weights of the linear control matrix. Fitness is determined by minimizing the accumulated error between estimated and desired visual observables across a set of 16 diverse setpoints (hover, vertical, and horizontal flight).
 - **Sim-to-Real Transfer Techniques:** Domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017) is applied during training (adding noise to flows, random attitude bias, varying initial conditions) to enhance robustness. Abstraction (Scheper & de Croon, 2017) is used by having low-level controllers handle rotor speeds, and calibrating biases for attitude and hover thrust. Smoothing and scaling of visual observables are also applied in the real world.

#### 3.5. Hardware and Real-World Deployment

- **Drone Platform:** A custom-built quadrotor (1.0 kg, 35 cm diameter) equipped with the DAVIS240C event-based camera, an UP Squared single-board computer (SBC), and an Intel Kapoho Bay (two Loihi chips) neuromorphic processor.
- **Communication:** ROS1 for camera-to-SBC event transfer, USB for SBC-to-Loihi spike transfer, and ROS2 for high-frequency SBC-to-PX4 flight controller communication.
- **Control Loop:** Events are processed and downsampled on the UP Squared, sent to Loihi, processed by the SNN, output spikes returned to UP Squared, decoded into corner flows, then combined with IMU data and setpoints to generate control commands that are sent to the PX4 autopilot. An OptiTrack motion capture system is used for position control between test runs and as a failsafe.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling results demonstrating the viability of a fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline for autonomous drone flight.

- **First Fully Neuromorphic Drone Flight:** The most significant finding is the successful deployment of the *first fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline* controlling a *freely flying drone*. The drone could accurately follow various ego-motion setpoints, performing hovering, landing, and maneuvering sideways, even while yawing simultaneously.

- **Real-time Performance and Energy Efficiency:**
 - The entire neuromorphic pipeline runs on-board the Intel Loihi processor with an execution frequency of **200 Hz**, which is crucial for agile drone flight.
 - It achieves remarkable energy efficiency, spending only **27 µJ per inference**.
 - A comparative analysis with an NVIDIA Jetson Nano (a common embedded GPU solution) shows that Loihi outperforms it by **three to four orders of magnitude** in energy consumption and **one to two orders of magnitude** in execution frequency. This substantial efficiency gain is a direct validation of the neuromorphic approach. Notably, Loihi’s execution frequency also improved with increasing event sparsity (i.e., less motion), demonstrating its inherent advantage in handling sparse, event-based data.

- **Robust Vision-Based State Estimation:**
 - The self-supervised trained vision SNN accurately estimates visual observables (scaled velocities and yaw rates) from raw event data. Qualitative results show close agreement between estimated and ground-truth motion, even for fast sequences where the camera spun at approximately 200 deg/s.
 - The SNN maintains sparse spiking activity (below 50% of available neurons active across all layers), despite not being explicitly trained for sparsity. This is a desirable characteristic for energy efficiency.
 - Quantitative analysis (Table 1) shows that while ANNs still outperform SNNs in terms of average endpoint error (EPE) for optical flow estimation, the architectural choices (processing only corners, limiting events, Loihi-specific quantization) were essential compromises for enabling deployment on the embedded hardware. Self-recurrency, though the weakest form of explicit recurrency, was chosen for its suitability for resource-constrained implementation.

- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer for Control:**
 - The linear network controller, trained solely in simulation using a genetic algorithm, successfully transferred to the real drone, enabling autonomous flight. This validates the effectiveness of the split-training approach, ground-truth-based flow simulation for control training, and domain randomization techniques.
 - While the controller achieved stable flight, it exhibited steady-state errors for some setpoints, particularly at higher horizontal speeds. This is attributed to the inherent limitations of a simple linear controller in dealing with the drone's nonlinear dynamics (e.g., relationship between attitude and velocity, drag effects) and potential sim-to-real mismatches in drag models.
 - Comparison with a hand-tuned Proportional-Integral (PI) controller revealed that the PI controller achieved faster setpoint tracking and reduced steady-state error, demonstrating the trade-offs between a fully learned, fixed-weight SNN controller and a conventional feedback controller.

- **Versatility and Robustness:**
 - The vision-based state estimation, when coupled with a PI controller, demonstrated versatility in complex maneuvers like flying a square trajectory (though with some yaw drift) and performing "frisbee-like" flight (flying straight while constantly yawing).
 - Robustness was shown across varying lighting conditions (normal, darkish, flickering lights), with the system maintaining tracking performance. Only extremely dark conditions, leading to almost no events, caused significant divergence in state estimation and control failure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a substantial leap forward in the field of neuromorphic robotics, demonstrating the viability and significant advantages of an end-to-end spiking neural network for complex, real-world robotic tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Pioneering Full Neuromorphic Pipeline:** This work represents the **first successful demonstration of a fully neuromorphic vision-to-control pipeline** for a freely flying drone. Previous efforts were often hybrid or limited in scope. By achieving autonomous flight with an SNN from raw event data to low-level control, the authors provide a crucial proof-of-concept for the future of neuromorphic autonomous systems.

- **Validation of Neuromorphic Hardware for Robotics:** The impressive energy efficiency (27 µJ per inference) and real-time performance (200 Hz) on Intel's Loihi processor validate the core promise of neuromorphic computing for power- and size-constrained robotic applications. This directly addresses the SWaP limitations that currently hinder the deployment of advanced deep learning models on small platforms like drones. This is a critical step towards enabling highly intelligent, onboard processing for micro-robots.

- **Advancement of SNN Training and Deployment:** The paper showcases effective strategies for training and deploying SNNs in challenging real-world scenarios. The split-training approach, combining self-supervised learning on real event data for vision and evolutionary algorithms in simulation for control (with ground-truth flow), offers a pragmatic solution to SNN training difficulties and the reality gap. The successful use of surrogate gradients and quantization-aware training for Loihi-compatible SNNs further contributes to the growing toolkit for SNN development.

- **Inspiration for Bio-Inspired Robotics:** By demonstrating a system that mimics the sparse, asynchronous processing of biological brains, this work provides a tangible step towards robots that can approach the agility, versatility, and robustness of animals such as flying insects. This opens new avenues for designing more adaptive and robust autonomous systems.

- **Future of Autonomous Systems:** The implications extend beyond drones to a wide range of autonomous robots operating under tight resource constraints, including small ground robots, underwater vehicles, and space exploration robots. The energy and latency benefits could enable longer operational durations, faster reaction times, and more complex decision-making capabilities without relying on heavy off-board processing.

- **Guidance for Neuromorphic Hardware Development:** The research also highlights current limitations of neuromorphic hardware, particularly concerning I/O bandwidth and interfacing options. The authors note reaching the limits of spike transfer rates to/from Loihi, and the necessity for a linear controller due to spike input constraints. This feedback is invaluable for guiding future developments in neuromorphic chip design, pushing for improved communication interfaces and greater on-chip integration of sensing and processing. The mention of transitioning from digital to analog neuromorphic hardware also hints at future directions for even greater efficiency.

In conclusion, this research is a landmark achievement, transforming the theoretical promise of fully neuromorphic control into a tangible reality for autonomous drone flight. It lays foundational groundwork for the next generation of highly agile, intelligent, and energy-efficient robots, fostering a future where bio-inspired computing principles power advanced robotic capabilities.
