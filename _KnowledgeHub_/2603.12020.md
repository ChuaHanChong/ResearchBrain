# Sim-to-reality adaptation for Deep Reinforcement Learning applied to an underwater docking application

> [!summary] Summary
> A PPO-based 6-DoF controller for autonomous underwater docking of the Girona AUV, trained inside a Stonefish high-fidelity digital twin adapted to a 20-thread multiprocessing RL framework. Realistic hydrodynamics, collision geometry, distance-scaled observation noise, and a reward mixing distance, yaw, action smoothness, adaptive collision penalty and sparse mission terms yield over 90% docking success in simulation and 8 of 10 successful real test-tank runs.

## Problem

- Prior underwater docking RL used fixed spawn positions and simplified kinematic models, biasing policies toward single trajectories rather than generalized 6-DoF control behavior.
- Earlier 3D docking work lacked collision models, terminating episodes on any contact, which distorts success rates for maneuvers that inherently require funnel contact.
- Very few docking policies transfer to physical vehicles; fast trainers reach speed by sacrificing realistic hydrodynamics, sensor noise and contact physics needed for transfer.

## Method

- ==Multiprocessing Stonefish digital twin==: 20 headless parallel training threads plus one rendered evaluation thread, driven through the same ROS interfaces the real AUV uses.
- ==Distance- and visibility-conditioned Gaussian observation noise==, with base jitter plus occlusion noise scaled by translational error magnitude when the docking station leaves the camera field of view.
- ==Composite reward== with weighted ==Mahalanobis distance==, exponential yaw and action-smoothness penalties, and an ==adaptive IMU-acceleration collision threshold== that doubles after impacts to avoid double-penalizing one contact.

## Results

- PPO reached above **90%** simulated docking success, with mean episode reward rising from about **-800** at start to **300-400**, after roughly **3** hours on an i7 with an RTX 4060.
- Real test-tank deployment in a **19x9x5 m** pool gave **8** successful dockings out of **10** missions, from randomized start positions and orientations, each lasting **30-50** seconds.
- Real force and torque traces matched simulated ones, including yaw oscillations, while X and Y errors converged with small overshoot and heave began only after lateral and yaw errors shrank.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modest parallelism with faithful hydrodynamics, contact and sensor models can beat massive-throughput simulators when the goal is transferable policies, not fast reward curves.
> - Scaling observation noise with target distance and visibility prevents overfitting to exact simulator coordinates, a cheap and general recipe for sim-to-real robustness.
> - Permitting soft contact rather than terminating on collision lets agents discover emergent tactics, here pitch braking and yaw dithering, that exploit mechanical guiding features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research presented in this paper contributes to the field of autonomous robotics, specifically focusing on Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for Autonomous Underwater Vehicles (AUVs) and the critical challenge of transferring learned policies from simulation to real-world deployment (sim-to-reality adaptation).

Traditional control methods, such as Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) and Model Predictive Control (MPC), offer reliable solutions for many autonomous tasks. However, DRL presents an alternative capable of adapting to unpredicted environmental conditions, offering increased robustness. This advantage motivates its application across various platforms, including Autonomous Surface Vehicles (ASVs), Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), and Humanoid Robotics Platforms (HRPs).

Within the DRL landscape, two primary bottlenecks exist: training latency and the sim-to-real gap. Efforts to accelerate training have led to high-performance platforms like MJX and Isaac Sim, but seamless sim-to-real transfer remains an active area of research.

In the specific context of underwater docking for AUVs, early DRL work, such as that by Anderlini et al. (2019) and Zhang et al. (2020), explored basic kinematic benchmarks, often using fixed starting positions and standardized dynamic models. Patil et al. (2021) conducted benchmarking studies comparing PPO, TD3, and SAC for continuous docking control. More recent efforts, including Zhang et al. (2023), have addressed environmental robustness and 3D complexity under variable conditions, although limitations such as simplistic collision models were noted.

The transition from simulation to physical hardware represents a current frontier. Bharti et al. (2025) demonstrated a notable sim-to-real transfer on a BlueROV platform using TD3 for visual servoing and docking. Zheng et al. (2025) proposed multi-layer simulation frameworks to bridge the reality gap, while Chu et al. (2025) developed an Adaptive Reward Shape PPO (ARSPPO) framework utilizing parallel simulation for faster training and successful real-world lake deployment. Yu and Lin (2025) integrated computer vision (YOLO) with DRL for docking. Most recently, Tuncay et al. (2026) highlighted the importance of high-speed training environments (like MJX) and high-fidelity validation simulators (like Stonefish) for complex 6-DoF control.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by developing a systematic approach to autonomous underwater docking. It specifically targets the Girona AUV, aiming to address the sim-to-real gap by leveraging a high-fidelity digital twin and a multiprocessing DRL framework. The work focuses on practical deployment, contributing to the limited body of research that successfully transfers DRL policies trained in simulation to real underwater platforms, particularly through experimental validation in a physical test tank.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent advantages of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) over traditional control methods for autonomous tasks, particularly in environments with unpredictable conditions. For underwater applications like AUV docking, DRL offers the distinct advantage of adapting to unforeseen environmental disturbances, where conventional control strategies such as PID or MPC often experience performance degradation. This robustness is considered a key factor for practical deployment in complex real-world scenarios.

Despite the potential benefits, two significant bottlenecks impede the widespread deployment of DRL: high training latency and the "sim-to-real" gap. The research explicitly aims to mitigate these challenges, focusing on facilitating a seamless transfer of DRL policies from simulated environments to physical robotic platforms.

The specific application addressed in this work is autonomous docking using the Girona Autonomous Underwater Vehicle (AUV). This task is selected due to the multiple challenges it presents in underwater environments, making it a suitable testbed for evaluating the adaptive capabilities of modern DRL approaches.

The main objectives of this paper, articulated through its stated contributions, are:

- **Accelerated Learning through Simulation Adaptation:** To adapt the Stonefish simulator into a multiprocessing DRL framework. This is aimed at significantly reducing the time required for training DRL agents, thereby addressing the high training latency bottleneck.
- **High-Fidelity Environment for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To develop a high-fidelity simulation environment within Stonefish. This environment is designed to incorporate realistic AUV dynamics, accurate collision models, and sensor inputs, which are considered crucial for minimizing the sim-to-real gap and ensuring that policies learned in simulation are transferable to reality.
- **Integration of DRL with Position-Based Servoing:** To integrate position-based visual servoing with DRL. The objective is to establish DRL as a robust replacement for standard control systems and traditional behavior trees in complex maneuvers like docking.
- **Experimental Validation in a Physical Environment:** To demonstrate successful autonomous docking in a physical test tank using the proposed DRL methodology. This objective is critical for validating the efficacy of the sim-to-real adaptation and confirming the practical applicability of the developed DRL controller on a real AUV platform.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more robust and adaptive control strategies for AUV docking, specifically seeking to overcome the practical hurdles of DRL training efficiency and the reality gap through a systematic approach that combines advanced simulation with real-world validation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on developing a DRL-based control policy for autonomous AUV docking, with a strong emphasis on mitigating the sim-to-real gap through high-fidelity simulation and subsequent physical validation.

**A. Simulation Environment**
The Stonefish simulator was selected and adapted to create an accurate digital twin of the Girona AUV and its docking task.
- **High-Fidelity Modeling:** Stonefish models the hydrodynamic behavior of the AUV, providing realistic vehicle dynamics. It also allows for the integration of environmental disturbances, with ocean currents being considered in this work as the primary perturbation.
- **Collision Handling:** The simulator incorporates realistic collision handling, enabling proper physical interaction between the AUV and the docking station (DS) during maneuvers. The DS model was simplified to include only guiding funnels, which facilitate docking by providing a ±25 cm clearance along the X and Y axes, to improve computational performance while maintaining critical interaction points (Fig. 1a, 1b).
- **Training Acceleration:** Stonefish-RL allows for faster-than-real-time simulations (up to 5 times speedup per thread). To further accelerate the learning process, a multiprocess adaptation of Stonefish was implemented, running 20 parallel headless training threads and one graphical thread for evaluation. This approach prioritizes realistic hydrodynamics and sensor models, acknowledging that it offers fewer parallel instances compared to frameworks like ISAAC Sim or MJX.

**B. Docking Problem Setup**
To ensure the learned control behavior is generalized and not biased, the AUV and DS spawning positions were randomized for each episode during training.
- **Localization:** Position-based visual servoing is employed for DS localization, utilizing a Three Dimensional Binary Marker (3DBM) detected by an onboard camera.
- **Sensor Modeling for Sim-to-Real:** During headless training, the camera model is simplified to a visibility condition. When the DS is within the AUV's field of view, its pose is updated with accurate measurements. Prior to visual detection, an approximate DS pose estimate (e.g., from Ultra Short Baseline (USBL)) is assumed to be available.
- **Realistic Observation Noise:** To prevent overfitting to perfect simulator coordinates and enhance sim-to-real transfer, Gaussian noise is injected into observations. The noise scale (`σ_k`) is dynamically calculated proportional to the Euclidean distance of the translational error (Eq. 2). The perturbed observation (`ˆo_k`) is generated by adding constant sensor jitter (`η_base`) and occlusion noise (`η_occ`), which is conditioned on the visibility of the docking station (Eq. 3).

**C. Algorithms and Policies**
The docking task is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
- **Algorithm Selection:** The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) DRL algorithm was chosen. PPO is an on-policy actor-critic method that uses a clipped surrogate objective to maintain policy stability. PPO was selected over Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) due to its superior stability and performance observed during initial physical experimentation in the test tank.
- **Objective:** The DRL algorithm aims to learn an optimal policy (`π*`) that maximizes the expected discounted return (`J(π)`).

**1) State Space:** The agent's observation at each time step `k` is a state vector `S = [ˆo_k, e_ψ, V_k, A_k]` (Eq. 1).
- `ˆo_k`: The perturbed translational error vector `[e_x, e_y, e_z]`, representing the relative position of the docking point in the AUV body frame.
- `e_ψ`: The yaw error in the AUV frame.
- `V_k`: Linear (`v_x, v_y, v_z`) and angular (`ω_ψ`) velocities in the AUV frame.
- `A_k`: AUV accelerations (`acc_x, acc_y, acc_z`) measured by the Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU).

**2) Action Space:** The agent outputs a six-degree-of-freedom force and torque vector `A = [F_x, F_y, F_z, T_r, T_p, T_ψ]` (Eq. 4), expressed in the AUV body frame. Although the Girona AUV cannot directly actuate roll due to its thruster configuration, a 6-DoF action vector is maintained for general formulation. The AUV's control system then distributes this commanded wrench among its five thrusters.

**3) Reward Function:** The total reward `R` is a sum of five components (Eq. 5): `R = r_dist + r_angle + r_smooth + r_collision + r_mission`.
- `r_dist`: Penalizes the Mahalanobis distance error between the AUV and DS in the AUV frame (Eq. 6). Weighted to prioritize X and Y axes for docking.
- `r_angle`: Penalizes yaw error exponentially (Eq. 7).
- `r_smooth`: Penalizes large variations between consecutive actions to encourage smooth control commands, which is beneficial for real-world deployment (Eq. 8).
- `r_collision`: An adaptive penalty for impacts, detected by variations in IMU accelerations (Eq. 9, 10). The adaptive threshold (`Γ_k`) prevents multiple penalties for a single collision and handles sensor bouncing, allowing the agent to utilize the guiding funnels for soft docking.
- `r_mission`: A sparse terminal reward, providing a large positive reward (`p_s`) for successful docking within a proximity threshold and a penalty (`p_f`) for truncated episodes, to guide the agent towards the global objective (Eq. 11).

The hyperparameters for PPO training, including learning rate, buffer size, batch size, gamma, steps per update, entropy coefficient, and clip range, are detailed in Appendix III. The simulation parameters for physics, RL inference, camera refresh rates, and initial spawning ranges are also specified.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from training, simulation, and physical test tank experiments, demonstrating the capabilities of the DRL agent for autonomous underwater docking.

**A. Training Performance**
The PPO agent underwent training for approximately 3 hours on an Intel Core i7 processor with an RTX 4060 Nvidia graphic card. Throughout the training process, the mean reward value increased significantly, from an initial value of approximately -800 to between 300 and 400. This increase indicates learning progress and policy optimization. Upon completion of training, the agent achieved a simulated success rate exceeding 90% for docking maneuvers.

**B. Simulation Results**
Simulations were conducted using the Stonefish simulator, with the learning agent interacting through the same ROS interfaces utilized by the real Girona AUV. This design choice aimed to ensure software architecture consistency and facilitate sim-to-real transfer, despite introducing minor communication overheads.
- **Docking Maneuvers:** The trained PPO agent successfully executed multiple docking maneuvers (Fig. 6) from randomized starting positions, with mission times ranging from 30 to 60 seconds. The DRL agent's inference rate was set to 5 Hz to align with the camera processing rate.
- **Control Behavior:** Analysis of the requested forces and torques, alongside the docking error in the North, East, Down (NED) frame (Fig. 7), revealed distinct control characteristics. Errors in X and Y axes showed direct convergence with minimal overshoot. Heaving along the Z-axis typically commenced after significant reduction in X, Y, and yaw errors.
- **Emergent Behaviors:** The DRL agent developed two unprogrammed tactical behaviors:
 1. **Pitch-based Braking:** The agent utilized pitch motion to decelerate and slow down, particularly when turning and diving towards the DS.
 2. **Yaw Oscillations:** Small oscillations in yaw were observed (evident in the torque plot in Fig. 7), which the authors infer as assisting the AUV in sliding into the DS. These oscillations were executed without triggering the adaptive collision penalty, indicating a controlled interaction.

**C. Test Tank Experiments**
Validation of the trained DRL policy was performed in a physical test tank (19×9×5 m) using the real Girona AUV.
- **Experimental Setup:** The setup mirrored the simulation, using a downward-facing camera and a 3DBM for DS pose estimation and subsequent relative position calculation for the observation vector.
- **Success Rate:** A total of 10 missions were performed, with 8 successful docking maneuvers. Six of these successful missions, initiated from different starting positions and random orientations, are depicted in Figure 8, with mission durations between 30 and 50 seconds.
- **Safety Measures:** During test tank experiments, AUV forces were clipped to 25% or 50% of its maximum capabilities for safety.
- **Sim-to-Reality Consistency:** The force and torque requests and the error profiles observed in the test tank experiments (Fig. 9) demonstrated a strong resemblance to the simulated results. Notably, the emergent yaw oscillations identified in simulation were also present during physical docking maneuvers, indicating a successful transfer of the learned policy to the real AUV and corroborating the efficacy of the sim-to-reality adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) applied to autonomous underwater docking, with a focus on practical deployment. The findings contribute to the field of marine robotics by demonstrating a methodology for transitioning DRL policies from high-fidelity simulation to physical hardware.

The work's primary significance lies in its systematic approach to mitigating the "sim-to-real" gap, which is a substantial challenge in DRL applications. By adapting the Stonefish simulator into a multiprocessing framework, the authors addressed training latency while retaining realistic hydrodynamic and sensor models. This balance between training speed and environmental fidelity is crucial for generating transferable policies. The achieved simulation success rate of over 90% serves as a foundational validation of the policy's effectiveness in a controlled digital environment.

Furthermore, the successful validation in a physical test tank, with 8 out of 10 successful docking runs by the Girona AUV, provides empirical evidence for the efficacy of the proposed sim-to-reality adaptation. This validation is critical as it moves beyond theoretical and simulation-based claims, confirming that the DRL controller can operate robustly in a real-world setting, despite sensor noise and the complexities of physical interaction. The implementation of safety limits on forces during physical tests indicates an awareness of practical deployment considerations.

A notable impact of this research is the demonstration of emergent behaviors, specifically pitch-based braking for deceleration and yaw oscillations for mechanical alignment, without explicit programming. These complex tactical maneuvers, which the DRL agent learned autonomously, are described as challenging to achieve with traditional PID or MPC controllers. This highlights DRL's capacity to develop nuanced and adaptive strategies, which can be particularly advantageous in dynamic and unpredictable underwater environments. Such emergent capabilities could lead to more agile and robust autonomous systems than those designed with predefined rules.

The paper suggests a reliable pipeline that combines high-fidelity simulation with robust reward shaping for deploying RL-based controllers in sensitive underwater environments. This pipeline has implications for future AUV autonomy, potentially enabling more complex missions and improved operational safety in tasks requiring precise maneuvers and interaction.

Future work outlined by the authors includes expanding environmental complexity (e.g., dynamic currents, dynamic docking stations) and incorporating domain randomization for thruster positioning. These steps indicate a continuous effort to further enhance the robustness and generalization capabilities of DRL controllers for diverse and challenging real-world underwater scenarios. Ultimately, this research advances the practical application of DRL in robotics by offering a validated methodology for achieving reliable transfer from simulation to reality.
