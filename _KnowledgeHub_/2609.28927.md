# Streaming-WAM: Action-Conditioned World-Action Model for Asynchronous Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Streaming-WAM is a world-action model for asynchronous robot manipulation that conditions future visual prediction on the action prefix already committed for execution during inference. A shared action expert encodes committed actions into condition slots that guide video latents, whose intermediate features steer generation of the remaining actions in one joint update. It reaches 98.35% average success on LIBERO with a 2.93x episode-time speedup.

## Problem

- World action models generate future visual predictions at inference time, which is costly and leaves the robot idle between action chunks, breaking continuous manipulation.
- Asynchronous execution overlaps inference with motion, but the robot keeps moving and changes the scene, so observation-only predictions become stale during the inference window.
- Prior asynchronous policies use the committed prefix only to constrain action continuation, never to condition visual prediction, so imagined futures ignore motion already scheduled.

## Method

- **Streaming** inference holds the eight committed actions as the incoming chunk's fixed prefix, launches a ==Stream Update== from the fresh observation, and hands off to the generated continuation when the prefix ends.
- A **Slot Encoder** builds condition slots aligned to the near-future latent span, with known slots encoding the prefix and learned unknown-state embeddings covering uncovered steps under a directed mask.
- ==Action-conditioned attention== adds a separately normalized condition branch to each block's visual attention; two-stage training from a one-step ==consistency-distilled== joint model keeps video supervision during action learning.

## Results

- LIBERO average success is **98.35%**, near Fast-WAM-Joint's **98.60%**, while mean episode time drops from 13.40 s to 4.58 s, a **2.93x** speedup over Fast-WAM.
- RoboTwin 2.0 reaches **91.74%** versus **87.00%** for Fast-WAM-Joint; real-world Stamp Paper episode time falls from 90 s to 38 s with **27/30** successes.
- Ablations drop success to **95.50%** without action-to-video conditioning and **96.35%** without the Slot Encoder; one-step prediction cuts visual MSE **38.6%** below the distilled baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Committed actions are a free conditioning signal in asynchronous control: using them for world prediction, not only prefix constraint, recovers accuracy lost to scene staleness.
> - Latency and throughput decouple: chunk-time speedups of 2.79x translated into only 1.46x episode-time gain, so model latency alone misstates execution efficiency.
> - Ordering of training stages matters: distilling before both adaptation stages gave 98.35% versus 94.50% when distillation followed them; over-extending conditioning to far futures hurt.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot working on the Stamp Paper manipulation task can keep acting while its model prepares the next prediction. That prediction, however, needs to account for actions already underway. Streaming-WAM gives those committed actions two jobs: they fix the opening of the next action chunk, and they condition its visual forecast. On Stamp Paper, mean Episode Time is 90 s for synchronous Joint-WAM and 38 s for Streaming-WAM. Episode Time is rollout duration, not inference latency.

Streaming-WAM real-robot rollout on Stamp Paper, with front and side views (author project page).

### 2. The next chunk must know what is moving

A world-action model (WAM) predicts future visual states and actions. Running that prediction can take long enough for the robot's surroundings to change before the next action chunk is ready. Asynchronous control lets the robot keep moving while the model works. A forecast based only on the last observation, though, can miss the effects of motion the robot has already committed to carrying out.

At a streaming update, the robot executes the committed actions. The model uses them as the fixed "prefix" of the incoming chunk, meaning its already committed opening actions. It also uses them as conditions for its visual prediction. The "postfix" is the continuation still to be generated.

Condition slots encode known committed actions, and learned unknown states cover the not-yet-known interval. Visual queries for the near-future latent group Z₁ attend to visual context and to condition slots in separate attention branches, and their outputs are added. Those intermediate visual features guide the action continuation in the same joint computation. Z₂ receives the conditioning through later video-attention layers.

In the tested schedule, the action chunk horizon is 32 and the committed prefix is eight actions. Each update launches as the robot executes the prefix. If the update is ready at the end of the overlap, the controller skips the shared prefix and switches to the continuation. If it is not ready, the robot pauses until inference finishes.

Proposed information flow, shown as a schematic rather than a measured result. Top: cold start followed by Stream Updates overlapping robot execution. Lower left: committed actions become the incoming chunk's fixed prefix and feed the known/unknown condition slots. ### 3. Does the visual pathway matter?

[[2512.05964|Training-Time RTC]] teaches action continuation from a clean prefix, but that training alone leaves the visual predictor unconditioned on the prefix. The skeptical test is an ablation that keeps the action prefix and future-video prediction but removes action-to-video conditioning.

On LIBERO, average success falls from 98.35% to 95.50% under this ablation, a 2.85-percentage-point difference. The average covers the Long, Spatial, Goal, and Object suites, and success rate is the fraction of successful trials. The ablations share initialization, data, training budget, losses, runtime optimizations, and evaluation protocol, with only the specified component changed. This supports the pathway in the evaluated system; it is not proof of a universal or exclusive cause.

LIBERO success rates (%) by suite, with averages. Full Streaming-WAM averages 98.35%. The "w/o Action Conditioning" ablation averages 95.50%, and the "w/o Slot Encoder" ablation averages 96.35%. Fast-WAM-Joint-CD reports 98.85%. Bold and underlining denote best and second-best values.

### 4. From prediction time to rollout time

The real-robot comparison uses two separate efficiency measures. Chunk Time is GPU-synchronized inference latency, excluding Streaming-WAM initialization. On Stamp Paper it is 682.1 ms for Joint-WAM and 122.6 ms for Streaming-WAM. Episode Time is mean rollout duration: 90 s versus 38 s. With 30 trials per method, observed successes are 26/30 for Joint-WAM and 27/30 for Streaming-WAM.

With the tested eight-action prefix at 30 Hz, the overlap window is about 267 ms. All reported real-world Streaming-WAM updates finished within it, with zero deadline misses and zero resulting pauses. These results come from that fixed-delay setting. The authors identify adapting to variable delays and more dynamic environments as future work.

### 5. The handoff changes the forecast

The committed prefix does more than keep action chunks continuous. It gives the next visual prediction information about motion already underway, and that visual context guides the continuation. The prefix-preserving ablation links this mechanism to task success in the tested setup.
