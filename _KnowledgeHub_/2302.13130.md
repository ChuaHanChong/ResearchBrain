# Point Cloud Forecasting as a Proxy for 4D Occupancy Forecasting

> [!summary] Summary
> Recasts self-supervised point cloud forecasting as 4D spacetime occupancy forecasting, factoring out sensor extrinsics and intrinsics so the model predicts scene geometry, then differentiably renders point clouds via ray-based volumetric rendering for training and evaluation. Beats SOTA by up to 3.26m L1 error and achieves first zero-shot cross-sensor generalization.

## Problem

- Point cloud forecasting entangles sensor extrinsics, sensor intrinsics, and scene dynamics, forcing algorithms to implicitly learn irrelevant sensor-specific patterns instead of world dynamics.
- Semantic occupancy forecasting requires costly human annotations like bounding boxes, tracks, and HD maps, limiting scalability to large unlabeled driving datasets.
- Existing point cloud forecasting methods cannot be compared across datasets or sensor rigs since predictions are tied to a specific sensor's coordinate frame and ray pattern.

## Method

- ==4D occupancy prediction network== that forecasts spacetime voxel occupancy from historic LiDAR point clouds and known sensor locations, independent of future sensor extrinsics/intrinsics.
- ==Differentiable volumetric ray renderer== that converts predicted occupancy into expected ray-stopping depth via ==voxel traversal== and probability-weighted expectation, analogous to NeRF rendering.
- ==Ray-based near-field and relative evaluation metrics== with ==ray clamping==, enabling any point-cloud or occupancy method to be scored on a shared, sensor-agnostic depth-error protocol.

## Results

- On nuScenes, method cuts L1 error to **1.40m** (1s) and **1.71m** (3s) versus S2Net's **3.49m**/**4.78m** and SPFNet's **4.58m**/**5.11m**, more than halving error.
- Zero-shot cross-sensor transfer from ArgoVerse2.0 to KITTI-Odometry beats ST3DCNN's **3.13m**/**3.25m** L1 with **1.71m**/**2.52m**, first such sensor-generalization result reported.
- Architecture ablation shows a static single-voxel-grid variant beats the dynamic variant at 1s horizon (**1.28m** vs **1.40m** L1) but dynamic wins at 3s horizon (**1.71m** vs **1.73m**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Disentangling nuisance variables (sensor pose/sampling) an agent already knows from the target prediction task simplifies learning and enables cross-domain transfer.
> - A simple non-learned aggregation-ray-tracing baseline is surprisingly strong, outperforming learned SOTA point-cloud forecasters, exposing weak baselines in prior evaluations.
> - Evaluation metrics tied to raw output format (points) rather than underlying geometry can misrank methods; ray-based depth error better reflects true scene understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous systems rely on accurate scene perception and forecasting for effective motion planning. Traditionally, this has involved complex perceptual modules such as mapping, object detection, tracking, and trajectory forecasting. These classical methods frequently depend on extensive human annotations, including semantic class labels, bounding boxes, object tracks, or high-definition (HD) maps. While effective in controlled settings, this reliance on costly and labor-intensive annotations presents significant scalability challenges for large, unlabeled datasets.

In response to these limitations, self-supervised learning tasks have emerged as a promising direction. One such task is 3D point cloud forecasting, which aims to predict future LiDAR point clouds from historical sequences without explicit human labeling. This approach is gaining traction due to its potential to exploit vast quantities of unannotated sensor data.

This paper critically examines the prevalent formulation of point cloud forecasting. It argues that predicting future point clouds directly obliges algorithms to implicitly learn not only the shapes and motions of objects in the scene but also characteristics specific to the sensor itself: its ego-motion (extrinsics) and its sampling pattern (intrinsics). The authors contend that an autonomous system's primary concern should be understanding the environment's dynamics, not the properties of its own sensors, particularly since ego-motion plans and calibrated sensor parameters are typically available to the vehicle.

The work repositions this problem by proposing "spacetime (4D) occupancy forecasting" as a more fundamental and generic intermediate representation of the world. This reframing disentangles the prediction of environmental dynamics from sensor-specific artifacts. The concept of occupancy forecasting itself is not entirely new; prior work has explored 2D bird's-eye-view (BEV) semantic or geometric occupancy grids. This paper extends the concept to full 3D and over time (4D geometric occupancy), learning from unannotated LiDAR data.

Furthermore, the methodology draws parallels with advances in novel view synthesis, particularly volumetric rendering techniques like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs). While NeRFs synthesize images by querying a volumetric model with known camera rays, this work synthesizes LiDAR scans by querying a 4D occupancy model with known sensor intrinsics and extrinsics, effectively predicting future depth for given rays. This connection highlights the paper's contribution to developing a differentiable rendering process for training 4D occupancy models.

In essence, this research aims to provide a more principled and scalable foundation for scene forecasting in autonomous driving by shifting from sensor-dependent point cloud prediction to a sensor-agnostic 4D occupancy representation, while maintaining self-supervision through an innovative differentiable rendering scheme.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations of this research are multi-faceted, addressing fundamental limitations in current scene forecasting paradigms for autonomous systems:

1. **Enhance Scene Perception and Forecasting for Motion Planning**: The overarching goal is to enable autonomous vehicles to predict how their environment will evolve, which is crucial for robust and safe motion planning. This objective is driven by the need for more reliable and adaptable perception systems.

2. **Overcome Limitations of Annotation-Dependent Methods**: Classical perception methods rely heavily on costly human annotations (e.g., semantic labels, bounding boxes, HD maps). These methods face significant challenges in scaling to the vast and diverse datasets required for widespread autonomous deployment. A primary motivation is to develop a self-supervisable approach that can leverage large volumes of unannotated LiDAR sequences, thereby improving scalability and reducing development costs.

3. **Address Implicit Sensor Learning in Point Cloud Forecasting**: The authors identify a key inefficiency in the standard formulation of 3D point cloud forecasting. They argue that algorithms trained on this task are compelled to implicitly learn three quantities:
 - **Sensor extrinsics**: The ego-motion of the autonomous vehicle.
 - **Sensor intrinsics**: The specific sampling pattern of the LiDAR sensor.
 - **Scene dynamics**: The shape and motion of objects in the environment.
 The motivation here is to argue that autonomous systems *should* predict the world's state, not their own sensor characteristics, as the vehicle already has access to its planned future motion (extrinsics) and calibrated sensor parameters (intrinsics). Learning these known quantities unnecessarily complicates the forecasting problem.

4. **Disentangle Sensor Characteristics from Scene Dynamics**: Building on the above critique, a central objective is to factor out sensor extrinsics and intrinsics from the learning process. By explicitly providing these sensor parameters, the task can be recast to focus solely on the primary quantity of interest: the dynamic state of the environment. This disentanglement is motivated by the expectation that it will simplify the learning problem, lead to more robust models, and enable better generalization.

5. **Propose Spacetime (4D) Occupancy as a Generic Intermediate Representation**: The paper introduces spacetime (4D) occupancy as a generic, sensor-independent representation of the world. This representation specifies whether a given 3D location is occupied at a particular time instance. The motivation is that such an occupancy grid is a more fundamental and versatile representation than a sparse point cloud, with greater potential for downstream tasks (e.g., motion planning) and for understanding the environment's geometry.

6. **Enable Training and Evaluation of 4D Occupancy Models without Ground-Truth 4D Occupancy**: A practical challenge for 4D occupancy forecasting is the difficulty and expense of obtaining ground-truth 4D occupancy data. To address this, the authors are motivated to develop a "rendering" mechanism that can synthesize point cloud data from predicted 4D occupancy, given known sensor extrinsics and intrinsics. This allows for self-supervised training using readily available unannotated LiDAR sequences and provides a scalable way to evaluate 4D occupancy algorithms by comparing rendered point clouds to actual future LiDAR scans.

7. **Facilitate Cross-Sensor and Cross-Dataset Evaluation and Generalization**: By disentangling sensor characteristics, the proposed approach aims to enable evaluation and comparison of forecasting algorithms across diverse datasets, sensor types, and vehicles. This is motivated by the desire to improve the transferability and robustness of models trained on one platform to another, an important step towards real-world deployability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology reformulates point cloud forecasting into 4D occupancy forecasting, employing a differentiable rendering pipeline for self-supervised learning.

**Input and Task Formulation:**
Traditional point cloud forecasting methods (denoted `g`) take a historical sequence of point clouds ($X_{-T:0}$) as input and directly predict future point clouds ($\hat{X}_{1:T}$). In contrast, the proposed approach (denoted `f`) takes a ray defined by its origin ($o_t$) and direction ($d$) from a future timestamp `t`, along with historical point clouds ($X_{-T:0}$) and sensor locations ($o_{-T:0}$), to predict the distance ($\hat{\lambda}$) the ray would travel. This effectively estimates depth for future rays, akin to view synthesis in implicit neural representations.

**Spacetime (4D) Occupancy Representation:**
The core of the method is the prediction of spacetime (4D) occupancy, defined as the occupied state of a 3D location at a particular time instance. A bounded spatial-temporal 4D volume, $V$, is discretized into spacetime voxels $v = (x, y, z, t)$. The true occupancy $z[v]$ for each voxel can be binary (0 for free, 1 for occupied).

An occupancy prediction network `h` (parameterized by `w`) is trained to predict a continuous occupancy score $\hat{z}[v] \in$ for each voxel, given historical point clouds and sensor locations:
$\hat{z} = h(X_{-T:0}, o_{-T:0}; w)$.

**Network Architecture (from Appendix):**
The architecture is based on an encoder-decoder structure, extending a prior work for BEV occupancy forecasting. To handle the 4D voxel grid ($X \times Y \times Z \times T$), it is reshaped into a 3D voxel grid ($X \times Y \times ZT$). This allows the application of 2D convolutions, where each channel in the input represents a slice of the world through the height (Z) and time (T) dimensions.

**Depth Rendering from Occupancy (Differentiable Renderer):**
To train the occupancy network, a differentiable renderer is employed to convert the predicted 4D occupancy grid into predicted depths for specific rays. Given a ray query ($o$, $d$):
1. **Voxel Traversal**: The ray's intersection with the occupancy grid is computed by traversing the voxels it passes through ($v_1, \dots, v_n$).
2. **Probability of Stopping**: The occupancy score $\hat{z}[v_i]$ of a voxel $v_i$ is interpreted as the conditional probability that a ray, having traversed previous voxels $v_1, \dots, v_{i-1}$ without stopping, will stop in $v_i$. The probability $p_i$ that a ray stops in voxel $v_i$ is computed as:
 $p_i = \left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \hat{z}[v_j])\right) \hat{z}[v_i]$.
3. **Expected Stopping Distance**: The predicted depth $\hat{\lambda}$ is calculated as the expected stopping point:
 $\hat{\lambda} = f(o, d) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \hat{\lambda}_i$, where $\hat{\lambda}_i$ is the stopping distance at voxel $v_i$.
4. **Handling Rays Exiting Volume**: If a ray does not terminate within the defined voxel grid, a virtual stopping point is introduced at the ground-truth location. The expected depth calculation then includes a term for the probability of the ray stopping outside the grid:
 $\hat{\lambda} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \hat{\lambda}_i + \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n} (1 - p_i)\right) \hat{\lambda}_{n+1}$, where $\hat{\lambda}_{n+1}$ is the ground-truth depth.

**Loss Function:**
The occupancy prediction network is trained using a simple L1 loss between the rendered depth $\hat{\lambda}$ and the ground-truth depth $\lambda$ for future rays derived from LiDAR point clouds:
$L(w) = \sum_{(o,\lambda,d) \in (X_{1:T}, o_{1:T})} |\lambda - f(o, d; X_{-T:0}, o_{-T:0}, w)|$.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The paper introduces novel evaluation metrics to assess forecasted scene geometry, addressing limitations of traditional point cloud metrics like Chamfer distance:
1. **Ray-based Error ($\epsilon$)**: For a query ray from origin $O$ to ground-truth endpoint $Q$ (distance $\lambda$) and a predicted endpoint $P$ (distance $\hat{\lambda}$), the error is defined as the absolute difference in ray length: $\epsilon = |\lambda - \hat{\lambda}|$.
2. **Near-field Error ($\epsilon_V$)**: To focus on regions relevant for autonomous vehicle planning, rays are "clamped" to a fixed bounding volume $V$ (operation $\phi_V$). The error is then the absolute difference between the clamped ground-truth and predicted ray lengths: $\epsilon_V = |\phi_V(OQ) - \phi_V(OP)|$.
3. **Relative Near-field Error ($\epsilon_{rel_V}$)**: This normalizes the near-field error by the ground-truth ray length: $\epsilon_{rel_V} = \epsilon_V / |OQ|$.
For point cloud forecasting algorithms, a surface is fitted to their predicted point clouds (via interpolation of depth among spherical projections) to enable querying with ground-truth rays and calculating these metrics.
Traditional **Chamfer distance** (vanilla $d$ and near-field $d_V$) are also reported for comparison.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on nuScenes, KITTI-Odometry, and ArgoVerse2.0 datasets. A bounded 4D volume is defined around the ego-vehicle. Forecasting horizons of 1 second and 3 seconds are evaluated, with specified input and output frame rates. Baselines include an aggregation-based ray-tracing method, SPFNet, S2Net, and ST3DCNN. Architectural ablations are performed with static (single voxel grid for all future times) and residual (static + residual grids for each timestep) variants.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across qualitative assessments, quantitative evaluations, and architectural ablations:

1. **Qualitative Superiority in Forecasted Geometry**: Qualitative comparisons demonstrate that the proposed 4D occupancy forecasting approach generates significantly clearer and more geometrically accurate point cloud forecasts compared to state-of-the-art (SOTA) point cloud forecasting methods (S2Net, SPFNet, ST3DCNN). The method produces crisper representations of scene geometry and exhibits the ability to "spacetime-complete" scenes by inferring future motion of dynamic objects and occluded parts of the static world. Interestingly, a simple aggregation-based ray-tracing baseline often produced qualitatively better results than existing SOTA point cloud forecasting methods, yet was still outperformed by the proposed method.

2. **Quantitative Improvement with Ray-Based Metrics**: On the nuScenes dataset, the 4D occupancy forecasting method substantially outperforms all baselines, including SOTA point cloud forecasters and the ray-tracing baseline, on the proposed L1 and absolute relative depth error metrics. For both 1-second and 3-second forecasting horizons, these errors are reduced by more than half compared to SOTA. This indicates a more precise prediction of future scene geometry as perceived by virtual rays, aligning with the qualitative observations.

3. **Performance on Chamfer Distance**: When evaluated using Chamfer distance metrics, the proposed approach shows strong performance, particularly in terms of near-field Chamfer distance. For vanilla Chamfer distance, some SOTA methods like S2Net occasionally outperform the proposed method, which the authors attribute to their method's specific optimization for capturing occupancy evolution within a predefined bounded volume, rather than global point distribution.

4. **Cross-Sensor Generalization (Zero-Shot Transfer)**: A notable finding is the demonstration of cross-sensor generalization, attributed to the disentanglement of sensor intrinsics/extrinsics from scene motion.
 - When trained on ArgoVerse2.0 (a different sensor platform) and tested on KITTI-Odometry, the proposed method significantly outperforms ST3DCNN (SOTA for KITTI-Odometry), even in a zero-shot transfer setting.
 - With limited target data access (e.g., 20% of KITTI-Odometry for fine-tuning after pre-training on ArgoVerse2.0), the method still shows strong performance, surpassing alternatives. This represents the first reported results illustrating generalization across different sensor rigs.

5. **Architectural Ablations Insights**:
 - **Static Variant**: A variant that predicts a single static voxel grid for all future timesteps proved to be a powerful baseline for short-horizon (1-second) forecasting. It outperformed the proposed dynamic variant for 1s forecasting and, by extension, the SOTA point cloud methods. This is explained by the fact that the majority of the world remains static, and a dense static map is highly effective for immediate future prediction. It also showed an ability to "hallucinate" occluded static regions, unlike the ray-tracing baseline.
 - **Dynamic Variant (Proposed)**: This variant, which predicts one voxel grid per future timestep, performed best at long-horizon (3-second) forecasting, indicating its capacity to capture dynamic changes over longer periods.
 - **Residual Variant**: This variant, attempting to predict a static grid plus residual dynamic grids, did not consistently outperform the dynamic variant, suggesting challenges in effectively regularizing the decomposition of static and dynamic elements.

6. **Foreground vs. Background Performance**: Analysis separating foreground (dynamic objects) and background (static environment) query rays revealed that all architectural variants performed less effectively on foreground objects compared to background. This is attributed to the predominant presence of background regions in the training data (over 90% of rays and voxels), leading to an implicit downweighting of foreground objects during training. Despite this, the dynamic variant showed better performance on background objects over longer horizons, while the residual variant showed some promise on foreground objects. For short horizons, the non-learnable ray tracing baseline was competitive or superior for some foreground object types.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a conceptual shift in how scene forecasting for autonomous systems can be approached, with several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Refined Problem Formulation for Autonomous Perception**: By recasting point cloud forecasting as 4D occupancy forecasting and explicitly factoring out sensor extrinsics and intrinsics, the work proposes a more principled and physically grounded problem formulation. This shift encourages algorithms to focus solely on the true dynamics of the environment rather than confounding them with sensor characteristics, which are often known or predictable by the ego-vehicle. This can lead to more robust and generalizable perception models.

2. **Generic Intermediate Representation**: The proposed spacetime (4D) occupancy grid serves as a generic, sensor-agnostic intermediate representation of the world. Such a representation is highly valuable for various downstream tasks in autonomous driving, including motion planning, collision avoidance, and scenario understanding, as it provides a dense, volumetric understanding of the environment's state over time, rather than sparse sensor observations.

3. **Enhanced Scalability through Self-Supervision**: The method maintains a self-supervised learning paradigm, utilizing unannotated LiDAR sequences. This addresses a critical limitation of traditional, annotation-heavy approaches, enabling scalability to vast datasets and reducing the enormous costs associated with human labeling, thus accelerating research and development in autonomous driving.

4. **Improved Forecasting Accuracy and Geometric Fidelity**: The quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate that the proposed method significantly outperforms existing point cloud forecasting SOTA in terms of predicting future scene geometry. The generation of crisper, more representative point clouds and the ability to "spacetime-complete" occluded regions indicate a higher fidelity understanding of the dynamic world. This improved accuracy in forecasting future geometry can directly translate to safer and more efficient motion planning for autonomous vehicles.

5. **Pioneering Cross-Sensor Generalization**: The research presents the first known results on zero-shot cross-sensor generalization in LiDAR forecasting. The ability to train a model on one sensor platform (e.g., ArgoVerse2.0) and deploy it effectively on another (e.g., KITTI-Odometry) without re-training or extensive fine-tuning is a significant breakthrough. This capability is crucial for the practical deployment of autonomous fleets, allowing for easier integration of new sensor types, platform upgrades, and mixed fleets, thereby reducing development cycles and costs.

6. **Novel Evaluation Paradigm**: The introduction of ray-based evaluation metrics provides a more intuitive and geometrically relevant way to assess the quality of forecasted scene geometry. Unlike traditional point cloud metrics that can be sensitive to point density or distribution artifacts, these new metrics focus on the accuracy of depth prediction along specific rays, offering a clearer picture of how well the algorithm understands the underlying physical environment. This can guide future research towards more meaningful performance objectives.

7. **Enabling Advanced Applications**:
 - **Novel Intrinsic-View Synthesis**: The learned occupancy grid can be queried to simulate point clouds from different LiDAR sensor types (e.g., varying beam patterns). This capability is highly impactful for sensor domain transfer research and for developing virtual testing environments that can realistically simulate various sensor configurations.
 - **Novel Extrinsic-View Synthesis**: The method allows for rendering dense depth maps from novel ego-vehicle viewpoints or trajectories in the future. This provides a dense, 360-degree understanding of future scene depth that is not obtainable from sparse LiDAR data alone, potentially enhancing planning algorithms that require more complete spatial awareness.

In summary, this work represents a fundamental step towards more robust, scalable, and generalizable scene forecasting for autonomous systems by advocating for a generic, sensor-independent 4D occupancy representation and demonstrating its practical advantages through a self-supervised, differentiable rendering framework.
