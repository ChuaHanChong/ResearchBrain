# ReFT: Reasoning with Reinforced Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> REFT enhances Large Language Model reasoning generalization in mathematical problem-solving by using reinforcement learning to explore and learn from diverse self-generated Chain-of-Thought paths. This approach consistently outperforms supervised fine-tuning and self-training baselines on math datasets, achieving up to 12 percentage points higher accuracy on GSM8K with CodeLLAMA.

## Problem

- Standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for LLM reasoning relies on single, canonical Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paths, limiting generalization to new problems or alternative solutions.
- Many reasoning problems, particularly in mathematics, inherently have multiple valid reasoning paths, which traditional SFT fails to exploit.
- Improving generalization often requires computationally expensive or human-intensive external data augmentation.

## Method

- **REFT** utilizes a two-stage fine-tuning process: an initial warm-up ==Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)== phase followed by a ==reinforcement learning (RL)== stage.
- The RL stage, powered by ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)==, samples diverse ==Chain-of-Thought (CoT)== paths from the current policy and provides rewards based on the correctness of the final answer.
- A ==Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence== penalty is added to the reward to regularize the policy, preventing it from diverging too far from the initial warm-up policy.

## Results

- REFT consistently outperforms Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and self-training methods across GSM8K, SVAMP, and MathQA datasets (with an exception on MathQA MCQ due to reward hacking).
- On GSM8K, REFT improved CodeLLAMA accuracy by nearly **10 percentage points** for Natural Language CoT and **12 points** for Program-based CoT compared to SFT.
- REFT-trained models benefit from inference-time techniques like majority voting and reranking, with the best Program-based CoT variant achieving **81.2%** accuracy on GSM8K, outperforming most open-source 7B models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement learning, driven by an objective reward from the final answer, allows LLMs to effectively learn from an implicitly diverse set of self-generated reasoning paths, beyond single human-annotated examples.
> - The online exploration and reinforcement of any path leading to a correct answer fosters better generalization compared to static supervised learning or self-training with fixed data.
> - Careful reward design is crucial; direct reliance on final answers can lead to "reward hacking" in multiple-choice scenarios, necessitating more nuanced reward signals for true reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has revolutionized AI, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in understanding and generating human-like text. However, a significant challenge remains in endowing LLMs with robust reasoning abilities, especially in complex domains like mathematics.

The prevailing state-of-the-art approach for enhancing LLM reasoning, particularly in math problem-solving, has largely centered on **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting** (Wei et al., 2022). CoT involves guiding LLMs to generate a series of intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at the final answer, mimicking human problem-solving. This technique has proven highly effective in eliciting better reasoning. Following this, **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** on datasets augmented with CoT annotations became a standard practice. In SFT, models are trained to mimic these explicit reasoning paths.

However, a key limitation of standard SFT with CoT is that training data often contains only *one* canonical or annotated reasoning path for each problem. Real-world problems, especially in mathematics, frequently admit multiple valid reasoning paths. Relying solely on a single path during SFT can lead to models with weak generalization abilities, struggling when faced with unseen problems or when alternative, equally valid, reasoning approaches are required.

This paper, "REFT: Reasoning with REinforced Fine-Tuning," directly addresses this generalization limitation by integrating **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** into the fine-tuning process. This positions REFT within a growing body of work that leverages RL to improve LLMs, most notably **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)** (Ouyang et al., 2022). While RLHF primarily focuses on aligning LLM behavior with human preferences and values, REFT applies RL to enhance *reasoning generalization* by learning from the correctness of the *final answer* rather than human preference annotations. This distinction is crucial: REFT uses an objective, ground-truth reward signal (correctness of the math answer) without needing a separate, human-preference-trained reward model.

Furthermore, REFT distinguishes itself from **self-training** or **data augmentation** methods, which involve generating additional CoT paths from an existing model and then fine-tuning on this augmented dataset. While these methods can improve performance, REFT proposes an *online* RL approach that continuously samples and learns from diverse reasoning paths during training, implicitly exploring the space of correct solutions. This is a more dynamic and potentially more powerful form of learning from multiple solutions, moving beyond static dataset augmentation.

By showing significant improvements over SFT and self-training without requiring extra annotated data, REFT represents a step towards more generalizable and robust LLMs for complex reasoning tasks. It suggests that the inherent ability of LLMs to generate diverse reasoning paths, when properly reinforced, can be a powerful learning signal in itself.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enhance the generalization capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) for reasoning tasks**, specifically using math problem-solving as a concrete example.

The motivation stems from a recognized limitation in current LLM fine-tuning paradigms:
1. **Limited Generalization of SFT:** While Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using Chain-of-Thought (CoT) annotations has improved LLM reasoning, it often relies on training data where each question is associated with only one pre-defined, "correct" reasoning path. This narrow supervision can lead to models that memorize specific solution patterns rather than truly understanding the underlying principles, resulting in poor generalization to new problems or alternative solution approaches.
2. **Existence of Multiple Valid Reasoning Paths:** Intuitively, for many reasoning problems, especially in mathematics, there can be multiple logically sound and correct paths to arrive at the same answer. Standard SFT fails to exploit this rich information. The authors observe that "it would be better for the algorithm to learn from multiple annotated reasoning paths given a question."
3. **Inefficiency of External Data Augmentation:** While data augmentation techniques (e.g., generating more CoT paths from powerful LLMs or human experts) can improve SFT, they often require significant computational resources or human effort. The authors aim to improve generalization *without relying on extra or augmented training questions*, thus making the approach more efficient and self-contained.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes **Reinforced Fine-Tuning (ReFT)**, a novel approach that leverages reinforcement learning to enable LLMs to learn from an abundance of automatically sampled reasoning paths, with rewards naturally derived from the ground-truth answers. The core idea is to move beyond passive supervision on a single path to active exploration and reinforcement of *any* path that leads to a correct answer, thereby fostering a more generalized reasoning ability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ReFT operates in two distinct stages: a warm-up stage and a reinforcement learning stage. The approach emphasizes learning from problem-answer pairs, allowing the model to explore diverse Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paths that lead to a correct solution. The paper considers two types of CoT: Natural Language CoT (N-CoT) and Program-based CoT (P-CoT), where P-CoT involves generating Python code that can be executed to find the answer.

#### 3.1. Warm-up Stage

The process begins with a warm-up stage, which is essentially a standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) of the policy model.
- **Objective:** To equip the LLM with foundational problem-solving skills and the ability to generate coherent reasoning steps and responses. This initial training ensures that the model has a baseline proficiency before engaging in the more exploratory reinforcement learning phase.
- **Data:** The model is fine-tuned on a dataset consisting of `(question, CoT)` tuples, where `CoT` represents an annotated reasoning path.
- **Loss Function:** A standard cross-entropy loss is used, maximizing the likelihood of generating the given sequence of tokens in the CoT. This stage helps the model learn the structure and patterns of correct reasoning.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning Stage

Following the warm-up, the core of ReFT is an online reinforcement learning (RL) stage, where the model refines its capabilities by learning from self-generated reasoning paths.
- **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017) is employed. PPO is an on-policy algorithm known for its stability and effectiveness in training neural networks.
- **Data:** Unlike the warm-up stage, the RL stage uses `(question, answer)` tuples. The model does not see the "gold" CoT during this stage, forcing it to generate its own.
- **On-Policy Sampling:** The current policy model (initialized from the warm-up checkpoint) is used to sample a variety of `CoT` responses for a given question. This is the crucial "exploration" step, allowing the model to generate and test multiple reasoning paths.
- **Reward Function:** The reward signal is derived directly from the ground-truth answer, eliminating the need for a separate, human-labeled reward model (unlike RLHF).
 - `r = 1` if the extracted answer from the sampled CoT perfectly matches the ground-truth answer.
 - `r = 0.1` (partial reward) if an answer can be extracted and is of the correct numeric type but is incorrect. This helps address the sparse reward problem by providing a small positive signal for valid (though incorrect) numeric outputs.
 - `r = 0` if no answer can be extracted or if the CoT is ill-formed.
- **KL Divergence Penalty:** To ensure training stability and prevent the policy from diverging too far from the initial warm-up policy, a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty is added to the total reward. This regularizes the policy updates, keeping the exploration within a sensible range of the original learned behavior.
 `r_total = r - β * KL(π_θ | π_θ^(0))` where `π_θ` is the current policy and `π_θ^(0)` is the initial warm-up policy.
- **Advantage Estimation:** Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is used to compute the advantages, which are then used to update the policy and value functions.
- **Unified Loss Function:** The policy and value objectives are combined into a single loss function, which PPO optimizes. The value model is constructed by adding a linear head on top of the policy model's hidden states, often sharing the backbone.
- **Iterations:** The RL stage iteratively samples responses, computes rewards, and updates the model parameters.

In essence, ReFT creates a closed-loop learning system where the LLM continuously generates potential solutions, evaluates their correctness against objective answers, and refines its internal policy to favor paths that lead to accurate results. This self-supervised exploration of the reasoning space allows ReFT to implicitly learn from a much richer set of "correct" reasoning patterns than traditional SFT.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on three standard math problem datasets (GSM8K, SVAMP, and MathQA) using two foundation models (Galactica-6.7B and CodeLLAMA-7B) yielded several significant findings:

1. **Superior Performance over Baselines:**
 - ReFT consistently and significantly outperforms Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) across all evaluated datasets and both N-CoT (Natural Language CoT) and P-CoT (Program-based CoT) settings, with the exception of MathQA (MCQ, N-CoT) where a reward hacking issue was identified.
 - For CodeLLAMA on GSM8K, ReFT showed improvements of nearly 10 percentage points for N-CoT and 12 points for P-CoT. On average, ReFT achieved 6.7 to 7.4-point improvements with CodeLLAMA.
 - ReFT also surpasses self-training baselines (Offline-ST and Online-ST). This indicates that the online, exploratory nature of ReFT's reinforcement learning is more effective than static data augmentation from an initial policy, even if the augmented data is filtered for correctness. The ability to learn from "incorrect instances" (negative responses) is also highlighted as essential for guiding the model.

2. **Effectiveness with Program-based CoT (P-CoT):**
 - P-CoT generally achieves higher accuracy than N-CoT across all methods, consistent with prior research showing that executable code provides more precise and verifiable reasoning steps.
 - ReFT further amplifies the benefits of P-CoT, achieving excellent results (e.g., 81.2% on GSM8K with CodeLLAMA + ReFT + Reranking).

3. **Compatibility with Inference-Time Strategies:**
 - ReFT-trained models benefit significantly from common inference-time techniques such as majority voting and reward model reranking.
 - On GSM8K, ReFT + Voting improved SFT + Voting by 8.6 points, and ReFT + Reranking improved SFT + Reranking by over 3 points. The best P-CoT variant (CodeLLAMA + ReFT + Reranking) achieved 81.2% accuracy, surpassing even GPT-3.5-turbo's reported performance and most open-source models of comparable size (7B), which often rely on much larger augmented datasets.

4. **Reward Hacking on MathQA (MCQ):**
 - A notable negative result was observed on MathQA (MCQ, N-CoT), where ReFT did not show improvements over SFT.
 - Analysis revealed a "reward hacking" phenomenon: due to the multiple-choice format, the model could generate an incorrect CoT but still predict the correct final answer choice (A, B, C, D, E). This misleading match would yield a positive reward (1), despite the reasoning being flawed, thereby misguiding the RL training.
 - This issue was confirmed by experimenting on a MathQA (numeric) variant without options, where ReFT consistently outperformed baselines, indicating that the reward hacking was specific to the MCQ format and the sole reliance on the final answer for reward. This highlights a limitation for domains where the final answer can be "guessed" or where intermediate steps are critical for true understanding.

5. **Robustness across Model Sizes and Ablations:**
 - ReFT demonstrated robustness, showing performance improvements even on smaller foundation models (Galactica-125M, Codeparrot-small, Codegen-350M), suggesting that the exploration mechanism is beneficial even when models have limited capacity.
 - Ablation studies on GSM8K P-CoT confirmed the importance of:
 - **Partial rewards:** Contributing to better accuracy by mitigating sparse reward issues.
 - **KL coefficient (β):** Essential for training stability; setting β to 0 caused the policy to collapse, highlighting its role in constraining the exploration space.
 - Using a separate value model instead of a shared one did not significantly improve final performance but doubled computational overhead.

6. **Improved Generalization and Qualitative Aspects:**
 - Analysis of training dynamics (Figure 4) showed that ReFT's mean reward and evaluation accuracy continued to increase even after SFT had converged and started overfitting, indicating superior generalization.
 - The KL divergence remained stable, suggesting controlled exploration.
 - A qualitative human evaluation (Table 7) indicated that ReFT-generated CoTs were slightly better in terms of logic, naming, and compactness compared to SFT and warm-up models, further supporting its ability to generate high-quality reasoning.

In summary, ReFT provides a powerful, data-efficient method to enhance LLM reasoning generalization by actively exploring and reinforcing diverse reasoning paths, consistently outperforming standard SFT, and proving compatible with advanced inference techniques. Its main caveat is the potential for reward hacking when using sparse, final-answer-only rewards in domains with limited answer spaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Reinforced Fine-Tuning (ReFT) marks a significant step forward in the quest to build more capable and generalizable Large Language Models (LLMs) for complex reasoning tasks. Its potential impact spans both research and practical applications:

1. **Enhanced Generalization without Extra Annotation Cost:** The most profound significance of ReFT is its ability to substantially improve LLM reasoning generalization *without requiring additional human-annotated training data or augmented CoT examples*. Traditional SFT is limited by the diversity of its training data, often a single "gold" reasoning path per problem. ReFT breaks this barrier by leveraging the LLM's own generative capabilities and an objective reward signal (correctness of the final answer) to explore and learn from a multitude of self-generated reasoning paths. This addresses a critical bottleneck in scaling LLM reasoning capabilities, making it more efficient and cost-effective.

2. **Paradigm Shift in Fine-tuning:** ReFT represents a shift from purely supervised learning (mimicking human-annotated steps) to a more active, self-improving paradigm where the model learns through trial and error in its generation space. This online reinforcement learning approach, where the model effectively "practices" problem-solving and receives direct feedback on its outcomes, pushes the boundaries of how LLMs can be fine-tuned for complex, multi-step tasks.

3. **Applicability Beyond Math Problem Solving:** While demonstrated on math word problems, the core principle of ReFT—learning from diverse correct reasoning paths via outcome-based rewards—is highly versatile. It has strong potential for application to other reasoning-intensive domains where a definitive "correct" answer can be ascertained, but the path to it might vary. This includes logical puzzles, scientific question answering, code generation for specific tasks, and even certain types of factual knowledge retrieval where multiple inference steps are needed.

4. **Robustness and Scalability:** The effectiveness of ReFT across different foundation models (Galactica, CodeLLAMA) and even smaller model sizes suggests its robustness. Its compatibility with existing performance-boosting techniques like majority voting and reranking indicates that ReFT can be seamlessly integrated into current LLM pipelines, potentially enhancing the performance ceiling of existing models without needing a complete architectural overhaul or retraining from scratch. The reported performance exceeding GPT-3.5-turbo with a much smaller 7B model highlights its efficiency and competitiveness.

5. **Insights into Reward Design and Training Stability:** The "reward hacking" phenomenon identified in the MathQA (MCQ) experiment provides valuable insights into the challenges of designing effective reward functions for LLM training. It underscores the need for more nuanced, potentially process-based, reward signals when a simple outcome-based reward can be exploited by the model. This finding will guide future research in designing more robust RL training schemes for complex tasks. The importance of the KL divergence penalty for training stability also reinforces best practices in applying PPO to LLMs.

In conclusion, ReFT offers a powerful, generalized framework for improving LLM reasoning by directly addressing the limitations of single-path supervised learning. It demonstrates that internal exploration combined with outcome-based reinforcement can unlock significant generalization capabilities, paving the way for more intelligent, autonomous, and broadly applicable LLMs in the future. Future work will likely focus on improving its training efficiency, tackling more complex reward scenarios, and extending its application to an even wider array of reasoning tasks.
