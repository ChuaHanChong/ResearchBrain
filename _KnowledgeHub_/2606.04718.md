# CoRe-MoE: Contrastive Reweighted Mixture of Experts for Multi-Terrain Humanoid Locomotion with Gait Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> CoRe-MoE, developed by researchers at Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) and collaborators, presents a unified framework enabling humanoid robots to achieve smooth transitions between walking and running gaits while robustly adapting to diverse terrains. The method attains high success rates across varied terrains (e.g., 94.3% on Up Stairs) and demonstrates successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, including high-speed running up to 2.5 m/s.

## Problem

- Unifying automatic gait transition (walking to running) and multi-terrain adaptation within a single policy is challenging due to gradient interference and distribution shifts.
- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures often fail to achieve clear expert specialization, resulting in diffuse activation patterns and limited performance for complex, varied conditions.
- Existing multi-gait or multi-terrain methods frequently rely on manual mode switching or separate policies, leading to discontinuous and unnatural robot movements.

## Method

- A two-stage ==reinforcement learning== pipeline is employed: Stage 1 learns stable walking/running gaits and smooth transitions on flat terrain, while Stage 2 builds upon this foundation for multi-terrain adaptation.
- An asymmetric actor-critic MoE architecture utilizes command velocity to drive gait selection in Stage 1 and incorporates a separate terrain-aware MoE in Stage 2 to provide fine-grained action adjustments.
- A SwAV-style ==contrastive learning== objective is introduced in Stage 2 to explicitly promote expert specialization within the MoE, guiding the gating network to better discriminate between different terrain features.

## Results

- CoRe-MoE achieved superior velocity tracking (0.8527) and success rates (**99.13%**) for adaptive gait locomotion on flat terrain compared to various baselines, exhibiting natural walking and running gaits with smooth transitions.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the contrastive learning objective (SwAV) was critical for expert specialization, improving success rates (e.g., **94.3%** on Up Stairs for CoRe-MoE vs. **84.6%** without SwAV) and accelerating convergence.
- The policy demonstrated robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, successfully executing continuous gait transitions up to 2.5 m/s and adapting to diverse real-world terrains and external disturbances.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the learning of fundamental gaits from terrain adaptation through a two-stage training framework significantly improves policy stability and performance.
> - Contrastive learning effectively guides MoE gating networks to form distinct, specialized experts by enhancing the discriminability of terrain features, leading to more precise and context-aware actions.
> - Fusing terrain-specific action adjustments from the second-stage MoE with a foundational gait policy from the first stage allows for robust adaptation to new terrains while preserving natural and stable base locomotion styles.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are increasingly being developed for deployment in complex, real-world environments, necessitating advanced locomotion capabilities. Historically, humanoid robot control relied on traditional model-based controllers, which often struggled with unknown dynamics and unstructured environments. The advent of reinforcement learning (RL) has significantly advanced humanoid locomotion, enabling robots to learn stable walking and running behaviors from proprioceptive feedback and maintain robustness on moderately challenging terrains.

However, several challenges persist in the broader research landscape. Pure proprioception-based methods, while robust to moderate disturbances, are reactive and cannot proactively anticipate hazards posed by complex terrains such as high steps or deep gaps. Integrating exteroceptive perception, such as LiDAR or depth cameras, has been explored to enable proactive adaptation. While LiDAR provides height information, its limitations in field-of-view, update frequency, and motion distortion can restrict effectiveness in dynamic scenarios. Depth cameras offer high-frequency perception suitable for end-to-end control.

A significant hurdle in developing versatile humanoid locomotion is the simultaneous achievement of smooth gait transitions (e.g., between walking and running) and robust adaptation to diverse terrains (e.g., stairs, slopes, obstacles) within a single, unified policy. Previous works often involve policies that rely on manually triggered gait switches or have limited generalization across fundamentally different terrain types. Furthermore, directly training a single policy for both gait switching and multi-terrain adaptation often leads to gradient conflicts and challenges due to the distribution shift introduced by terrain variations.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have emerged as a promising approach for multi-skill learning and multi-terrain adaptation by segmenting the policy into specialized experts. However, a common problem in direct MoE training is the lack of clear expert specialization, where activation patterns remain dispersed and overlapping, thereby limiting the policy's ability to fully leverage the MoE structure for precise control and adaptability.

This work, CoRe-MoE, positions itself within this landscape by addressing these specific challenges. It aims to unify gait transition and multi-terrain adaptation within a single policy, drawing inspiration from human locomotion which primarily utilizes walking and running. The research specifically tackles the issues of gradient interference and distribution shifts by proposing a two-stage training framework. Critically, it seeks to enhance MoE expert specialization through a novel contrastive learning objective, thereby improving the policy's ability to generate distinct, terrain-aware actions and achieve smooth, natural locomotion across varied terrains without manual intervention.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and adaptive control framework for humanoid robots that enables smooth and natural transitions between fundamental gaits (walking and running) while maintaining stable and adaptable locomotion across complex and varied terrains, all within a single, unified policy.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and observations:

1. **Humanoid Locomotion in Real-World Environments:** Humanoid robots are increasingly expected to operate in human-centered environments, which inherently involve abrupt terrain changes like stairs, slopes, and obstacles. To perform effectively, robots must not only maintain basic stability but also proactively adjust their motion in response to upcoming terrain features.
2. **Efficiency of Human Gaits:** Humans predominantly rely on walking and running to navigate diverse terrains, rarely employing numerous specialized gaits. This observation suggests that robotic control strategies should focus on mastering these two core gaits and enabling seamless transitions between them, rather than learning an extensive library of artificial gait types which may offer limited practical adaptability.
3. **Limitations of Existing RL Approaches:**
 - **"Blind Locomotion":** Policies based solely on proprioception are reactive and cannot anticipate hazards, leading to instability or falls on critical terrains.
 - **Gradient Interference:** Jointly achieving both gait switching and multi-terrain adaptation within a single policy often results in gradient conflicts during training.
 - **Distribution Shift:** The wide range of visual and dynamic variations introduced by complex terrains further exacerbates learning difficulty.
 - **Manual Gait Switching:** Many existing multi-gait or multi-terrain locomotion policies require manual triggers to switch between different gaits or motion modes, leading to abrupt transitions, reduced continuity, and unnatural motion, especially under varying terrain conditions.
4. **Challenges with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE):** While MoE architectures show promise for multi-skill learning, direct joint training often fails to achieve clear expert specialization. Experts' activation patterns can be dispersed and highly overlapping, preventing the policy from fully exploiting the advantages of MoE for precise, terrain-specific control.
5. **Need for Proactive Adaptation:** To overcome the reactive nature of blind locomotion, integrating exteroceptive perception (e.g., depth images) is crucial for proactive, terrain-aware motion adjustment.

To address these motivations, CoRe-MoE was designed to integrate adaptive gait policies, visual perception (depth images), and a novel contrastive learning approach for MoE. The core idea is to decouple gait generation from terrain adaptation through a two-stage training process, leveraging contrastive learning to ensure that MoE experts develop clear specializations for different terrain types. This aims to enable robust, natural, and continuous locomotion, mirroring human-like adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CoRe-MoE framework employs a two-stage reinforcement learning pipeline, designed to decouple gait generation from terrain adaptation and ensure stable training and coherent motion. The problem is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) and solved using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with an asymmetric actor-critic architecture. The actor receives non-privileged proprioceptive observations and depth images, while the critic receives additional privileged base linear velocity.

**A. Stage 1: Adaptive Gait Locomotion Policy**
The first stage focuses on learning stable and controllable fundamental gaits (walking and running) with smooth transitions on flat terrain.
- **Architecture:** A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture is used, incorporating historical proprioceptive observations (angular velocity, gravity direction, velocity command, joint angles, joint velocities, last action). Depth images are explicitly *not* used in this stage.
- **Motion Priors:** Two separate Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) networks are employed, one for walking and one for running. These are trained on motion capture data (AMASS and LAFAN sequences) to encourage realistic gaits. Walking trajectories are augmented with samples like stair climbing and obstacle crossing to enhance diversity.
- **Gating Network:** The MoE gating network receives the commanded velocity as input. It dynamically activates the walking AMP expert under low-speed commands (0-1 m/s) and the running AMP expert under high-speed commands (1-2.5 m/s), facilitating automatic gait switching.
- **Action Generation:** The final action is computed as a weighted sum of the expert outputs via softmax gating.
- **Reward Function:** The reward `r_t` combines `r_task` (for locomotion stability and velocity tracking), `r_amp` (from the activated AMP network to enforce realistic motions), and `r_reg` (a regularization term for smooth actions).
- **Output:** After 25,000 training iterations, the entire Stage 1 policy network is frozen, serving as a stable foundation for fundamental gait behaviors.

**B. Stage 2: Adaptive Multi-Terrain Policy**
The second stage builds upon the frozen Stage 1 policy to enhance multi-terrain adaptation while preserving the learned gait styles.
- **Architecture:** A separate MoE architecture is introduced for this stage. Its capacity is expanded to 8 experts. Each expert receives historical proprioceptive observations *and* historical depth image sequences.
- **Terrain-Aware Branch:** This MoE branch is specifically designed to generate terrain-aware adjustments (`Δa_t`). The depth image input allows for proactive adaptation to terrain geometry.
- **Contrastive Learning for Expert Specialization:** A SwAV-based contrastive learning objective is applied to the gating network.
 - **Objective:** To encourage the network to learn structured terrain representations and promote clear expert specialization.
 - **Mechanism:** It aligns the gating output embedding with the encoded representation of the historical depth image sequence. Positive pairs are formed by historical observations and their corresponding depth sequences from the same trajectory; otherwise, they are negative.
 - **SwAV Loss:** The method uses prototype matrices and computes source/target probabilities, applying the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm to avoid trivial solutions. The final SwAV contrastive loss minimizes the cross-entropy between these probability distributions, encouraging terrain-specific clustering of expert activations.
- **Action Fusion:** The Stage 2 policy generates residual terrain-aware adjustments (`Δa_t`). These are added to the actions `u_t` from the frozen Stage 1 policy.
 - **Blending Coefficients:** Different blending coefficients are applied to different body parts: `ϵ_upper = 0.1` for the upper body and `ϵ_lower = 0.5` for the lower body. This preserves the stable core gait while allowing precise, terrain-dependent foot placement and body posture adjustments. A sensitivity analysis validated these choices.
 - **Final Action:** `a_t = u_t + ϵ · Δa_t`.
- **Reward Function:** In this stage, the reward function retains only `r_task` and `r_reg`, focusing on terrain adaptation and smooth actions without the AMP component.
- **Training Details:** This stage is trained for 20,000 iterations. The contrastive learning module uses 16 prototypes and a temperature parameter of 0.1.

**C. Training and Generalization Enhancements**
- **Simulation Environment:** All policies are trained in NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab across 4096 parallel environments for efficient gradient estimation.
- **Reward Functions:** A comprehensive set of reward terms (Table II) is used, including standard locomotion objectives and safety considerations like preventing missteps and enforcing safe foothold margins.
- **Domain Randomization:** Moderate domain randomization (Table III) is applied to dynamics (mass, friction), depth perception (noise, artifacts), and command inputs to improve real-world generalization (sim-to-real transfer).
- **Curriculum Learning:** A 10-level curriculum strategy is used, where the environment difficulty (target velocity, terrain complexity) is gradually increased based on the achieved velocity reward, promoting structured learning of robust behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of CoRe-MoE was evaluated through extensive experiments in simulation and real-world deployment, addressing three key questions.

**A. Evaluation of the Adaptive Gait Locomotion Policy (Stage 1)**
- **Performance on Flat Terrain:** CoRe-MoE significantly outperformed baseline methods (Mixed AMP, Sel AMP, w/o MoE) in terms of Velocity Tracking Score (0.853 ± 0.004), Success Rate (99.13 ± 0.15%), and Average Traversal Ratio (99.85 ± 0.08%). The low standard deviations indicated consistent performance.
- **Naturalness of Gaits and Transitions:** While baselines exhibited unnatural behaviors (e.g., a shuffling gait for Mixed AMP, over-striding for Sel AMP), CoRe-MoE produced distinct, natural walking and running gaits with smooth transitions, attributed to its separate AMP experts and dynamic selection based on commanded velocity. This also resulted in more coherent arm swings.

**B. Evaluation of the Adaptive Multi-Terrain Policy (Stage 2)**
- **Ablation Studies on Complex Terrains:** A comprehensive ablation study (Table VI) across various complex terrains (slopes, stairs, obstacles) demonstrated the contribution of each component:
 - **`w/o MoE` and `Masked Expert`:** Showed severe performance degradation (e.g., 35.6% success on Up Stairs for w/o MoE, 2.1% for Masked Expert), highlighting the critical role of the MoE architecture and correct expert selection for multi-terrain adaptation.
 - **`w/o Fusion`:** Resulted in reduced stability and generalization, performing poorly on challenging terrains (e.g., 9.4% success on High Up Stairs), confirming the necessity of the action fusion mechanism for cross-terrain robustness.
 - **`w/o SwAV` and `SimCLR`:** Both led to lower success rates and slower convergence compared to CoRe-MoE. `SimCLR` performed better than `w/o SwAV` but still underperformed CoRe-MoE (e.g., 76.8% vs. 86.4% success on High Up Stairs for SimCLR and CoRe-MoE respectively), indicating that SwAV's structured semantic modeling was superior for expert specialization over instance-level contrastive learning.
- **Overall Multi-Terrain Performance:** CoRe-MoE consistently achieved the highest success rates (e.g., 98.7% on Up Slope, 94.3% on Up Stairs, 97.6% on High Obstacle) and traversal ratios across all evaluated complex terrains, demonstrating superior robustness and generalization. Failures were minimal and primarily observed in the most challenging scenarios (e.g., High Up Stairs, Random Obstacle) due to extreme terrain variations.
- **Effect of Contrastive Learning:** t-SNE visualizations of MoE gating outputs (Figure 6) confirmed that SwAV contrastive learning resulted in clearly separated clusters for different terrain types, while embeddings without SwAV showed significant overlap. This empirically validated that contrastive learning effectively improved the gating network's ability to discriminate terrain features and activate appropriate experts.

**C. Comparison with Prior Locomotion Methods**
- **Unified Policy and Efficiency:** CoRe-MoE was compared against Hiking in the Wild and MoRE (Table VII). Unlike these baselines, which rely on manual gait switching or specified motion modes, CoRe-MoE utilized a *unified policy with automatic gait transitions*.
- **Superior Performance:** CoRe-MoE achieved the best overall performance, with the highest Velocity Tracking Score (0.89 ± 0.01) and superior success rates across flat ground (99.9 ± 0.1%), slopes (98.8 ± 0.3%), stairs (91.1 ± 0.4%), and obstacles (93.5 ± 0.3%).
- **Energy Efficiency:** CoRe-MoE demonstrated the lowest Cost of Transport (CoT) at 0.61 ± 0.01, indicating superior energy efficiency. This was attributed to its automatic walk-run transition mechanism, which dynamically selected the most energy-efficient gait for the commanded velocity.

**D. Real-World Experimentation (Unitree G1 Robot)**
- **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The trained policy was directly deployed onto a Unitree G1 humanoid robot without any fine-tuning. The onboard Intel RealSense D435i depth camera provided real-time perception.
- **Robustness on Unstructured Terrains:** Despite significant discrepancies between simulation and real-world environments (geometry, surface materials, lighting), the robot demonstrated stable locomotion with accurate foothold placement across outdoor terrains, including uneven ground, natural steps, slopes, and obstacles.
- **Natural Gait Transitions:** The robot performed continuous transitions between walking, running, and returning to walking on flat ground (Figure 8), maintaining natural and coherent behavior without manual mode switching, contrasting with the abrupt transitions observed in methods like Hiking in the Wild (Figure 1).
- **Dynamic Stability:** The robot exhibited strong stability and recovery capability under external perturbations, demonstrated by maintaining balance and quickly recovering from a sudden impact while running at high speeds (Figure 9).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CoRe-MoE framework represents a notable advancement in humanoid robot locomotion, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Unified and Adaptive Locomotion Control:** This work addresses a long-standing challenge by successfully integrating smooth gait transitions (walking to running) and robust multi-terrain adaptation within a single, unified policy. This eliminates the need for manual mode switching or separate specialized controllers, simplifying the control architecture and enhancing autonomy.
2. **Enhanced Adaptability and Robustness:** The framework demonstrates superior performance across a wide variety of challenging terrains—including stairs, slopes, steps, obstacles, and unstructured outdoor environments—surpassing baseline methods in success rate, stability, and generalization. This robustness is critical for deploying humanoids in complex, dynamic real-world scenarios.
3. **Effective Mixture-of-Experts Utilization:** The proposed SwAV-based contrastive learning objective provides an effective mechanism to overcome a common limitation of MoE architectures, promoting clear expert specialization for different terrain conditions. This allows the policy to generate precise, terrain-aware adjustments rather than generic modifications, thereby maximizing the benefits of the MoE structure.
4. **Energy Efficiency through Automatic Gait Adaptation:** By automatically transitioning between walking and running based on commanded velocity, CoRe-MoE achieves a lower Cost of Transport compared to methods that rely on fixed gaits or manual switching. This improved energy efficiency is vital for increasing the operational endurance of humanoid robots.
5. **Successful Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The direct deployment of the policy on a physical Unitree G1 robot without any fine-tuning, using only an onboard depth camera, highlights the framework's practical applicability and its ability to generalize from simulation to the real world despite inherent discrepancies. This capability significantly reduces development time and resources for real-world robotic applications.
6. **Paving the Way for Advanced Humanoid Tasks:** The demonstrated natural gait transitions, dynamic stability under disturbances, and accurate foothold control lay a strong foundation for future research in more dynamic and human-like whole-body coordination tasks, such as manipulation while walking, or navigation in highly constrained and unpredictable environments. This work brings humanoid robots closer to mimicking the versatility and efficiency of human locomotion.
