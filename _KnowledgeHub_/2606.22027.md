# RARM: Confidence-Gated Progress Reward Modeling for RL in Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> RARM introduces a confidence-gated progress reward model that transforms a single successful demonstration into dense, stable feedback for reinforcement learning in robot manipulation tasks. This approach significantly enhances learning efficiency and robustness in complex, long-horizon scenarios across both simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for long-horizon, contact-rich robot manipulation tasks is challenging, as sparse rewards lead to inefficient exploration and hand-crafted dense rewards are labor-intensive and lack generalization.
- Existing learned reward models often require extensive task-specific data or produce "false positive" progress estimates, leading to unreliable feedback and potential exploitation by RL agents.
- A method was needed that offered both data-light reward generation and confidence-aware progress estimation to overcome these limitations.

## Method

- **RARM** employs a lightweight visual comparator, trained via self-supervised contrastive learning on general-purpose videos, to derive dense rewards from a single successful task demonstration.
- It introduces a ==confidence-gating mechanism==, calibrating per-clip thresholds from the reference demonstration, to suppress unreliable progress estimates and prevent agents from exploiting false positives.
- Rewards are granted only for confident, forward progress (1{pt>pt−1}1\{p_t > p_{t-1}\}1{pt>pt−1}), providing a stable and reliable signal for reinforcement learning in manipulation tasks.

## Results

- RARM demonstrated superior overall success rates across 9 simulated manipulation tasks, showing particular effectiveness in long-horizon scenarios compared to existing baselines.
- In real-world robotic experiments, RARM enabled DSRL to achieve up to a **4x** improvement in success rate on complex dual-arm tasks like Bimanual Clothes Folding with limited on-robot data, outperforming sparse reward baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the confidence-gating mechanism, cross-attention comparator, and temporal processing were crucial for reliable progress estimation and robust performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised contrastive learning on general-purpose videos can effectively train a visual comparator to understand temporal progress without task-specific labels.
> - Incorporating a confidence-gating mechanism, derived from the reference itself, is essential to filter unreliable progress estimates and mitigate reward hacking in complex manipulation scenarios.
> - Rewarding only confident changes in progress, localized along a single reference demonstration, creates a stable and dense signal that accommodates varying agent behaviors like pauses and recoveries.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated potential for training robotic manipulation policies, particularly for tasks requiring corrective and recovery behaviors beyond demonstrated trajectories. However, a significant bottleneck in applying RL to complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks is the design of effective reward functions.

Current approaches to reward design face several limitations:
- **Sparse Success Rewards:** These provide weak and delayed supervision, making exploration inefficient for long-horizon tasks.
- **Hand-crafted Dense Rewards:** While providing more frequent feedback, they are labor-intensive to design, task-specific, and generalize poorly across different tasks or robot embodiments.
- **Learned Reward Models:** These offer an alternative by automatically deriving dense feedback, often by estimating task progress.
 - **VLM-based Reward Models:** Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) incorporate broad semantic priors but are computationally expensive to query and can produce "false-positive" rewards. These false positives occur when a state appears visually plausible but is physically incorrect, leading the RL agent to exploit these misleading signals.
 - **Task-specific Progress Models:** Smaller models may reduce inference costs but typically require substantial per-task data, such as in-domain videos, success/failure annotations, or subtask labels. They can also struggle under distribution shifts.
 - **Uncertainty-Aware Methods:** Some methods, like VIPER, Diffusion Reward, and PROGRESSOR, attempt to model uncertainty in progress estimation to prevent overconfident rewards. However, these often still rely on task-specific or task-related data, limiting their transferability to novel tasks without additional data collection.

This research addresses a gap where existing methods rarely achieve both data-light and confidence-aware progress reward estimation simultaneously. The paper aims to develop a reward model that is lightweight, requires minimal task-specific data (ideally a single demonstration), and can reliably withhold reward when its progress estimate is uncertain, thereby mitigating the issue of false positives and reward hacking. This approach contributes to making RL more practical and scalable for real-world robot manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust and efficient reward modeling approach, named Reference-Anchored Reward Model (RARM), for reinforcement learning in robot manipulation tasks. The motivation stems from the inherent difficulties of reward design in RL, particularly for complex, long-horizon manipulation.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Overcoming Reward Design Bottlenecks:** Traditional reward design methods for RL in manipulation are problematic. Sparse success rewards provide insufficient feedback, hindering learning in tasks with many steps. Hand-crafted dense rewards are difficult, time-consuming, and brittle, failing to generalize across variations in tasks, objects, or environments. RARM aims to automate the generation of dense, informative rewards.
- **Achieving Data-Lightness:** Existing learned reward models often necessitate significant amounts of task-specific data, such as numerous demonstrations, explicit progress labels, or fine-grained subtask annotations. This requirement can be as demanding as the data needed for imitation learning. A key motivation for RARM is to minimize this data burden, specifically by deriving a dense reward signal from a *single successful demonstration* without requiring robot-specific data or per-task reward engineering.
- **Ensuring Confidence-Awareness:** A critical limitation of many progress-based reward models, especially those leveraging Vision-Language Models (VLMs), is their susceptibility to assigning high rewards to states that are visually plausible but physically incorrect. This can mislead the RL agent, causing it to "reward hack" and converge to suboptimal or failed policies. RARM is motivated to explicitly address this by incorporating a confidence mechanism that suppresses rewards when the progress estimate is unreliable, thus guiding the agent towards physically correct task completion.
- **Enhancing Transferability and Generalization:** The research aims to develop a reward model that is trained once on general-purpose videos with a self-supervised objective, rather than on task- or robot-specific datasets. This design choice is motivated by the need for a reward model that can readily transfer to new, unseen manipulation tasks and diverse robot embodiments without requiring retraining or extensive fine-tuning.
- **Providing a Stable and Exploitable Reward Signal:** Raw similarity scores between a rollout and a reference can be noisy and unstable, leading to reward hacking or training instability. The objective is to convert these similarities into a stable, monotonically increasing progress signal that rewards only *confident forward advancement*, rather than static high-similarity states or minor fluctuations.
- **Maintaining Low Computational Overhead:** While providing dense rewards, the model should be computationally efficient at deployment to allow for fast reward queries within the RL control loop, avoiding the high inference costs associated with large foundation models.

In summary, RARM's motivation is to provide a practical, data-efficient, and reliable solution for reward design in RL, enabling agents to learn complex manipulation tasks more effectively and generalize across varying conditions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Reference-Anchored Reward Model (RARM) is designed as a lightweight visual comparator that converts a single successful demonstration into a confidence-gated, dense progress reward. The methodology comprises three main stages: contrastive learning for reward model training, the model architecture, and the reward computation for policy learning.

#### 3.1. Contrastive Learning from Unlabeled Video (Reward Model Training)

RARM is trained once using self-supervised contrastive learning on general-purpose videos, requiring no task-specific robot data, action labels, or progress annotations. The core idea is to learn to compare short, fixed-length video clips (T=5 frames) based on their temporal evolution.

- **Data Source:** The training corpus consists of videos exhibiting "directed temporal change" (i.e., scenes that evolve rather than remaining static or reverting). Examples include standard video object segmentation benchmarks like DAVIS and MOSE, filtered to remove near-static sequences.
- **Clip Sampling:** For each video, an "anchor clip" is sampled from a contiguous anchor region.
 - **Positives:** Additional clips are randomly drawn from the *same* anchor region, ensuring they share the anchor’s local temporal context.
 - **Negatives:** These are drawn from three distinct sources to provide diverse contrast:
 1. Clips from *disjoint* temporal regions of the same video.
 2. *Time-reversed* clips from the anchor region, which preserve appearance but invert dynamics.
 3. Clips from *other* videos entirely.
- **Data Augmentation:** To promote robustness, random resized cropping and color jitter are applied independently to each clip (though consistently across frames within a single clip). All clips from the same video share the same random horizontal flip, maintaining temporal consistency while diversifying visual appearance.
- **Objective Function:** The model minimizes the soft-nearest-neighbors loss (L_SNN). This loss encourages the comparator to assign higher similarity scores to anchor-positive pairs and lower scores to anchor-negative pairs, scaled by a temperature parameter (τ=0.1).

#### 3.2. Model Architecture

The comparator, denoted g_θ(·, ·), is designed to produce a scalar clip similarity score. It comprises a clip encoder and a cross-attention comparator head.

- **Clip Encoder:**
 - **Frame Embedding:** Each frame within a short video clip is initially embedded using a pretrained DINOv3 model, augmented with lightweight LoRA adapters.
 - **Spatial Pooling:** The resulting patch-token features are spatially pooled to reduce dimensionality.
 - **Temporal Processing:** Temporal rotary position encodings are added to the pooled features to incorporate temporal order information. These are then processed by a lightweight self-attention layer that operates across frames, generating a temporally aware clip representation.
 - **Siamese Architecture:** The clip encoder is shared across both input clips during a comparison, forming a Siamese branch.
- **Cross-Attention Comparator and Head:**
 - **Directional Comparison:** Given two clip representations, X and Y, from the clip encoder, X tokens are used as queries, and Y tokens as keys and values in a cross-attention block. This design creates a directional comparison ("how does the rollout clip X compare to the reference clip Y?").
 - **Score Generation:** The output of the cross-attention block is projected to a lower dimension, and a lightweight head then maps these comparison tokens to the final scalar similarity score g_θ(X, Y). A higher score indicates stronger temporal compatibility.

#### 3.3. Reward Computation for Policy Learning (Deployment)

At deployment, RARM uses a single successful demonstration as a "progress anchor" to guide RL training.

- **Reference Preparation:** For a given task, a single reference demonstration video is processed into a sequence of overlapping clips {r_j}. Each reference clip r_j is assigned a monotonic progress value π_j ∈ based on its normalized temporal position within the demonstration. This requires no manual annotation.
- **Rollout-Reference Comparison:** During RL training, the agent's recent frames form a current rollout clip c_t. This c_t is compared against *all* reference clips {r_j} using the trained comparator to find the best-matching reference clip: j*(t) = argmax_j g_θ(c_t, r_j). This all-to-all comparison allows the policy to diverge in speed or execute corrective actions without needing strict temporal synchronization.
- **Confidence Gating:** A crucial component is the per-clip confidence threshold δ_j. This threshold is calibrated offline by analyzing the match-score statistics of the reference demonstration against itself (i.e., g_θ(r_j, r_k) for various j, k). If the score g_θ(c_t, r_j*(t)) is below this threshold, the match is deemed uncertain.
- **Progress Estimation (p_t):** The current estimated progress p_t is updated based on two conditions:
 1. The match score must exceed the confidence threshold: g_θ(c_t, r_j*(t)) ≥ δ_j*(t).
 2. The estimated progress from the best match must represent forward movement: π_j*(t) > p_{t-1}.
 If both conditions are met, p_t is updated to π_j*(t); otherwise, p_t remains at its previous value p_{t-1}. This mechanism prevents rewarding uncertain matches or repeated visits to the same progress level.
- **Reward Signal (r_prog_t):** The agent receives a positive reward (r_prog_t = 1) only when its progress estimate p_t increases (i.e., p_t > p_{t-1}). Low-confidence matches and stagnant progress yield zero reward. This rewards confident *change* in progress rather than raw similarity, promoting stable learning and reducing susceptibility to reward hacking.
- **RL Integration:** RARM serves as a drop-in dense reward function. In simulation, it is integrated with DrQ-v2, and in real-world experiments, with DSRL (Diffusion Steering RL) for fine-tuning a pre-trained VLA policy (π_0).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RARM was conducted across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against various baselines and through ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Tasks:** RARM was evaluated on 9 simulated manipulation tasks: 4 from MetaWorld (short-horizon, e.g., coffee pull, button press) and 5 from LIBERO-10 (long-horizon, e.g., stove + pot, cloth folding variants).
- **RL Setup:** Policies were trained from scratch using DrQ-v2 for 1 million environment steps with 5 random seeds.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Reference-based:** RoboCLIP (video-language embedding similarity) and TemporalOT (optimal transport for trajectory alignment).
 - **VLM-based:** GVL, RoboDopamine, and RoboMeter (progress prediction via large vision-language models).
 - **Ablation:** "Similarity Reward" (RARM without progress-change computation, using raw maximum rollout-reference similarity).
- **Results (Q1 & Q2):**
 - RARM consistently achieved the highest overall success rates across all tasks, demonstrating its ability to provide effective dense rewards for RL training from scratch.
 - **Short-Horizon Tasks:** RARM reached high success rates faster and more consistently than baselines. While some baselines eventually learned useful policies, RARM showed quicker convergence.
 - **Long-Horizon Tasks:** RARM exhibited particularly significant gains on LIBERO-10 tasks. Reference-similarity baselines (RoboCLIP, TemporalOT) struggled, suggesting their brittleness to variations in rollout speed, pauses, or necessary corrections. VLM-based baselines provided semantic progress but were often noisy or over-optimistic on incomplete rollouts. RARM's approach of localizing progress and rewarding confident *changes* provided a more stable signal.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Importance of Progress-Change Reward:** The "Similarity Reward" ablation, which directly used raw similarity scores, performed weaker than full RARM. This finding supports the design choice of rewarding confident *changes* in progress rather than raw similarity, which helps prevent reward hacking from static high-similarity states or noisy score fluctuations.
- **Component Analysis:** Further ablations on a long-horizon LIBERO task and a short-horizon MetaWorld task demonstrated the importance of RARM's core components:
 - Removing the **confidence gate** led to unreliable matches being rewarded.
 - Removing the **cross-attention comparator** weakened the ability to accurately match rollout and reference clips.
 - Removing the **temporal processor** degraded the model's capacity to distinguish forward motion from time-reversed motion.
 - All ablated variants performed worse, indicating that confidence gating, learned comparison, and temporal reasoning jointly contribute to reliable progress estimation.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Comparisons (Cloth Folding)

- **Problem with Baselines:** On a challenging cloth-folding task, VLM-based baselines (GVL, RoboMeter, RoboDopamine, VIP) frequently assigned high cumulative rewards to failed rollouts (e.g., >80% of success reward), often front-loading most of the reward before the task was complete. This illustrated their difficulty in distinguishing visually plausible failures from true success.
- **RARM's Behavior:** RARM's reward profile closely tracked the oracle's linear progress on successful rollouts. Crucially, on failed rollouts, RARM's cumulative reward saturated at a significantly lower level (e.g., 50% of success total), demonstrating its ability to withhold reward for low-confidence or non-progressing clips.
- **Necessity of Confidence Gate:** An ablation of RARM without the confidence threshold resulted in a failed rollout accumulating *more* reward than a successful one. This qualitatively reinforced the necessity of the proposed confidence-gated progress estimation.

#### 4.4. Real-World Experiments

- **Setup:** RARM was integrated as a dense reward for DSRL (Diffusion Steering RL) to fine-tune a pre-trained π_0 VLA policy on an AIRBOT Play dual-arm platform.
- **Tasks:** Four real-world tasks were used: Pick Eraser, Drawer Open (single-arm, short-horizon), Bimanual Hand-over, and Bimanual Clothes Folding (dual-arm, long-horizon, multi-stage).
- **Comparisons (Q3):** Base π_0 policy, DSRL with sparse binary success reward (BinR), and DSRL with RARM.
- **Results:**
 - The base π_0 policy showed limited success (1-2/10 rollouts), highlighting the need for fine-tuning.
 - **Short-Horizon & Bimanual Hand-over:** RARM consistently outperformed BinR, achieving 1-3 additional successes at various training budgets and enabling faster learning (e.g., 6/10 vs. 4/10 on Drawer Open at 30 rollouts). The dense progress signal allowed DSRL to learn from fewer real-robot episodes.
 - **Bimanual Clothes Folding:** This multi-stage, deformable-object task proved particularly challenging. BinR barely improved on the base policy (2/10 success at 90 rollouts) due to the sparse success signal in an environment where complete folds rarely occurred during exploration. RARM, by contrast, recovered a dense progress signal even for partial folds (e.g., Stage 1 completion), leading to a significant improvement (8/10 success at 90 rollouts), a 4x gain over BinR. This demonstrates RARM's value in tasks where sparse rewards are nearly non-existent.
- **Robustness to Lighting and Environmental Noise:** RARM, trained on general videos with aggressive augmentation, produced stable, monotone progress signals in uncontrolled real-world conditions (object pose randomization, ambient illumination drift, background clutter) without task-specific fine-tuning. This supports its generalization capabilities.
- **Scalability for Unseen Tasks (Appendix):** Experiments showed that RARM could leverage synthetic reference demonstrations generated by video generation models (e.g., SeeDance 2.0) or visually mismatched references (different object instances) with comparable success rates. This suggests potential for automated reward pipeline generation.
- **Inference Speed and GPU Cost (Appendix):** RARM exhibited low inference overhead (<10ms/frame) and used significantly less GPU memory (5100 MB) compared to VLM-based reward models (13200-19000 MB), making it suitable for real-time robotic applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Reference-Anchored Reward Model (RARM) offers several contributions that could influence the development and application of reinforcement learning in robotics:

- **Addressing the Reward Design Bottleneck:** RARM provides a practical and effective method for generating dense, progress-aware rewards, which has long been a challenge for applying RL to complex robotic manipulation tasks. By reducing reliance on laborious hand-crafted rewards or extensive task-specific data, it lowers the barrier to entry for RL.
- **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The ability to derive a robust reward signal from a *single successful demonstration* is a notable advantage. This substantially reduces the data collection burden, which is critical in real-world robotics where on-robot data is expensive and time-consuming to acquire. This data efficiency facilitates more rapid experimentation and deployment of RL policies.
- **Increased Reliability and Stability of Learning:** The confidence-gated mechanism is a key innovation. By explicitly filtering out low-confidence or potentially misleading progress estimates, RARM mitigates the problem of "reward hacking" observed in other learned reward models. This leads to more stable RL training and policies that are less prone to exploiting visually plausible but physically incorrect states.
- **Improved Generalization and Transferability:** RARM's training approach, using self-supervised contrastive learning on general-purpose videos, enables it to learn robust visual comparisons that generalize across diverse visual conditions, objects, and tasks. Its demonstrated effectiveness in both simulation and real-world environments, without task-specific reward model fine-tuning, highlights its strong transfer capabilities. The robustness to visual perturbations (glare, color shifts, occlusion, viewpoint changes) further underscores its practical utility.
- **Enabling Long-Horizon and Multi-Stage Tasks:** The dense, stable progress signal provided by RARM is particularly impactful for long-horizon, multi-stage manipulation tasks (such as clothes folding) where sparse success rewards are insufficient for meaningful learning. RARM can provide gradients even on partial task completions, making otherwise intractable tasks amenable to RL.
- **Scalability for Automated Pipelines:** The results indicating RARM's compatibility with synthetic or visually mismatched reference demonstrations suggest a path toward highly scalable reward generation. This could enable automated pipelines where text-to-video foundation models generate approximate progress anchors on demand, further reducing human involvement in task setup.
- **Practical Applicability:** With its low inference overhead and comparatively low GPU memory footprint, RARM is suitable for integration into real-time control loops on robotic platforms, making it a viable solution for real-world deployment.

In summary, RARM offers a robust, data-efficient, and generalizable reward modeling framework that significantly advances the state of the art in applying reinforcement learning to complex robot manipulation, with implications for broader automation and robotics research.
