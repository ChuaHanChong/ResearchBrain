# Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) to extract highly interpretable and monosemantic features from the internal activations of pre-trained language models, demonstrating superior interpretability over other methods and enabling more precise causal localization of model behaviors.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) operate as "black boxes," making their internal decision-making processes opaque and hindering trust and safety assessments.
- Individual neurons in LLMs often exhibit polysemanticity, activating for multiple distinct concepts, making it difficult to assign clear human-understandable interpretations.
- Superposition, where models represent more features than available neurons by encoding them into an overcomplete basis, further complicates feature disentanglement and understanding.

## Method

- Train **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)** in an unsupervised manner on cached internal activation data from pre-trained language models (e.g., ==residual stream==, MLP sublayers).
- The SAEs utilize an ==overcomplete hidden layer== with ==ReLU activation== and an ==L1 sparsity penalty== to reconstruct the input activations, encouraging a sparse combination of learned features.
- Interpretability is quantitatively assessed using automated ==autointerpretability scores== via powerful LLMs, and causal importance is identified through ==activation patching== and ==Automated Circuit Discovery (ACDC)== on specific tasks.

## Results

- Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) features achieved substantially higher autointerpretability scores compared to individual neurons, PCA, and ICA, particularly in early layers of the Pythia-70M model.
- SAE features enabled more precise causal localization for model behavior on the Indirect Object Identification task, requiring fewer features and smaller edit magnitudes than PCA to replicate counterfactual behavior.
- Qualitative analyses demonstrated SAE features were highly monosemantic (e.g., activating for specific tokens like 'apostrophe' or 'newline') and showed intuitive causal effects on model logits and facilitated automated circuit detection across layers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Polysemanticity and superposition in language models can be effectively addressed by learning an overcomplete, sparse basis using sparse autoencoders.
> - Sparse autoencoders can discover truly monosemantic features that are more interpretable and causally relevant than traditional neurons or components from other dimensionality reduction methods.
> - The sparsity penalty applied during autoencoder training is crucial for extracting disentangled features that enable finer-grained causal attribution and facilitate mapping internal computational circuits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper positions itself centrally within the rapidly evolving field of **mechanistic interpretability (MI)**, which aims to reverse-engineer the internal computations of neural networks, particularly large language models. The broader landscape of AI development has seen a tremendous surge in the capabilities of LLMs, yet their internal decision-making processes remain largely opaque. This opacity poses significant concerns regarding trust, reliability, and potential risks, including future scenarios where advanced AI systems might behave deceptively (Ngo et al., 2022). Mechanistic interpretability seeks to mitigate these risks by breaking down complex network behavior into human-understandable components, allowing for targeted analysis and intervention (Cammarata et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Elhage et al., 2021).

A key roadblock in MI has been the phenomenon of **polysemanticity**, where individual neurons within a neural network appear to activate in response to multiple, semantically distinct concepts or features (Olah et al., 2020). This "feature entanglement" makes it difficult to assign concise, human-understandable explanations to what individual neurons represent. Furthermore, for architectural components like the transformer's residual stream, there is little theoretical reason to expect meaningful features to align neatly with the native neuron basis (Elhage et al., 2023).

The paper addresses a hypothesized cause of polysemanticity: **superposition**. Superposition occurs when a neural network represents more distinct features than it has available neurons or dimensions in a given layer. To achieve this, the network learns an "overcomplete basis" of non-orthogonal features, meaning features are no longer neatly orthogonal to each other and thus cannot be uniquely assigned to single neurons. This phenomenon is theorized to be viable only if features are sufficiently sparse in their activation, as high sparsity reduces interference between non-orthogonal features. The core challenge then becomes disentangling these superimposed features from the network's activation space.

This work directly builds upon prior research that explores methods for finding sparse, interpretable representations. It specifically references and is motivated by **sparse dictionary learning**, a well-established technique (Olshausen & Field, 1997; Lee et al., 2006) which has seen application in understanding biological vision (Olshausen & Field, 2004) and, more recently, in transformer visualization (Yun et al., 2021). Critically, this paper acknowledges its direct predecessor, Sharkey et al. (2023), which also explored taking features out of superposition using sparse autoencoders.

The current paper distinguishes itself from other lines of research that aim to achieve sparsity during the *original training* of neural networks, such as architectural modifications (Correia et al., 2019), L1 penalties on neuron activations (Kasioumis et al., 2021), or neuron pruning (Frankle & Carbin, 2018). While these methods seek to build sparse representations *into* the model from inception, they often face challenges in training state-of-the-art models or consistently improving interpretability (Meister et al., 2021). In contrast, this paper proposes a *post-training*, unsupervised approach using sparse autoencoders on pre-trained models. This allows for the analysis of existing powerful LLMs without the prohibitive cost and complexity of retraining them from scratch with new constraints.

By offering a scalable and unsupervised method to resolve superposition and identify interpretable features, this work aims to provide a fundamental tool for the MI community. It seeks to provide a "lens" through which the complex causal dependencies within LLMs can be viewed as sparse, ultimately contributing to the ambitious long-term goal of "enumerative safety" – a complete, human-understandable explanation of a model's computations to guarantee its safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation behind this research stems from the critical need to understand the internal workings of increasingly capable and widely deployed AI systems. As AI models become more powerful and autonomous, their opaque decision-making processes pose significant risks, including potential biases, unexpected failures, and even future scenarios where advanced AI might intentionally deceive humans. The authors explicitly state that mechanistic interpretability is crucial to mitigate such risks by enabling researchers to "reverse engineer parts of their internal processes and make targeted changes to them."

The key objectives and motivations of this paper can be summarized as follows:

- **To address the "black box" problem in AI:** The fundamental goal is to move beyond viewing large language models as impenetrable systems and instead gain a human-understandable explanation of their internal computations. This transparency is seen as essential for building trust and ensuring safe deployment.
- **To resolve polysemanticity and superposition:** The paper identifies polysemanticity as a primary roadblock to interpretability, where individual neurons activate for multiple, unrelated concepts. It posits that this arises from superposition, a phenomenon where models represent more features than they have neurons by encoding features into an overcomplete set of non-orthogonal directions in activation space. The central objective is to "take features out of superposition" by identifying these underlying, sparsely activating, and theoretically monosemantic directions.
- **To identify concise, human-understandable features:** The authors aim to discover distinct computational units (features) that are more interpretable and monosemantic than traditional neurons or directions identified by alternative decomposition methods. These features should ideally represent single, semantically coherent concepts.
- **To develop a scalable and unsupervised method:** The proposed solution, using sparse autoencoders, is intended to be practical for large models. It should not require manual labeling or extensive domain expertise, making it broadly applicable across different LLMs and internal layers. The method's low computational cost relative to model training is also a key objective.
- **To demonstrate improved interpretability quantitatively:** A critical objective is to rigorously test if the learned features are indeed more interpretable. This involves using automated metrics, such as autointerpretability scores, and comparing results against various baselines (e.g., individual neurons, PCA, ICA).
- **To enable finer-grained causal attribution:** The research aims to show that the identified features are not only interpretable but also causally responsible for specific model behaviors. A key objective is to pinpoint the features causally underlying a given task (e.g., Indirect Object Identification) with greater precision than previously possible, allowing for targeted interventions.
- **To lay a foundation for mechanistic understanding and control:** By successfully extracting interpretable features and demonstrating their causal role, the work aims to provide a fundamental building block for future mechanistic interpretability research. This includes enabling automated circuit discovery and ultimately facilitating targeted model editing and steerability, crucial for AI alignment efforts.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the urgent need for AI transparency and safety, with its core objective being to develop a practical and effective method for extracting meaningful, disentangled, and causally-relevant features from the complex internal representations of large language models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this research revolves around the application of **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)** to identify interpretable features within the internal activation spaces of pre-trained language models. The approach is unsupervised and aims to uncover a "sparse dictionary" of features that can reconstruct the original activations.

#### 3.1. Data Collection and Autoencoder Setup

The first step involves collecting internal activation data from a pre-trained language model. The authors primarily use Pythia-70M and Pythia-410M models (Biderman et al., 2023), which are open-source LLMs developed by EleutherAI. They sample activations from specific internal layers: the **residual stream** (the central information highway of a transformer), **MLP sublayers**, or **attention head sublayers**. This data is gathered by running the LLM over a large, diverse text corpus, The Pile (Gao et al., 2020), and caching the activations.

The sparse autoencoder architecture is a simple neural network with:
- **A single hidden layer:** This hidden layer represents the sparse dictionary of features.
- **ReLU activation function:** Applied to the hidden layer, ensuring non-negativity and contributing to sparsity.
- **Tied weights:** The weight matrix of the encoder (`M`) and the decoder (`M^T`) are transposes of each other. This design choice is motivated by the expectation that the directions used to *detect* a feature should be the same as those used to *define* it, reducing memory costs and ambiguity. However, the authors note in Appendix C.3 that untied weights can sometimes be more effective for MLP layers.
- **Overcomplete basis:** The size of the hidden layer (`d_hid`) is set to `R * d_in`, where `d_in` is the dimension of the language model's internal activation vector, and `R` is a hyperparameter (e.g., 2, 4, 8) that controls the ratio of dictionary features to model dimensions. This allows the autoencoder to learn more features than the original layer has dimensions, enabling the representation of superposition.
- **Row Normalization:** The rows of the encoder matrix `M` (which represent the dictionary features) are normalized to prevent the model from trivializing the sparsity penalty by scaling down feature magnitudes.

#### 3.2. Training Objective

The autoencoder is trained to minimize a loss function `L(x)` for an input activation vector `x`:
`L(x) = ||x - ˆx||^2_2 + α||c||_1`
This loss comprises two main terms:
- **Reconstruction Loss (`||x - ˆx||^2_2`):** A standard L2 (mean squared error) term that encourages the autoencoder's output (`ˆx`) to closely reconstruct the original input activation (`x`).
- **Sparsity Loss (`α||c||_1`):** An L1 regularization term applied to the hidden layer activations (`c`, the coefficients of the features). This term encourages the model to use a sparse combination of dictionary features for reconstruction, meaning only a few features are active for any given input. The hyperparameter `α` controls the strength of this sparsity penalty.

The autoencoders are trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-3, typically on 5-50 million activation vectors for 1-3 epochs, with training runs completing in under an hour on a single GPU.

#### 3.3. Interpreting Dictionary Features (Quantitative Evaluation)

To quantitatively assess the interpretability of the learned features, the authors leverage the automated interpretability approach introduced by Bills et al. (2023). This method involves:
- Sampling text fragments where a specific dictionary feature activates.
- Prompting a powerful LLM (GPT-4) to generate a human-readable description (interpretation) of when that feature fires.
- Using another LLM (GPT-3.5) to simulate the feature's activations on new text fragments based on the generated description.
- Calculating the correlation between the simulated and actual activations, yielding an "autointerpretability score." A higher score indicates better interpretability.
The scores are typically "top-and-random," meaning they consider fragments with high activations as well as randomly selected fragments. These scores are compared against various baselines: the default neuron basis, random directions, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and Independent Component Analysis (ICA).

#### 3.4. Identifying Causally-Important Features (Task-Specific Evaluation)

To demonstrate that the learned features are not just interpretable but also causally responsible for specific model behaviors, the authors employ **activation patching** (Vig et al., 2020), adapted for dictionary features, on the **Indirect Object Identification (IOI) task** (Wang et al., 2022). This task involves sentences like "Alice gave a snack to Bob," where the model must identify the indirect object.
- **Procedure:** The model is run on a "base" sentence and a "counterfactual target" sentence (where the indirect object is changed). The activations of the dictionary features from the target sentence are used to "patch" (edit) the corresponding activations in the base sentence's residual stream.
- **Measurement:** The change in the model's output logits is measured using KL divergence between the patched model's output and the target sentence's output.
- **Feature Selection:** To determine which features to patch, they use the Automated Circuit Discovery (ACDC) algorithm (Conmy et al., 2023). ACDC identifies an ordering of features such that patching the top-ranked features optimally reduces the KL divergence, effectively finding the most causally relevant features for the task. This is compared to patching using PCA components.

#### 3.5. Case Studies (Qualitative Analysis)

The methodology also includes qualitative analyses of individual dictionary features to confirm their monosemanticity and causal effects:
- **Input Analysis:** Plotting token activation histograms to identify the specific tokens and contexts that cause a feature to activate, demonstrating its conceptual coherence.
- **Output Analysis:** Performing "less-than-rank-one ablation" where a feature's activation is removed from the residual stream, and observing the direct impact on the model's next-token predictions (logits). This reveals the feature's causal role in shaping model output.
- **Intermediate Features / Circuit Detection:** Tracing causal dependencies across layers by ablating features in earlier layers and observing their effect on the activation of a target feature in a later layer. This allows for the construction of "causal trees" or circuits, providing a fine-grained understanding of information flow.

This comprehensive methodology, combining quantitative and qualitative analyses, aims to rigorously validate the efficacy of sparse autoencoders in extracting meaningful and causally relevant features from opaque LLMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the effectiveness of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) in extracting interpretable features from large language models and shedding light on their internal mechanisms.

#### 4.1. Enhanced Interpretability of Dictionary Features

- **Superior Interpretability Scores:** A primary finding is that features learned by SAEs are substantially more interpretable than those identified by alternative methods (e.g., individual neurons, random directions, PCA, ICA), as measured by automated autointerpretability scores (Bills et al., 2023). This holds true especially in the early layers of the language model (e.g., Pythia-70M residual stream).
- **Layer-wise Performance:** The strength of this interpretability advantage tends to decline in later layers of the model, becoming comparable to ICA in layer 4 and showing minimal improvement in the final layer. The authors suggest this might be due to the increased complexity of later-layer features, which are built upon earlier layers, or limitations of the automated interpretation protocol itself in capturing more abstract or output-related meanings.
- **Robustness Across Dictionary Sizes:** The improved interpretability is not confined to highly overcomplete dictionaries (where `R` > 1) but is observed even in dictionaries smaller than the underlying model dimension. However, larger dictionaries, while having similarly interpretable individual features, are better at reconstructing overall variance.
- **Correlation with Kurtosis and Skew:** The interpretability scores show a positive correlation (0.15-0.20) with the degree of positive skew and kurtosis of feature activations. This supports the theoretical intuition that interpretable features tend to have heavy-tailed, non-Gaussian activation distributions, which sparse coding methods are designed to identify. This also explains why ICA, which maximizes non-Gaussianity, performs better than PCA among baselines.

#### 4.2. Precise Causal Localization for Model Behavior

- **Finer-Grained Attribution:** On the Indirect Object Identification (IOI) task, SAE features enable more precise localization of the specific model components responsible for a given behavior. Compared to PCA, fewer SAE features are required to achieve a target level of KL divergence (i.e., to replicate the counterfactual model behavior).
- **Reduced Edit Magnitude:** Not only do SAE features require fewer patches, but they also allow for more thorough editing (lower KL divergence) with smaller overall edit magnitudes. This indicates that the SAE features align more closely with the true, causally independent conceptual units within the model.
- **Importance of Sparsity:** The effectiveness in causal localization is directly linked to the sparsity penalty (`α`). Non-sparse dictionaries (α=0) do not exhibit this improved localization, underscoring that the L1 penalty is crucial for extracting these causally relevant components.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Demonstrations of Monosemanticity and Causal Effects

- **Highly Monosemantic Features:** Case studies provide compelling qualitative evidence for the monosemanticity of learned dictionary features. Examples include features that activate almost exclusively for specific tokens like apostrophes, periods, the word "the," or newline characters. This stands in stark contrast to the polysemantic nature of individual neurons in the default basis. The paper even shows multiple distinct apostrophe features that activate in different syntactic contexts (e.g., possessives vs. contractions).
- **Intuitive Causal Effects on Logits:** Ablating (removing the activation of) these monosemantic features leads to logical and predictable changes in the model's next-token predictions. For instance, ablating the "apostrophe" feature primarily reduces the logit for the subsequent "s" token, consistent with its role in predicting contractions or possessives.
- **Automated Circuit Detection:** The learned features facilitate automated causal tracing across layers. By ablating features in earlier layers and observing their impact on a target feature in a later layer, the authors demonstrate the ability to construct "causal trees." An example circuit for a "closing parenthesis" feature shows how successive layers detect preceding contexts (dates, acronyms, phrases) that lead to the final prediction of a closing parenthesis.

#### 4.4. Identified Limitations

- **Incomplete Reconstruction:** The SAEs do not achieve zero reconstruction loss, meaning some information from the original activations is not captured. Substituting reconstructed activations for original ones leads to an increase in perplexity (e.g., from 25 to 40 for Pythia-70M Layer 2).
- **Challenges with MLP Layers:** While promising, the current training pipeline struggles to robustly learn overcomplete bases for MLP layers, especially in middle and later layers. Many "dead" features (those that rarely or never activate) are learned, reducing the effective overcompleteness. The authors note that using untied weights for encoder/decoder (M and M^T) helps mitigate this issue.
- **Autointerpretation Limitations:** The automated interpretation method, while scalable, has limitations. It may struggle with complex feature patterns (e.g., those spanning multiple tokens) and does not currently incorporate information about a feature's causal effect on model output.

Overall, the findings strongly support the hypothesis that sparse autoencoders can effectively disentangle superimposed features in LLMs, leading to more interpretable, monosemantic, and causally attributable computational units.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in this paper have significant implications for the field of AI, particularly for mechanistic interpretability, AI safety, and the development of more transparent and controllable language models.

#### 5.1. A Scalable and Unsupervised Foundational Tool for Mechanistic Interpretability

The most immediate impact of this work is providing a **scalable, unsupervised, and computationally efficient** method for disentangling features from superposition in LLMs. Unlike retraining models with new sparsity constraints, this post-training approach allows researchers to analyze existing, powerful foundation models without prohibitive computational costs (requiring orders of magnitude less compute than the original model training). This broadens access to mechanistic interpretability research and makes it feasible to apply these techniques across a wide array of pre-trained models and layers. By addressing polysemanticity, the method moves beyond the limitations of analyzing individual neurons, offering a more semantically meaningful decomposition of model activations.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Model Transparency and Human Understanding

The demonstrated ability to find **highly interpretable and monosemantic features** is a crucial step towards making LLMs less "black box." When researchers can identify specific internal features corresponding to human-understandable concepts (e.g., an "apostrophe" feature or a "legal terms" feature), it fosters a deeper and more intuitive understanding of *what* the model is doing internally and *how* it processes information. This increased transparency is essential for building public trust in AI systems.

#### 5.3. Enabling Finer-Grained Causal Attribution and Targeted Model Editing

The finding that SAE features allow for **more precise localization of causal responsibility** for specific model behaviors (as shown on the IOI task) is highly significant. If a small, identifiable set of features is causally responsible for a particular behavior, it opens the door to **targeted model editing and steerability**. Instead of broad interventions, researchers could precisely activate, deactivate, or modify specific features to alter or correct undesirable model behaviors (e.g., reduce biases, remove specific capabilities, or inject desired knowledge) without disrupting other functionalities. This capability is paramount for AI safety and alignment efforts.

#### 5.4. Advancing Automated Circuit Discovery

The ability to automatically trace causal dependencies between dictionary features across layers represents a significant step towards **automated circuit discovery**. Understanding how information flows and is transformed through sequences of features, forming "circuits" for specific computations, is a long-standing goal in interpretability. This work provides a concrete method for beginning to map these internal computational graphs, which could eventually lead to a comprehensive understanding of complex model behaviors.

#### 5.5. Progress Towards "Enumerative Safety"

The authors explicitly link their work to the ambitious dream of **"enumerative safety"** (Elhage et al., 2022b) – the idea of producing a complete, human-understandable explanation of a model's computations to provide formal guarantees that it will not perform dangerous behaviors like deception. By offering a systematic way to identify and interpret a model's fundamental features, this research provides a foundational step toward achieving such a complete "list" of model capabilities and their internal mechanisms, which is critical for ensuring the safe and beneficial development of advanced AI.

In summary, this research provides a practical, robust, and conceptually sound method for peering inside the complex representations of large language models. Its impact lies in democratizing mechanistic interpretability, significantly enhancing our ability to understand, control, and ultimately ensure the safety of powerful AI systems, paving the way for a future where AI's capabilities are matched by our understanding and trustworthiness.
