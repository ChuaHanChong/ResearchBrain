# An Empirical Study on Stage-Information Interfaces for VLA Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> An empirical study identifies effective ways to integrate stage-level information into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for multi-stage robotic tasks, finding that appending a normalized ordinal stage index to the robot state, introduced after initial full-task fine-tuning, improves task success rates by up to 4.69 percentage points over using only the full-task instruction.

## Problem

- Despite advancements in VLA models, the high cost of collecting diverse robot demonstrations for long-horizon tasks remains a practical bottleneck.
- While segmenting complex tasks into subtasks with language annotations is technically feasible, it's unclear how this stage-specific information should be represented and integrated into the VLA action policy during training.
- Initial direct fine-tuning experiments with explicit stage information did not consistently improve performance, and sometimes even reduced it, compared to using only the full-task instruction.

## Method

- The study frames ==subtask-conditioned VLA fine-tuning== as an interface problem between a ==stage-tracking module== and an action policy.
- It empirically compares two methods for conveying active-stage information: explicitly as current-stage text (**TASKCTX**) and as a ==normalized ordinal stage index== (**ORDINAL STAGE-STATE**) embedded within the robot state.
- These interfaces are evaluated under two training paradigms: ==direct fine-tuning== from a pretrained VLA model and a ==baseline-first continuation== strategy where stage information is introduced after an initial phase of full-task learning.

## Results

- In direct fine-tuning, the baseline policy (full-task instruction only) achieved a mean success rate of **57.45%**, outperforming both TASKCTX (**50.24%**) and ORDINAL STAGE-STATE (**54.36%**).
- In baseline-first continuation fine-tuning, the ORDINAL STAGE-STATE interface achieved the highest mean success rate of **53.75%**, surpassing the continued baseline (**49.07%**) by **4.69 percentage points**.
- The TASKCTX interface in continuation fine-tuning showed only a marginal improvement (**50.00%**) over the continued baseline (**49.07%**), consistently underperforming the ordinal state method.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly introducing explicit stage information during initial fine-tuning did not automatically improve policy performance and could even be detrimental compared to a full-task baseline.
> - The representation of stage information is crucial, with embedding an ordinal index in the robot state generally outperforming appending textual stage descriptions to the language prompt.
> - The timing of introducing stage information matters; it proved more effective when integrated after the VLA policy had already established a baseline understanding of the full task via full-task instruction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of long-horizon robotic manipulation, specifically concerning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Recent advancements in VLA systems, such as RT-2, OpenVLA, Octo, RoboFlamingo, and SmolVLA, have enhanced the capability of imitation-based robot learning by leveraging large-scale vision-language knowledge. These systems, alongside continuous-action models like π0 and GR00T N1, facilitate direct action generation. However, a persistent practical bottleneck remains the high cost and effort associated with collecting diverse robot demonstrations.

A promising approach to mitigate this cost involves extracting more supervision from existing robot data by augmenting trajectories with descriptive annotations, particularly segment-level descriptions of what the robot is doing at each stage of a multi-stage task. This concept is supported by prior work in long-horizon systems that utilize intermediate language to organize behavior, including SayCan (connecting high-level reasoning to skills), RT-H (inserting language motions), and DeMiAn (enriching demonstrations with segment-level descriptions). These efforts have demonstrated the technical feasibility of detailed subtask annotation.

Despite the plausibility of segment-level annotations, the paper identifies a gap: the benefit of such segmentation is not automatically realized. There is an open question regarding *how* this subtask information should be represented and introduced to the action policy during training. The current study specifically positions itself within this gap, focusing on the *interface problem* between a stage-tracking module and the VLA action policy. It seeks to empirically compare different methods of exposing active-stage information (semantic text versus an ordinal index in state) to a fine-tuned VLA model, rather than focusing on the generation of annotations or the progress tracking itself. This contrasts with progress-aware systems like ProgVLA, ProgressVLA, SeqVLA, CycleVLA, and See-Plan-Rewind, which focus on estimating task progress or using completion for transitions and recovery. While this work employs a progress module for internal stage advancement, its primary contribution lies in evaluating the efficacy of distinct communication channels for stage information to the action policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a practical intuition related to long-horizon manipulation tasks: if a complex task, which often involves several action stages (e.g., "put both moka pots on the stove"), is segmented into subtasks, providing language descriptions for the current active stage should lead to a more precise description of the required action. Consequently, the action distribution within each segment was hypothesized to become narrower, potentially simplifying the training of a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy.

However, the introductory section indicates that initial observations did not consistently support this intuition. Specifically, in direct fine-tuning experiments, both stage-text and ordinal-state variants performed below a baseline policy that received only the full-task instruction. This discrepancy led to a reformulation of the core research question, shifting from *whether* segmentation is beneficial to *how* subtask information should be represented and integrated into action-policy training.

Based on this, the paper articulates two primary objectives, framed as contributions:

1. **Formulate and Compare Stage-Information Interfaces:** To conceptualize subtask-conditioned VLA fine-tuning as an interface problem between a stage-tracking module and an action policy. The objective is to empirically compare two specific methods for conveying active-stage information: explicitly as current-stage text and as a normalized ordinal stage index embedded within the robot state. This comparison is conducted under shared annotations and control logic, ensuring that only the interface mechanism varies.
2. **Evaluate Interface Performance Across Training Arrangements:** To assess the effectiveness of these two interfaces under two distinct training paradigms. The first is *direct fine-tuning* from a pretrained VLA model, where the stage information is introduced from the beginning of downstream adaptation. The second is *continuation fine-tuning*, where the policy first undergoes adaptation using only the full-task instruction (establishing a baseline), and then the stage-conditioned interfaces are introduced and fine-tuned further. This dual evaluation aims to understand how the timing of interface introduction impacts learning and performance.

The overarching goal is to determine if and how explicit stage information can benefit VLA policy learning, particularly in the context of fine-tuning powerful pre-trained models like GR00T N1.6, and to identify the most effective representation and introduction strategy for such information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the integration of stage-level information into a VLA model for long-horizon manipulation tasks. The core methodological framework involves a closed-loop pipeline where a progress module tracks the active task stage, and this information is then supplied to an action policy via different interfaces.

**VLA Backbone and Fine-Tuning Scope:**
The study utilizes the pretrained NVIDIA GR00T N1.6-3B model. This model employs a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to encode camera images and task language. Robot inputs are connected via state and action projectors to a 32-layer Diffusion Transformer (DiT), which is responsible for predicting relative action chunks using flow matching.
For fine-tuning, the visual encoder and all but the top four language-model layers are frozen to conserve computational resources and leverage the pre-trained knowledge. The trainable components include these top four language layers, the action-side projectors, vision-language layer normalization, and the DiT module, all adapted to the LIBERO benchmark.

**Action Segmentation:**
Action segmentation is performed offline using Qwen2.5-VL. This process takes the original task instruction and generates an ordered sequence of stage phrases (c_k) along with corresponding frame ranges ([b_k, e_k]) for each demonstration. It is crucial to note that a single, fixed set of these stage phrases and frame boundaries is used across all experimental methods. The annotation procedure itself is not a variable in the study; the focus is solely on how the *resulting* stage information is presented to the action module. These frame ranges are used to label training demonstrations but are not provided as an oracle during policy rollout. Semantic stages can group multiple low-level motions (e.g., approach, grasp, transport, placement) if they serve a single subgoal.

**Progress Module:**
A dedicated progress model (g_phi) is employed to estimate the completion of the active stage during rollout. This model is initialized from the pretrained GR00T checkpoint. During training, it receives the visual observation (o_t), robot state (s_t), full-task instruction (l_task), and the active stage phrase (c_k). Similar to the action policy, its visual encoder and all but the top four language-model layers are frozen. The top four language layers, action-side projector, diffusion/DiT module, and action-head VLLN are trainable, concluding with a linear scalar head and sigmoid to predict progress p_t (normalized to). Training uses Smooth L1 loss, with action loss disabled and zero action tokens.
During rollout, the system maintains a stage pointer (k_t). The progress model queries ˆp_t = g_phi(o_t, s_t, [l_task; c_k_t]). The stage pointer advances to the next stage when ˆp_t exceeds 0.8, provided at least 15 frames have passed in the current stage. The completion estimate itself is an internal signal used only for pointer advancement and is explicitly *not* provided to the action policy.

**Action-Module Interfaces:**
Three distinct methods for providing information to the action module are compared:
1. **ORIGINAL (Baseline):** The action module receives no explicit stage information. It operates solely on the visual observation (o_t), robot state (s_t), and the full-task instruction (l_task).
2. **Semantic Stage Interface (TASKCTX):** The active stage phrase (c_k_t) is appended to the full-task instruction (l_task). The action module receives o_t, s_t, and the concatenated language prompt `[l_task; c_k_t]`.
3. **Ordinal Stage-State Interface:** The original full-task instruction (l_task) is preserved. The one-based active-stage index (k_t) is normalized to a value z_t within [-1, 1] using `z_t = 2 * ((k_t - 1) / (K_max - 1)) - 1`, where K_max is the maximum number of stages across all tasks (6 for LIBERO-10). This normalized ordinal value z_t is then appended to the robot state (s_t). The action module receives o_t, the augmented robot state `[s_t; z_t]`, and l_task. The GR00T state encoder is pre-configured to accommodate this additional input dimension without requiring model tensor resizing.

**Training Arrangements:**
Two training arrangements are investigated:
1. **Direct Fine-tuning:** All three interface variants (ORIGINAL, TASKCTX, ORDINAL STAGE-STATE) are fine-tuned directly from the pretrained GR00T model for 5,000 steps.
2. **Baseline-First Continuation:** First, the ORIGINAL policy is fine-tuned for 5,000 steps (establishing a full-task baseline checkpoint). Then, from this specific checkpoint, the TASKCTX and ORDINAL STAGE-STATE variants are fine-tuned for an additional 1,000 steps. The ORIGINAL variant also continues for 1,000 steps from its 5k checkpoint to provide a direct comparison.
In both arrangements, within each paired run, all methods share the same source initialization and total training budget.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on the LIBERO-10 benchmark. A single action model is trained across all ten tasks using 30 demonstrations per task. A fixed progress model is trained for 10,000 steps and shared across all action-policy runs and arrangements. Evaluation involves 50 episodes per task with n_envs = 10 and a fixed rollout seed. Each arrangement is repeated across three paired action-policy runs using distinct random seeds (101, 202, 303) to account for training variability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical study produced distinct outcomes depending on the interface representation and the training arrangement. The results are based on mean success rates across three paired action-policy runs, with standard deviations reported.

**Direct Fine-Tuning:**
This arrangement evaluated the performance when stage information interfaces were introduced from the beginning of the downstream adaptation process.
- **ORIGINAL (full-task instruction only):** Achieved a mean success rate of 57.45% (± 8.20).
- **TASKCTX (current-stage text appended to task instruction):** Achieved a mean success rate of 50.24% (± 6.32).
- **ORDINAL STAGE-STATE (normalized ordinal stage index appended to robot state):** Achieved a mean success rate of 54.36% (± 8.17).

Under direct fine-tuning, neither of the explicit stage-conditioned interfaces (TASKCTX or ORDINAL STAGE-STATE) surpassed the performance of the baseline ORIGINAL method. The TASKCTX method performed 7.21 percentage points lower than ORIGINAL on average. The ORDINAL STAGE-STATE method, while outperforming TASKCTX by 4.12 percentage points on average and in two out of three paired runs, still remained 3.09 percentage points below the ORIGINAL baseline. This indicates that the direct introduction of stage information did not automatically enhance policy learning.

**Baseline-First Continuation:**
This arrangement first fine-tuned the policy with the full-task instruction (ORIGINAL) for 5,000 steps, and then continued training for an additional 1,000 steps, introducing the stage-conditioned interfaces at this later point.
- **ORIGINAL (continued from baseline):** Achieved a mean success rate of 49.07% (± 2.54).
- **TASKCTX (continued from baseline):** Achieved a mean success rate of 50.00% (± 3.56).
- **ORDINAL STAGE-STATE (continued from baseline):** Achieved a mean success rate of 53.75% (± 4.36).

In the continuation fine-tuning arrangement, a different pattern emerged. The ORDINAL STAGE-STATE interface achieved the highest mean success rate of 53.75%. It outperformed both TASKCTX and ORIGINAL across all three paired runs, showing improvements of 3.75 and 4.69 percentage points, respectively, over their means. The TASKCTX interface marginally exceeded the ORIGINAL baseline by 0.93 percentage points on average and in one out of three runs. This suggests that when stage information is introduced after an initial phase of learning the full-task mapping, the ordinal state-based representation can be more effective.

**Summary of Result Scope:**
The reported standard deviations quantify variability across the three independent paired action-policy runs. A single, fixed progress model was used across all experiments. The absolute success rates are not directly comparable between the direct and continuation arrangements due to differing initialization histories and total training steps. However, internal comparisons within each arrangement are robust. Consistently, ORDINAL STAGE-STATE showed a higher mean than TASKCTX in both direct and continuation settings, though neither stage-conditioned interface outperformed the ORIGINAL baseline during direct fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of effective strategies for integrating subtask information into VLA policies, particularly in the context of fine-tuning powerful pre-trained models for long-horizon manipulation.

**Refutation of Initial Intuition:** The direct fine-tuning results demonstrate that the intuitive belief that explicit stage descriptions would automatically simplify action learning by narrowing action distributions is not universally supported. In fact, directly introducing stage information from the outset can, in some cases, lead to reduced performance compared to a baseline using only the full-task instruction. This highlights that the specific design of the interface for introducing such information is critical and is itself part of the learning problem.

**Importance of Interface Representation and Timing:** The study underscores that both the *representation* of stage information and the *timing* of its introduction are significant factors affecting policy performance.
- **Representation:** The higher performance of the ORDINAL STAGE-STATE interface compared to TASKCTX, particularly in continuation fine-tuning, suggests that embedding stage information as a low-dimensional numerical value within the robot state can be more effective than modifying the language input. This is attributed to how GR00T processes information: the ordinal index enters through the state projector and participates in self-attention from the initial DiT blocks, allowing it to influence action computation directly. Conversely, TASKCTX alters the language prompt, requiring adaptation primarily in the limited trainable upper language layers and the action module, and potentially fragmenting data representations across different language contexts.
- **Timing:** The contrasting results between direct and continuation fine-tuning are noteworthy. While stage information did not help initially, its introduction via ORDINAL STAGE-STATE after the policy had established a baseline understanding of the full task proved beneficial. This implies that integrating stage information as a compact adjustment to an already learned action representation can be more compatible and effective than introducing a potentially disruptive language context change early in training.

**Practical Implications for VLA Deployment:** For researchers and practitioners developing VLA models for complex, multi-stage robotic tasks, this work offers practical guidance. When fine-tuning pre-trained generalist VLA models, simply appending current-stage text to the task instruction may not yield benefits and could even be detrimental. Instead, encoding the active stage as a normalized ordinal index within the robot state appears to be a more robust and effective approach, especially when fine-tuning an existing full-task policy. This method may offer a way to extract more value from existing segmented demonstrations without requiring extensive modifications to the VLM's language processing capabilities.

**Future Research Directions:** The paper also identifies limitations, suggesting avenues for future work. The findings are specific to LIBERO-10, GR00T N1.6-3B, a particular parameter-freezing setup, and fixed training budgets. Further research is needed to determine if these patterns generalize to other benchmarks, different VLA architectures, alternative fine-tuning schedules, and critically, to real-world robot deployments. Investigating the impact of varying the trainable layers, exploring different normalization schemes for ordinal states, or evaluating more sophisticated language encoding methods for stage information could provide additional insights.
