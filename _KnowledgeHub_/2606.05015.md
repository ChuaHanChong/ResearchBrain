# Generalization of World Models under Environmental Variability for Vision-based Quadrotor Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at NTNU systematically analyzed the generalization of DreamerV3 world models for vision-based quadrotor navigation under environmental variability, finding that self-supervised cross-environment reconstruction quality predicts real-world deployability better than simulated policy performance. Their models successfully achieved autonomous closed-loop navigation in a real, unseen cluttered corridor and completed 12-meter open-loop traverses based solely on imagination.

## Problem

- Consistent generalization of learned predictive representations (world models) across diverse environments is a critical challenge for embodied AI, particularly for vision-based navigation in agile robots like quadrotors.
- Prior work often evaluates world models in fixed settings or requires precise digital twins, lacking systematic analysis of how increasing environmental randomness affects world model quality and generalization.
- Existing aerial robot navigation approaches with world models either do not investigate cross-environment transfer or rely on pre-processed sensor data, limiting understanding of raw model robustness to visual variability.

## Method

- Defined four levels of environmental randomness in simulation (L1-L4, varying obstacle/spawn/goal configuration) and trained DreamerV3-based world models using ==self-supervised== learning on raw depth images for each level, with a sequential hyperparameter search to identify robust configurations.
- Fine-tuned actor policies using ==reinforcement learning== entirely within the latent imagination of the world models, introducing control-level uncertainty to isolate dynamics mismatch.
- Evaluated models using a cross-environment protocol in simulation and deployed them on a physical quadrotor in unseen real-world environments for both closed-loop and open-loop navigation.

## Results

- Models consistently preferred large RSSM capacity (ddet=dhid=1024d_{det}=d_{hid}=1024ddet=dhid=1024) and long batch sequences (Lbatch=64L_{batch}=64Lbatch=64) across all training randomness levels during self-supervised learning.
- WM3, trained on Sobol-sampled environments, showed the most robust policy performance in simulation, maintaining win rates above **72.0%** on out-of-distribution layouts, demonstrating better generalization compared to other training distributions.
- In real-world deployment, models with strong SSL cross-environment reconstruction quality (WM1, WM2, WM4) successfully performed closed-loop navigation in a previously unseen, cluttered corridor, traversing gaps as narrow as 0.67 meters, and all models completed 12-meter open-loop traverses solely based on imagination.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World model quality is predominantly governed by the capacity of the stochastic state (ddiscd_{disc}ddisc) and training sequence length (LbatchL_{batch}Lbatch), with larger values generally yielding better performance.
> - Cross-environment reconstruction quality during self-supervised pretraining serves as a more reliable predictor of real-world deployability than simulated reinforcement learning policy performance.
> - An intermediate level of environmental randomness (e.g., Sobol quasi-random sampling) during pretraining can offer an optimal balance for representational diversity and policy optimization in simulation, though maximal real-world transfer may depend on raw reconstruction fidelity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) has seen significant advancements through predictive representation learning, where agents develop an internal model of environmental dynamics in response to their actions. Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) leverages such learned dynamics models, often referred to as world models, to generate synthetic experiences through latent imagination. This approach aims to enhance sample efficiency, facilitate long-horizon reasoning, and enable scalable decision-making in complex, high-dimensional problems. Beyond policy optimization, world models are increasingly recognized as a general computational primitive for embodied intelligence, supporting tasks like state estimation, trajectory prediction, and self-supervised representation learning across various robotic systems.

Despite these developments, a critical challenge remains: the consistent generalization of learned predictive representations across diverse environments. This generalization capability is considered essential for training robust world model-based navigation policies. Traditional approaches often evaluate within fixed settings, assuming high similarity between training and deployment environments, or even requiring precise digital twins. While these methods can yield effective control, they do not inherently guarantee that the learned predictive model captures transferable environmental structures. This limitation is particularly pronounced in vision-based navigation for agile robotic systems, such as quadrotors, where predictive performance can significantly degrade due to environmental variability in structure, obstacle configuration, lighting, and texture, compounded by rapid viewpoint changes inherent to agile flight.

Previous work has explored world models for aerial robot navigation. Some studies train separate models for specific tracks without investigating cross-environment transfer. Others demonstrate sim-to-real transfer but often mitigate the visual reality gap through specialized preprocessing of sensor data. These existing efforts highlight the need for a systematic analysis of how world model quality and generalization are affected by increasing environmental randomness, a gap that this paper aims to address in the context of vision-based autonomous navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a systematic and comprehensive robustness analysis of DreamerV3-based world models with respect to environmental variability and generalization for depth image-based collision-free quadrotor navigation. The authors explicitly aim to understand whether learned predictive representations consistently generalize across different environments, a prerequisite for training reliable world model-based navigation policies.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations and existing limitations:
- **Lack of systematic analysis:** Prior research applying world models to autonomous aerial robot navigation has not systematically investigated how environmental variability impacts generalization. Existing methods often rely on specific environments or pre-process visual input to reduce the sim-to-real gap, which limits understanding of raw world model robustness.
- **Importance of generalization:** For practical deployment of autonomous systems, especially agile robots like quadrotors navigating complex real-world settings, the ability of world models to generalize to unseen or varied environments is crucial. Current evaluation practices often implicitly assume high similarity between training and deployment, which may not hold in reality.
- **Understanding degradation under randomness:** It is essential to understand how world model quality degrades, or maintains performance, as environmental randomness increases. This knowledge is fundamental for achieving reliable sim-to-real transfer.
- **Unprocessed sensor streams:** Unlike some prior work that uses pre-processed observations, this research aims to train and evaluate models using raw depth image data, reflecting a more challenging and realistic scenario for vision-based navigation.
- **DreamerV3's reconstruction objective:** The choice of DreamerV3 is motivated by its reconstruction objective, which allows for direct comparison of imagined observations with real sensor readings, providing a quantifiable measure of imagination fidelity.

To address these points, the investigation proceeds through four stages:
1. **SSL Pretraining Analysis:** Systematically study how environmental randomness during Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) pretraining influences learned representations by cross-evaluating models trained across varying randomness levels. This aims to characterize world model sensitivity to domain shifts in vision-based quadrotor flight.
2. **Hyperparameter Identification:** Conduct a structured hyperparameter search during the SSL phase to identify configurations that yield robust world models, thereby providing practical guidelines for training DreamerV3-based systems.
3. **RL Fine-tuning Evaluation:** Apply the cross-environment protocol to Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning, isolating the impact of control-level uncertainty (through randomized controller parameters) on policy robustness.
4. **Real-World Deployment:** Evaluate all trained models on a physical quadrotor in previously unseen real environments, including challenging closed-loop navigation and an open-loop scenario where the quadrotor relies entirely on its internal world model imagination after a brief sensory input.

Ultimately, the goal is to characterize world model robustness from simulation to the real world and to establish a practical baseline for future deployable MBRL systems by identifying reliable predictors for real-world performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates collision-free 3D navigation for a quadrotor using onboard depth perception. The objective is to learn a control policy that enables the quadrotor to reach a goal position while avoiding obstacles, solely utilizing MBRL. The methodology consists of three main stages: data collection, self-supervised world model pretraining with hyperparameter optimization, and reinforcement learning fine-tuning, primarily conducted within the AerialGym simulator. This is followed by real-world deployment.

#### 3.1. Data Collection

To analyze world model robustness to domain shift, four distinct levels of environmental randomness (L1, L2, L3, L4) were defined within a simulated 16x7x7 meter indoor environment containing five cuboid obstacles. All levels shared the same environment geometry, differing only in how obstacles, spawn, and goal positions were configured at the beginning of each episode.
- **L1 (Least Randomized):** Fixed obstacle configuration; only spawn and goal positions were uniformly sampled.
- **L2 (Structured Diversity):** Five distinct fixed obstacle configurations were used, one of which matched L1; at each episode, one configuration was selected.
- **L3 (Spatial Diversity):** Obstacle positions were drawn from a Sobol quasi-random sequence, ensuring low-discrepancy coverage and more uniform distribution across the room than pure random sampling.
- **L4 (Maximally Stochastic):** Obstacles, spawn, and goal positions were all sampled uniformly and independently at every episode.
For each randomness level, trajectories were collected using a set of exploration strategies to ensure diverse data. These included high-frequency strategies (uniform random sampling, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) noise, Lissajous curves, pseudo-spline smooth random walk) to cover the action space and a low-frequency reactive depth-based controller (safe exploration) that steered the quadrotor by comparing mean depth across image sectors. Data was collected independently for each level L_i, resulting in world models WM_i.

#### 3.2. World Model Training (Self-Supervised Learning - SSL)

The world models were built upon DreamerV3, a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) that learns environmental dynamics and optimizes policies in latent imagination. The RSSM comprised a deterministic recurrent state ($h_t$) and a categorical stochastic state ($z_t$). The model components included a recurrent model, an encoder, a dynamics predictor, a decoder (for depth image $D_t$ and state $s_t$), and a reward predictor. The latent state $\{h_t, z_t\}$ captured memory and uncertainty, and the decoder enabled comparison between imagined and real observations.
The world model was trained by minimizing a joint objective loss function, $L_{\phi} = L_{rec} + L_{rew} + \beta L_{KL}$, where $L_{rec}$ reconstructed $D_t$ and $s_t$, $L_{rew}$ predicted the reward, and $L_{KL}$ regularized the stochastic state by aligning the encoder and dynamics predictor.

A sequential (coordinate-wise) hyperparameter search was conducted independently for each randomness level to identify robust configurations:
- **Deterministic State Size ($d_{det}$):** Capacity of the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) hidden state in the RSSM.
- **MLP Hidden Size ($d_{hid}$):** Width of transition and representation networks.
- **Discrete Latent Size ($d_{disc}$):** Number of classes per categorical variable in the stochastic state $z_t$, determining the resolution of the discrete bottleneck for uncertainty encoding.
- **Batch Length ($L_{batch}$):** Length of trajectory sequences sampled during training, impacting long-range dependency learning.
Each candidate configuration was evaluated by its reconstruction loss on held-out trajectories from the same randomness level. The best configuration per level was then cross-evaluated across all other levels for both context and imagination phases.

#### 3.3. Actor Training & World Model Fine-tuning (Reinforcement Learning - RL)

The best-performing world model from each randomness level underwent RL fine-tuning entirely within its latent imagination. An actor policy was optimized to maximize expected discounted cumulative reward. The reward function combined a proximity term to the goal with a time-efficiency bonus, rewarding goal-reaching with remaining time steps. Crash and explicit win bonuses were set to zero.
The actor unrolled trajectories through the RSSM, and a critic estimated the value function along these imagined rollouts. Gradients were backpropagated through the imagined trajectory to train the actor. To introduce control-level uncertainty, the parameters of the low-level flying vehicle controller were randomized at each episode, isolating the impact of dynamics mismatch from visual or obstacle variability. The same cross-environment evaluation protocol used in the SSL phase was applied to test each policy across all randomness levels in both context and imagination phases.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation comprised three stages: SSL pretraining analysis, RL fine-tuning, and real-world deployment.

#### 4.1. Self-Supervised Pretraining Analysis

- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The sequential hyperparameter sweep indicated a consistent preference across all environments for large RSSM capacity ($d_{det}=d_{hid}=1024$) and long batch sequences ($L_{batch}=64$). The discrete latent size ($d_{disc}$) showed a mild preference for smaller values (32 vs. 64) at higher environmental randomness (L3/L4). The most influential hyperparameters were consistently $d_{disc}$ and $L_{batch}$, with $d_{disc}$ exhibiting the strongest and most variable impact (35-50% relative loss gap) and $L_{batch}$ providing a stable 35% impact. RSSM sizes ($d_{det}$, $d_{hid}$) had a moderate effect ($\approx 20\%$), while seed variance was negligible ($<5\%$). These findings suggest that world model quality is primarily governed by the capacity of the stochastic state and training sequence length, rather than being heavily dependent on the specific training environment.
- **Cross-Environment Reconstruction Quality (SSL):** During the context phase (conditioned on real observations), all models achieved low Mean Squared Error (MSE) and high Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) on their respective training distributions (diagonal entries). Performance degraded off-diagonal, particularly for WM1 and WM2 when evaluated on higher-randomness environments, indicating limited diversity in their training data. Intriguingly, world models trained in more structured environments (L1, L2) showed better reconstruction quality in the imagination phase (purely hallucinated predictions) on their native environments compared to models trained on highly stochastic environments (L3, L4).

#### 4.2. Actor Training & World Model Fine-tuning

- **Policy Performance:** In terms of win, crash, and timeout rates, WM1 achieved the highest win rate (99.5%) on its native L1 environment. However, its performance sharply declined with increasing evaluation randomness, with a 44.5% crash rate in the Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) layout. This suggested overfitting to the fixed L1 obstacle structure. WM3, trained on Sobol-sampled placements, demonstrated the most consistent performance, maintaining win rates above 89.5% within the training distribution and over 72.0% on the OOD layout, indicating better generalization. WM4, despite being trained in the most diverse environment, did not surpass WM3 in policy performance, suggesting that maximal stochasticity might hinder policy convergence during RL fine-tuning. Timeout rates were negligible across all models, confirming that failures were predominantly collisions.
- **RL Imagination Reconstruction:** The overall reconstruction quality of RL-fine-tuned world models was lower than in the SSL phase, which was expected as the models were optimized for policy return rather than perceptual fidelity. Context-phase MSE and SSIM remained consistent, indicating that the RSSM could still assimilate real observations. However, degradation was concentrated in the imagination phase, where policy-optimized dynamics accumulated errors over open-loop horizons. WM1 showed the sharpest drop in SSIM under OOD evaluation, reinforcing that low training randomness limited generalization across both policy and world model dimensions.
- **Combined Trend:** A consistent trend emerged: world models that generalized well across environments during SSL pretraining tended to yield more robust policies after RL fine-tuning. While WM4 exhibited the best generalization at the SSL stage, WM3 produced the strongest policy, suggesting that an intermediate level of training randomness (Sobol sampling in L3) struck an optimal balance between representational diversity and the stability required for policy optimization.

#### 4.3. Real-World Deployment

- **Closed-Loop Navigation:** All trained models were deployed on a real quadrotor in a previously unseen indoor environment, specifically a cluttered corridor narrower than the simulation environment and containing seven planar panels (obstacles). WM1, WM2, and WM4 successfully traversed the full 13-meter path, navigating through gaps as narrow as 0.67 meters and 0.85 meters (marginally wider than the 0.5-meter quadrotor body). WM3, however, entered a looping behavior and failed to recover, despite remaining collision-free. This result contradicted simulation predictions, where WM3 was the most robust policy. All successful models maintained stable performance, indicating tolerance to real-world depth stream noise. An earlier closed-loop deployment in a 5-panel environment (closer to simulation) showed all four models successfully reaching the target.
- **Open-Loop Imagination:** In a more demanding open-loop setting, the quadrotor was given a brief 2.5-second context window of real sensory input, after which all sensors were cut off, and the model navigated entirely based on its imagined depth and state. All models successfully completed a 12-meter traverse. Early predictions closely matched the real depth stream, accurately hallucinating corridor walls and obstacle edges. Fidelity gradually degraded over longer horizons as latent dynamics errors compounded.
- **Key Real-World Predictor:** The real-world experiments revealed a critical finding: every model that demonstrated strong generalization during SSL pretraining (as measured by cross-environment reconstruction quality) successfully transferred to the real world. Conversely, WM3, which performed best in the RL simulation policy evaluation, failed to navigate in the cluttered real environment. This indicates that SSL cross-environment reconstruction quality is a more reliable predictor of real-world deployability than simulation-based RL win rates, and that intermediate training randomness, while optimal in simulation, does not guarantee the best real-world outcome.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic and comprehensive characterization of world model generalization under environmental variability, particularly for vision-based quadrotor navigation. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Robustness Characterization:** The study presents the first systematic characterization of world model sensitivity to domain shifts in vision-based quadrotor flight. By cross-evaluating models trained across varying levels of environmental randomness, it offers a deeper understanding of how robust predictive representations are formed and maintained.
- **Practical Guidelines for MBRL Systems:** The detailed hyperparameter search during the SSL phase identifies the discrete latent size ($d_{disc}$) and batch length ($L_{batch}$) as dominant factors governing world model quality. These findings provide practical guidelines for researchers and practitioners training DreamerV3-based systems, potentially reducing the empirical search space and improving training efficiency.
- **Reliable Sim-to-Real Transfer Predictor:** A key finding is that cross-environment reconstruction quality during SSL pretraining is a more reliable predictor of real-world deployability than simulated RL policy performance (win rates). This insight challenges conventional wisdom that often prioritizes simulated policy performance and offers a new metric for evaluating the likelihood of successful sim-to-real transfer for MBRL systems. This could lead to more efficient development cycles by enabling early identification of generalizable models before costly RL fine-tuning and real-world testing.
- **Advancement in Autonomous Navigation:** The successful deployment of world models on a real quadrotor, including challenging open-loop navigation over a 12-meter traverse purely on imagination, demonstrates a significant step towards truly autonomous and resilient robotic systems. This capability could be crucial for missions in GPS-denied or sensor-degraded environments.
- **Validation Framework:** The proposed cross-environment validation framework serves as a practical baseline for future deployable MBRL systems. By explicitly accounting for environmental variability, it encourages the development of more generalizable and robust robotic learning paradigms.
- **Open-Sourced Contribution:** The open-sourcing of the methodology contributes to transparency and reproducibility within the research community, allowing other researchers to build upon these findings and advance the field.

The study's findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of world model generalization, moving beyond simple performance metrics to provide actionable insights into architectural choices, training strategies, and evaluation protocols for robust, real-world robotic deployment.
