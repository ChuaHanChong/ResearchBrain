# RoboChemist: Long-Horizon and Safety-Compliant Robotic Chemical Experimentation

> [!summary] Summary
> A dual-loop framework named RoboChemist, developed by researchers at BAAI and Tsinghua University, enables autonomous execution of long-horizon and safety-compliant chemical experiments through the integration of Vision-Language Models and Vision-Language-Action models. It achieved a 23.57% higher average success rate and a 0.298 higher average compliance rate in primitive tasks compared to existing robotic baselines, exhibiting strong generalization across diverse chemical scenarios.

## Problem

- Long-horizon chemical protocols require precise manipulation of delicate glassware and deformable substances, along with strict adherence to safety and procedural norms.
- Existing Vision-Language Model (VLM) based systems struggle with transparent labware and deformable liquids due to limitations in depth perception and object segmentation.
- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often lack high-level semantic understanding and robust closed-loop feedback, leading to inefficient, incorrect, or unsafe behaviors in complex chemical tasks.

## Method

- **RoboChemist** uses a ==dual-loop framework== that integrates a VLM (outer loop) for high-level cognitive functions and a VLA model (inner loop) for low-level physical execution.
- The VLM acts as a planner to decompose tasks, an ==instruction-aware visual prompt== generator for safety-compliant guidance, and a monitor to assess subtask success and compliance, enabling condition-based re-execution.
- ==Instruction-aware visual prompting==, powered by an advanced VLM, provides precise spatial grounding for the VLA model, even when manipulating transparent or hazardous materials.

## Results

- RoboChemist achieved a **23.57%** higher average success rate and a **0.298** higher average compliance rate across seven primitive chemical tasks compared to state-of-the-art VLA baselines.
- The system demonstrated consistent and robust performance in five complex, multi-step chemical experiments, successfully completing tasks with up to five primitive steps where baselines significantly declined.
- It exhibited strong generalization capabilities across novel objects, reagents, containers, experimental workflows, and various environmental conditions without requiring task-specific fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating high-level VLM semantic reasoning with low-level VLA dexterity is crucial for enabling versatile and safety-compliant manipulation in complex chemical environments.
> - Instruction-aware visual prompting provides precise guidance that overcomes the limitations of traditional robotic perception with transparent objects and deformable substances.
> - A VLM-driven closed-loop monitoring and feedback system significantly enhances task robustness and compliance, allowing for recovery from initial failures and ensuring adherence to experimental protocols.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic chemistry aims to automate laboratory procedures, thereby potentially freeing human experts from repetitive or hazardous tasks and accelerating scientific discovery. Despite this potential, the automation of chemical experiments faces significant challenges. These challenges stem from the long-horizon nature of lab protocols, the need for precise manipulation of delicate glassware and deformable substances, and the inherent safety criticality associated with hazardous materials. Successful execution in this domain requires not only task completion but also strict adherence to procedural norms, such as specific grasping points or controlled heating thresholds, to ensure both experimental validity and personnel safety.

Recent advancements in Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have enabled the development of scalable robotic policies across various environments. However, their application in chemical laboratory settings reveals distinct limitations. Previous VLM-based systems, such such as VoxPoser and ReKep, typically rely on depth sensors and object segmentation, which exhibit performance degradation when dealing with transparent labware or deformable substances, common elements in chemistry labs. Conversely, existing VLA models, exemplified by systems like RDT and π0, demonstrate proficiency in action grounding but often lack the capacity for high-level semantic understanding and comprehensive closed-loop feedback. This deficiency can result in unsafe or failed behaviors when performing complex, multi-step chemical protocols.

Within the broader context of robotic automation in chemistry, many existing systems often rely on specialized hardware configurations and pre-defined instructions, which can limit their flexibility and generalization across diverse tasks and experimental setups. While some efforts have partially addressed these limitations, such as Organa and ArChemist, they frequently remain focused on single-arm manipulation and specific scenarios.

RoboChemist positions itself within this landscape by addressing these identified gaps. It proposes a dual-loop framework that integrates VLMs and VLAs, aiming to combine the high-level semantic reasoning capabilities of VLMs with the low-level dexterity of VLAs. This approach specifically seeks to overcome the limitations of prior VLM systems with transparent objects and the lack of semantic feedback in VLA systems, thereby enabling versatile, safety-compliant, and generalizable manipulation in complex chemical environments without requiring specialized hardware or rigid task-specific programming. The work also contributes to the visual prompting research area by introducing an instruction-aware visual prompting strategy that leverages advanced VLMs for precise, safety-compliant guidance in visually ambiguous or sensitive contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic system, termed RoboChemist, capable of autonomously executing complex, long-horizon chemical experiments with a high degree of safety and compliance with laboratory protocols.

The motivation for this work arises from several challenges inherent in traditional and existing robotic approaches to chemical experimentation:

1. **Liberation from Repetitive and Hazardous Tasks:** Human experts frequently engage in repetitive and potentially hazardous laboratory procedures. Automating these tasks can free up human resources for more complex research and mitigate safety risks associated with handling dangerous chemicals or performing delicate operations.
2. **Acceleration of Scientific Discovery:** The manual nature of many chemical experiments can be a bottleneck in research. Robotic chemists have the potential to significantly accelerate scientific discovery by enabling faster throughput, continuous operation, and reproducible experimentation.
3. **Complexity of Chemical Protocols:** Chemical experiments are inherently long-horizon, involving sequences of multiple steps, each requiring precision. They often involve deformable substances (e.g., liquids, powders), delicate glassware, and hazardous materials, all of which necessitate careful and compliant handling.
4. **Requirement for Safety and Compliance:** Beyond mere task completion, chemical experiments demand strict adherence to procedural norms. This includes safe grasping points for instruments, controlled heating thresholds, and appropriate pouring techniques to prevent spills or contamination. Failure to comply with these norms can lead to experimental failure, data invalidity, or safety incidents.
5. **Limitations of Current VLM-based Systems:** Existing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) applied to robotics often rely on depth perception and object segmentation. In a chemical lab, transparent containers (beakers, test tubes) and deformable substances (liquids) pose significant challenges for these sensing modalities, leading to unreliable object recognition and manipulation.
6. **Deficiencies in Existing VLA Models:** While Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models excel at grounding language instructions to low-level actions, they frequently lack high-level semantic understanding and a robust closed-loop feedback mechanism. This can result in actions that are either inefficient, incorrect, or unsafe when confronted with the nuances of complex chemical tasks. Current VLA approaches often yield low success and compliance rates in laboratory settings due to this absence of semantic-level monitoring and corrective feedback.

To address these motivations and overcome the identified limitations, the specific objectives of RoboChemist are:

- To integrate the high-level reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with the low-level action execution of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.
- To implement the VLM as a multi-functional component, serving as a planner for task decomposition, a visual prompt generator for guiding VLA models with task-specific visual cues, and a monitor for assessing subtask success and compliance.
- To develop an instruction-aware visual prompting strategy using advanced VLMs (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL) to enable precise and safe manipulation of transparent and hazardous materials.
- To design a closed-loop system that allows for condition-based re-execution of primitive actions, thereby improving robustness and adaptability in long-horizon tasks.
- To achieve demonstrably higher success rates and compliance rates in both primitive and multi-step chemical tasks compared to existing state-of-the-art VLA baselines.
- To demonstrate strong generalization capabilities to previously unseen reagents, containers, and experimental workflows without requiring task-specific fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoboChemist implements a dual-loop framework that synergistically integrates Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to perform robotic chemical experimentation. This architecture is designed to combine high-level semantic reasoning with low-level dexterous control while ensuring safety and compliance.

#### 3.1. Dual-Loop Framework Overview

The core of RoboChemist is a closed-loop system where a VLM and a VLA model interact.

- **Outer Loop (VLM-centric):** The VLM primarily functions as the high-level cognitive component.
 - **Planner:** Upon receiving a chemical experiment task from a researcher, along with the specified apparatus and reagents, the VLM decomposes the long-horizon task into a sequence of primitive, executable subtasks.
 - **Visual Prompt Generator:** For each identified subtask, the VLM generates detailed operational guidelines, adhering to chemical experiment protocols. It then creates visual prompts—such as bounding boxes or keypoints—that highlight safe grasp points or target regions in the robot's visual field (front-view image). This instruction-aware visual prompting, powered by a VLM like Qwen2.5-VL, enables precise spatial grounding even for transparent or hazardous materials.
 - **Monitor:** After the VLA model attempts a subtask, the VLM observes the current scene images. It evaluates whether the subtask was successfully completed and if it adhered to regulatory compliance. If the task is unsuccessful or incomplete (e.g., pouring until a solution becomes colorless), the VLM instructs the VLA to re-execute the current primitive task, forming a closed-loop feedback mechanism that approximates continuous behavior and ensures safety.

- **Inner Loop (VLA-centric):** The VLA model is responsible for the low-level physical execution.
 - **Actor:** The VLA model receives the visually prompted image from the VLM, observations from other camera viewpoints, and corresponding textual instructions. Using these inputs, it executes the specified primitive task. This setup facilitates goal-conditioned and safety-compliant control based on the VLM's guidance.
 - **Enhanced Inner Loop Training:** To improve the VLA model's robustness and self-correction, the training data includes scenarios where, following an initial unsuccessful attempt, the system automatically performs a second execution attempt. This augments the VLA model's feedback capabilities beyond single-shot successful trials.

#### 3.2. Hardware Platform and Data Collection

- **Hardware:** The system employs a Cobot Magic ALOHA dual-arm robot, providing 7 degrees of freedom per arm. It is equipped with four Intel RealSense D435 depth cameras, from which only RGB data is utilized (from wrist, front, and top-down viewpoints). An NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU handles both data collection and model inference.
- **Data Collection:** A dataset of 400 samples was collected for each of seven defined primitive tasks to fine-tune the VLA model. To ensure generalization, diversity was introduced in experimental scene setups and object selections. For example, liquid pouring tasks varied liquid color, volume, container type (beakers, test tubes, graduated cylinders), and overall environmental layout. Data collection and inference both operate at 20 Hz, with data stored in Parquet or HDF5 formats.

#### 3.3. Model Training and Baselines

- **VLA Model Fine-tuning:** The π0 VLA model serves as the backbone and was fine-tuned using the collected data. Training was conducted for 30K steps on four L20 GPUs. The inputs for fine-tuning included images from the four camera viewpoints, VLM-generated visual prompts (grasp and target points) for the initial experimental scene of each primitive task, diverse language instructions (generated using GPT-4o variations), and the robot's proprioceptive state.
- **Baselines for Comparison:** To evaluate RoboChemist's performance, it was compared against state-of-the-art bimanual manipulation baselines: ACT, RDT, and π0. All baseline models were fine-tuned with the same collected dataset and experimental setup as RoboChemist.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

Two primary metrics were used to assess performance for both primitive and complete tasks (each conducted over 20 trials):

- **Success Rate (SR):** Calculated as the ratio of successful trials to the total number of trials. For complete multi-step tasks, a trial is considered successful only if all constituent primitive tasks are completed successfully.
- **Compliance Rate (CR):** This metric quantifies the precision and adherence to experimental norms. Scores range from 0 (failure) to 1 (successful and compliant), with intermediate scores (e.g., 0.5) for partial success or non-compliant but non-failing actions (e.g., grasping at an incorrect position). Detailed CR criteria were defined for each primitive task (e.g., specific grasp points, correct heating locations, absence of spillage).

#### 3.5. Chemical Tasks

- **Primitive Tasks:** Seven fundamental chemical operations were defined and evaluated: grasping a glass rod, heating a platinum wire, inserting a platinum wire into a solution, pouring liquid, stirring a solution with a glass rod, transferring solid, and pressing a button.
- **Complete Chemical Experiment Tasks:** Five multi-step chemical experiments were selected for evaluation, each comprising 1 to 5 primitive tasks: mixing NaCl and CuSO4 solutions, thermal decomposition of Cu(OH)2, flame test for CuSO4 solution, evaporation of NaCl solution, and acid-base neutralization reaction.
- **Generalizability Evaluation:** The system's ability to generalize was assessed across novel objects, reagents, containers, and workflows that were not explicitly included in the training data. This also included variations in environment (e.g., different platforms, lighting, cluttered scenes, occlusion) and embodiment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

RoboChemist demonstrates improved performance over existing baselines in robotic chemical experimentation, characterized by enhanced success rates, compliance with experimental norms, and robust generalization capabilities.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance in Primitive Tasks

RoboChemist, particularly with its closed-loop system, achieved notable improvements over baseline methods in executing primitive chemical tasks. Across seven primitive tasks, RoboChemist with the closed-loop (w/ CL) achieved a 23.57% higher average success rate and a 0.298 average increase in compliance rate compared to state-of-the-art VLA baselines.
- For "Grasp Glass Rod," RoboChemist w/ CL achieved a 95% success rate and 0.875 compliance rate, significantly outperforming π0 (40% SR, 0.200 CR), RDT (20% SR, 0.100 CR), and ACT (55% SR, 0.325 CR).
- In "Pour Liquid," RoboChemist w/ CL reached 95% SR and 0.800 CR, exceeding π0 (80% SR, 0.475 CR).
- Similar trends were observed across all primitive tasks, where RoboChemist consistently achieved higher SR and CR, indicating more reliable and procedurally compliant actions.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Visual Prompting

The introduction of visual prompting as a guiding mechanism contributed to performance improvements even without the full closed-loop system.
- RoboChemist without the closed-loop (w/o CL), which still incorporated visual prompting, showed better performance than the baseline π0. For instance, in "Pour Liquid," RoboChemist w/o CL achieved 80% SR and 0.663 CR, demonstrating a substantial increase in compliance over π0 (0.475 CR), despite the same success rate.
- A comparative evaluation of visual prompting methods (Table 4) revealed that ReKep, relying on RGB-D cameras, struggled with transparent object reconstruction, leading to lower success rates. MOKA, while using mark-based prompting, showed relatively lower compliance due to less instruction-aware prompt generation. RoboChemist's VLM-based visual prompting strategy, directly annotating 2D visual cues, achieved superior results in both success and compliance.

#### 4.3. Impact of the Closed-Loop System

The integration of the outer closed-loop system, where the VLM monitors and provides feedback, further enhanced performance.
- Comparing "RoboChemist w/o CL" to "RoboChemist w/ CL" in primitive tasks, the closed-loop version consistently showed higher success and compliance rates. For example, "Grasp Glass Rod" improved from 85% SR (w/o CL) to 95% SR (w/ CL) and from 0.750 CR to 0.875 CR. This indicates the closed-loop’s effectiveness in enabling condition-based re-execution and recovery from initial failures.
- Analysis of the inner loop enhancement through varied training data configurations (Appendix A.6) showed that a balanced mix of successful and "second-attempt" trials (e.g., 300 successful / 100 second-attempt) resulted in the highest average success rate for VLA models, demonstrating improved robustness and self-correcting ability.

#### 4.4. Performance in Complete Chemical Tasks

RoboChemist demonstrated consistent and robust performance in multi-step complete chemical experiments.
- While baselines experienced a decline in success rates as task complexity increased, RoboChemist maintained high performance for tasks composed of 2-3 primitive steps and successfully completed more complex tasks involving up to five steps.
- For the "Acid-Base Neutralization Reaction," RoboChemist achieved a 40% SR and 0.300 CR for the final "Add HCl Acid" step (which implies overall task completion), significantly surpassing π0 (5% SR, 0.013 CR).
- The system successfully performed various reactions including NaCl and CuSO4 mixing (observing color changes), thermal decomposition of Cu(OH)2 (observing color change and mist), CuSO4 flame tests (producing green flames), evaporation of NaCl solution (forming crystals), and acid-base neutralization (indicator color change).

#### 4.5. Generalizability

RoboChemist demonstrated robust generalization across multiple dimensions:
- **Primitive Tasks:** It successfully applied learned primitive skills to novel objects and materials not encountered during training (e.g., grasping and placing various labware, stirring solid reagents, heating different wires and test tubes, inserting thermometers). This showed robust skill transfer (Table 6).
- **Complete Tasks:** Leveraging the VLM's chemical knowledge, RoboChemist generalized to perform novel complete chemistry tasks corresponding to the four basic types of chemical reactions (combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement) and inferred physical properties like flame colors for different metal ions. It accurately observed and interpreted phenomena such as vigorous bubbling during decomposition and precipitation formation.
- **Environment and Embodiment:** RoboChemist maintained strong performance in the "Pour Liquid" task under challenging variations, including different robot platforms, occlusions, cluttered scenes, varied lighting conditions, diverse table textures, spatial height changes, and cross-embodiment scenarios (Figure 18). This underscores the robustness enabled by VLM feedback and visual prompting.

#### 4.6. Error Breakdown

An analysis of failure sources (Appendix A.8) from 20 cases indicated that the most frequent errors (35%) arose from visual prompt misalignment in cluttered scenes. VLM monitoring failures and VLA violations of prompt constraints each accounted for 20% of errors, while task decomposition errors (10%) and VLA action failures without recovery (15%) represented smaller proportions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoboChemist framework represents a step towards the development of autonomous and safety-compliant robotic systems for chemical experimentation, holding several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Accelerated Scientific Discovery:** By automating repetitive, labor-intensive, and hazardous chemical procedures, RoboChemist can significantly accelerate the pace of scientific research. It enables continuous experimentation, reduces human error, and facilitates high-throughput screening, potentially leading to faster discovery of new materials, drugs, and chemical processes.
2. **Enhanced Safety in Laboratories:** Chemical laboratories often involve hazardous reagents and delicate operations. RoboChemist's emphasis on safety compliance through instruction-aware visual prompting and closed-loop monitoring can substantially reduce human exposure to dangerous substances and mitigate risks associated with manual handling, contributing to a safer working environment.
3. **Increased Efficiency and Reproducibility:** Automating experimental protocols ensures greater consistency and reproducibility compared to manual execution, which can be subject to variability in human performance. This enhances the reliability of experimental results and streamlines the validation of scientific findings. Furthermore, it liberates human experts to focus on higher-level analytical and creative tasks rather than routine procedures.
4. **Bridging the Gap Between AI and Embodied Robotics:** The dual-loop framework effectively integrates high-level cognitive reasoning from VLMs with low-level physical action grounding from VLAs. This approach addresses a critical challenge in robotics: enabling intelligent agents to understand complex, abstract instructions and translate them into precise, context-aware physical manipulations in unstructured environments. The ability to handle transparent labware and deformable substances, a known weakness for many robotic perception systems, is particularly significant in this context.
5. **Robust Generalization and Flexibility:** RoboChemist's capacity to generalize across unseen reagents, containers, and experimental workflows without extensive task-specific fine-tuning highlights its potential for broad applicability. This flexibility can reduce the overhead of reprogramming for new experiments, making robotic chemists more adaptable and economically viable for diverse research needs. The system's ability to infer and respond to chemical phenomena (e.g., color changes, gas evolution) further underscores its adaptability.
6. **Foundation for Future Robotic Chemists:** This work provides a robust framework and proof-of-concept for the next generation of general-purpose robotic chemists. It lays the groundwork for integrating more advanced manipulation capabilities, improved sensor feedback (e.g., for precise quantification and temporal control), and even autonomous experimental setup assembly, which are identified as current limitations.
7. **Interpretability of Robotic Actions:** The VLM's role as a planner and monitor, explicit in decomposing tasks, generating safety guidelines, and verifying outcomes, offers a degree of interpretability to the robotic system's operations. This can foster trust and facilitate debugging in complex experimental sequences.

In summary, RoboChemist advances the state of robotic chemical experimentation by offering a robust, safety-compliant, and generalizable platform. Its unique dual-loop VLM-VLA integration provides a foundation for enhancing laboratory automation with increased reliability and reduced human intervention, ultimately supporting accelerated scientific discovery.
