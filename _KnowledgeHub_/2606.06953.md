# LIMMT: Less is More for Motion Tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed the LIMMT framework, featuring a General Quality Selection (GQS) pipeline, to curate high-quality motion data for humanoid tracking. This data-centric approach enables training with just 3% of a curated dataset to achieve superior performance (e.g., Any2Track success rate 0.956 vs. 0.942 with full data) and better generalization on both simulated and physical robots, challenging the assumption that more data is always better.

## Problem

- Large, uncurated motion capture datasets for humanoid robot learning often contain physical inconsistencies and noise, corrupting the imitation signal.
- Existing physics-based imitation-Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods struggle to leverage these large datasets, showing limited improvement or even suboptimal solutions.
- Training on extensive motion libraries incurs high computational costs for sampling, curriculum design, and optimization.

## Method

- Propose **LIMMT** (Less Is More for Motion Tracking), a data-centric framework that prioritizes motion data quality over quantity.
- Introduce General Quality Selection (GQS), a three-stage pipeline to systematically curate high-value motion libraries.
- The pipeline includes physics-based feasibility filtering, semantic motion embedding via a Periodic Autoencoder, and global weighted farthest point sampling based on action diversity and complexity.

## Results

- Training with only **3%** of GQS-curated AMASS data consistently outperformed using the full, raw AMASS dataset across multiple tracking systems, achieving higher success rates and lower errors.
- The GQS framework demonstrated plug-and-play generalizability, improving performance for different tracking algorithms and showing robust cross-dataset generalization.
- Policies trained on GQS-curated data successfully transferred zero-shot to the physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, matching or exceeding the performance of policies trained on full datasets, especially in dynamic tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Motion data quality, encompassing physics feasibility, action diversity, and action complexity, is more influential than data quantity for robust humanoid tracking.
> - Eliminating physically infeasible motions is critical, as these "toxic" samples corrupt the learning signal and significantly degrade policy performance.
> - Curated, compact datasets provide cleaner and more informative gradients, guiding the policy towards better solutions more efficiently from early training stages.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robot learning often relies on motion tracking, which involves converting reference motion libraries into physically executable behaviors for locomotion, athletic skills, and complex controllers. The field has observed a rapid increase in the availability of motion capture (MoCap) data, ranging from high-quality studio datasets like LaFAN1 and AMASS to large-scale collections derived from videos using pose or mesh estimation techniques. This trend might suggest that humanoid tracking would benefit from indiscriminate data scaling, similar to advancements seen in vision and language domains.

However, existing physics-based imitation-Reinforcement Learning (RL) systems have not consistently improved with the expansion of uncurated datasets. State-of-the-art tracking methods frequently continue to utilize smaller, high-quality MoCap data. Large, in-the-wild datasets often contain temporal jitter, foot sliding, ground penetration, implausible contacts, and other physical inconsistencies. These artifacts can corrupt the imitation signal, leading to suboptimal solutions or reward hacking in the learning process. Furthermore, training on massive motion libraries incurs significant computational costs associated with reference sampling, curriculum design, and long-horizon optimization. Consequently, increased data volume can introduce noise and complexity, potentially causing learning systems to converge prematurely to unstable solutions.

This research addresses these challenges by adopting a data-centric perspective, questioning the assumption that "more data always leads to better generalization" in physics-based humanoid motion tracking. It contrasts with approaches focused on developing new RL algorithms or network architectures, instead concentrating on the properties of the training data itself. The work positions itself as the first data-centric study specifically investigating motion data quality for physics-based humanoid tracking. It aims to refine the data used for training, thereby improving the efficiency and robustness of motion tracking policies, and offers an alternative strategy to the current trend of simply scaling dataset size.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate "What makes motion data valuable for learning robust humanoid tracking policies?" The authors challenge the conventional belief that simply increasing the quantity of motion data will inherently lead to improved performance in physics-based imitation-RL.

The core motivation stems from the observation that large, uncurated motion datasets often contain significant noise and physically implausible artifacts. These issues can lead to several problems:
- **Corrupted Imitation Signal:** Low-quality or erroneous motion clips can introduce inconsistent and physically meaningless gradients, which can steer policy learning toward brittle or unstable solutions.
- **Computational Inefficiency:** Training on extensive datasets is computationally expensive, increasing the cost of data sampling, curriculum design, and optimization, without a proportional increase in performance.
- **Suboptimal Convergence:** Noisy data can cause learning systems to converge prematurely to local optima that are not robust or generalizable.

The key insight proposed is that **data quality can exert a greater influence than data quantity** because it directly shapes the initial optimization trajectory. High-quality motion targets provide consistent, physically meaningful gradients, promoting stable and effective policy learning from early stages. Conversely, low-quality or redundant motions can waste computational resources and degrade final performance.

To operationalize this insight, the authors define motion data quality along three complementary dimensions:
1. **Physics Feasibility:** This dimension assesses whether a motion can be realized by a rigid-body humanoid without severe artifacts (e.g., prolonged floating, ground penetration, or joint velocity violations). Infeasible motions are considered detrimental as they can distort the representation space and hinder effective learning.
2. **Action Diversity:** This dimension evaluates whether the motion library covers a broad range of distinct behaviors rather than containing repetitive patterns. A lack of diversity can lead to diminishing returns, as redundant motions offer little new information for learning.
3. **Action Complexity:** This dimension considers whether motions provide informative and dynamic supervision, prioritizing segments with higher energy, velocity, or acceleration over static or simple movements. Complex motions are hypothesized to induce richer state-action visitation and stronger learning signals.

The motivation is to develop a principled curation framework, named LIMMT (Less Is More for Motion Tracking), which systematically addresses these three quality dimensions. The framework aims to transform large, noisy motion corpora into compact, high-value training libraries that are more computationally efficient to use and facilitate more robust policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces General Quality Selection (GQS), a three-stage pipeline designed to curate high-quality motion data for humanoid tracking. GQS is structured hierarchically to address feasibility, diversity, and complexity in a specific order.

**Stage I: Physics-based Feasibility Filtering**
This initial stage aims to eliminate physically infeasible motions from the raw dataset, which are considered detrimental to policy learning. The process involves a two-stage evaluation tailored for the target robot:

- **Hard Constraints:** A binary validity check is applied. Motions are discarded if their duration is less than 0.5 seconds (insufficient context) or if joint velocity violations exceed a safety margin of 0.05 rad/s (mechanically impossible movements).
- **Soft Scoring Function:** For motions passing the hard constraints, a quality score $S_{phy}$ is computed based on weighted penalties for six physical violation modes:
 - **Floating ($L_{float}$):** Detects non-physical suspension by measuring the ratio of frames where the robot is continuously airborne.
 - **Ground Penetration ($L_{pen}$):** Measures the average depth of mesh penetration into the floor.
 - **Velocity Violation ($L_{vel}$):** Captures the mean magnitude by which joint velocities exceed hardware limits.
 - **Foot Sliding ($L_{slide}$):** Penalizes horizontal foot velocity when the foot height is below 5cm.
 - **Self-Collision ($L_{col}$):** Penalizes collisions between robot links.
 - **Jerk ($L_{jerk}$):** Measures the rate of change of joint acceleration.
 The total score is calculated as $S_{phy}(T) = 100 - \sum_{i} w_i L_i$, where $w_i$ are weights calibrated through a data-driven sensitivity analysis. Motions with $S_{phy} \geq 90$ are retained, ensuring physical executability. This stage prioritizes filtering harmful artifacts that could distort downstream representation learning.

**Stage II: Semantic Motion Embedding**
After filtering, this stage aims to learn a continuous motion manifold that captures the structural and rhythmic similarity of behaviors, independent of superficial Euclidean pose differences.

- **Periodic Autoencoder (PAE):** A PAE is used to embed motions. The network processes a temporal window (e.g., 4.0 seconds) of joint positions, velocities, and root velocities. Instead of a static latent vector, the encoder maps the input to dynamic parameters in a frequency domain: Amplitude (A), Frequency (F), Phase Shift (phi), and Offset (b), each as a k-dimensional vector (k=8). A sinusoidal prior is used to analytically reconstruct the latent trajectory. The decoder then reconstructs the motion trajectory from these explicit latent dynamics.
- **Phase-Robust Global Descriptors:** Unlike VAEs, the PAE uses a deterministic mapping optimized via reconstruction loss, preserving physical scale and temporal frequency.
- **Global Embedding Extraction:** For global sampling, a time-invariant representation is required. The dynamic signature of a motion is primarily determined by its intensity (Amplitude) and pace (Frequency). A local descriptor $h_w = [A_w, F_w]$ is constructed for each window, and the final global embedding for a full trajectory is obtained by temporal averaging: $z_{global} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{w=1}^N [A_w, F_w]$. This yields a compact, phase-invariant embedding that provides a metric space for global sampling.

**Stage III: Global Weighted Farthest Point Sampling (FPS)**
This final stage selects a compact training library from the semantically embedded motions, considering both behavioral coverage and dynamic complexity.

- **Motion Complexity Metric:** The training value of each motion is quantified by its physical attributes. Complexity $C(x)$ is defined as a weighted combination of kinetic energy and acceleration magnitude: $C(x) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (\| \dot{q}_t \|_2^2 + \lambda \| \ddot{q}_t \|_2^2)$, where $\dot{q}_t$ and $\ddot{q}_t$ are joint velocities and accelerations. This metric is then rank-normalized to $\hat{C}(x) \in$.
- **Sampling Strategy:** Global Weighted FPS is performed. The selected set $S$ is initialized with the motion exhibiting the highest complexity (anchor). In subsequent iterations, a candidate motion $u$ is selected that maximizes a hybrid score: $Score(u) = \alpha \cdot \hat{D}(u, S) + (1-\alpha) \cdot \hat{C}(u)$, where $\hat{D}(u, S)$ is the normalized distance to the nearest neighbor in $S$ within the latent space, and $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter balancing spatial exploration (diversity) and complexity maximization. This approach ensures broad behavioral coverage while prioritizing dynamically richer motions when candidates are geometrically similar.

The staged design is critical: feasibility filtering occurs first to prevent physically broken motions from influencing the semantic embedding. Embedding learning operates on feasible data to ensure a meaningful manifold. Complexity weighting is applied last to avoid over-selecting high-energy artifacts. This hierarchy allows GQS to convert a large, noisy corpus into a compact, high-value training set.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that validate the effectiveness of the LIMMT framework and its GQS pipeline across various experimental setups.

**Main Results on AMASS Dataset (Table 1, Figure 2):**
- **"Less-is-More" Effect:** Training with only 3% of the AMASS dataset using GQS (Less Is More for Motion Tracking) consistently outperformed training with the full, raw AMASS dataset across multiple metrics. For Any2Track, GQS 3% achieved a Success Rate (SR) of 0.956 and an MPJPE of 0.108 rad, surpassing the Full Data baseline (SR 0.942, MPJPE 0.114 rad). Similarly, for TWIST2, GQS 3% achieved SR 0.861 and MPJPE 0.092 rad, compared to the Full Data baseline (SR 0.825, MPJPE 0.099 rad).
- **Failure of Random Subsampling:** A random 3% subsample of the raw dataset resulted in a catastrophic performance collapse (Any2Track SR 0.838, TWIST2 SR 0.649), indicating that data reduction alone is insufficient; intelligent curation is critical.
- **Plug-and-Play Generalizability:** The performance gains from GQS were observed across two distinct state-of-the-art tracking systems, Any2Track and TWIST2, demonstrating that GQS improves the fundamental training signal rather than exploiting specific algorithm idiosyncrasies.
- **Impact of Physics Filtering:** Even applying only the physics filter (Stage I) to 100% of the AMASS data improved the Success Rate (e.g., Any2Track SR from 0.942 to 0.954), confirming the importance of removing infeasible motions.
- **Optimal Data Ratio:** GQS 10% achieved the highest Success Rate for Any2Track (0.959) and TWIST2 (0.868), suggesting an optimal trade-off between efficiency and performance at a moderate data reduction.

**Component Analysis (Table 2, Figure 3):**
- **Criticality of Physics Filtering:** Removing the physics-based feasibility filter (Stage I) led to a significant performance drop (e.g., Any2Track SR at 3% data falling from 0.956 to 0.911), confirming its crucial role in eliminating toxic samples that hinder policy learning, especially in low-data regimes.
- **Synergistic Effect of Diversity and Complexity:** Policies trained with only complexity weighting or vanilla FPS (without complexity bias) performed suboptimally compared to the full GQS framework, indicating that behavioral diversity and dynamic complexity work together to achieve superior performance.
- **Improved Optimization Trajectory:** Analysis of training dynamics (Figure 3) showed that GQS-curated data resulted in higher rewards and lower tracking errors from the early stages of training (before 0.5 billion steps), suggesting that curated data provides cleaner gradients that guide the policy toward better solutions sooner.

**Cross-Dataset Generalization (Figure 4):**
- **In-Domain Precision on PHUMA:** On the PHUMA dataset, GQS consistently achieved lower MPJPE across various data ratios compared to using the full dataset, even when the Success Rate was near saturation. GQS surpassed the performance ceiling using only 30% of the data.
- **Cross-Domain Robustness:** When policies trained on PHUMA were evaluated zero-shot on the unseen AMASS dataset, the 10% GQS-curated subset outperformed the full PHUMA dataset in Success Rate (92.8% vs 91.0%). This indicated that GQS-curation prevented overfitting to source domain artifacts and improved generalization.

**Physical Score Analysis (Figure 5, Table 3, Figure 6):**
- **Non-Monotonic Relationship:** Performance did not peak with the highest-scoring (most physically plausible) motions. Instead, it peaked at the 60-70% percentile of physical scores (SR 96.3%), suggesting that perfectly feasible motions may lack dynamic richness. The physics filter effectively identified toxic data but did not alone rank the utility of feasible motions for learning.
- **Data-Driven Weight Calibration:** Sensitivity analysis showed that filtering "toxic" metrics like Floating and Foot Sliding significantly improved performance, justifying high penalty weights. Conversely, filtering high-Velocity or high-Jerk motions degraded performance, indicating these "friendly" violent motions are valuable for learning robust dynamics and should receive low weights.

**Additional Experiments (Appendix B):**
- **Global vs. Clustered Selection (Figure 7):** Global Weighted FPS consistently outperformed cluster-based selection, particularly in low-data regimes, by implicitly re-balancing the data distribution and prioritizing information density rather than inheriting dataset imbalances.
- **Real-World Deployment (Figure 8, Table 6):** Policies trained on 10% GQS-curated data successfully transferred to the Unitree G1 humanoid robot without fine-tuning. They matched or surpassed Full-Data policies in real-world tracking Success Rate and MPJPE across daily, expressive, and athletic motion categories, particularly excelling in dynamically demanding tasks.
- **Cross-Corpus Evaluation on LaFAN1 (Table 7):** Training on a small, stylistically different dataset (LaFAN1) and testing on AMASS still yielded modest but consistent improvements with GQS, suggesting the data-centric advantage generalizes across domain gaps.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity (Table 8):** The performance was stable within reasonable ranges for key hyperparameters (physics threshold, foot-sliding threshold, PAE window size, and embedding dimension), indicating the robustness of the GQS design.
- **Adaptive Ratio Selection (Table 9):** A lightweight heuristic, Adaptive Ratio Selection (ARS), was introduced to estimate an optimal subset ratio based on the Effective Diversity Ratio (EDR) of the HME embeddings. ARS predictions closely matched empirically optimal ratios on both AMASS and PHUMA.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LIMMT framework and its GQS pipeline present a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robot learning by challenging the prevalent assumption that simply scaling motion data quantity leads to proportional improvements in physics-based motion tracking. The work establishes a "Less-Is-More" paradigm, demonstrating that strategic data curation can yield superior performance with substantially reduced data volumes.

The significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Paradigm Shift in Data Utilization:** By formally defining and operationalizing motion data quality through physics feasibility, action diversity, and action complexity, the research provides a principled alternative to indiscriminate dataset scaling. This redefinition of "quality over quantity" can guide future data acquisition and curation efforts, moving away from simply collecting more data towards collecting higher-value data.
- **Enhanced Learning Efficiency:** Demonstrating that training with as little as 3% of a curated dataset can outperform training with the full, raw corpus has substantial implications for computational costs. Reduced training data translates to faster training times, lower energy consumption, and decreased demands on computational resources (e.g., fewer GPUs and shorter training steps). This makes advanced humanoid control more accessible and sustainable for researchers and developers.
- **Improved Policy Robustness and Generalization:** The curated datasets lead to policies with higher success rates, lower tracking errors (MPJPE), and better generalization capabilities. This is evident in the plug-and-play performance across different tracking algorithms (Any2Track, TWIST2), cross-dataset generalization to PHUMA, and particularly in the successful zero-shot transfer and improved real-world tracking on the physical Unitree G1 robot. The framework's ability to filter out physically infeasible motions and prioritize dynamically rich behaviors contributes to policies that are more stable and transferable to real-world conditions.
- **Actionable Curation Framework:** The GQS pipeline provides a structured, interpretable, and adaptable framework for motion data curation. Its three-stage design (physics filtering, semantic embedding, complexity-weighted sampling) offers a clear methodology that can be adopted and integrated into existing tracking systems. The data-driven insights for weight calibration and the proposed Adaptive Ratio Selection heuristic further enhance its practical utility.
- **Implications for Future Research:** This work opens new avenues for research into data-centric AI for robotics. It suggests exploring methods for repairing low-quality motions or distilling knowledge from large, noisy corpora. The concept of applying quality-focused curation to other robotic learning domains, such as Vision-Language-Action models in robotic arm manipulation, is also proposed as a future direction, indicating the broad applicability of the core ideas.

In summary, LIMMT offers a robust and efficient approach to motion data curation, leading to more performant and generalizable humanoid tracking policies. It highlights the critical importance of data quality in complex physical simulation and real-world robot deployment, setting a new benchmark for data preparation in physics-based reinforcement learning.
