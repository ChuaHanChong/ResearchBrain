# Soft Robotic Exogloves for Dexterous Mobility -- Towards Personalized Rehabilitation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Stevens Institute of Technology engineered a personalized soft robotic exoglove for hand rehabilitation, utilizing 3D topological scans to precisely fit individual hand anatomy and integrating a closed-loop pressure control system. The system achieved accurate pressure regulation (4.60% overshoot, 0.0272 psi steady-state error) and demonstrated robust actuation of metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints, while identifying that a strain-limiting layer hindered optimal kinematic alignment.

## Problem

- Existing soft robotic gloves often rely on standardized actuator geometries, leading to misalignment with individual finger joints, reduced comfort, and decreased rehabilitation effectiveness.
- Previous personalization methods for soft exogloves were limited by manual biomechanical measurements or less precise 2D images, lacking the precision of 3D anatomical data for precise actuator-to-joint alignment.
- Hand rehabilitation requires precise control over multiple degrees of freedom and fine motor movements, which standardized or imprecisely personalized devices struggle to provide effectively.

## Method

- Developed a personalized soft robotic exoglove design based on 3D topological scans of an individual's hand to customize actuator dimensions and a rigid dorsal base.
- Fabricated multi-layer soft pneumatic actuators (==fast PneuNet (fPN)== design) with embedded flex sensors and integrated them onto a 3D-printed dorsal base.
- Implemented a ==closed-loop PID controller== using proportional valves for accurate and stable internal pressure regulation of the pneumatic actuators.

## Results

- The personalized design achieved precise alignment of actuator bending regions with the MCP and PIP joints, demonstrating robust operation with maximum von Mises stress (**1.74 MPa** at **150 kPa** pressure) well below silicone tensile strength.
- FEA simulations revealed that while joint mobilization was achieved, the actuator applied non-ideal compressive axial forces to the proximal phalanx, indicating areas for design improvement.
- The closed-loop PID pressure controller demonstrated effective performance with a rise time of **0.937 s**, a settling time of **1.359 s**, a maximum overshoot of **4.60%**, and a steady-state error of **0.0272 psi**, exhibiting compliance and disturbance rejection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - 3D topological scanning provides the necessary precision for anatomical personalization, enabling superior actuator-to-joint alignment for effective finger movement assistance.
> - Finite Element Analysis (FEA) can identify inefficiencies in actuator-to-finger force transmission (e.g., non-ideal axial compression), guiding future design optimizations for more effective joint rotation.
> - A fabric strain-limiting layer, initially conceived for structural integrity, can paradoxically hinder kinematic alignment by preventing necessary axial elongation of the soft actuator during finger flexion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of rehabilitation robotics, specifically focusing on soft robotic technologies for upper-limb and hand rehabilitation. Rehabilitation robotics aims to restore motor function in individuals affected by neuromuscular diseases by providing repeatable and task-oriented therapy. Soft robotics is increasingly utilized in this domain due to its inherent compliance and enhanced safety, which are beneficial for human-robot interaction.

Upper-limb rehabilitation, particularly hand rehabilitation, presents distinct challenges compared to lower-limb systems. Hand rehabilitation requires addressing multiple degrees of freedom, precise joint coordination, and fine motor control, often necessitating devices that can assist delicate finger movements. Wearable robotic gloves have emerged as a solution for assisting finger motion in rehabilitation settings.

Existing soft robotic gloves employ various actuation strategies. Cable-driven systems, which mimic biological tendons, offer lightweight designs suitable for grasping tasks but can involve complex routing, friction, backlash, and limitations in scalability and personalization. Pneumatically actuated soft gloves provide an alternative, characterized by simpler mechanisms and greater compliance, making them suitable for wearable rehabilitation devices.

Within pneumatic soft exogloves, several actuator types exist:
- **Fiber-reinforced actuators:** These use an elastomeric body wrapped with fibers, often being bulky and having limited range of motion.
- **Textile-based actuators:** These consist of a pneumatic bladder encapsulated in textiles, offering a lightweight option but often involving complex fabrication processes that can hinder scalability.
- **Elastomeric actuators:** Designs such as the PneuNet actuators, particularly the fast PneuNet (fPN) design, encode bending behavior through geometric chamber design, allowing for rapid bending, a greater range of motion, and targeted bending toward specific finger joints.

While these approaches have demonstrated some effectiveness, a significant limitation identified in the existing landscape is the reliance on standardized actuator geometries that are not tailored to individual hand anatomy. This can lead to misalignment between the actuator's bending regions and the human finger joints, potentially reducing user comfort and the overall effectiveness of the rehabilitation. Although some previous research has explored personalized soft pneumatic exogloves, the personalization methods often relied on manual biomechanical measurements or two-dimensional rectified images, which may lack the precision offered by three-dimensional anatomical data.

This research addresses these gaps by presenting a pneumatically actuated soft robotic exoglove explicitly designed for anatomical personalization using 3D topological scans. It aims to improve actuator-to-joint alignment, enhance comfort, and facilitate more effective assistance for dexterous manipulation and fine motor skill rehabilitation, thereby advancing the state of personalized soft robotic rehabilitation devices.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to design, fabricate, model, and test a personalized pneumatically-actuated soft robotic exoglove. This overarching goal was driven by the motivation to develop rehabilitation devices that overcome the limitations of standardized designs, particularly concerning their effectiveness for fine motor control and dexterous manipulation.

The specific objectives and their underlying motivations included:

- **Personalized Design and Fabrication using 3D Scans:** A key objective was to precisely tailor the exoglove to an individual user's hand anatomy. This involved utilizing 3D topological scans of a participant's hand to customize the dimensions and joint placement of the pneumatic actuators and to model a rigid dorsal base that conforms to the hand's contours. The motivation for this approach was to address the issue of misalignment between standardized actuator geometries and individual human finger joints. Such misalignment in generic designs can lead to reduced comfort, inefficient force transmission, and compromised effectiveness, particularly for intricate finger movements essential for dexterous tasks and fine motor skill rehabilitation. By customizing the actuator geometry and joint placement, the researchers aimed to ensure that the bending regions of the actuators align accurately with the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints of the finger.

- **Integration of Sensing Capabilities:** The researchers aimed to embed flex sensors within the actuators to enable the measurement of joint angles. This objective was motivated by the need for quantitative feedback regarding the extent of finger flexion, which is crucial for monitoring rehabilitation progress and for implementing future closed-loop control strategies (e.g., position or force control).

- **Modeling Physical Human-Robot Interaction (pHRI):** A detailed objective was to perform Finite Element Analysis (FEA) to study the pHRI between the personalized soft actuator and a simplified biomechanical finger model derived from the individual's 3D scans. This was motivated by the desire to gain a deeper understanding of the forces and deformations occurring at the human-robot interface. Specifically, the researchers aimed to analyze the interaction of two active degrees of freedom (MCP and PIP joints) in series to identify the viability of fPN actuator joints as functional modules and to characterize the distribution of contact forces on the finger, distinguishing between desired shear forces for joint rotation and potentially undesirable axial compressive forces. This analysis provides insights for design optimization.

- **Development and Evaluation of Pneumatic Pressure Control:** The research aimed to implement and test a closed-loop PID controller for regulating the internal pressure of the soft pneumatic actuators. This objective was motivated by the necessity of achieving accurate and targeted mobility of the MCP and PIP joints. Pressure control was considered a fundamental step and a benchmark for future, more advanced control strategies such as position or force control, while also inherently providing an implicit safety mechanism by limiting applied forces through pressure limits. The motivation was to demonstrate stable, accurate, and robust control performance under static and dynamic reference inputs, suitable for human-robot interaction scenarios in rehabilitation.

Collectively, these objectives were motivated by the goal of laying a groundwork for optimizing exoglove designs to provide effective assistance in dexterous manipulation and neuromuscular rehabilitation of fine motor skills, ultimately striving towards a new level of neuroplasticity regeneration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research encompassed design, fabrication, modeling, and experimental testing phases, all centered on anatomical personalization for a single participant.

**A. Design Phase:**
The design process initiated with 3D topological scans of Participant A's left hand using an Artec Eva 3D scanner. These scans provided precise anatomical measurements for customizing the exoglove components.
- **Actuator Design:** The soft bending actuators were based on the fast PneuNet (fPN) design, characterized by external gaps between air chambers to achieve high bending angles and rapid actuation. Each actuator featured two joints, specifically designed to target the MCP and PIP joints of the finger. The number of air chambers per joint was tailored to the size of the corresponding finger joint (six chambers for the MCP joint, four for the smaller PIP joint). The distance between these actuator joints was determined by the length of the proximal phalanx, ensuring alignment with the finger's anatomy. The actuator structure comprised three layers: a main layer (containing air chambers and inlet), a base layer (embedding a flex sensor and a cotton fabric strain-limiting layer), and a thin silicone sealing layer to combine them.
- **Dorsal Base Design:** The rigid dorsal base, designed to secure the actuators and conform to the hand's contours, was derived directly from the 3D hand scan. The raw point cloud data was processed in Meshmixer for solidification and cropping, then imported into SolidWorks. A negative imprint of the dorsal hand was created and subsequently used to generate a customized shell. The base design included clamps for anchoring and aligning actuators, clips for organizing tubing and wiring, and loops for Velcro straps to secure the glove to the hand.

**B. Fabrication Phase:**
- **Dorsal Base Fabrication:** The exoglove base was 3D printed using standard polylactic acid (PLA) plastic on a Bambu Lab X1 Carbon FDM printer with 15% infill.
- **Actuator Fabrication:** The soft actuators were fabricated using silicone mold casting. Molds for the main and base layers were 3D printed from PLA. The chosen silicone material was Sorta Clear 40 (Smooth-On, Inc.), a two-part platinum cure silicone rubber with a Shore hardness of 40A.
 - **Air Inlets:** Fabricated and cured first, then affixed to the main layer mold.
 - **Main Layer:** Silicone (Sorta Clear 40) was poured into the main layer mold, with Velcro blocks inserted to prevent air entrapment.
 - **Base Layer:** Silicone was poured into the base bath, followed by the placement of a cotton fabric (strain-limiting layer) under tension. A flex sensor was then positioned in the middle of the fabric, and additional silicone was poured.
 - **Sealing Layer:** After curing, the main layer was demolded. The base layer, remaining in its mold, received a thin layer of uncured silicone, onto which the main layer was carefully placed for alignment and bonding.
 - Post-curing, the actuator was demolded, trimmed, and pneumatic tubing was inserted and secured.

**C. Analysis and Results Phase:**
- **Flex Sensor Calibration:** A 3D-printed test jig was used to calibrate the embedded flex sensors. Resistance measurements were taken across a bending range of 0-90 degrees in 10-degree increments. The flex sensor was integrated into a voltage divider circuit connected to an Arduino Mega for data acquisition. A second-order polynomial function was fitted to convert voltage readings into angle measurements, demonstrating an accuracy of ±5 degrees, with higher accuracy at smaller angles (0-50 degrees).
- **Finite Element Analysis (FEA):** FEM simulations were conducted using Ansys Workbench.
 - **Free Bending Actuator:** A static structural analysis was performed on a single actuator. A ramp pressure of 0 to 150 kPa was applied to internal faces. The Sorta Clear 40 silicone was modeled as a hyperelastic material using a third-order Yeoh model. Frictionless contacts were defined between external air chamber faces to account for expansion. This analyzed the von Mises stress distribution and deformation under pressure.
 - **Physical Human-Robot Interaction (pHRI):** Another static structural analysis simulated the interaction between the soft actuator and a simplified biomechanical finger model of Participant A. The finger model was a singular part with living hinges representing the MCP and PIP joints. The actuator was positioned dorsally as it would be worn. The finger material was modeled as Ecoflex 00-50 (third-order Yeoh model) to approximate human flesh. Frictionless contacts were established between the actuator and finger surfaces. An internal pressure ramp of 0 to 40 kPa (5.8 psi) was applied to the actuator. The simulation analyzed von Mises stress plots on both the actuator and the finger model, focusing on contact forces and joint bending. Visual comparisons were made with physical testing results at 20 psi.
- **Personalization Experiments (Strain-Limiting Layer):** Comparative physical tests were conducted on actuators with and without the fabric strain-limiting layer. The objective was to observe the kinematic alignment between the actuator joints and finger joints during inflation and bending.
- **Pneumatic Control and Actuation:** A closed-loop PID controller was implemented for pressure regulation. The pneumatic control station from previous work was modified by replacing on/off valves with two 2/2 proportional valves to enhance control smoothness. A pressure sensor provided feedback, and safety protocols included automatic air exhaustion if pressure exceeded 40 psi (also the pump's mechanical limit). Controller gains were empirically tuned.
 - **Performance Evaluation:** Experiments included tracking a staircase pressure reference, a sinusoidal pressure command, and a step response with external disturbances (another person pushing against the actuated finger). Key metrics such as rise time, settling time, maximum overshoot, and steady-state error were recorded for the step response.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings across the design, modeling, and experimental phases:

- **Anatomical Personalization and Fabrication:**
 - Topological 3D scans enabled the precise tailoring of the exoglove components to the individual participant's hand anatomy. This included customizing the dimensions of the pneumatic actuators to align their bending regions with the MCP and PIP joints, and successfully modeling the rigid dorsal base to conform to the contours of the dorsal palm.
 - The multi-layer fabrication process for the soft actuators using silicone mold casting was successful in producing the desired fPN geometries with embedded sensors.

- **Actuator Mechanical Robustness (FEA - Free Bending):**
 - FEA simulations of the actuator under free bending conditions indicated that the chosen fPN design is robust. When subjected to an internal pressure of 150 kPa, the maximum von Mises stress observed on the actuator was approximately 1.74 MPa. This value is substantially below the tensile strength of Sorta Clear 40 silicone (5.52 MPa), suggesting safe operation at the tested pressure levels.

- **Physical Human-Robot Interaction (pHRI) Analysis (FEA):**
 - The anatomically personalized biomechanical finger model, combined with FEA, provided a framework for analyzing the pHRI.
 - Simulations, applying up to 40 kPa (5.8 psi) internal pressure, demonstrated that the actuator could achieve joint mobilization. However, the analysis identified that the actuator applied non-ideal compressive axial forces to the proximal phalanx, in addition to desired shear forces on the middle phalanx. This suggests an inefficiency in the current design's force transmission for finger joint rotation.
 - A visual comparison between the FEA results and physical testing of the exoglove (at 20 psi) showed comparable overall pHRI behavior, supporting the qualitative validity of the simplified biomechanical model.

- **Impact of Strain-Limiting Layer:**
 - Experimental testing revealed that the presence of the fabric strain-limiting layer in the actuator's base hindered performance. When the actuator bent, the strain-limiting layer prevented the actuator from stretching axially, causing misalignment between the actuator's bending joints and the corresponding human finger joints (e.g., the finger's PIP joint would slip past the actuator joint).
 - Conversely, actuators fabricated without the strain-limiting layer maintained better alignment with the finger joints during actuation, as the silicone's intrinsic elasticity allowed for some necessary axial elongation for concentric wrapping around the flexed human joint. This indicated that the strain-limiting layer was not required for bending and, in fact, negatively impacted kinematic alignment for this specific application.

- **Pneumatic Control and Actuation Performance:**
 - The implemented closed-loop PID pressure controller, utilizing proportional valves, demonstrated accurate and targeted control over the internal pressure of the soft pneumatic actuators.
 - **Staircase response:** Showed stable regulation across discrete pressure setpoints.
 - **Sinusoidal response:** Demonstrated effective dynamic tracking, with the measured pressure closely following the reference signal with good phase alignment.
 - **Step response with disturbance rejection:** The controller exhibited rapid rejection of external perturbations (e.g., from another person pushing the finger), maintaining the desired pressure and configuration while providing compliance.
 - **Quantitative Step Response Metrics:** Achieved a rise time of 0.937 s, a settling time of 1.359 s, a maximum overshoot of 4.60%, and a steady-state error of 0.0272 psi. These metrics indicate a fast transient response and accurate pressure tracking suitable for rehabilitation scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable contribution to the field of rehabilitation robotics by advancing the personalization of soft robotic exogloves for dexterous mobility. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Personalization for Improved Effectiveness:** The work establishes a comprehensive methodology for anatomically personalizing soft robotic exogloves using 3D topological scans. This approach moves beyond standardized designs or less precise 2D measurements, directly addressing the critical issue of actuator-to-joint misalignment. By achieving superior structural conformability and kinematic alignment with individual hand anatomy, the research lays a foundation for more effective and comfortable hand rehabilitation, particularly for fine motor skills requiring precise joint articulation.

- **Informative Design Optimization through pHRI Modeling:** The integration of Finite Element Analysis (FEA) to model physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) provides a valuable analytical framework. The identification of non-ideal compressive axial forces on the proximal phalanx, alongside desired shear forces, serves as a direct benchmark for future design optimization. This insight is crucial for developing actuators that more efficiently and effectively bend finger joints, maximizing therapeutic benefit while potentially minimizing discomfort or unintended forces.

- **Advancing Actuator Design and Performance:** The experimental finding regarding the detrimental effect of the strain-limiting layer on kinematic alignment offers a significant design improvement. Removing this layer allows for necessary axial elongation, enabling the actuator to conform more effectively to the flexed human joint. This finding directly informs the development of future soft actuators that better integrate with human kinematics.

- **Robust and Safe Control for Human Interaction:** The successful implementation and evaluation of closed-loop PID pressure control demonstrate the feasibility of achieving accurate, stable, and dynamically responsive actuation. The inherent safety feature of implicitly limiting applied force through pressure limits, along with the system's compliance and robustness to external disturbances, underscores its suitability for close human-robot interaction in rehabilitation settings. This robust control system is a crucial prerequisite for developing sophisticated therapeutic interventions.

- **Pathway Towards Mass Customization and Accessibility:** By outlining steps for a digital manufacturing protocol using 3D scans, this work provides a scalable framework for future mass customization of rehabilitation devices. This has the potential to make personalized rehabilitation technologies more accessible to a wider patient population, moving beyond one-off custom solutions and facilitating the creation of devices precisely matched to individual needs.

- **Enabling Future Rehabilitation and Assistance:** The demonstrated ability to provide high-degree-of-freedom, personalized joint articulation for MCP and PIP joints represents a promising step towards enabling assistance in dexterous manipulation and neurorehabilitation of fine motor skills. This technology has the potential to aid individuals in performing activities of daily living and to facilitate neuroplasticity regeneration, offering a new avenue for functional recovery in individuals with neuromuscular diseases.

In conclusion, this research significantly contributes to the development of personalized, effective, and safe soft robotic exogloves, offering a foundation for future advancements in neurorehabilitation and assistive technologies for hand function.
