# Gait-Adaptive Perceptive Humanoid Locomotion with Real-Time Under-Base Terrain Reconstruction

> [!summary] Summary
> A perceptive locomotion framework allows a humanoid robot to navigate complex terrains by integrating real-time under-base terrain reconstruction from a downward-looking depth camera with a unified reinforcement learning policy that controls joint targets and gait frequency. This approach enabled the Limx Oli robot to achieve robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for omnidirectional locomotion on various real-world obstacles like stairs and gaps.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with stable locomotion on complex and unstructured terrains due to inherent instability and limited awareness of immediate ground conditions.
- Existing locomotion controllers either lack environmental perception or suffer from outdated, incomplete, or high-latency terrain information directly beneath the robot's base and feet.
- Decoupled gait timing and whole-body control limit a robot's ability to precisely adapt its stepping patterns in response to intricate terrain geometries.

## Method

- A downward-looking depth camera and U-Net architecture perform real-time (50 Hz) reconstruction of dense, egocentric under-base height maps, specifically designed to overcome self-occlusions.
- A unified reinforcement learning policy outputs both whole-body joint targets and a scalar gait frequency, enabling intrinsic adaptation of step timing to commanded motion and local terrain.
- A Successive Teacher–Student (S-TS) training scheme efficiently transfers knowledge from privileged simulation observations to realistic partial observations for robust sim-to-real deployment.

## Results

- The perception module achieved robust real-time under-base height map reconstruction, effectively filling occlusions and preserving sharp terrain boundaries.
- The S-TS training scheme demonstrated superior performance over alternative methods, leading to higher success rates in simulated complex terrains and more effective knowledge transfer.
- The Limx Oli humanoid robot exhibited stable zero-shot sim-to-real omnidirectional locomotion on diverse real-world terrains, including forward/backward/sideways stair traversal and stepping over a 46 cm gap.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A perception module focused on dense, under-base terrain reconstruction, particularly with an auxiliary edge prediction head, is critical for accurately capturing sharp terrain features relevant for humanoid locomotion.
> - Integrating gait frequency as a learned action within a unified policy allows for dynamic and adaptive step timing, crucial for navigating varying terrain complexities.
> - The Successive Teacher-Student training strategy efficiently guides the learning of complex perceptive policies from partial observations by leveraging privileged information during early training stages.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid locomotion on complex and unstructured terrains remains a grand challenge in robotics. Unlike quadrupedal robots, humanoids possess a high center of mass and a small support polygon, making them inherently less stable and more sensitive to terrain irregularities and mistimed steps. This paper directly addresses the limitations of current state-of-the-art methods in navigating such environments.

The existing research landscape can be broadly categorized into:

- **Proprioception-only Reinforcement Learning (RL) Controllers:** Recent advancements in RL have enabled impressive bipedal and humanoid controllers that achieve agile, dynamic locomotion and robustness to disturbances on flat or mildly uneven terrain. However, these "blind" policies, exemplified by works like those in, primarily act as fall-prevention systems rather than deliberate planners for footholds and gait patterns on truly complex terrains like long staircases or wide gaps. They lack explicit awareness of the external environment.
- **Perception-Enhanced Locomotion:** To overcome the limitations of "blind" policies, researchers have integrated exteroceptive sensors.
 - **Forward-facing depth cameras**– process visual information to infer traversable terrain. However, their narrow field of view and reliance on temporal memory often lead to outdated terrain information directly beneath the robot or around its feet, especially during turns, slowdowns, or backward motion. This makes them unsuitable for omnidirectional locomotion on intricate obstacles.
 - **LiDAR-based elevation maps**– offer broader coverage by fusing LiDAR scans with odometry. While powerful, they typically require a separate, complex mapping and pose-estimation pipeline, which introduces latency, computational overhead, and can suffer from drift or incomplete coverage in the critical region directly beneath the robot's base and feet.
- **Gait Timing and Modulation:** Many locomotion controllers treat gait timing (step frequency/phase) as an external, pre-specified, or simply modulated signal. This decoupling of gait from whole-body motion and terrain perception limits the robot's ability to adapt its stepping precisely and deliberately in response to complex terrain. While some learning-based methods embed gait-phase actions, they are often purely proprioception-driven and primarily for disturbance rejection, not for terrain-aware gait adaptation.

This work specifically addresses the gaps in these existing approaches by proposing a framework that achieves **tight integration** of terrain perception, gait timing, and whole-body control. It recognizes the critical need for accurate, real-time sensing of the *under-base terrain* and for the policy to *jointly learn* gait adaptation alongside joint commands, rather than treating them as separate problems. By doing so, it aims to move humanoid locomotion beyond mere robustness on flat ground to deliberate and adaptive navigation of highly structured and challenging environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties full-sized humanoid robots face when navigating complex terrains. The authors highlight that even with advanced control techniques, the challenges of limited perception, ambiguous terrain cues, and inadequate gait adaptation often lead to balance loss on long staircases or large gaps.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a Perceptive Locomotion Framework with Adaptive Gait Control:** The overarching goal is to create a unified system that merges terrain sensing, gait regulation, and whole-body control into a single, end-to-end reinforcement learning policy. This aims to overcome the limitations of decoupled perception and gait generation modules.
2. **Introduce an Under-Base Terrain Perception Module:** A specific objective is to design a perception system tailored for humanoid walking that focuses on the terrain directly beneath the robot's base and around its feet. This module must provide real-time, dense, and accurate terrain information, particularly in regions prone to self-occlusion by the robot's body and legs.
3. **Design a Unified Policy for Joint and Gait Control:** The research aims to create a single policy network that simultaneously outputs both whole-body joint targets and a scalar gait-frequency signal. This allows the policy to learn to regulate step timing and whole-body posture jointly, enabling intrinsic adaptation of gait rhythm to commanded motion and local terrain geometry.
4. **Employ an Efficient Training Scheme for Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A practical objective is to develop a stable and data-efficient method for training complex perceptive policies. This involves transferring knowledge from privileged (full-information) observations in simulation to realistic partial and noisy observations encountered during real-world deployment. The Successive Teacher–Student training scheme is proposed to achieve this.

By achieving these objectives, the authors motivate a more deliberate, precise, and adaptive form of humanoid locomotion capable of handling highly challenging environments beyond what current "blind" or loosely coupled perceptive controllers can manage.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is centered around a novel perceptive locomotion framework that integrates real-time terrain reconstruction, a unified reinforcement learning policy for joint and gait control, and an efficient teacher-student training architecture.

**A. Under-Base Terrain Perception Module:**
The core of the perceptive input is a downward-looking depth camera mounted under the robot's base. This unique vantage point focuses on the critical support region around the feet. The system processes a *single depth frame* in real-time (50 Hz) using a lightweight **U-Net** architecture. This U-Net reconstructs a dense, egocentric height map. Crucially, it is designed to overcome self-occlusions from the robot's legs.
The U-Net is trained with a multi-task learning approach:
- **Height Head:** Predicts the refined height map, supervised by an L1 loss against ground-truth height maps.
- **Edge Head:** An auxiliary branch that predicts an edge map, supervised by a combination of Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and Dice losses. This edge head is vital for preserving sharp terrain boundaries (e.g., stair edges), which might otherwise be smoothed out, and for filling in occluded regions accurately. In deployment, only the height head's output is used by the locomotion policy.

**B. Unified Policy and Action Space:**
The framework employs a single reinforcement learning policy that takes both proprioceptive and perceptual observations as input.
- **Observations:** Include user commands (linear/angular velocities), body angular velocity, projected gravity, joint positions/velocities, previous actions, and current gait signals (frequency, sine/cosine of phase). The key perceptual input is the reconstructed under-base height map.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs a 32-dimensional vector comprising 31 joint target positions and a scalar gait frequency action.
- **Gait Adaptation:** The gait frequency action `f_t` directly controls a global gait phase `ϕ_t`, updated as `ϕ_t = mod(ϕ_t-∆t + ∆t · f_t, 1.0)`. This allows the policy to intrinsically adjust step timing. The raw `f_t` is scaled, clipped, and filtered for stability. This unified approach enables the policy to jointly reason about *when* to step and *how* to configure the body, creating a tight coupling between perception, gait, and joint control.

**C. Successive Teacher–Student (S-TS) Training Scheme:**
This single-stage training method efficiently transfers knowledge from privileged to partial observations using an asymmetric Actor-Critic approach with shared policy and critic networks.
- **Teacher:** Interacts with the environment using privileged, noise-free observations (including base linear velocity, joint torques/accelerations, foot contact forces/heights, base height). It learns a strong locomotion policy rapidly.
- **Student:** Interacts using only noisy proprioceptive and perceptual observations (reconstructed height map).
- **Teacher-Student Switch Gate:** Controls the proportion of environments assigned to the teacher and student during training.
 - **Early Stage (Teacher-Exclusive):** Only the teacher interacts, generating trajectories. The student learns through a supervised auxiliary task, aligning its latent representation with the teacher's using an MSE loss. This helps the student learn a robust representation from partial observations without affecting environment interaction.
 - **Later Stage (Parallel Interaction):** The switch gate gradually increases the student's involvement, allowing both teacher and student to generate trajectories. The shared policy is optimized using PPO losses from both agent types, ensuring the student adapts to realistic partial inputs while benefiting from the teacher's guidance.
- **Reward Design:** Leverages standard locomotion rewards (velocity tracking, base height), specific gait rewards (upper/lower body motion, action smoothness/limits for gait frequency), and foot-placement rewards to encourage safe footholds on complex terrain.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Extensive domain randomization is applied in IsaacLab (mass, CoM, inertia, friction, PD gains, external perturbations) to bridge the simulation-reality gap and enhance the controller's robustness.

**D. Robot Platform and Deployment:**
The framework is validated on the "Limx Oli" full-sized humanoid robot (1.65m, 55kg, 31 DoF), equipped with an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX and an Intel RealSense D435i depth camera. The control policy and reconstruction module run onboard at 50 Hz, with joint commands tracked by 1 kHz PD controllers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings and compelling results that validate the efficacy of the proposed framework:

**A. Robust Under-Base Terrain Reconstruction:**
The perception module successfully reconstructs dense under-base height maps from single depth frames in real-time. As shown in Figure 8, the U-Net effectively fills in occluded regions caused by the robot's legs and accurately preserves sharp height discontinuities crucial for terrain features like stairs and gaps. An ablation study (Table II) quantitatively demonstrates the importance of the auxiliary edge branch, with the full model achieving the best scores in BCE, DICE, and MAE for height and edge prediction. This confirms that explicitly modeling edges is vital for capturing accurate terrain geometry.

**B. Superior Training Performance with Successive Teacher–Student (S-TS):**
Comparative analysis of training progress (Figure 6) reveals that the S-TS framework significantly outperforms alternative training schemes:
- The `STS-Teacher` rapidly ascends to high curriculum levels, showcasing the efficiency of learning with privileged information.
- The `STS-Student` curve closely tracks and eventually approaches the teacher's performance, indicating effective knowledge transfer and learning from partial observations.
- `CTS` (Concurrent Teacher–Student) learns more slowly and converges to lower terrain levels, suggesting that simultaneous teacher-student updates from the beginning introduce conflicting gradients.
- `Baseline` (PPO-only) and `STS w/o Gait` saturate at even lower levels, underscoring the benefits of both privileged guidance and the adaptive gait control.

**C. Enhanced Locomotion Robustness on Complex Terrains:**
Simulation experiments (Figure 7) measure the success ratio across various challenging terrains (stairs, gaps, varying speeds):
- The `STS` method consistently achieves the highest success ratios across all tested scenarios, including backward stair ascent, forward stair climbing at moderate and high speeds, and gap crossing.
- It demonstrates superior stability and adaptability compared to `CTS`, `Baseline`, and `STS w/o Gait`, especially as terrain difficulty and speed increase. This validates that the successive teacher-student schedule yields a stronger student policy and that the unified gait-frequency action is critical for precise foot placement.

**D. Adaptive Gait Behavior:**
Analysis of the gait frequency action (Figure 9) demonstrates the policy's ability to adapt its stepping rhythm in real-time:
- On flat terrain, the gait frequency increases with commanded forward velocity, leading to more efficient walking.
- When encountering stairs or performing turns, the gait frequency automatically slows down, allowing for more deliberate and precise foot placement, which is crucial for maintaining balance and coordination. This highlights the unified policy's effective fusion of terrain perception and proprioceptive feedback to adjust gait based on both high-level commands and local terrain conditions.

**E. Successful Real-World Deployment (Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
The most compelling results are from the real-world deployment on the Limx Oli robot (Figure 1 and Figure 10):
- The controller, trained entirely in simulation, transfers robustly to hardware without any task-specific fine-tuning.
- Oli demonstrates stable omnidirectional locomotion on diverse real-world terrains, including forward, backward, and sideways stair traversal, turning on a previously unseen spiral staircase (showcasing zero-shot generalization), stepping over a 46 cm gap, and traversing loose gravel.
- Throughout these complex maneuvers, the robot maintains balance, tracks commanded walking directions, and exhibits human-like gait adaptation, confirming the practical utility and robustness of the proposed perceptive locomotion framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant stride in the field of humanoid robotics, with substantial implications for future applications:

- **Advancement in Humanoid Locomotion on Complex Terrains:** The paper effectively addresses a fundamental challenge for full-sized humanoids: robust and adaptive locomotion on highly structured and irregular terrains like staircases and gaps. By enabling deliberate, terrain-aware stepping rather than mere fall-prevention, it pushes the capabilities of humanoids closer to real-world deployment.
- **Pioneering Perception-Action Integration:** The work showcases a highly effective method for tightly coupling real-time exteroceptive perception (under-base height maps) directly into an end-to-end reinforcement learning control policy. This tight integration, especially for gait timing, resolves long-standing limitations where perception and control were often decoupled or loosely integrated, leading to more coherent and adaptive behaviors.
- **Adaptive Gait Control as a Key Enabler:** The unified policy's ability to jointly adapt gait timing and whole-body motion based on both commands and perceived terrain is a critical innovation. This intelligent gait modulation allows the robot to deliberately navigate obstacles, secure precise footholds, and avoid missteps, significantly enhancing safety and efficiency.
- **Efficient and Robust Training Paradigm:** The Successive Teacher–Student (S-TS) training scheme offers a practical and data-efficient method for learning complex perceptive policies. By leveraging privileged information during early training and gradually transitioning to student-controlled environments, it facilitates robust sim-to-real transfer, a perennial challenge in robotics. This methodology can be highly impactful for training other complex robotic skills.
- **Validated Real-World Applicability:** The successful zero-shot deployment on a full-sized humanoid robot (Limx Oli) in diverse and challenging real-world scenarios is a powerful testament to the framework's robustness and generalizability. This demonstrates that the research has moved beyond simulation to deliver tangible results on complex hardware, paving the way for practical applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a strong foundation for future advancements. The authors explicitly outline plans to extend the framework to higher-speed locomotion, more proactive obstacle avoidance, and autonomous waypoint tracking. These extensions could further enhance the versatility and autonomy of humanoids in logistics, search and rescue, hazardous environment exploration, and human-assistance roles.

In summary, this paper provides a comprehensive and impactful solution to a critical problem in humanoid robotics, demonstrating both theoretical innovation and practical, real-world utility.
