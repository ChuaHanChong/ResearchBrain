# Value Vision-Language-Action Planning & Search

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of British Columbia developed V-VLAPS, a framework that augments Monte Carlo Tree Search with a learnable value function to guide Vision-Language-Action models in robotic manipulation. This approach increased task success rates by up to 31 percentage points on challenging tasks and reduced MCTS simulations by up to 14%.

## Problem

- Large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, trained via behavior cloning, exhibit brittleness and fail in out-of-distribution states, lacking long-horizon reasoning abilities.
- Existing MCTS-augmented VLA planning (VLAPS) relies solely on the VLA policy prior and simple visit-count heuristics, without an explicit, grounded estimate of expected future return for states.
- VLAPS struggles to efficiently correct for inaccurate VLA priors, requiring extensive, slow exploration to overcome misleading action probabilities.

## Method

- A lightweight ==MLP== is trained as a ==value function== to estimate the expected future return from VLA latent state representations.
- A dataset is collected by performing rollouts with a fixed VLA policy, pairing extracted latent readout vectors with ==Monte Carlo value targets== derived from sparse terminal rewards.
- The trained value function is integrated into the ==MCTS== node selection score, directly replacing the traditional Q-value term with the learned state value to bias action selection towards high-value states.

## Results

- V-VLAPS increased overall task success rates by **5.2 percentage points** on the spatial suite and **2.8 percentage points** on the object suite compared to VLAPS.
- On a particularly challenging spatial task (Task 9), V-VLAPS achieved a **47%** success rate, a **31 percentage point** improvement over VLAPS (**16%**) and significantly higher than the base VLA model's **0%**.
- The integration of the value head reduced the average number of MCTS simulations required by approximately **5%** for spatial tasks and **14%** for object tasks, indicating improved search efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA model latent spaces inherently contain sufficient information to learn a value function capable of distinguishing between successful and unsuccessful task trajectories.
> - Explicitly providing a learned value estimate to MCTS offers a more informed signal than solely relying on policy priors and visit counts, allowing for more efficient correction of prior inaccuracies.
> - Augmenting VLA-guided MCTS with a value function aligns the planning system with successful value-guided search paradigms, adapting them for continuous robotic control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically addressing the challenges of deploying generalist robotic policies in real-world environments. Recent advancements in robot learning have seen the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as Octo, RT-1, and RT-2, which are transformer-based policies capable of predicting action sequences from multimodal observations and natural language instructions. These models serve as powerful priors for various robotic behaviors due to their training on large-scale datasets.

However, a known limitation of VLA models is their reactive nature. They operate primarily through behavior cloning, meaning they predict the next action based on the current observation and instruction without explicit long-horizon planning. This reactive behavior can lead to fragility when encountering out-of-distribution (OOD) states, distribution shifts, or complex, multi-step tasks, resulting in failures from which the policy may not recover.

To address these limitations, a line of research has explored augmenting pretrained VLA models with planning search algorithms. Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is a prominent algorithm in this context, having demonstrated success in domains like board games (e.g., AlphaGo) by simulating future outcomes. Neary et al. (2025) introduced Vision-Language-Action Planning and Search (VLAPS), which integrates a pretrained VLA model into an MCTS framework. In VLAPS, the VLA provides action proposals and abstractions to guide the search, using a PUCT-style scoring rule that combines VLA-derived priors with visit-count-based exploration. While VLAPS improves performance by enabling planning, it lacks an explicit, learned value function to estimate expected future returns. Consequently, if the VLA prior is suboptimal, the planner relies solely on exhaustive count-based exploration to correct this bias, rather than a direct signal of state value.

Concurrently, other research has explored the information encoded within VLA latent features. Gu et al. (2025), for instance, showed that a small probe trained on the latent features of a generalist VLA could predict task success or failure with cross-task generalization. This observation suggested that VLA representations might contain a more granular signal related to continuous value, beyond just binary success/failure.

V-VLAPS positions itself at the intersection of these two areas. It builds directly upon the VLAPS framework by integrating a learned value function derived from the VLA's internal features, inspired by the success of probes like SAFE. The aim is to enhance MCTS node selection by providing a learned estimate of future returns, thereby allowing the planner to bias its search toward higher-value branches and potentially overcome the limitations of a purely prior- and count-driven exploration, especially in challenging, long-horizon scenarios. This approach aims to bring VLA-guided planning closer to the value-guided tree search strategies employed in high-performance game-playing AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to improve the robustness and effectiveness of robotic manipulation policies derived from Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by integrating a learned value function into a planning and search framework.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations in current VLA-based robotic control:

1. **Reactive Nature of VLA Models:** VLA models, typically trained via behavior cloning, operate reactively, predicting actions based on immediate observations and instructions. This fundamental reliance on an imitation prior makes them susceptible to brittle behavior when confronted with out-of-distribution (OOD) states or unexpected environmental variations. Their performance can degrade significantly in scenarios that deviate from their training distribution.
2. **Insufficient Long-Horizon Planning:** For complex, long-horizon tasks, purely reactive policies often struggle to maintain coherence and achieve goals that require a sequence of dependent actions. The lack of explicit foresight or a mechanism to evaluate long-term consequences limits their ability to robustly navigate multi-step challenges.
3. **Limitations of Prior VLA-Guided Planning (VLAPS):** While methods like VLAPS (Neary et al., 2025) integrate MCTS with VLA models to enable planning, they primarily rely on the VLA model as an action prior and use visit-count heuristics for exploration. This means that if the VLA policy assigns high probability to suboptimal actions, the planner has no internal learned signal to identify these poor choices. It can only correct such biases through exhaustive, count-based exploration, which may be inefficient or infeasible under time constraints. The search, therefore, remains heavily dependent on the quality of the imitation prior rather than an explicit understanding of the underlying reward structure or expected future returns.
4. **Untapped Information in VLA Representations:** Prior research, specifically SAFE (Gu et al., 2025), demonstrated that the latent features within generalist VLA models encode information correlated with task success or failure. This observation suggests that these internal representations might also contain a more continuous signal indicative of state value, which could be leveraged for proactive planning. The motivation is to convert this passive failure prediction capability into an active search guidance mechanism.

Therefore, the key objective is to address the absence of a learned value signal in VLA-guided planning. By training a lightweight value head on the latent representations of a frozen VLA model, which predicts Monte Carlo returns from offline rollouts, the authors aim to introduce a mechanism that can guide MCTS. This value-guided approach is hypothesized to direct the search toward higher-value branches more effectively, especially when the policy prior is weak or misleading, thereby leading to improved task success rates and more robust execution in challenging robotic manipulation tasks. The goal is to facilitate more informed decision-making within the MCTS framework by providing a grounded estimate of future returns, moving beyond reliance on mere action likelihoods and visit counts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The V-VLAPS methodology extends the VLAPS framework by incorporating a learned value function to guide the Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). This involves three main stages: data collection for value head training, training of a lightweight value head, and integration of this value head into the VLAPS scoring rule.

#### 3.1. Data Collection for Value Head Training

To generate training data for the value head, the authors perform rollouts of a fixed, pretrained VLA policy (Octo) on tasks from the LIBERO tabletop manipulation benchmark. These rollouts are conducted without any planning component, using the reactive Octo policy.
- **Initial States:** For each task, rollouts are collected from a subset of initial states (indices 0, 3, 6, and 9 out of 10). This design allows for evaluating the value head's generalization to unseen initial states during later evaluation.
- **Episode Trajectories:** At each decision step `t`, the Octo VLA receives an observation `o_t` and a language instruction `g`. It computes a latent readout vector `h_t` (a 768-dimensional representation of past observations and the task) and outputs an action chunk `c_t`. This chunk, comprising a sequence of low-level actions, is executed in the environment. This process generates an episode as a sequence of (observation, chunk, next observation) tuples.
- **Reward Definition:** The environment provides a sparse terminal reward: 1 for successful task completion before a timeout, and 0 for failure or timeout. All intermediate rewards are zero.
- **Monte Carlo Value Targets:** For each decision step `t` in an episode, a Monte Carlo value target `G_t` is calculated. If the episode is successful, `G_t = γ^(T-t)`, where `T` is the final decision step and `γ` (set to 0.99) is the discount factor. If the episode fails, `G_t = 0`. This `G_t` represents an estimate of the state value under the VLA policy.
- **Dataset Formulation and Rebalancing:** Each latent readout `h_t` is paired with its corresponding `G_t` to form a training example `(h_t, G_t)`. To mitigate the heavy skew towards zero-valued targets (due to failed episodes and small `γ^(T-t)` for early steps in successful episodes), a rebalancing strategy is applied. Targets are binned into ten equal-width intervals on, and the lowest bin (containing values near zero) is downsampled to match the combined size of the other nine bins.

#### 3.2. Value Head Training

A lightweight neural network, termed the "value head," is trained to predict the scalar state value from the VLA's latent representation.
- **Architecture:** The value head is a three-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). It takes the 768-dimensional Octo readout vector `h_t` as input, processes it through a single hidden layer of 3072 ReLU units, and outputs a scalar value estimate `v_t`. The total parameter count is approximately 2.4 million, intentionally kept small to minimize computational overhead during online planning.
- **Training Objective:** The network `V_θ(h_t)` is trained using mean squared error (MSE) to regress onto the Monte Carlo value targets `G_t`: `L(θ) = E[(V_θ(h_t) - G_t)^2]`.
- **Optimization:** Stochastic gradient descent with the Adam optimizer is used, with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10^(-4) and a cosine decay schedule. Batch size is 64, and training runs for 100 epochs. Crucially, the parameters of the underlying Octo VLA are kept frozen throughout this training process; only the value head parameters `θ` are updated.

#### 3.3. Value Integration into MCTS (V-VLAPS)

The learned value head is integrated into the node selection rule of the VLAPS MCTS algorithm, specifically by modifying its PUCT-style scoring function.
- **VLAPS Scoring Rule:** The original VLAPS scoring rule for selecting a candidate action chunk `a_i` from a node `v` is `U_VLAPS(v, a_i) = ψ_Φ_v(a_i | I_t, L_T) * sqrt(log(N(v)) / (1 + N(v, a_i)))`. This term balances the VLA-derived action prior (`ψ_Φ_v`) with exploration based on visit counts (`N(v, a_i)`).
- **V-VLAPS Extension:** V-VLAPS adds a learned successor-state value `Q_θ(v, a_i)` to this rule. For each candidate action `a_i`, the simulator rolls the chunk forward from node `v` to obtain a successor state `s'`. The Octo readout `h(s')` for this successor state is computed, and the trained value head `V_θ(h(s'))` is evaluated to produce `Q_θ(v, a_i)`. Since intermediate rewards are zero and transitions are deterministic in the LIBERO simulator, `Q_θ` directly estimates the continuation value after choosing `a_i`.
- **Final V-VLAPS Score:** The new node selection score is `SCORE(v, a_i) = λ_V * Q_θ(v, a_i) + U_VLAPS(v, a_i)`. A fixed value coefficient `λ_V = 1` is used. This means V-VLAPS differs from VLAPS solely by the addition of the learned value term.
- **MCTS Parameters:** All other aspects of the MCTS procedure, including the action sampler, action library, LIBERO simulator, and MCTS hyperparameters (e.g., exploration weight, max depth, rollout length), remain consistent between VLAPS and V-VLAPS conditions.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Conditions:** Three conditions are evaluated: (1) `VLA` (reactive Octo without planning), (2) `VLAPS` (prior planning baseline), and (3) `V-VLAPS` (proposed method).
- **Environments:** Five LIBERO suites are used: `libero object`, `libero spatial`, `libero goal`, `libero 10` (long-horizon tasks), and `libero 90` (diverse manipulation tasks).
- **Evaluation:** Each (condition, suite, budget) combination is evaluated over 100 episodes. For the four 10-task suites, this means 10 tasks x 10 initial states. For `libero 90`, 10 tasks are sampled, and all 10 initial states per sampled task are used. Crucially, 6 out of 10 initial states per task for evaluation are unseen by the value head during its training.
- **Search Budgets:** Two per-episode wall-time budgets are used: 600 seconds (default) and 1800 seconds (extended).
- **Resources:** All evaluations were conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a detailed comparison of V-VLAPS against its baseline (VLAPS) and a reactive VLA model (Octo), across various LIBERO suites and computational budgets.

#### 4.1. Main Results: Success Rate Comparison

Table 1 summarizes the aggregate success rates:
- **Planning Benefit:** Both MCTS-based conditions (VLAPS and V-VLAPS) demonstrably outperform the reactive Octo VLA without planning. On average, they achieved approximately 27 percentage points (pp) higher success rates than the VLA model, reproducing previous findings.
- **Default Budget (600s):** At the default 600s per-episode search budget, V-VLAPS matched VLAPS in aggregate, with both achieving an average success rate of 87.4% across the five suites. Per-suite differences were generally small, not exceeding 3pp, suggesting that at this budget, the learned value head did not provide a substantial aggregate advantage over the prior- and count-based exploration of VLAPS.
- **Extended Budget (1800s):** With an increased search budget of 1800s, V-VLAPS showed an improved average success rate of 91.6%, surpassing VLAPS's 88.4% by +3.2pp. This gap was not uniform across all suites.
 - **Significant Gains:** The most notable improvements for V-VLAPS over VLAPS were observed on `libero object` (+6pp, from 87% to 93%) and `libero 10` (+4pp, from 81% to 85%).
 - **Minimal Impact on Other Suites:** For `libero spatial`, `libero goal`, and `libero 90`, both VLAPS and V-VLAPS already achieved high success rates (≥ 88%) at 600s. In these suites, the larger budget or the addition of the value head resulted in minimal changes (at most ±2pp), suggesting these tasks might be near ceiling performance for the current methods.

#### 4.2. Wall-time Scaling and Task-Specific Analysis

The gains observed for V-VLAPS with increased budget were not symmetric:
- **`libero object`:** V-VLAPS improved by +8pp (from 85% to 93%) when the budget increased from 600s to 1800s, while VLAPS improved by +5pp (from 82% to 87%). This caused the performance gap between V-VLAPS and VLAPS to double from +3pp to +6pp.
- **`libero 10`:** V-VLAPS exhibited a larger improvement of +10pp (from 75% to 85%), whereas VLAPS gained +4pp (from 77% to 81%). This resulted in a flip in relative performance, with V-VLAPS overcoming an initial -2pp deficit to achieve a +4pp lead.
- **Task 7 Case Study (`libero object`):** To further investigate the effect of budget, `libero object` task 7 was tested with an extended set of 20 initial states at 1800s. V-VLAPS successfully completed 17/20 (85%) episodes, while VLAPS completed 13/20 (65%). Notably, on the standard 10 initial states for task 7, VLAPS's performance decreased from 8/10 at 600s to 6/10 at 1800s, while V-VLAPS's performance significantly increased from 5/10 to 10/10. This indicates that for certain hard tasks, plain MCTS (VLAPS) did not benefit from additional search time, but the value-guided variant (V-VLAPS) did.

#### 4.3. Failure-Mode Analysis

An analysis of failures on `libero object` tasks 6, 7, and 8 at the 600s budget revealed a common pattern:
- **Root-level Timeouts:** A substantial majority of failures (77.8% for VLAPS, 86.7% for V-VLAPS) were categorized as root-level timeouts. This means the MCTS spent the entire allocated budget (around 600-611 seconds) exploring alternatives at the very first decision step without committing to any action chunk.
- **Weak Value Separation at Root:** In these timeout scenarios, the value head's predictions for candidate actions at the initial decision step were consistently low and very similar (between -0.01 and 0.02). This indicates that near the root of hard tasks, when candidate branches are still far from task completion, the predicted continuation values are close to zero and lack sufficient discriminative power to guide the search effectively.
- **Implication for Budget:** This finding suggests that the plateau in performance at the 600s budget is largely due to search computation being spent in regions where the value head's ranking is not yet impactful. The value term is expected to become more useful only when the search progresses to deeper states where different branches begin to exhibit more clearly separated continuation values.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The V-VLAPS research contributes to the field of robotic control by demonstrating an effective method for integrating learned value functions into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) planning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Core Contribution: Bridging Failure Detection and Active Planning

The primary significance of V-VLAPS lies in extending the utility of VLA latent features from passive failure detection to active search guidance. Previously, studies like SAFE (Gu et al., 2025) showed that VLA features encode outcome-discriminative information, enabling probes to predict task success or failure. V-VLAPS takes this a step further by demonstrating that by training a small MLP on these same features to predict continuous Monte Carlo returns, and integrating these predictions into a PUCT-style MCTS scoring rule, the VLA's internal representations can actively bias the search toward higher-value branches. This translates a post-hoc monitoring capability into a proactive planning component.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Robustness and Efficiency for Long-Horizon Tasks

The results indicate that V-VLAPS can improve task success rates, particularly on more challenging, long-horizon manipulation tasks (e.g., `libero object`, `libero 10`) when sufficient computational budget is available. This suggests a path towards more robust robotic policies that can handle complex sequences of actions and recover from potential misdirection by the base VLA policy. The learned value function allows the MCTS to make more informed decisions by considering the estimated long-term return of different action sequences, rather than relying solely on the immediate likelihoods provided by the behavior-cloned VLA or blind exploration. This brings VLA-guided planning closer in spirit to successful game-playing systems like AlphaGo, which leverage both policy and value networks.

#### 5.3. Informed Resource Allocation in Planning

The failure-mode analysis highlights that in the early stages of planning for difficult tasks, the value function may not offer strong discrimination due to all initial branches appearing similarly far from completion. However, as the search deepens, the value function's ability to differentiate between promising and unpromising paths increases. This finding is significant for understanding how and when value-guided planning is most effective, suggesting future optimizations where search budgets could be dynamically allocated or search strategies adapted based on the discriminative power of the value function at different tree depths. This could potentially lead to more efficient use of computational resources during deployment.

#### 5.4. Limitations and Future Research Directions

The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point to avenues for future research and potential impact:
- **Generalizability Across VLAs:** The experiments exclusively used Octo as the VLA backbone. Future work is needed to determine if the findings generalize to other prominent VLA models like π0 or RT-2.
- **Off-Policy Value Training:** The value head was trained on rollouts generated by the unguided VLA, meaning its predictions are technically off-policy with respect to the V-VLAPS execution. Training the value head on rollouts generated by V-VLAPS itself could lead to a virtuous cycle of iterative refinement, potentially yielding greater performance gains and better convergence of the value estimates.
- **Value-Guided Pruning:** The prevalence of root-level timeouts suggests that the value head could be used not just for ranking but also for actively pruning unpromising branches during expansion. Implementing such pruning could significantly reduce wasted computation and allow MCTS to explore more relevant parts of the search space earlier, potentially improving performance even at lower budgets.
- **Alternative Architectures and Objectives:** Exploring different value head architectures, such as aggregating input from multiple transformer layers, or alternative training objectives (e.g., classification-based objectives like HL-Gauss), could further unlock the potential of VLA features for value estimation.

#### 5.5. Societal and Ethical Considerations

The paper's impact statement notes that the specific contribution does not introduce new societal or ethical concerns beyond those already associated with the underlying VLA models, planning algorithms, and failure detection systems it builds upon. A key practical consideration highlighted is the significant computational cost associated with the per-episode test-time search budgets (hundreds to thousands of seconds), which shifts computational burden from training to deployment and implies associated energy consumption. This aspect is relevant for the practical feasibility and environmental footprint of deploying such advanced robotic systems.
