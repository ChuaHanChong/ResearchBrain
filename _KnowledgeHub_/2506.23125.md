# Learning Motion Skills with Adaptive Assistive Curriculum Force in Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed the Adaptive Assistive Curriculum Force (A2CF) framework, which uses adaptive assistive forces to accelerate the acquisition of complex motion skills in humanoid robots. This approach achieved 30% faster learning convergence and over 40% lower failure rates across tasks like walking, dancing, and backflips, with successful sim-to-real transfer.

## Problem

- Current reinforcement learning and imitation learning methods face limitations in learning stability, efficiency, and balancing exploration-exploitation for complex humanoid robot motions.
- Humanoid robots struggle with inefficient exploration and lack instinctive physical support during training, leading to slow convergence, suboptimal policies, and frequent falls.
- Acquiring sophisticated, high-dimensional movements demands high levels of stability and robustness, making the learning process precarious and prolonged without initial guidance.

## Method

- ==Introduced Adaptive Assistive Curriculum Force (A2CF)==, a dual-agent reinforcement learning framework where a dedicated assistive force agent provides state-dependent support to a motion policy agent.
- Employed an adaptive curriculum to gradually diminish assistive forces as the robot's proficiency increases, dynamically adjusting force bounds based on force magnitude and skill acquisition indicators.
- Integrated mechanisms like privileged information (PI), task-dependent initial distributions of spatial force (ID), and random masking (RM) to refine assistive learning, improve generalization, and prevent over-reliance.

## Results

- A2CF achieved **30%** faster convergence and over **40%** lower failure rates compared to baselines in tasks like bipedal walking, choreographed dancing, and backflips on the Unitree G1 robot.
- Ablation studies confirmed that privileged information (PI) improved learning efficiency, initial force distribution (ID) was critical for phase-dependent tasks like backflips, and random masking (RM) facilitated earlier independence from assistance.
- Learned policies for walking and dancing successfully transferred from simulation to a physical Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating robust performance across diverse real-world terrains and accurate execution of complex dance routines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adaptive, curriculum-based external spatial forces, inspired by human motor learning, can significantly accelerate and stabilize the learning of complex robot motor skills.
> - A dual-agent system that jointly optimizes motion policy and assistive force allows for efficient exploration and guides the robot towards robust, autonomous control.
> - Strategic components such as privileged information for the assistive agent, tailored initial force distributions, and random force masking are vital for improving learning efficiency, preventing over-dependence, and ensuring successful sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of complex and robust motion skills, such as dancing and acrobatic movements, represents a significant challenge in robotics. Current reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL) methods, while advanced, often face limitations in terms of learning stability, efficiency, and the balance between exploration and exploitation. These challenges frequently lead to slow learning processes, suboptimal policies, and difficulties in acquiring high-dimensional control skills for humanoids.

Existing learning-based locomotion research includes methods for robust walking across various terrains using transformer-based RL policies (Radosavovic et al., 2024), human-like locomotion with motion tracking and domain randomization (HiLo, 2025), and frameworks for mastering challenging terrains via denoising world model learning (DWL, 2024). Other works focus on recovery motions, such as standing up from various postures (HoST, 2025; HumanUp, 2025). Notably, HoST introduces an externally applied vertical force to accelerate learning, but this force is non-adaptive and state-independent, distinguishing it from the adaptive approach proposed in the present work.

Imitation learning has also been extensively applied to humanoid whole-body control, enabling robots to mimic human demonstrations. Examples include applying Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) for human-like gaits (Zhang et al., 2024), universal human-to-humanoid teleoperation and learning (OmniH2O, 2024), advanced expressive whole-body control (ExBody2, 2024), and deep imitation learning for loco-manipulation tasks (TRILL, 2023; Matsuura et al., 2023).

This research positions itself within this landscape by drawing inspiration from human motor skill acquisition, where external physical assistance (e.g., parental support for infants learning to walk, or coach guidance for athletes) plays a crucial role in accelerating learning, preventing falls, and guiding learners away from ineffective strategies. The paper proposes an adaptive approach to providing such assistance within a robotic learning framework, aiming to overcome the current limitations of stability and efficiency by integrating state-dependent, curriculum-based assistive forces. Unlike prior work that may use static or non-adaptive external forces, this method focuses on dynamically adjusting assistance as the robot's proficiency evolves, ultimately enabling the robot to perform tasks autonomously.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a reinforcement learning framework that accelerates the acquisition of complex motion skills in humanoid robots and improves the robustness and performance of the learned policies. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations:

**Inspiration from Human Motor Learning:**
The paper's core motivation stems from observations in human development and athletic training.
- **Infant Development:** Infants learning to walk often receive physical support from parents or assistive devices like walkers. This external aid helps them develop confidence, balance, and coordination, preventing falls and allowing them to explore movements more safely and effectively.
- **Athletic Training:** Individuals learning highly complex and dangerous movements, such as backflips, typically receive direct physical guidance and support from coaches. This guidance prevents injuries, helps refine technique, and enables the learner to successfully execute difficult maneuvers that might otherwise be too risky or challenging to attempt independently in early stages.
- **Learning Efficiency:** In both scenarios, external assistance is observed to expedite the learning process. It prevents learners from getting "stuck" in suboptimal or unsafe movement patterns and guides them towards more efficient and stable strategies. Crucially, this assistance is usually gradually reduced as the learner gains proficiency, ultimately leading to independent skill execution.

**Challenges in Robotic Motion Learning:**
Translating human learning paradigms to robotic systems presents unique difficulties that motivate the proposed solution.
- **Inefficient Exploration:** In the early stages of reinforcement learning, robots often struggle with inefficient exploration, failing to discover effective motion strategies. This can lead to slow convergence or getting trapped in local optima, where the robot develops a suboptimal policy that it cannot easily escape.
- **Lack of Instinctive Support:** Unlike humans, robots do not possess inherent mechanisms for instinctive physical support during training. When a robot loses balance or attempts a difficult maneuver, it typically fails without external intervention, which can terminate episodes and hinder continuous learning.
- **Maintaining Stability and Robustness:** Acquiring sophisticated movements like dancing or backflips demands high levels of stability and robustness. Without initial guidance, the robot may frequently fall or perform unstable motions, making the learning process precarious and prolonged, particularly for high-dimensional humanoid control tasks.

**Specific Objectives of the Proposed Framework (A2CF):**
To address these motivations and challenges, the research sets forth several specific objectives:
1. **Introduce A2CF:** To propose a reinforcement learning framework, Adaptive Assistive Curriculum Force (A2CF), that integrates adaptive assistive forces to guide humanoid robots through complex motion learning.
2. **Dual-Agent Training:** To develop a dual-agent system where a dedicated assistive force agent is jointly trained with the motion policy agent. The assistive agent applies state-dependent forces, and its assistance gradually diminishes as the robot's proficiency increases.
3. **Enhance Assistive Learning:** To incorporate specific mechanisms—privileged information (PI), tailored initial state distributions (ID), and random masking (RM)—to refine the assistive learning paradigm. These mechanisms aim to improve generalization capabilities and prevent the robot from becoming overly reliant on external support.
4. **Validate Performance:** To empirically demonstrate through both simulation and real-world experiments that A2CF significantly enhances learning efficiency, reduces failure rates, and improves the final task performance of humanoid robots across diverse, complex motion skills (walking, dancing, and backflips), ultimately yielding robust, support-free policies that transfer effectively to physical hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research frames the problem of learning motion skills as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), defined by (S, A, T, R, Ω, O, γ). The state space (S) includes the observation (o_t) and privileged information (o_priv_t) available only during simulation. The observation (o_t) comprises sensory data like angular velocity (w_t), gravity projection (g_t), joint positions (q_t) and velocities (q̇_t), previous action (a_t-1), and current command (c_t). The action space (A) expands to include both the motion agent's actions and the assistive force agent's actions. The goal is to learn an optimal policy (π*) that maximizes the expected cumulative reward.

**A. Learning with Adaptive Assistive Spatial Force (A2CF)**

1. **Expanded Action Space:** A dual-agent system is employed, featuring a motion policy agent and an assistive force agent. The motion agent's action (a_motion_t) corresponds to desired joint positions (q_des_t), which are executed via a PD controller. The assistive force agent's action (a_assi_t) is a 6-dimensional spatial force F_t = [f_t, m_t], comprising linear force and moment, applied to the robot's base link (pelvis). The combined action space is a_t = [a_motion_t, a_assi_t]. This joint action learning approach aims to accelerate learning by simultaneously optimizing both joint control and external assistance.

2. **Assistive Force Curriculum:** Inspired by human learning, a curriculum-based mechanism regulates assistive forces. The assistive force agent's action space is bounded by a 6D hypercube B_k, centered at the origin, with half-width η_k for each dimension. The bound η_k is adaptively updated (Algorithm 1) based on the magnitude of the applied force F_k and a skill acquisition indicator. If the normalized force magnitude ||F_k||/||η_k|| falls below a threshold (1-ε), η_k is decreased by a factor of (1-δ), indicating diminishing need for assistance. If it exceeds a threshold (1+ε), η_k is increased by (1+δ). Once a skill is considered learned (`isSkillLearned`), η_k is consistently decayed by (1-δ) to phase out external support for real-world deployment. The hyperparameters ε and δ are set to 0.5 and 0.2, respectively.

3. **Initial Distribution of Spatial Force (ID):** To provide a strong prior for the assistive force agent, the initial bound η_0 of the hypercube is set in a task-dependent manner. This initialization reflects human-inspired understanding of force requirements for different tasks. For example, in walking, lateral (xy-plane) forces are prioritized, while in backflips, z-direction forces become critical during jump and air phases. Specific initial bounds for linear forces (f_t) and moments (m_t) are detailed in Table IV for walking, backflip (phase-dependent), and dancing tasks.

4. **Assistive Force Agent with Privileged Information (PI):** The assistive force agent, operating exclusively in simulation, leverages privileged information (PI) to enhance its guidance. This includes terrain information (for walking on rough terrains), current assistive force bounds, robot linear velocity, and domain randomization parameters (e.g., ground friction, disturbance speed, mass). This mirrors how a human coach might use unobservable factors to provide targeted assistance.

5. **Random Mask of Spatial Force (RM):** To prevent over-reliance on assistive forces, a random masking mechanism is introduced. During each training iteration, components of the assistive force F_t are selectively disabled with a probability ζ (set to 0.2). The final applied force F_assi_t = M_t ⊙ F_t, where M_t is a binary mask. This intermittent removal of assistance encourages the robot to develop autonomous control and reduces dependency, akin to a parent occasionally letting go while teaching a child to walk.

**B. Overall Training Architecture**

An Asymmetric Actor-Critic (AAC) structure is utilized for training. The motion policy agent receives observations (o_t) and compressed historical information (o_H_t), while the Critic receives the full state (s_t), including privileged information. A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) compresses historical data to estimate the robot's linear velocity (v_t) and generate a latent representation (z_t). Both the motion policy and assistive force agents receive z_t, v_t, and o_t as inputs. The assistive force agent also receives a subset of privileged information. The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used for policy optimization.

**C. Task-Specific Training Frameworks**

The overall reward function combines a motion reward (r_motion), which includes task-relevant and regularization terms, and an assistive force reward (r_force). The r_force term penalizes excessive use of assistance and is introduced only after the motion agent achieves 80% of its skill acquisition target.

1. **Walking:** The robot learns to follow command velocities (c_t = [v_cmd_x, v_cmd_y, ω_cmd_z]) on terrains of varying difficulty. Rewards prioritize velocity tracking error, cost of transport (energy efficiency), and regularization terms such as feet air time, feet slide, action rate, joint acceleration, DOF position limits, and orientation (Table I).

2. **Backflip:** This complex task employs a phase-based training approach with a five-phase finite state machine (FSM): Stand, Crouch, Jump, Air, and Land. Transitions are based on robot height and foot contact. Task command (c_t) is a start trigger. Reward functions are phase-dependent, incorporating vertical velocity rewards during Crouch and Jump phases, and regularization terms for balance, yaw angular velocity, action rate, joint acceleration, and DOF position limits (Table II).

3. **Dancing:** The robot learns expressive whole-body motions by imitating demonstrations from the LAFAN1 motion dataset, retargeted to the Unitree G1 robot. The task command (c_t) includes desired base displacement (Δp_cmd), base rotational difference (Δq_cmd), and desired joint positions (q_cmd). Rewards consist of exponential tracking for these references, along with regularization terms for feet slide, action rate, DOF acceleration, and DOF torque (Table III).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The A2CF method was evaluated across three benchmarks: bipedal walking, choreographed dancing, and backflips, using the Unitree G1 EDU humanoid robot in Isaac Lab simulation and for real-world deployment where feasible. Training was conducted with PPO on 4096 parallel environments.

**A. Walking Task**
The walking policy was trained on a 10-level curriculum of rough terrains, including flat, slopes, rough, discretized, and stairs (up to 23 cm height). The policy was evaluated against a Baseline (DreamWaQ) and an ablation of A2CF without Privileged Information (A2CF w/o PI).
- **Learning Speed:** A2CF demonstrated significantly faster learning. It reached the maximum terrain level around 4,000 iterations, whereas the Baseline required approximately 10,000 iterations to achieve comparable performance (Figure 4).
- **Assistive Force Decay:** The assistive force in A2CF diminished to a negligible threshold of 0.1 N (for a 35 kg robot) around 7,000 iterations, indicating the emergence of an autonomous, support-free walking policy.
- **Performance:** A2CF consistently outperformed the Baseline in tracking both forward speed and angular velocity, leading to more accurate locomotion.
- **Ablation Study (A2CF w/o PI):** The absence of privileged information hindered learning efficiency. A2CF w/o PI learned slower, and the assistive force decayed more gradually, highlighting the critical role of PI in accelerating learning and enabling faster decay of assistance. PI also contributed to improved velocity tracking performance.

**B. Backflip Task**
The backflip policy was trained on flat terrain with randomized friction and payload masses. Success was defined by reaching and stabilizing in the land phase. Comparisons were made between Baseline, A2CF, and A2CF without Initial Distribution of Spatial Force (A2CF w/o ID). An additional Baseline with Reward Tuning (Baseline w/ RT) was also included.
- **Success Rate:** A2CF achieved a success rate exceeding 90% in learning the backflip policy, demonstrating efficient acquisition of this complex acrobatic skill (Figure 5).
- **Ablation Study (A2CF w/o ID):** The ablation study underscored the importance of task-dependent initial force distribution. Using uniform initial limits across all backflip phases (as in A2CF w/o ID) negatively impacted learning performance, suggesting that the tailored initialization of force bounds (Table IV) significantly contributes to the effectiveness of assistance, particularly when force requirements vary across motion phases.

**C. Dancing Task**
The dancing policy was trained on flat terrain with randomized physical parameters, utilizing retargeted motion clips from the LAFAN1 dataset. Task success was determined by reaching a joint position tracking error threshold. The methods compared were Baseline, A2CF, and A2CF without Random Mask (A2CF w/o RM).
- **Tracking Accuracy:** A2CF showed faster learning and achieved higher accuracy in joint position tracking compared to the Baseline (Figure 6). While improvements in base position and orientation tracking speed were not pronounced, A2CF exhibited a substantial increase in their tracking accuracy.
- **Ablation Study (A2CF w/o RM):** The random masking mechanism was found to be crucial. A2CF w/o RM showed slower decay of assistive forces, indicating that the intermittent removal of assistance facilitated an earlier transition to independent task execution and reduced the agent's reliance on external support.

**D. Sim2Sim2Real Results**
- **Walking:** The learned walking policy demonstrated successful Sim2Sim2Real transfer. It generalized well to diverse real-world terrains, including flat surfaces, grass, and uneven cobblestone paths, exhibiting robust performance on a physical robot (Figure 7).
- **Backflip:** Due to experimental constraints, real-world deployment for the backflip task was not conducted. However, the policy was successfully transferred and validated in the Genesis simulator, yielding similar results to the Isaac Lab simulation (Sim2Sim), supporting its potential real-world applicability (Figure 7).
- **Dancing:** The dancing policy also achieved successful Sim2Sim2Real transfer, with the robot performing the dance routine in a physical environment with high accuracy (Figure 8).

Overall, the experimental results indicate that A2CF consistently facilitates faster learning and improved task performance across various complex humanoid motion skills. The ablation studies confirm the individual contributions of privileged information, initial force distribution, and random masking to the robustness and efficiency of the learning process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robot control by introducing the Adaptive Assistive Curriculum Force (A2CF) framework. Its primary significance lies in providing a robust and efficient method for teaching complex motion skills to humanoid robots, directly addressing long-standing challenges related to learning stability, exploration efficiency, and policy robustness in high-dimensional control tasks.

**Accelerated and Robust Skill Acquisition:**
The work demonstrates quantifiable improvements in learning. A2CF achieves convergence 30% faster than baseline methods and lowers failure rates by over 40% (as stated in the abstract), producing policies that are robust and capable of support-free execution. This acceleration in learning translates to reduced training time and computational resources, making the development of complex robotic behaviors more practical.

**Human-Inspired Learning Paradigm:**
By drawing inspiration from how humans (infants, athletes) learn with external support, the A2CF framework offers a biologically plausible and intuitive approach to robot learning. The adaptive nature of the assistive force, which diminishes as proficiency increases, ensures that robots ultimately learn to perform tasks autonomously without becoming over-reliant on external aid.

**Enhanced Generalization and Stability:**
The integration of specific components—Privileged Information (PI), Initial Distribution of Spatial Force (ID), and Random Masking (RM)—is crucial for the framework's effectiveness. PI allows the assistive agent to provide more targeted and effective guidance, especially in varied environments. ID provides beneficial priors that shape initial learning behavior. RM actively mitigates over-dependency on assistance, promoting generalization and enabling the robot to develop robust, independent control strategies more quickly.

**Successful Sim2Real Transfer:**
The successful transfer of learned policies from simulation to real-world humanoid robots for both walking across diverse terrains and performing complex dance routines highlights the practical applicability of A2CF. This capability is vital for real-world robotics, where training solely on physical hardware is often impractical due to cost, safety, and time constraints. The Sim2Sim validation for backflips further supports the potential for real-world deployment of such acrobatic skills.

**Future Impact:**
The A2CF framework has the potential to impact the development of more agile, versatile, and autonomous humanoid robots. By simplifying the acquisition of complex whole-body motions, it could pave the way for humanoids performing tasks in unstructured environments, assisting humans in various capacities, or engaging in human-robot interaction scenarios that require sophisticated physical behaviors.

The proposed future work, which includes extending assistive forces to other robot links (e.g., hands) and enabling robots to autonomously leverage environmental objects (e.g., walls) for self-assistance, suggests a pathway towards even more sophisticated and context-aware learning capabilities. This could lead to a new dimension of autonomy where robots actively seek and utilize environmental cues for self-improvement and task execution, further bridging the gap between human and robotic learning.
