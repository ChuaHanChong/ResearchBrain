# Learn What's Left, Not What's Mastered: Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> SA-MRPO fixes multi-reward GRPO by normalizing each reward objective independently, then discounting each objective's advantage contribution by a batch-level saturation ratio raised to exponent gamma. This reallocates gradient effort toward under-optimized objectives (e.g. correctness) away from near-maxed ones (e.g. length/format), improving the harder objective in 12 of 15 math benchmarks and up to 9.2% on AMC23.

## Problem

- Fixed weighted-sum scalarization before group normalization collapses distinct reward profiles to identical scalars, so rollouts with different behavior get the same GRPO advantage.
- Existing multi-reward methods hold relative objective weights fixed throughout training regardless of how close each objective already is to its ceiling.
- Reward-decoupling methods like GDPO fix the resolution-loss problem but still let training keep spending gradient budget on already-saturated objectives instead of harder ones.

## Method

- ==Saturation ratio==: per-objective batch-mean reward normalized against its attainable min/max range, giving a scalar in [0,1] estimating how solved that objective already is.
- ==Saturation-aware reweighting==: each objective's ==group-normalized advantage== is scaled by weight times (1-saturation)^gamma before summing, down-weighting near-ceiling objectives.
- **SA-MRPO** algorithm: aggregate saturation-weighted advantages are batch-renormalized and plugged into the standard ==clipped GRPO surrogate==, strictly generalizing ==GDPO== (gamma=0) and ==GRPO== (single objective).

## Results

- On Qwen2.5-7B/3B math reasoning, SA-MRPO beats GDPO on **12 of 15** benchmark comparisons, with **+5.0** points on AIME24 and **+3.5** on MATH500 for the 7B three-objective setting.
- On adaptive reasoning with an explicit length-saturation region, SA-MRPO improves accuracy on all five benchmarks by **3.8%** average, up to **+9.2%** on AMC23 (**28.3%** to **37.5%**).
- On code generation (APPS/CodeContests/Codeforces/TACO), SA-MRPO raises pass rate over GDPO by up to **2.3** points on **3 of 4** benchmarks while keeping bug rates comparable.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scalarizing heterogeneous rewards before normalization destroys information even when weights are fixed; per-objective normalization plus saturation-adaptive weighting recovers it without touching the policy-update rule.
> - Saturation-aware reweighting can flip the sign of a rollout's aggregate advantage, not just rescale magnitude, since it changes relative objective dominance.
> - Reallocating gradient toward under-optimized objectives is not free: if objective gradients conflict, boosting one can erode a previously saturated one, so this is an allocation heuristic, not a monotonic-retention guarantee.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) is increasingly applied to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has become a prominent approach in this domain. A key development within RLVR is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), along with its variants, which estimates advantages from groups of rollouts, thereby obviating the need for a learned value function, a component typically required by methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

However, the practical application of RL to LLMs often involves optimizing for multiple reward objectives simultaneously. For instance, an LLM's response may need to be correct, adhere to length or format constraints, ensure safety, or possess executability. Traditional multi-objective RL approaches, including those built upon GRPO, typically address this by scalarizing the reward vector into a single value, often through a fixed weighted sum. This scalarized reward is then used for group-wise standardization.

This research paper identifies and addresses two limitations inherent in this standard scalarization approach:
1. **Reward Resolution Loss:** Scalarizing multiple distinct reward profiles can lead to different combinations of objective rewards yielding the same scalar sum, thereby making these distinct profiles indistinguishable to the optimization process.
2. **Ignorance of Objective Saturation:** The use of fixed weights for objective aggregation means that all objectives retain the same relative importance throughout training, irrespective of their current level of performance or "saturation." This can result in optimization effort (gradient budget) being allocated to objectives that are already well-satisfied, while other, more challenging or under-optimized objectives receive insufficient attention.

The paper positions its proposed method, Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization (SA-MRPO), as an advancement beyond existing multi-reward group relative policy optimization techniques. While methods like Group reward-Decoupled Policy Optimization (GDPO) decouple reward dimensions before aggregation and others like DVAO or GD²PO consider reward variance or advantage conflicts, they do not explicitly account for how close each objective is to saturation. SA-MRPO aims to fill this gap by dynamically adjusting objective importance based on remaining headroom, thereby improving the efficiency and effectiveness of multi-reward policy optimization for LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the identified shortcomings of current multi-reward policy optimization strategies, particularly in the context of reinforcement learning for large language models. When LLMs are trained with multiple objectives (e.g., correctness, length, format), existing methods typically aggregate these objectives into a single scalar reward using a fixed weighted sum. This design choice leads to two fundamental problems that SA-MRPO aims to resolve:

1. **Loss of Reward Resolution:** The scalarization of multiple reward objectives before group-wise standardization results in information loss. Rollouts with distinct profiles across individual reward dimensions can produce the same scalarized sum. Consequently, the GRPO advantage, which is derived from this scalar sum, assigns identical advantages to these distinct rollouts. This means the policy update cannot differentiate between nuanced performance trade-offs across objectives if their aggregated scores are the same, potentially hindering fine-grained optimization. For example, a response that is perfectly formatted but incorrect might receive the same scalar reward as a correct response with a slightly off format, making them indistinguishable for the policy update.

2. **Neglect of Objective Saturation:** With fixed objective weights, the relative importance of each reward objective remains constant throughout the training process. This implies that optimization effort continues to be allocated uniformly or proportionally to these fixed weights, regardless of how well an objective has already been optimized (i.e., its "saturation" level). As an objective approaches its maximum possible reward, its potential for further improvement diminishes. Continuing to dedicate significant gradient budget to such a saturated objective is inefficient, as it diverts resources from other objectives that still have substantial "headroom" for improvement and are more difficult to satisfy. This can lead to a suboptimal allocation of training effort, where harder, under-optimized objectives are neglected in favor of already-solved ones.

To address these issues, the key objectives of the SA-MRPO work are:
- To **preserve per-objective normalization** by standardizing each reward objective independently within its group of rollouts, thus avoiding the initial loss of reward resolution.
- To **adaptively reweight each objective's contribution** to the overall advantage. This reweighting mechanism is designed to be "saturation-aware," dynamically adjusting an objective's influence based on a batch-level estimate of its current degree of saturation.
- To **reallocate optimization effort** towards under-optimized objectives. By progressively downweighting objectives as they approach their maximum attainable reward, SA-MRPO aims to focus gradient updates on areas with greater potential for improvement.
- To **empirically maintain performance** on objectives that are already well-satisfied, even as optimization pressure shifts towards less saturated objectives.
- To demonstrate that this saturation-aware reweighting can **influence the direction of policy updates** (by reversing the sign of an aggregate advantage) rather than merely rescaling their magnitude.
- To show that SA-MRPO **strictly generalizes existing methods** like GDPO (when saturation-aware reweighting is disabled, i.e., γ=0) and GRPO (in the single-objective setting), indicating its broad applicability and theoretical foundation.

The overarching motivation is to develop a more intelligent and efficient multi-reward policy optimization framework that can dynamically prioritize learning "what's left" to improve, rather than continually attempting to master "what's already mastered."

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SA-MRPO builds upon the foundation of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) but introduces modifications to how advantages are computed for multi-reward settings. The core idea is to normalize each reward objective independently and then adaptively reweight their contributions based on a batch-level estimate of objective saturation.

**Preliminaries (GRPO and Multi-Reward Extension):**
The paper first establishes the context of RL for an auto-regressive language model. For a query $q_i$, a behavior policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ generates a group of $G \ge 2$ rollouts, $\{o_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^G$. Each rollout $o_{i,j}$ receives $n$ rewards, $r^{(i,j)}_k$, from objective functions $R^{(k)}$.
In standard GRPO for multiple objectives, individual rewards are combined into a scalar sum: $r^{(i,j)}_{sum} = \sum_{k=1}^n w_k r^{(i,j)}_k$, where $w_k$ are prescribed weights. This scalar sum is then standardized within the group to compute the GRPO advantage:
$A^{(i,j)}_{GRPO} = \frac{r^{(i,j)}_{sum} - \text{mean}(\{r^{(i,g)}_{sum}\}_{g=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r^{(i,g)}_{sum}\}_{g=1}^G)}$.
This advantage is then used in a clipped surrogate objective for policy updates.

**SA-MRPO's Saturation-Aware Group Relative Advantage:**
SA-MRPO deviates from this by addressing the two limitations (reward resolution loss and fixed weights ignoring saturation). It incorporates both the relative quality of a rollout per objective and the current optimization state of each objective into a single aggregate advantage.

1. **Per-Objective Group Statistics:** For each objective $k$ and query $q_i$, the mean ($\mu^{(i)}_k$) and standard deviation ($\sigma^{(i)}_k$) of its reward across the rollouts in the group are computed:
 $\mu^{(i)}_k = \text{mean}(\{r^{(i,g)}_k\}_{g=1}^G)$
 $\sigma^{(i)}_k = \text{std}(\{r^{(i,g)}_k\}_{g=1}^G)$

2. **Batch-Level Saturation Ratio ($s^{(k)}$):** To estimate the current optimization state of objective $k$, SA-MRPO calculates its average reward over the entire current batch of queries and rollouts ($\bar{r}^{(k)}$). Given the attainable minimum ($r^{(k)}_{min}$) and maximum ($r^{(k)}_{max}$) reward bounds for objective $k$, the saturation ratio is defined as:
 $s^{(k)} = \frac{\bar{r}^{(k)} - r^{(k)}_{min}}{r^{(k)}_{max} - r^{(k)}_{min}} \in$
 A lower $s^{(k)}$ implies more remaining headroom for improvement, while a higher $s^{(k)}$ indicates closer proximity to the maximum reward.

3. **Effective Objective Weight ($ew_k$):** The contribution of each objective $k$ to the aggregate advantage is dynamically adjusted using an effective weight ($ew_k$), which considers both its prescribed importance ($w_k$) and its saturation ratio ($s^{(k)}$):
 $ew_k = w_k (1 - s^{(k)})^\gamma$
 Here, $\gamma \ge 0$ is a "saturation exponent" hyperparameter. As $s^{(k)}$ approaches 1 (saturation), the term $(1 - s^{(k)})^\gamma$ approaches 0, thus progressively downweighting the objective. Conversely, for under-optimized objectives (low $s^{(k)}$), the weight remains closer to $w_k$. Increasing $\gamma$ amplifies this reweighting effect, more aggressively suppressing saturated objectives.

4. **Per-Objective Normalized Advantage ($A^{(i,j)}_k$):** For each objective $k$, the relative quality of rollout $o_{i,j}$ within its group is measured by a standardized advantage:
 $A^{(i,j)}_k = \frac{r^{(i,j)}_k - \mu^{(i)}_k}{\sigma^{(i)}_k}$

5. **Aggregate Saturation-Aware Advantage ($\tilde{A}^{(i,j)}$):** These components are combined to form the raw saturation-aware group relative advantage for rollout $o_{i,j}$:
 $\tilde{A}^{(i,j)} = \sum_{k=1}^n ew_k A^{(i,j)}_k$
 This sum integrates the rollout's relative performance for each objective with the dynamic importance assigned by its saturation level.

6. **Batch-Level Normalization:** To stabilize training, the aggregate advantages are normalized across the entire batch of rollouts:
 $\bar{A}^{(i,j)}_{SA} = \frac{\tilde{A}^{(i,j)} - \text{mean}(\{\tilde{A}^{(x,y)}\}_{x,y})}{\text{std}(\{\tilde{A}^{(x,y)}\}_{x,y})}$

7. **Policy Update:** The final $\bar{A}^{(i,j)}_{SA}$ is then directly used in the standard clipped group relative surrogate objective of GRPO, without modifying the underlying policy update mechanism itself.
 $J_{SA-MRPO}(\theta) = E\left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{j=1}^G \frac{1}{|o_{i,j}|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i,j}|} \min \left( \rho_{i,j,t}(\theta)\bar{A}^{(i,j)}_{SA}, \text{clip} (\rho_{i,j,t}(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \bar{A}^{(i,j)}_{SA} \right) \right]$
 where $\rho_{i,j,t}(\theta)$ is the probability ratio between the new and old policies.

**Generalization and Special Cases:**
- **GDPO:** When $\gamma=0$, $(1 - s^{(k)})^\gamma = 1$, making $ew_k = w_k$. In this scenario, SA-MRPO reduces to GDPO, which independently normalizes each reward dimension before aggregation with fixed weights.
- **GRPO:** In the single-objective setting ($n=1$), SA-MRPO further reduces to GRPO, as there's only one term in the sum for $\tilde{A}^{(i,j)}$.

**Discussion on Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges that reallocating optimization effort might, in cases of strong objective conflict, lead to a degradation of an already-optimized objective. This occurs if the negative impact from conflicting gradients of less saturated objectives outweighs the reduced self-improvement term for the saturated objective. SA-MRPO is presented as an adaptive allocation rule rather than a method guaranteeing monotonic retention of every saturated objective. It also notes that nominal reward headroom doesn't always equate to optimizable headroom, as model capacity might limit further improvement even if the prescribed reward range is not fully realized.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of SA-MRPO was evaluated across diverse tasks: mathematical reasoning, adaptive reasoning, and code generation, using various base models and reward configurations. The primary comparison was against GDPO, which represents the state-of-the-art in multi-reward group relative policy optimization without saturation awareness.

#### 4.1. Mathematical Reasoning

- **Setup:** Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct models were trained with two-objective (correctness + length) and three-objective (correctness + length + format) reward settings. Correctness ($R_{correct}$) was binary (1 for correct, 0 otherwise). Length ($R_{length}$) was binary (1 if $\le 4000$ tokens, 0 otherwise). Format ($R_{format}$) was binary, checking for specific XML-style structure. Evaluation was on AIME24, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva Math, and OlympiadBench, reporting pass@1 accuracy and "EXCEED" (fraction of responses exceeding length budget).
- **Results:**
 - SA-MRPO achieved higher accuracy than GDPO in 12 out of 15 benchmark comparisons across the three configurations (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with 3 obj, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct with 2 obj, and 3 obj).
 - For Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (3 obj), SA-MRPO improved accuracy on 4 of 5 benchmarks, with gains up to 5.0 percentage points on AIME24 and 3.5 points on MATH500.
 - Similar patterns were observed for Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, with improvements on 4 of 5 benchmarks in both two- and three-objective settings.
 - These accuracy gains were generally accompanied by only minor changes in the EXCEED metric, indicating that the improvement in correctness did not necessitate significant compromise on the auxiliary length objective.

#### 4.2. Adaptive Reasoning (with Explicit Saturation)

- **Setup:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B was trained with two rule-based objectives: correctness ($R_{correct}$) and a graded length efficiency reward ($R_{length}$). The length reward was designed with an explicit saturation region: it reached maximum (1) if response length was $\le B_{min}=1024$ tokens, decreased linearly between $B_{min}$ and $B_{max}=2048$, and was 0 if $\ge B_{max}$. This setup directly tested SA-MRPO's ability to reallocate effort when one objective (length) was likely to saturate early. Evaluation used accuracy and average response length (LEN).
- **Results:**
 - SA-MRPO improved accuracy over GDPO on all five mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with an average gain of 3.8 percentage points.
 - The most substantial improvement was on AMC23, where accuracy increased from 28.3% (GDPO) to 37.5% (SA-MRPO), a 9.2 percentage point gain.
 - SA-MRPO produced moderately longer responses (average LEN 459 vs. 333 tokens for GDPO). However, these lengths remained below the $B_{min}=1024$ saturation threshold, indicating that the length objective was still well-satisfied.
 - This outcome supported the hypothesis that SA-MRPO successfully reduced the relative influence of the saturated length objective, allowing for increased reasoning capacity and accuracy on the correctness objective.

#### 4.3. Code Generation

- **Setup:** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct was trained on the Eurus-2-RL dataset with two rule-based objectives: test case pass rate ($R_{pass} = \text{passed test cases} / \text{total test cases}$) and executability ($R_{exec} = 1$ if code compiles and runs without error, $0$ otherwise). Executability was considered an "easier" objective to satisfy than the more demanding pass rate. Evaluation was on APPS, CodeContests, Codeforces, and TACO, reporting PASS (average pass rate) and BUG (fraction of programs with errors).
- **Results:**
 - Both SA-MRPO and GDPO significantly improved functional correctness and executability compared to the base model.
 - SA-MRPO achieved higher pass rates on three of the four benchmarks: APPS (+0.6 pp), CodeContests (+1.4 pp), and Codeforces (+2.3 pp). On TACO, its pass rate was slightly lower (-0.4 pp).
 - The BUG rates remained comparable between SA-MRPO and GDPO, both being substantially lower than the base model.
 - These results extended the saturation-aware allocation effect beyond mathematical reasoning, demonstrating that SA-MRPO can prioritize the more difficult functional correctness objective while maintaining gains on the basic executability objective.

#### 4.4. Effect of the Saturation Strength ($\gamma$)

- **Setup:** Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct were trained for one epoch with correctness and length rewards. Various values of the saturation exponent $\gamma$ (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0) were tested.
- **Results:**
 - As $\gamma$ increased, a systematic shift was observed: higher $\gamma$ generally led to higher correctness rewards during training, while the length reward gradually decreased.
 - This confirmed that $\gamma$ controls the allocation of optimization effort. Larger $\gamma$ values more aggressively suppressed the highly saturated length objective, directing more pressure toward correctness.
 - In downstream evaluation, every positive value of $\gamma$ improved average mathematical reasoning accuracy relative to $\gamma=0$ (which corresponds to GDPO).
 - $\gamma=0.5$ achieved the highest average accuracy and strongest performance on AIME24 and AMC23.
 - Larger $\gamma$ values (e.g., 0.75, 1.0) maintained competitive accuracy but generally increased the EXCEED rate, consistent with the reduced emphasis on the length objective. This demonstrated a direct tradeoff controlled by $\gamma$ between improving under-optimized objectives and preserving saturated auxiliary objectives.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization (SA-MRPO), which presents a refined approach to multi-objective reinforcement learning, particularly relevant for the complex task of aligning large language models. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several dimensions:

1. **Enhanced Multi-Objective Optimization Efficiency:** The core contribution of SA-MRPO is its ability to dynamically reallocate optimization effort during training. By explicitly accounting for objective saturation, it shifts gradient budgets from objectives that are already well-satisfied towards those with greater remaining room for improvement. This addresses a fundamental inefficiency in traditional multi-objective RL that uses fixed reward weights, potentially leading to more efficient and targeted learning.

2. **Improved Performance on Harder Objectives:** The empirical results consistently demonstrate that SA-MRPO leads to improved performance on the more challenging or under-optimized objectives (e.g., correctness in mathematical reasoning, pass rate in code generation). This is achieved while generally maintaining the performance of easier or saturated objectives, suggesting a beneficial trade-off that prioritizes areas of greater learning potential. This capability is crucial for LLM development, where complex reasoning or nuanced behavioral objectives often require dedicated optimization.

3. **Theoretical Generalization and Flexibility:** SA-MRPO strictly generalizes existing methods like GDPO (when the saturation exponent $\gamma=0$) and GRPO (in a single-objective setting). This indicates that it provides a more comprehensive framework for multi-reward policy optimization, encompassing prior approaches as special cases. The introduction of the saturation exponent $\gamma$ offers a tunable parameter to control the strength of this adaptive allocation, providing flexibility for practitioners to balance objective priorities.

4. **Broader Applicability Across Domains:** The method's effectiveness was demonstrated across diverse tasks, including mathematical reasoning, adaptive reasoning (with a contrived saturation scenario), and code generation. This broad empirical validation suggests that the principle of saturation-aware reweighting is not domain-specific but rather a general mechanism applicable to various multi-objective RL problems in LLMs and potentially other domains where verifiable and bounded rewards are available.

5. **Implications for LLM Alignment:** The findings suggest that effective LLM alignment, which often involves balancing numerous objectives (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, factuality, efficiency), should consider the dynamic importance of these objectives. SA-MRPO offers a mechanism to move beyond static weighting schemes, potentially leading to more robust and well-rounded LLMs that can adaptively prioritize different aspects of their behavior during training.

6. **Guidance for Future Research:** The work highlights the importance of considering "what's left to learn" rather than just the current reward signals. This perspective could inspire further research into dynamic objective prioritization, capacity-aware allocation, or more sophisticated models of "optimizable headroom" in multi-objective learning. Understanding the trade-offs involved when reallocating optimization pressure, particularly concerning potential degradation of previously optimized objectives, remains an area for further investigation.

In summary, SA-MRPO provides a pragmatic and theoretically grounded solution to a significant challenge in multi-reward policy optimization. Its ability to intelligently reallocate learning effort has the potential to make RL training for LLMs more efficient and effective, leading to models with improved capabilities on complex and difficult objectives.
