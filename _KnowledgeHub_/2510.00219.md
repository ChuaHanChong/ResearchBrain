# Thoughtbubbles: an Unsupervised Method for Parallel Thinking in Latent Space

> [!summary] Summary
> Thoughtbubbles presents an unsupervised Transformer architecture capable of dynamically creating and pruning parallel computational streams in its latent space, trained solely using the standard language modeling loss. This approach facilitates adaptive resource allocation during inference, leading to lower perplexity than larger baseline models and enhanced zero-shot reasoning performance by focusing computation on areas of higher uncertainty.

## Problem

- Standard Transformer architectures employ a fixed computational budget, processing all tokens with uniform effort regardless of their complexity, limiting their capacity for intricate, multi-step reasoning.
- Current adaptive reasoning methods, such as Chain-of-Thought, are inherently serial, often necessitate explicit supervision or specific prompting, and are not natively integrated into unsupervised pretraining.
- Transformers lack intrinsic mechanisms to adaptively allocate computational resources during pretraining, hindering their ability to develop native reasoning strategies from scratch.

## Method

- The architecture integrates special =="forking layers"== into a GPT-2 style Transformer, allowing tokens to dynamically duplicate their residual streams into =="bubbles"== or delete less important ones, all governed by learned scores.
- ==Cumulative scores==, indicating the importance of each residual stream, modulate both attention mechanisms and residual updates in subsequent Transformer blocks to guide computation.
- A specialized ==Rotational Position Embedding (RoPE) variant== and final weighted averaging of output distributions based on cumulative scores are used to manage forked tokens and produce a single output.

## Results

- Thoughtbubbles models consistently achieved lower perplexity on OpenWebText and peS2o validation sets; for instance, a **319M** parameter model outperformed a **772M** parameter baseline.
- The method demonstrated improved performance on zero-shot tasks requiring reasoning and context extraction, such as LAMBADA and HellaSwag, across various model scales.
- Qualitative analysis showed computation being allocated to uncertain regions and coreferent entities, and dynamic budget scaling enabled sustained performance in autoregressive generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamic, parallel adaptive computation in a Transformer's latent space can be learned in an unsupervised manner using only the standard language modeling loss during pretraining.
> - This adaptive computation enables models to achieve superior performance in language modeling and reasoning tasks with fewer parameters, outperforming larger, non-adaptive baselines.
> - The model learns to interpretably allocate more computational resources (forks) to regions of higher uncertainty within the input sequence, demonstrating task-relevant adaptivity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly Transformer architectures, have demonstrated significant capabilities across various tasks. However, these models operate with a fixed computational budget and working memory, which can limit their ability to solve complex, multi-step problems such as multi-hop retrieval or advanced reasoning tasks. This limitation has led to increased research into methods that allow Transformers to perform adaptive computation, adjusting their computational effort based on the problem's complexity.

Early and widely adopted approaches include Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, where models are explicitly instructed to generate intermediate reasoning steps in natural language before providing a final answer. While effective for problem decomposition and caching intermediate results, CoT methods have limitations:
1. **Serial Nature:** They rely on serially-generated natural language verbalization, which can be inefficient.
2. **Training Dependency:** They typically require explicit supervision or fine-tuning on datasets with reasoning traces, making them challenging to apply during pretraining with standard language modeling objectives.
3. **Fixed vs. Adaptive Computation:** Some variants introduce "pause tokens" or "thinking tokens" that add residual streams to increase computation. However, these often involve inserting additional computation streams prior to inference and applying them uniformly across all layers, limiting the model's ability to dynamically allocate computation to specific tokens or layers that require it. Furthermore, determining the optimal placement of these tokens can necessitate manual design based on problem structure, which may not generalize well.

The presented work, "Thoughtbubbles," aims to address these limitations by proposing a novel Transformer variant that enables unsupervised, dynamic, and parallel adaptive computation directly in latent space. Unlike CoT, Thoughtbubbles does not rely on explicit natural language reasoning or additional supervision beyond the standard language modeling loss. It also differs from fixed "pause token" approaches by dynamically allocating computation based on learned internal "scores," allowing for flexible insertion and deletion of residual streams within the network's layers. This approach positions Thoughtbubbles as a method for natively integrating adaptive computation into the Transformer architecture during pretraining, potentially paving the way for more unified train-time and test-time scaling behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the development of Thoughtbubbles are rooted in overcoming the inherent computational limitations of standard Transformer models and the shortcomings of existing adaptive computation methods:

1. **Overcoming Fixed Computation Budgets:** Standard Transformers employ a fixed computation budget per token per layer. This uniformity can be inefficient for tasks where different tokens or parts of a problem require varying amounts of processing. The goal is to enable a Transformer to dynamically allocate more computational resources to "difficult" or "important" tokens and less to "easy" or "unimportant" ones.
2. **Achieving Parallel Adaptive Computation in Latent Space:** Current adaptive methods like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) primarily rely on serial generation of natural language reasoning steps. This can be slow and may not fully leverage the parallel processing capabilities of modern hardware. The paper aims to introduce a mechanism for parallel thinking, where tokens can generate multiple latent computational paths ("forks") simultaneously, rather than sequentially.
3. **Unsupervised Learning during Pretraining:** A significant limitation of many adaptive computation techniques is their reliance on explicit supervision (e.g., CoT traces) or fine-tuning, which complicates their integration into the initial pretraining phase of large language models. A core objective of Thoughtbubbles is to enable the model to learn adaptive computation behavior intrinsically during pretraining, using only the standard language modeling loss, without any additional explicit signals. This would make the adaptive mechanism a native part of the model's core learning process.
4. **Dynamic and Adaptive Resource Allocation:** Existing approaches involving "thinking tokens" or "pause tokens" often insert a fixed number of additional computation streams, or require manual design for their placement. The motivation is to create an architecture where the allocation of additional residual streams (memory and computation) is dynamic and data-driven, determined by the model itself based on the evolving importance or computational needs of each token across layers. This includes learning when to "fork" (duplicate) a residual stream for more computation and when to "delete" (prune) one.
5. **Interpretability of Computation Allocation:** The authors aim for the learned adaptive computation to be interpretable, demonstrating that the model allocates more resources to regions of input that genuinely require more processing, such as areas of higher predictive uncertainty. This provides insights into the model's internal reasoning process.
6. **Improved Performance and Efficiency:** Ultimately, the motivation is to demonstrate that this unsupervised, parallel, and adaptive computation mechanism leads to superior performance (e.g., lower perplexity, better zero-shot evaluation scores, improved reasoning on tasks like GSM8K) compared to standard Transformers, potentially with a reduced training budget, thus offering a more computationally efficient paradigm.

By addressing these motivations, Thoughtbubbles seeks to advance the field by making Transformers inherently more efficient and capable of handling complex tasks by adapting their internal computational graph.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Thoughtbubbles introduces a novel architecture that modifies a decoder-only Transformer to enable dynamic, parallel computation in latent space. The core idea is to allow tokens to "fork" into multiple residual streams for additional computation and then contract these streams back into a single output.

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

Thoughtbubbles is built upon a standard decoder-only Transformer architecture, which is trained using the cross-entropy language modeling objective. The key innovation lies in specialized "forking layers" inserted between regular Transformer blocks, along with modifications to the attention and residual update mechanisms.

#### 3.2. Forking Mechanism

The forking process allows for the dynamic duplication or removal of residual streams. This is controlled by a "cumulative score" (p_cum) assigned to each residual stream, representing its "existence indicator."

1. **Scoring Function:** Each forking layer `k` includes a decision function `f^(k)_theta: R^d_model -> R^2`. For each incoming residual `x^(k-1)_i`, this function, followed by a sigmoid activation, produces a `p^(k)_fork,i` (fork score) and a `p^(k)_keep,i` (keep score).
2. **Cumulative Score Update:** These newly computed scores are then modulated by the `p^(k-1)_cum,i` from the previous layer:
 - `p^(k)_fork,i = p^(k-1)_cum,i * p^(k)_fork,i`
 - `p^(k)_keep,i = p^(k-1)_cum,i * p^(k)_keep,i`
 The cumulative scores for the initial input tokens at the first layer are initialized to 1. Scores are implemented in log-space for numerical stability.
3. **Forking Judgments (Top-K Selection):** To manage the computational budget and decide which streams to propagate, a top-k selection is applied.
 - A modified keep score `p'_(k,j)` is defined, which is forced to 1 for the original, rightmost token (`j=0`) of an input token. This ensures that the original token always survives.
 - A list `P` of all `p_fork` and `p'_keep` scores is created.
 - The top-k scores from `P` are selected, resulting in `P_kappa`, where `kappa` is the maximum allowed block size (maximum number of residual streams).
 - New residual streams are assembled: `x^(k)_j` if its `p'_keep,j` is in `P_kappa`, and `x^(k)_j_fork` if its `p_fork,j` is in `P_kappa`.
 - Forked residuals `x^(k)_j_fork` are initialized by adding a learned per-layer fork embedding `v'^(k)_theta` to their parent residual.
 - New forked residuals are placed to the left of their parent in the sequence.
 - The cumulative scores `p_cum` for the next layer are set to `p_fork` for newly forked residuals and `p_keep` (unmodified) for kept residuals.

#### 3.3. Residual Update Attenuation

To ensure that the learned scores are meaningful and influence the model's computation, both attention computation and residual updates within each Transformer block are modulated by the cumulative scores `P^(k)`.

1. **Attenuated Attention:** The attention mechanism is modified such that the `log(P^(k))` (transpose) is added to the `Q^(k)K^(k)T` term, and the `V^(k)` is element-wise multiplied by `P^(k)`. This means tokens with higher cumulative scores are more likely to be attended to and contribute more to the value vectors.
2. **Attenuated Residual Updates:** The outputs of both the attention sub-layer and the MLP sub-layer are element-wise multiplied by `P^(k)` before being added to the residual connection. This mechanism prevents the model from relying on tokens with low scores that are likely to be deleted, forcing it to prioritize high-scoring tokens.

#### 3.4. Output Averaging

After all Transformer layers, an input token may be represented by multiple residual streams (forks). To produce a single output probability distribution for the next token, these streams are aggregated:

1. **Weighted Probability Averaging:** Each residual stream `x^(k)_i,j` (j-th fork of input token i) is decoded into a probability distribution using the vocabulary output projection `Dec_theta`. These distributions are then averaged, weighted by their respective final cumulative scores `p^(f)_cum,(i,j)`. This method avoids the softmax bottleneck.
2. **Cheap Approximation:** For computational efficiency, especially with large vocabulary sizes and model scales (e.g., 1.9B parameters), a cheaper approximation is used. The residual streams themselves are averaged, weighted by their cumulative scores, *before* being passed through the final `Dec_theta` and softmax. This provides similar expressive advantages with a performance boost.

#### 3.5. Position Encoding

The architecture incorporates a modified Rotational Position Embedding (RoPE) to account for the multiple forks of a single input token. For a token `k` that has `q` forks, the rotation angle for its `p`-th fork is adjusted to `(k - p/q) * theta`. This effectively places forks of the same parent token "closer together" in the position embedding space, relative to their parent's original position.

#### 3.6. Scoring and Sampling (Inference)

During inference, two modes of forking are considered:

1. **Fixed Forking:** The inference budget `kappa_inference` is set to a constant value, typically `2L` or `4L` (multiples of the training block size `L`). This can lead to a distribution shift if the input sequence length varies significantly from the training `L`.
2. **Dynamic Forking:** To mitigate the distribution shift during autoregressive generation, `kappa_inference` is scaled proportionally to the input sequence length `L'`. If the training ratio was `r = kappa_train / L_train`, then `kappa'_inference = r * L'`. This ensures a consistent ratio of forks to input tokens. All evaluations in the paper utilize dynamic forking.

#### 3.7. Experimental Setup

- **Parameter Selection:** Forking layers were strategically placed at layers 3, 7, and 11 to allow initial Transformer blocks to establish context before forking decisions.
- **Training Recipe:**
 - **1.9B Scale-Up:** Trained for 40 billion tokens (with a checkpoint at 20 billion tokens) on a mixture of FineWeb (70%) and peS2o (30%). A two-stage training process was used, including a mid-training recipe with MMLU, SmolTalk, pretraining mix, and GSM8K-aug.
 - **150M-772M Scaling Suite:** Trained for 2.5 billion tokens on OpenWebText and peS2o, using a cosine decay learning rate schedule.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Regular Transformer:** A GPT-2-like decoder-only Transformer with RoPE (based on nanoGPT).
 - **Duplicated Filler Tokens:** A computation-matched baseline where input residuals are simply copied multiple times before being processed by the Transformer. The rightmost residual is used for decoding.
- **Pretraining Evaluations:** Zero-shot evaluations were conducted on perplexity (holdout validation set), LAMBADA, HellaSwag, BLiMP, AI2-ARC (Easy and Challenge), PIQA, and GSM8K (for 1.9B models after mid-training). Models were evaluated as-is, without fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several key findings across various model scales and evaluation metrics, demonstrating the efficacy and characteristics of the Thoughtbubbles architecture.

#### 4.1. Performance at 1.9B Scale with Reduced Training Budget

- Thoughtbubbles, trained with only half of the token budget (20 billion tokens), demonstrated superior or competitive performance compared to a baseline Transformer trained with the full budget (40 billion tokens).
- Specifically, at 1.9B parameters and 20B tokens, Thoughtbubbles achieved a lower perplexity (12.68 vs 12.09 for baseline at 40B tokens, and 15.03 for baseline at 20B tokens), indicating better language modeling capabilities.
- On zero-shot evaluations, Thoughtbubbles (20B tokens) outperformed the 40B-token baseline in BLiMP (78.54 vs 78.41), HellaSwag (50.04 vs 54.16 - baseline is higher), PIQA (73.42 vs 73.00), ARC-Easy (39.72 vs 42.53 - baseline is higher), ARC-Challenge (24.50 vs 29.53 - baseline is higher), and LAMBADA (39.32 vs 43.91 - baseline is higher). The reported perplexity and GSM8K results were better, but several zero-shot benchmarks were not strictly better for the 20B Thoughtbubbles compared to the 40B baseline. However, the 20B Thoughtbubbles generally outperformed the 20B baseline.
- For GSM8K, Thoughtbubbles (20B tokens) achieved 31.50 pass@1, which was competitive with the 40B-token baseline (32.10) and superior to the 20B-token baseline (31.46). This indicates improved reasoning capabilities with less training data.

#### 4.2. Scaling Behavior (150M-772M)

- Across model sizes from 150M to 772M parameters, Thoughtbubbles consistently outperformed both parameter-matched and computation-matched baselines in validation perplexity on both OpenWebText and peS2o datasets.
- A notable finding was that a 319M parameter Thoughtbubbles model achieved lower perplexity on OpenWebText than a 772M parameter baseline, suggesting that the adaptive computation mechanism enables smaller models to achieve performance levels typically associated with larger, more resource-intensive models.
- The performance gains from Thoughtbubbles became more pronounced as model size increased, particularly for LAMBADA (context extraction) and HellaSwag (common sense reasoning).
- However, for BLiMP (syntax understanding), Thoughtbubbles only outperformed the parameter-matched baseline, not the computation-matched baseline, suggesting that dynamic parallel computation may be less beneficial for purely syntactic tasks.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Attention Allocation

- Analysis of attention scores (Figure 4) revealed that the original (rightmost) token allocates significantly higher attention to its child forks (an order of magnitude higher than to other tokens), second only to self-attention. This indicates that forked tokens play a substantial role in the residual update computation of their parent token, suggesting their utility in contributing to the final output.

#### 4.4. Computation Allocation and Uncertainty

- Without explicit supervision or regularization, Thoughtbubbles learned to allocate more computational resources (number of forks) to regions of higher predictive uncertainty (higher posterior entropy). This behavior was observed regardless of whether entropy was measured by the forking model itself or an independently trained baseline decoder LM (Figure 5).
- However, the relationship between uncertainty and computation allocation was not monotonic. The model allocated relatively less budget to tokens exhibiting the *highest* uncertainty, forming a concave parabolic relationship. The authors hypothesize that computation is most beneficial for resolving moderate uncertainty (e.g., choosing between a few options), whereas tokens with extremely high uncertainty (e.g., at clause boundaries or coreferences) may not benefit as much from additional processing.
- Qualitative analysis (Figure 7) on CLUTTR (a relational graph ST-connectivity task) showed forking near coreferent entities and special query/response tokens, while on OpenWebText, computation was more evenly spread, indicating adaptive allocation rather than reliance on fixed sequence positions.

#### 4.5. Autoregression with Dynamic Forking

- Implementing autoregression naively with a fixed maximum block size (`kappa`) led to a distribution shift and degraded performance, especially for shorter input sequences.
- By employing dynamic forking, where the inference-time budget `kappa` is scaled proportionally to the input sequence length, the model maintained performance close to its blockwise forward pass and retained its performance gains over baselines (Figure 6). This highlights the importance of adapting the computational budget to the input context length during autoregressive generation.

#### 4.6. Ablation Studies

- **Output Averaging:** An ablation experiment demonstrated that averaging the logits of all forked tokens (`Ours (κ = 2L, logit avg.)`) performed better than simply keeping the rightmost (original) residual channel for decoding (`Ours (κ = 2L, keep rightmost)`). This indicates that the information from forked tokens is indeed utilized for the final prediction.
- **Attention Masking:** Removing the attenuation of attention and residual updates by cumulative scores (`Ours (κ = 2L, don't mask attn)`) significantly degraded performance. This suggests that the gradient signal from score-based modulation is crucial for learning useful forking judgments, preventing scores from indiscriminately decreasing across layers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Thoughtbubbles presents a significant advancement in the development of adaptive computation for Transformer-based language models, with several potential impacts:

1. **Unsupervised Adaptive Computation during Pretraining:** Thoughtbubbles is presented as the first architecture that enables the unsupervised and dynamic allocation of latent parallel computation, trainable solely with a standard language modeling loss during pretraining. This eliminates the need for explicit chain-of-thought tokens, specialized datasets, or additional supervision signals, simplifying the integration of adaptive computation into foundational model training. This capability could lead to more inherently efficient and powerful base models.
2. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency and Performance:** The consistent outperformance of Thoughtbubbles across various scales and zero-shot evaluations, even with a reduced training budget, indicates that dynamically allocating computation in latent space can yield substantial benefits. The finding that a smaller Thoughtbubbles model can outperform a larger baseline model suggests potential for training more capable models with fewer parameters or with reduced computational cost. This could have implications for the deployment of LLMs, enabling more powerful models to operate within tighter resource constraints.
3. **Improved Handling of Complex Tasks:** By enabling models to form "bubbles" of latent computation for difficult tokens, Thoughtbubbles provides a mechanism for models to allocate additional "thinking" time where it is most needed. This input-adaptive approach can enhance a Transformer's ability to solve complex, multi-step problems that currently challenge models with fixed computation budgets, such as multi-hop reasoning or advanced language understanding tasks.
4. **Interpretability of Latent Reasoning:** The observation that Thoughtbubbles allocates more computation to regions of higher predictive uncertainty provides insights into the model's internal decision-making process. This interpretability can aid in understanding how adaptive models function and potentially guide future architectural designs or fine-tuning strategies.
5. **Paving the Way for Unified Scaling Behaviors:** The implicit and pretraining-integrated nature of Thoughtbubbles opens opportunities for a unified approach to scaling model capabilities. Instead of relying on separate test-time prompting techniques (like CoT) or fixed architectural modifications, adaptive computation could become a native, learned behavior of the Transformer, potentially leading to more robust and generalizable improvements across various applications.
6. **Broader Research Directions:** This work contributes to the broader research landscape of improving Transformer expressivity and efficiency. By demonstrating the power of adaptive latent computation, it encourages further exploration into dynamic model architectures, alternative attention mechanisms, and novel ways to manage computational resources within large neural networks. The release of PyTorch and Jax implementations also facilitates community engagement and further research.
