# OKAMI: Teaching Humanoid Robots Manipulation Skills through Single Video Imitation

> [!summary] Summary
> OKAMI, developed by researchers from UT Austin and NVIDIA Research, enables humanoid robots to learn complex manipulation skills by observing just a single RGB-D video demonstration. The method uses a two-stage approach that generates a reference plan from human actions and then applies object-aware retargeting to adapt these motions to the robot's environment, achieving a 71.7% average success rate on diverse tasks and demonstrating that its generated data can train robust closed-loop policies.

## Problem

- Teaching robots complex manipulation tasks in unstructured environments typically requires extensive programming, teleoperation data, or sample-inefficient reinforcement learning.
- Existing imitation learning methods often require demonstrations recorded from the robot's perspective, struggle with generalizing to new object instances or spatial layouts, and usually need multiple demonstrations.
- Adapting human demonstrations to robots, especially humanoids with different kinematics and environmental contexts, presents a challenge for robust skill transfer.

## Method

- **OKAMI** employs a two-stage process: '==Reference Plan Generation==' extracts a structured plan (task-relevant objects, human motions, keyframes) from a single RGB-D human demonstration video using vision-language models and motion reconstruction.
- 'Object-Aware Retargeting' adapts the extracted human motions to the robot's environment at execution time by separately mapping body motions and hand poses, adjusting trajectories based on new object locations and robot kinematics.
- This system leverages GPT-4V for object identification, Grounded-SAM for segmentation, SLAHMR for human pose estimation, and factorized retargeting to ensure adaptability to different object instances and spatial layouts.

## Results

- OKAMI achieved a **71.7%** average success rate across diverse manipulation tasks (picking, placing, pushing, pouring) on a real humanoid robot.
- The method substantially outperformed the ORION baseline by **58.3%**, demonstrating the importance of full-body motion retargeting for humanoid imitation.
- Data generated by OKAMI successfully trained closed-loop visuomotor policies via behavioral cloning, achieving an average success rate of **79.2%**, which improved with increased trajectory data (e.g., from **58.3%** to **75.0%** for 'sprinkle-salt' with more trajectories).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object-aware retargeting, which adapts human motions based on their relationship to task-relevant objects rather than simple kinematic mimicry, is crucial for robust skill transfer to humanoid robots.
> - A two-stage approach separating reference plan generation from object-aware retargeting allows for efficient skill acquisition from a single human video demonstration.
> - The data generated by OKAMI's open-loop execution can be used to train more robust closed-loop visuomotor policies via behavioral cloning, with performance improving as more trajectories are generated.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Teaching robots to perform complex manipulation tasks in unstructured environments remains a significant challenge in robotics. While humans can effortlessly learn new skills by observing others, robots typically require extensive programming, teleoperation data, or reinforcement learning to acquire new capabilities. OKAMI (Object-aware Kinematic Adaptation for Manipulation from Imitation) introduces a novel approach that enables humanoid robots to learn manipulation skills by watching just a single RGB-D video demonstration.

Developed by researchers from UT Austin and NVIDIA Research, OKAMI leverages the kinematic similarity between humans and humanoid robots to transfer manipulation skills effectively. The method addresses a fundamental challenge in robotics: how to efficiently teach robots new tasks without requiring extensive data collection or programming expertise.

### 2. Background and Context

Traditional approaches to teaching robots manipulation skills have relied on methods such as:

1. Teleoperation: Human operators directly control the robot to perform tasks, which requires expertise and is time-consuming.
2. Motion planning: Requires precise modeling of the environment and task specifications.
3. Reinforcement learning: Sample-inefficient and often requires extensive training in simulation.

Imitation learning offers a promising alternative by enabling robots to learn from demonstrations. However, most imitation learning approaches face challenges:

- They require demonstrations recorded from the robot's perspective
- They struggle with generalizing to new object instances or spatial layouts
- They often need multiple demonstrations to learn effectively

OKAMI addresses these limitations by introducing a two-stage approach that separates the reference plan generation from motion retargeting. This allows the system to understand the key components of a manipulation task from a single human demonstration and adapt the motions to the robot's environment.

### 3. The OKAMI Method

OKAMI frames the problem as teaching a robot to execute manipulation tasks in previously unseen environments through observation of human demonstrations. Formally, it can be modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where:

- States (s) represent RGB-D observations of the environment
- Actions (a) represent motion commands to the robot
- The reward function (r) indicates task completion
- The goal is to find a policy π(a|s) that maximizes task success probability

The core innovation of OKAMI is its two-stage approach:

1. **Reference Plan Generation**: Extract a structured plan from the human demonstration video
2. **Object-Aware Retargeting**: Adapt the human's motions to the robot's environment and kinematic structure

This approach allows OKAMI to generalize across different object instances, spatial layouts, and even human demonstrators, while only requiring a single video as input.

### 4. Reference Plan Generation

The reference plan generation stage processes a demonstration video to extract:

1. **Task-relevant objects**: Using GPT-4V (a vision-language model), OKAMI identifies objects in the video that are relevant to the task. Grounded-SAM segments these objects, and Cutie tracks them throughout the video.

2. **Human motion reconstruction**: SLAHMR (with improvements for hand pose estimation) reconstructs the human's full-body and hand poses as SMPL-H model sequences.

3. **Keyframe identification**: CoTracker and changepoint detection algorithms identify critical moments in the manipulation sequence.

4. **Target/reference object identification**: For each keyframe, GPT-4V analyzes the motion and semantic context to determine which objects are being manipulated (targets) and which serve as reference points.

The output of this stage is a reference plan containing point clouds of task-relevant objects and SMPL-H trajectory segments between keyframes, representing the human's manipulation sequence.

### 5. Object Aware Retargeting

The object-aware retargeting stage adapts the reference plan to the robot's environment at execution time. This involves:

1. **Object localization**: The robot uses its sensors to locate the task-relevant objects in its environment.

2. **Factorized retargeting**: OKAMI separately retargets body motions and hand poses:
 - Body motions are mapped to the task space, with trajectories scaled and adjusted to the robot's kinematics
 - Trajectory warping adapts the robot's arm to reach the new object locations
 - Inverse kinematics computes appropriate joint configurations
 - Human hand poses are mapped to the robot's finger joints

The key innovation in this stage is the object-aware nature of the retargeting. Rather than simply trying to mimic the human's joint angles or end-effector positions, OKAMI understands how the human's movements relate to objects in the environment and adapts the robot's motions accordingly. This allows the robot to successfully perform tasks even when objects are in different positions or are of different sizes compared to the demonstration.

This approach can be mathematically represented as:

For each keyframe i, the robot's pose q_i is determined by:
q_i = IK(T_i × f(p_i, O_demo, O_robot))

Where:
- p_i is the human's pose at keyframe i
- O_demo and O_robot are the object configurations in the demonstration and robot environments
- f is the trajectory warping function that adapts motions based on object relationships
- T_i is the transformation between human and robot coordinate frames
- IK is the inverse kinematics function

### 6. Experimental Results

OKAMI was evaluated on a variety of manipulation tasks in both simulation and on a real humanoid robot. The experiments demonstrated:

1. **High success rate across diverse tasks**: OKAMI achieved a 71.7% average success rate across manipulation tasks including picking, placing, pushing, and pouring.

2. **Significant improvement over baselines**: OKAMI substantially outperformed the ORION baseline (which does not consider human body poses) by 58.3%, highlighting the importance of full-body motion retargeting for humanoid imitation.

3. **Robustness to demonstrator variation**: The system maintained good performance when using videos from different human demonstrators, with success rates ranging from 58.3% to 83.3% depending on the task and demonstrator.

4. **Successful real-world deployment**: The approach was validated on a physical humanoid robot, demonstrating its practical applicability beyond simulation.

The experiments also revealed that the main failure modes were missed grasping (where the robot failed to properly grasp an object) and failed completion (where the robot grasped the object but failed to complete the task correctly).

### 7. Behavioral Cloning and Data Scaling

An important finding was that the data generated by OKAMI could be used to train closed-loop visuomotor policies via behavioral cloning. These policies achieved an impressive 79.2% average success rate, higher than the open-loop OKAMI execution itself.

The researchers also demonstrated that increasing the number of generated trajectories improved the success rate of the behavioral cloning policies. For example:

For the "sprinkle-salt" task, increasing from 50 to 100 trajectories improved the success rate from 58.3% to 75.0%. Similarly, for the "bagging" task, the success rate increased from 66.7% to 83.3%. This suggests that OKAMI can serve as an effective data generator for training more robust robot policies.

### 8. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its impressive capabilities, OKAMI has several limitations:

1. **Upper-body focus**: The current implementation focuses on upper-body motion retargeting within tabletop workspaces, limiting its applicability to tasks requiring full-body coordination or locomotion.

2. **RGB-D requirement**: OKAMI relies on RGB-D videos, which restricts its use with widely available RGB-only internet videos.

3. **Object variation sensitivity**: The system shows limited robustness against large variations in object shapes.

Future research directions identified by the authors include:

1. Incorporating lower-body retargeting to enable locomotion behaviors during video imitation
2. Implementing a whole-body motion controller for more complex tasks
3. Extending OKAMI to use web videos with RGB information only
4. Integrating more powerful foundation models for a general understanding of object interaction

### 9. Conclusion

OKAMI represents a significant advance in the field of imitation learning for humanoid robots. By leveraging the kinematic similarity between humans and humanoids and incorporating object awareness into the motion retargeting process, the system enables robots to learn complex manipulation skills from just a single video demonstration.

The approach's ability to generalize across different object instances, spatial layouts, and human demonstrators makes it particularly promising for real-world applications. Additionally, the demonstration that OKAMI-generated data can be used to train robust visuomotor policies via behavioral cloning suggests a path toward scaling up robot skill learning.

As robotic systems become more prevalent in both industrial and domestic settings, methods like OKAMI that simplify the skill acquisition process will play a crucial role in making robots more adaptable and useful in everyday environments. The object-aware approach to retargeting also aligns with how humans understand and transfer skills, focusing on the relationships between body movements and objects rather than just mimicking specific trajectories or joint angles.
