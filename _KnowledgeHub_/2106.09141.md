# Probing Image-Language Transformers for Verb Understanding

> [!summary] Summary
> DeepMind researchers probed image-language transformers, revealing their limited ability to distinguish fine-grained differences in verbs, subjects, and objects within visual contexts. They introduced SVO-Probes, a new diagnostic benchmark with controlled negative examples, finding that pretraining on cleaner, smaller datasets resulted in better understanding of these nuanced semantic distinctions.

## Problem

- Image-language transformers often achieve high performance on broad tasks but may lack deep, fine-grained understanding of how linguistic elements relate to visual content.
- Existing benchmarks are frequently object-centric, offer limited verb vocabularies, or lack controlled negative examples to systematically evaluate specific linguistic components like verbs.
- It is unclear whether these models truly grasp the semantics of actions and relations, or if they primarily rely on nouns, language priors, or superficial correlations.

## Method

- A novel dataset, **SVO-Probes**, was created with over 48,000 image-sentence pairs and controlled subject, verb, and object negative examples to isolate linguistic components.
- State-of-the-art ==image-language transformer== models were evaluated in a ==zero-shot classification== setting on SVO-Probes to directly probe their pretrained representations.
- Various architectural choices and the influence of pretraining data characteristics (e.g., dataset size and noise) were analyzed for their impact on fine-grained understanding.

## Results

- All evaluated models performed poorly on SVO-Probes, with the best model achieving an overall accuracy of **69.5%** and the MMT model reaching **64.3%** overall.
- MMT demonstrated a verb negative accuracy of **27.8%**, which was notably lower than its subject negative (**39.5%**) and object negative (**52.4%**) accuracies.
- Models pretrained on MSCOCO achieved a significantly higher negative pair accuracy (**60.9%**) compared to those pretrained on Conceptual Captions (**34.8%**), indicating improved robustness to fine-grained mismatches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Verb understanding consistently proved to be the most challenging aspect for all image-language models, demonstrating lower accuracy on verb-negative examples compared to subjects or objects.
> - Pretraining on larger, noisier datasets like Conceptual Captions leads models to over-predict matches, hindering their ability to discern fine-grained semantic differences, whereas cleaner datasets like MSCOCO yield better performance.
> - Negative image-sentence pairs are more effective than positive ones for differentiating between model architectures and revealing the depth of their multimodal comprehension.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multimodal artificial intelligence has seen significant advancements with the development of image–language transformers. These models, which integrate visual and linguistic information, have demonstrated strong performance across various downstream tasks, including visual question answering (VQA) and image retrieval, often achieved through fine-tuning on task-specific datasets. However, a growing body of research questions the depth of understanding embedded within the pretrained representations of these models. Prior studies have indicated that common benchmarks may not fully capture genuine multimodal comprehension. For instance, object retrieval models have been shown to neglect linguistic structure (Akula et al., 2020), VQA models frequently over-rely on language priors (Goyal et al., 2017; Agrawal et al., 2018), and image captioning metrics may fail to detect "hallucinated" objects (Rohrbach et al., 2018).

Previous efforts to address these limitations have introduced datasets and tasks to specifically examine object-level understanding or frequent object interactions. Examples include the FOIL benchmark (Shekhar et al., 2017), which tests a model's ability to differentiate sentences based on a single noun, and datasets like HICO and VRD (Chao et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2016a) focusing on human-object interactions or visual relationships. However, these datasets are typically constrained by a limited number of objects (often the 80 objects in MSCOCO) or verbs, and many do not provide controlled negative examples necessary for granular evaluation of specific linguistic components. For instance, V-COCO (Gupta and Malik, 2015) and ImSitu (Yatskar et al., 2016) include verbs but lack structured negative examples for controlled verb analysis.

This research addresses a gap in the evaluation of fine-grained multimodal understanding, particularly concerning verbs. While objects have received considerable attention, the ability of image–language models to differentiate between actions, a crucial aspect of real-world understanding (e.g., "eating an apple" versus "cutting an apple"), has been less thoroughly investigated with controlled methods. The work positions itself by introducing a novel benchmark designed to specifically probe verb understanding and, more broadly, the capacity of pretrained image–language transformers to grasp subtle differences in linguistic elements (subjects, verbs, and objects) within an image context. It builds upon the zero-shot probing methodology used in prior noun-focused work like FOIL, but extends it to systematically evaluate verbs with a much broader vocabulary and controlled negative samples.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to rigorously investigate the quality of pretrained representations in state-of-the-art multimodal image–language transformers. Specifically, the authors aim to determine:
1. If these models can distinguish different types of verbs in visual contexts.
2. The extent to which these models rely solely on nouns in a given sentence, rather than integrating verb information for multimodal understanding.
3. Which categories of verbs pose particular challenges for these models.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several observations within the field:
- **Gap in Fine-Grained Multimodal Understanding:** Despite their impressive performance on various downstream tasks, there is an ongoing concern that current image–language transformers might achieve high scores by exploiting dataset biases, language priors, or superficial correlations, rather than developing a robust, fine-grained understanding of how language elements relate to visual content. For example, distinguishing "eating an apple" from "cutting an apple" requires a nuanced understanding of the verb's interaction with the object and visual scene, which may not be fully captured by broad performance metrics.
- **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** Current benchmarks, while valuable, often fall short in systematically evaluating specific linguistic components, especially verbs. Many focus on objects, offer a limited verb vocabulary, or lack the controlled negative examples necessary to isolate the model's understanding of a single changed element (subject, verb, or object). This makes it difficult to ascertain if models truly grasp the semantics of actions.
- **Zero-Shot Probing of Pretrained Representations:** The research is motivated by the need to understand what knowledge is implicitly encoded within the *pretrained* representations of these models, rather than what they can achieve after extensive fine-tuning on a specific task. A zero-shot evaluation setting allows for directly probing the inherent capabilities learned during large-scale pretraining.
- **Relevance to Real-World Applications:** The ability of image–language models to accurately relate different aspects of language, particularly verbs, to images is critical for robust performance in real-world applications such as precise image retrieval, accurate image captioning, and reliable visual question answering. If models primarily rely on nouns or superficial cues, their real-world applicability could be limited in scenarios requiring nuanced action comprehension.

To address these motivations, the authors developed a novel benchmark, SVO-Probes, specifically designed to provide controlled evaluations of subject, verb, and object understanding in a zero-shot classification setting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research involves the creation of a new dataset, SVO-Probes, and its subsequent use to evaluate a family of pretrained image–language transformer models in a zero-shot classification setting.

#### 3.1. Task Setup and Dataset Collection (SVO-Probes)

The task is designed as a zero-shot classification problem where a model determines if a given sentence correctly describes an image ("match") or not ("not match"). This setup allows for direct probing of pretrained representations without fine-tuning.

- **Verb List Creation:** To ensure a diverse and visually relevant set of verbs, the authors began by selecting verbs from the Conceptual Captions (CC) training dataset that were also present in the imSitu dataset (Yatskar et al., 2016), which identifies visually recognizable verbs. They further filtered for verbs occurring at least 50 times in CC-train, resulting in a substantial list.
- **Curating Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) Triplets:** From CC-train sentences, SVO triplets were extracted using dependency parse trees. Triplets with pronouns, subjects or objects shorter than two characters, or a frequency less than five were discarded. A critical constraint for SVO-Probes was that each selected triplet must have at least one verb negative (i.e., a plausible alternative verb that changes the meaning), enabling the focused evaluation of verb understanding. This process yielded 11,230 SVO triplets involving 421 unique verbs.
- **Collecting Images:** For each curated SVO triplet, five images were retrieved using the Google Image Search API. Images with URLs found in the Conceptual Captions dataset were removed to avoid direct overlap with pretraining data. Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) workers then verified if the subject, verb, and object were depicted in each image, requiring agreement from at least two out of three annotators. Images marked as cartoons were also discarded. Approximately 58% of images passed this initial verification.
- **Collecting Sentences:** Given a verified image and its corresponding SVO triplet, AMT workers were tasked with writing a grammatical English sentence that used all words from the triplet and accurately described the image. Annotators were instructed to avoid adding extraneous information to keep the focus on the SVO elements. About 86% of images successfully passed this phase.
- **Confirming Negative Image–Sentence Pairs:** The crucial step for controlled evaluation involved creating negative examples. For each positive image-sentence pair (derived from an SVO triplet, e.g., `<girl, lie, grass>`), a negative image was generated by modifying one element of the triplet (subject, verb, or object) while keeping the others constant. For example, a verb-negative for `<girl, lie, grass>` might be `<girl, sit, grass>`, paired with an image of a girl sitting on grass. AMT workers then validated these negative pairs by confirming that the *original positive sentence* did *not* match the *negative image*. Pairs where at least two out of three annotators agreed were included, with 68% passing this final stage.
- **Dataset Statistics:** The final SVO-Probes dataset comprises over 48,000 image–sentence pairs covering 421 verbs, with controlled negative examples for subjects, verbs, and objects.

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

- **Models Evaluated:** The study evaluates several architectural variations of image–language transformers, based on implementations by Hendricks et al. (2021). These variations include:
 - **MMT (Multimodal Transformer):** Replicates the ViLBERT architecture (Lu et al., 2019), using three loss functions (Masked Language Modeling, Masked Region Modeling, Image-Text Matching) and hierarchical co-attention.
 - **Merged–MMT:** Similar to UNITER (Chen et al., 2020), where queries from language (image) attend to both language and image keys/values.
 - **Lang–MMT / Image–MMT:** Variations where queries are restricted to only language or image modalities, respectively.
 - **SMT (Single-Modality Transformer):** Lacks multimodal attention.
 - **No-MRM–MMT / No-MLM–MMT:** MMT variants lacking either the Masked Region Modeling or Masked Language Modeling loss.
- **Pretraining Data:** Models were primarily pretrained on Conceptual Captions (CC), a large, automatically curated dataset. For comparative analysis, one MMT model (MMT-COCO) was pretrained on the smaller, manually-annotated MSCOCO dataset.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Accuracy was calculated for positive and negative image–sentence pairs, as well as an overall average. For negative pairs, accuracy was further broken down by subject-negative, verb-negative, and object-negative types. Models classify a pair as "match" if the image-text classification output is > 0.5, and "not match" otherwise. This classification metric was chosen over image retrieval recall to better penalize models for high scores on negative pairs.
- **Comparative Evaluation on FOIL:** Models were also evaluated on the FOIL dataset (Shekhar et al., 2017) to provide a baseline for noun understanding and to compare with prior work.
- **Detailed Analyses:** The methodology also included analyses to investigate the influence of:
 - Frequency of SVO triplets in pretraining data on negative pair accuracy.
 - Semantic similarity between negative and positive SVO elements (using word2vec cosine similarity) on classifier scores.
 - Image similarity to the pretraining dataset on model performance.
 - The choice of pretraining dataset (noisy CC vs. cleaner MSCOCO) on robustness to fine-grained differences.
 - Identification of the easiest and hardest verbs for the MMT model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the verb understanding capabilities and general robustness of image–language transformers.

#### 4.1. Evaluation on FOIL (Noun Understanding)

- The MMT model (pretrained on CC) achieved an overall average accuracy of 55.4% on FOIL. While its accuracy on positive pairs was very high (99.0%), its performance on negative pairs was significantly low (11.8%). This indicates that MMT frequently overpredicted that image–sentence pairs matched, failing to distinguish between sentences differing only by a noun.
- MMT-COCO (pretrained on MSCOCO) showed improved performance on FOIL, with an average accuracy of 72.0% and negative pair accuracy of 49.0%. While better, it still exhibited a tendency to overpredict matches, suggesting a persistent challenge in fine-grained noun differentiation.

#### 4.2. Comparing Models on SVO-Probes (Subject, Verb, Object Understanding)

- **Overall Performance:** All evaluated image–language transformers performed poorly on SVO-Probes. The MMT model achieved an overall average accuracy of 64.3%, with significantly lower accuracy on negative pairs (34.8%) compared to positive pairs (93.8%). The best-performing model (No-MRM–MMT) reached 69.5% overall accuracy, indicating the challenging nature of the benchmark.
- **Verb Difficulty:** A consistent and primary finding was that verbs were the most challenging linguistic component for all models to understand. For MMT, verb negative accuracy was 27.8%, notably lower than subject negative accuracy (39.5%) and object negative accuracy (52.4%). This pattern held across all architectural variants.
- **Subject vs. Object Difficulty:** Subjects were found to be harder to distinguish than objects across all models. For MMT, subject negative accuracy was 39.5% compared to object negative accuracy of 52.4%. Further analysis using word2vec embeddings revealed that subject words in negative examples tended to be more semantically similar to their positive counterparts than object words, partially explaining this difference.
- **Impact of Architectural Choices:**
 - No-MRM–MMT, which lacked the masked region modeling loss, performed better on SVO-Probes than the full MMT, suggesting that MRM might not be necessary for good Flickr image retrieval performance and could potentially hinder the learning of fine-grained representations needed for SVO-Probes.
 - Lang–MMT, which performed worse on Flickr image retrieval, surprisingly outperformed MMT on SVO-Probes, particularly on negative pairs, indicating that its image representations, which are not updated via an attention mechanism, might lead to different generalization properties.
- **Negative Pairs as Differentiators:** The accuracy on negative pairs exhibited more variation across models and negative types, making them more effective than positive pairs for distinguishing between different model architectures and probing nuanced understanding.

#### 4.3. Accuracy and Frequency at Training

- The relative difficulty of negative types (verbs > subjects > objects) remained consistent regardless of the frequency of the SVO triplets in the Conceptual Captions pretraining data. This indicates that the observed difficulty is not merely a consequence of infrequent examples.
- A small positive correlation was found between SVO similarity (using word2vec) and classifier scores for negative pairs (e.g., 0.264 for subjects, 0.277 for objects). This implies that when the negative SVO is semantically very close to the positive one, models are more likely to incorrectly classify it as a match. The correlation was weaker for verbs (0.145), suggesting other factors contribute to verb difficulty.

#### 4.4. Similarity to Pretraining Data

- Images that were more similar to the Conceptual Captions pretraining data led to higher (worse) scores for negative pairs in MMT compared to less similar images. This effect was more pronounced in MMT than in Lang–MMT, suggesting that MMT's stronger attention mechanism might overfit to specific training images, making it less robust to variations.

#### 4.5. The Choice of Pretraining Dataset

- MMT-COCO (pretrained on the smaller, manually-annotated MSCOCO dataset) significantly outperformed MMT (pretrained on the larger, automatically-curated, and noisier Conceptual Captions dataset) on SVO-Probes. MMT-COCO achieved 68.0% overall accuracy compared to MMT's 64.3%.
- Crucially, MMT-COCO demonstrated considerably better accuracy on negative pairs (60.9%) than MMT (34.8%), and unlike MMT, it did not excessively overpredict matches for positive pairs. This suggests that models pretrained on noisy datasets like Conceptual Captions learn to incorrectly treat "somewhat semantically related" image–sentence pairs as matches, hindering their ability to detect fine-grained mismatches.

#### 4.6. Which Verbs Are the Hardest?

- Specific verbs were identified as consistently difficult across models, including "cut," "argue," and "break." Easiest verbs included "tackle," "reach," "direct," "battle," and "skate."
- Counter-intuitively, verbs explicitly labeled as "visual" in the imSitu dataset were found to be harder for the MMT model, indicating that the definition of visuality might not directly correlate with learnability for these models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of image–language transformers by providing a detailed analysis of their verb comprehension capabilities, revealing several critical insights and opening new avenues for future work.

- **Highlighting Limitations in Fine-Grained Multimodal Understanding:** The study demonstrates that despite their strong performance on broad downstream tasks, current image–language transformers struggle significantly with fine-grained multimodal understanding, particularly when distinguishing between nuanced differences in actions (verbs), and even subjects and objects. This challenges the assumption that high-level task performance directly translates to robust, semantically grounded representations.
- **Introducing a Novel Diagnostic Benchmark:** The creation of SVO-Probes provides the research community with a valuable diagnostic tool. This dataset, with its systematic construction of positive and controlled subject-, verb-, and object-negative examples, enables targeted evaluation of specific linguistic components in a zero-shot setting. This facilitates a deeper understanding of model strengths and weaknesses beyond aggregate metrics.
- **Implications for Pretraining Data Quality:** The finding that a model pretrained on a smaller, manually-annotated dataset (MSCOCO) outperforms one pretrained on a larger, automatically-curated but noisier dataset (Conceptual Captions) is significant. It suggests that noise within large pretraining datasets can lead models to learn superficial correspondences and overpredict matches, thereby hindering their ability to discern fine-grained semantic differences. This has important implications for the design and curation of future large-scale multimodal datasets, advocating for methods to improve data cleanliness or for model architectures more robust to noisy data.
- **Emphasizing the Challenge of Verb Understanding:** The consistent result that verb understanding is more challenging than subject or object understanding across various model architectures underscores a critical area for future research. Current models may be biased towards object recognition, requiring new architectural designs, loss functions, or pretraining strategies specifically aimed at improving action and relational comprehension.
- **Guiding Model Development:** The identification of specific architectural choices (e.g., the potential hindrance of MRM loss in some contexts) and the influence of pretraining data characteristics offer concrete directions for developing more robust and semantically precise multimodal transformers. Future models could benefit from improved mechanisms for discerning subtle action differences and a greater resilience to training data imperfections.
- **Shifting Evaluation Paradigms:** This work encourages the research community to move beyond predominantly object-centric evaluations and to develop benchmarks and models that explicitly address the complexities of verb and relational understanding. This shift is crucial for advancing towards models that possess a more comprehensive and human-like grasp of visual-linguistic interactions.
