# Any-point Trajectory Modeling for Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Any-point Trajectory Modeling (ATM) introduces a two-stage framework that leverages abundant unlabeled video data to pre-train a model capable of predicting future 2D point trajectories. This pre-trained model then guides a robot policy, trained on minimal action-labeled demonstrations, leading to enhanced performance on diverse manipulation tasks and enabling cross-embodiment skill transfer.

## Problem

- High cost and time consumption of collecting sufficient action-labeled demonstration data for robot learning, leading to a significant data scarcity bottleneck.
- Prior video pre-training methods struggle to effectively bridge the gap to policy learning, either by only learning general visual features (lacking temporal dynamics) or by computationally intensive pixel-level prediction (prone to hallucinations).
- Difficulty in transferring robot skills across different embodiments (e.g., human to robot) or robot morphologies due to reliance on task-specific features or limited generalization of learned representations.

## Method

- A two-stage framework: first pre-trains a '==Track Transformer==' model to predict future 2D trajectories of arbitrary points from action-free video datasets, using a self-supervised approach with off-the-shelf point trackers and masked modeling.
- Second, a visuomotor policy is trained with behavioral cloning on a minimal set of action-labeled demonstrations, taking the current observation and the predicted future point trajectories from the frozen Track Transformer as input.
- The policy architecture integrates the predicted trajectories through both early and late fusion, effectively transforming the end-to-end control problem into a sub-goal following task without requiring explicit language instructions during policy execution.

## Results

- Outperformed state-of-the-art video pre-training baselines on the LIBERO benchmark, achieving an average **63%** success rate compared to the highest baseline of **37%** on over 130 language-conditioned manipulation tasks.
- Demonstrated successful human-to-robot and robot-to-robot (cross-morphology) skill transfer by leveraging unlabeled human or different robot videos for trajectory modeling.
- Improved data efficiency, achieving comparable or better performance with only **4%** of action-labeled robot demonstrations relative to baselines requiring **20%** or more.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Any-point trajectory modeling provides a structured, physically grounded, and computationally efficient representation for motion dynamics, avoiding the issues of pixel-level video prediction models.
> - Decoupling the learning of general motion priors from abundant unlabeled video data and task-specific policy learning with minimal labeled data makes robot skill acquisition more scalable and data-efficient.
> - Predicted point trajectories serve as dense, concrete subgoals, simplifying the downstream policy learning problem and enabling more robust control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning, especially through imitation from human demonstrations, has seen significant advancements. However, a persistent bottleneck remains the high cost and labor-intensiveness of collecting action-labeled demonstration data, which often requires teleoperation. While large datasets have been shown to scale performance in robot learning, the collection process for these datasets (e.g., 130K trajectories taking 17 months in some cases) is a major constraint.

To overcome this, researchers have explored leveraging readily available, unlabeled video data. However, extracting control-specific information from videos without explicit action labels is challenging. Prior approaches have largely fallen into two categories:

1. **Feature Representation Learning:** Methods like R3M and TCN use self-supervised objectives to learn a general visual feature representation from videos, which can then be used by a policy. While useful, these representations primarily describe the *current state* and largely neglect the crucial *transition dynamics* necessary for predicting future states and guiding actions.

2. **Video Prediction Models:** More recently, approaches like UniPi have attempted to pre-train models to predict future image frames from current ones. These predicted frames can serve as sub-goals for a goal-conditioned policy. However, this approach faces significant challenges:
 - **Computational Demands:** Learning to predict every pixel in future frames is computationally intensive for both training and inference.
 - **Hallucinations and Physical Infeasibility:** Pixel-level prediction can lead to unrealistic or physically impossible future states, as it couples physical motion with extraneous visual details like texture and lighting. This lack of "physical grounding" hinders robust control.
 - **Open-loop Execution:** High inference costs often force these models into open-loop execution, where a sequence of future frames is predicted once at the beginning, making policies less robust to real-world uncertainties.

Furthermore, efforts to incorporate structured representations like key points, meshes, or neural 3D representations have shown promise for data efficiency, but they often limit policies to specific tasks due to strong inductive biases. Learning from human videos is another valuable avenue, but prior works often rely on explicitly extracting specific features like hand poses or contact regions, which can lose information about general object dynamics.

**"Any-point Trajectory Modeling (ATM)"** directly addresses these limitations by proposing a novel, structured representation for bridging video pre-training and policy learning. Instead of predicting future pixels or relying on abstract features, ATM predicts the future *trajectories of arbitrary points* within a video frame. This approach offers several key advantages within the broader landscape:

- **Abstraction and Inductive Bias:** By focusing on point trajectories, ATM naturally incorporates physical inductive biases such as object permanence and continuous motion, leading to a more faithful abstraction of physical dynamics compared to pixel-level prediction.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Modeling point trajectories is significantly less computationally demanding than predicting entire image frames, enabling closed-loop execution and more robust policies.
- **Universality and Generalizability:** Unlike task-specific structured representations, arbitrary point trajectories provide rich, universal motion information that can apply across a wide range of manipulation tasks and even different embodiments (humans, various robots).
- **Dense Guidance:** The predicted trajectories provide explicit, dense guidance or "subgoals" to the control policy, simplifying the downstream policy learning problem.

Thus, ATM positions itself as a robust, efficient, and highly generalizable method for unlocking the vast potential of unlabeled video data, pushing the boundaries of data-efficient robot skill acquisition and generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind "Any-point Trajectory Modeling" stems from the urgent need to address the data scarcity problem in robot learning. While deep learning models thrive on large datasets, collecting sufficient action-labeled demonstrations for robotics is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. The authors explicitly state this as a "significant bottleneck."

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out to achieve several key objectives:

1. **Effective Utilization of Unlabeled Video Data:** The overarching goal is to develop a framework that can extensively leverage large-scale, readily available *action-free video datasets* (e.g., from human activities or other robots) to pre-train models for robot control. This aims to shift the reliance from costly action-labeled data to more accessible video sources.

2. **Bridge Video Pre-training and Policy Learning:** The authors seek a novel method to extract *control-specific information* from videos and translate it into a form usable by a visuomotor policy. Previous attempts struggled with this translation, either by only learning general features or by producing computationally expensive and often unrealistic pixel-level predictions.

3. **Provide Detailed Control Guidance:** A core objective is for the pre-trained model to offer "detailed control guidance" to the downstream policy. This guidance should be concrete and actionable, transforming the challenging end-to-end policy learning problem into a simpler sub-goal following task. The aim is to enable the learning of robust policies with *minimal* action-labeled data.

4. **Overcome Limitations of Prior Video Prediction:** Specifically, the work aims to circumvent the challenges associated with pixel-level video prediction models: their high computational demands, tendency for hallucinations (unrealistic predictions), and difficulty in abstracting physical motion from visual appearance. By modeling point trajectories, the authors aim for a more physically grounded and efficient representation.

5. **Enable Cross-Embodiment and Cross-Morphology Transfer Learning:** A significant objective is to demonstrate that the learned trajectory representation is universal enough to facilitate transfer of skills from human videos or videos of robots with different physical characteristics (morphologies) to a target robot. This would greatly expand the pool of usable training data.

In essence, the motivation is to make robot learning more scalable, data-efficient, and generalizable by tapping into the immense, currently underutilized reservoir of unannotated video data, while introducing a computationally efficient and physically intuitive representation for motion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Any-point Trajectory Modeling (ATM)" framework is designed as a two-stage process, neatly decoupling the learning of motion priors from videos and the learning of control actions.

**Stage 1: Any-point Trajectory Modeling from Action-Free Video Datasets**

The first stage focuses on pre-training a "Track Transformer" to predict future 2D point trajectories (tracks) in a video frame.

- **Self-supervised Track Annotation:**
 - The process begins with an action-free video dataset ($T_o$).
 - To obtain ground truth trajectories for training, the authors leverage recent advancements in off-the-shelf vision models for accurate point tracking, specifically using Co-Tracker.
 - For each video, a time step is randomly sampled, and initial points are randomly sampled on that frame. The tracker then generates the corresponding 2D trajectories of these points across the video.
 - A crucial heuristic is applied to address the issue of static background points: initially, a grid of points is tracked. Then, points that exhibit minimal movement (low variance in position over time) are filtered out, and new points are resampled around the remaining *dynamic* locations. This ensures that the generated tracks are informative, focusing on objects and agents in motion.

- **Multimodal Track Modeling (The Track Transformer):**
 - The core of Stage 1 is the "Track Transformer" model. It's trained to predict future 2D coordinates of queried points conditioned on the current image observation, the initial positions of the points, and a language instruction (if available for the task).
 - **Input Encoding:** Different modalities are encoded into a shared embedding space:
 - **Tracks:** Future positions of points are masked out (masked prediction objective). Each point is encoded into a token.
 - **Language Instruction:** A pre-trained BERT encoder is used to obtain a task embedding.
 - **Images:** Images are split into patches, and a portion (50%) of these patches are randomly masked out, similar to Masked Autoencoders (MAE).
 - **Transformer Architecture:** All these tokens are fed through a large transformer model.
 - **Output Decoding:** The track tokens are decoded into future trajectories. Additionally, the masked image patches are reconstructed as an auxiliary task, which the authors find beneficial for more complex tasks.
 - **Rationale:** This pre-training process allows the Track Transformer to learn a "motion prior" – how objects and points move within video frames – abstracting away pixel-level details and incorporating inductive biases like object permanence. It learns "what to do next" by generating concrete future states.

**Stage 2: Track-guided Policy Learning with Few-shot Action-Labeled Demos**

Once the Track Transformer is pre-trained, it is frozen and used to guide a control policy, which is trained on a minimal amount of action-labeled demonstration data ($T_a$).

- **Arbitrary Points Tracking for Policy:**
 - For policy inference, the heuristic filtering used in pre-training (which requires knowing future point movements) is avoided. Instead, a fixed set of 32 points on a grid is used as input to the pre-trained Track Transformer. The authors note ATM's robustness to the input point distribution due to its permutation invariance.

- **Track-guided Policy Architecture:**
 - The policy $\pi(a_t | o_t, p_{t:t+H})$ takes the current observation ($o_t$) and the *predicted* future tracks ($p_{t:t+H}$) from the frozen Track Transformer as input to predict actions ($a_t$).
 - The policy architecture is transformer-based (inspired by ViT-T and similar to prior works like), designed to process visual observations (multiple views), proprioception, and the predicted tracks.
 - **Fusion Strategy:** The predicted tracks are integrated into the policy architecture at two points:
 - **Early Fusion:** Tracks are incorporated *before* the main transformer blocks (e.g., as part of the initial token sequence).
 - **Late Fusion:** Tracks are also combined with the spatial CLS token *after* the transformer blocks, right before the action prediction MLP head. This dual integration ensures effective utilization of the guidance.
 - **Key Insight:** Crucially, the policy **no longer needs the language instruction** as a direct input during this stage. The predicted tracks inherently provide the fine-grained subgoals and task specification, effectively reducing the complex policy learning problem to a simpler "sub-goal following" or "inverse dynamics" problem.
 - **Training:** The policy is trained using behavioral cloning with Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss on the limited action-labeled demonstration dataset. The weights of the policy model are randomly initialized, not copied from the pre-trained Track Transformer.

This two-stage approach allows the system to first learn general motion dynamics from abundant unlabeled video data and then efficiently adapt to specific tasks with minimal action-labeled robot demonstrations, by essentially learning to execute the high-level motion plans provided by the pre-trained trajectory model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents extensive experimental results in both simulation and the real world, along with valuable ablation studies, to validate the effectiveness of the Any-point Trajectory Modeling (ATM) framework.

**A. Video Pre-training for Imitation Learning (Simulation & Real World)**

- **Simulation (LIBERO Benchmark):**
 - ATM was evaluated on over 130 language-conditioned manipulation tasks across five challenging LIBERO benchmark suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long, 90), using only 10 action-labeled and 50 action-free robot videos per task.
 - **Significant Outperformance:** ATM significantly surpassed strong state-of-the-art video pre-training baselines (Behavioral Cloning (BC), R3M-finetune, VPT, UniPi). On average, ATM achieved a 63% success rate compared to the highest baseline success rate of 37%, marking an improvement of over 80%.
 - **Strength in Complex Tasks:** ATM showed particularly superior performance on LIBERO-Goal and LIBERO-Long tasks, which require a deeper understanding of task goals and longer-horizon planning. The authors attribute this to ATM's closed-loop future trajectory prediction, which provides clear, dynamic guidance unlike static language instructions.
 - **Baseline Analysis:**
 - **BC:** Performed poorly, highlighting the need for additional video data.
 - **VPT:** Performed poorly due to errors in pseudo-action labels generated by the inverse dynamics model.
 - **R3M-finetune:** Showed some improvement over BC but still lagged, suggesting that representation learning alone is insufficient without explicit temporal dynamics.
 - **UniPi:** Underperformed, especially on complex tasks, attributed to video prediction models generating physically unrealistic or hallucinatory future frames, and their high computational cost preventing closed-loop execution.
 - **Universality with Diffusion Policy:** ATM's benefits extended to the state-of-the-art Diffusion Policy. When integrated as an additional conditioning input ("ATM Diffusion Policy"), it consistently improved performance across all LIBERO suites, demonstrating its versatility across different policy architectures.

- **Real World Experiments:**
 - Conducted on a 6-DOF UR5 robot for 5 language-conditioned manipulation tasks.
 - Consistent with simulation, ATM significantly outperformed BC and other video pre-training baselines, validating its efficacy in real-world settings with partial observations and proprioception states.

**B. Human-to-robot and Robot-to-robot Transfer**

- **Human-to-Robot Transfer:**
 - ATM was tested on tasks like "fold cloth," "put tomato into pan," and "sweep toys" using 10 robot teleoperation demos and 100 action-free human manipulation videos.
 - **Key Finding:** ATM trained with human videos significantly outperformed both vanilla BC (on robot demos only) and ATM trained with only the limited robot videos. This demonstrates ATM's ability to effectively leverage out-of-distribution human videos for robust trajectory prediction and subsequent policy learning. The visualizations showed human videos were critical for learning accurate trajectory predictions.

- **Robot-to-robot Transfer (Cross-morphology):**
 - Demonstrated transfer from Franka arm videos to a UR5 policy for a pick-and-place task, using 160 action-free Franka videos and 10 action-labeled UR demos.
 - **Key Finding:** Additional cross-embodiment data from the Franka arm led to significantly better results for the UR5 policy. Even when the trajectory model was trained *only* on Franka videos, it led to much better performance than BC without Franka videos, highlighting the power of the point trajectory representation for skill transfer across different robot platforms.

**C. Ablation Analysis**

- **Effect of Action-labeled Trajectories:** ATM significantly improved data efficiency. With only 4% of action-labeled demos, ATM achieved comparable (or better) performance to BC trained with 20% of the demos, and performance approached BC with 100% data when ATM was trained on 20% demos.
- **Effect of Track Length:** An optimal track length of 16 future steps generally yielded the best performance. Shorter lengths (e.g., 4 steps) significantly decreased success rates, while excessively long tracks (e.g., 32, 64 steps) could also negatively impact performance, especially for precision-focused tasks, likely due to increased noise in longer predictions.
- **Effect of Image Masking in Track Transformer:** The auxiliary task of reconstructing masked image patches during track transformer pre-training proved beneficial, leading to a slight performance improvement, suggesting it helps the model jointly reason about different image regions.
- **Effect of Early and Late Fusion in Policy Architecture:** The study revealed that "late fusion" (integrating predicted tracks with the CLS token just before the action MLP head) was critical for performance, leading to the most significant drops when removed. This indicates that the predicted tracks serve as highly effective subgoals that are best utilized at the final stages of action prediction.

In summary, the main findings strongly support ATM as a robust, data-efficient, and generalizable framework for robot learning. Its ability to leverage large volumes of unlabeled video data, provide dense and effective guidance through point trajectories, and transfer knowledge across embodiments marks a significant step towards more scalable robot skill acquisition.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Any-point Trajectory Modeling (ATM)" framework represents a significant advancement in robot learning, with profound implications for how robots acquire new skills and interact with the world. Its significance can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Addressing the Data Bottleneck:** This is arguably the most critical impact. By demonstrating that robust visuomotor policies can be learned from *minimal* action-labeled data when complemented by abundant action-free video data, ATM offers a scalable solution to the long-standing challenge of data collection in robotics. This opens the door to training robots on vast, readily available, and passively collected video sources (e.g., YouTube videos of humans, unlabelled robot recordings), dramatically accelerating the pace of robot skill acquisition.

2. **Novel and Effective Representation for Dynamics:** The shift from pixel-level video prediction or abstract feature learning to *any-point trajectory modeling* is a core innovation. This particle-based representation is:
 - **Interpretable:** Point trajectories are easy to visualize and understand, offering insights into the learned motion priors.
 - **Structured:** It imposes a natural structure that captures continuous motion and object permanence, which are fundamental physical inductive biases. This makes the learned dynamics more robust and physically grounded, overcoming the hallucination issues of pixel-level models.
 - **Computationally Efficient:** Predicting and processing 2D point trajectories is far less demanding than full video frames, enabling closed-loop execution of policies, which is vital for real-world robustness and reactivity.

3. **Enhanced Generalization and Transfer Learning:**
 - **Cross-Task Generalization:** ATM's strong performance across diverse language-conditioned manipulation tasks (LIBERO benchmark) indicates its ability to generalize to new scenarios, objects, and task goals, which is crucial for deploying robots in varied environments.
 - **Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The successful demonstration of learning from human videos and videos of different robot morphologies is a groundbreaking achievement. This capability means that robot learning is no longer limited to data collected by the *exact* target robot, but can leverage a much broader spectrum of demonstrations from humans or other robots, further amplifying data scalability. This could pave the way for robots learning directly from human demonstrations without complex teleoperation systems or explicit hand tracking.

4. **Simplifying Policy Learning:** By transforming the complex end-to-end visuomotor policy learning problem into a simpler "sub-goal following" task, ATM makes policy training more efficient and effective. The pre-trained trajectory model essentially provides the "what to do," allowing the policy to focus on the "how to do it" with minimal action-labeled examples. The finding that the policy no longer needs language instructions directly, as the tracks convey the task's essence, is a strong testament to this simplification.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several exciting avenues for future research. As the authors mention, exploring reinforcement learning for the trajectory-following stage could eliminate the need for *any* action-labeled data. Furthermore, scaling ATM to even more "in-the-wild" video datasets, which present challenges like multi-modal distributions and diverse camera motions, is a natural next step that could lead to truly generalist robots.

In conclusion, "Any-point Trajectory Modeling" offers a pragmatic, efficient, and highly effective framework that significantly pushes the boundaries of data-efficient robot learning. Its ability to unlock the potential of unlabeled video data for skill acquisition and transfer makes it a pivotal contribution towards developing more capable, adaptable, and autonomous robotic systems.
