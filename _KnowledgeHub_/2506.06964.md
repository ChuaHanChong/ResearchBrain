# Offline RL by Reward-Weighted Fine-Tuning for Conversation Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Adobe Research developed Refit and Swift, two reward-weighted fine-tuning algorithms for offline reinforcement learning (RL) with Large Language Models (LLMs) tailored for multi-turn conversational optimization. These methods directly leverage explicit reward signals from pre-collected datasets, demonstrating improved accuracy and conversation quality over existing supervised fine-tuning and direct preference optimization baselines in multi-turn question answering tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional online RL for LLMs often requires extensive, costly, or unsafe online interactions, making offline learning from pre-collected datasets essential.
- Existing offline RL methods for LLMs, such as those based on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), often indirectly optimize the reward signal, leading to potential information loss and instability from complex components like token-level propensity scores.
- A need existed for practical, stable, and statistically efficient offline RL algorithms that could directly optimize LLM policies using explicit reward functions for conversational tasks without high variance or numerical instability.

## Method

- The authors recast offline RL as '==reward-weighted fine-tuning==,' developing **Refit**, an algorithm that directly optimizes a lower bound of the online RL objective by weighting the log-probabilities of actions by the trajectory's overall reward, thus avoiding unstable token-level propensity score ratios.
- They introduced Swift, an extension of Refit, which incorporates context-specific standardized rewards to mitigate high empirical variance from unscaled reward signals, promoting more stable learning and improved policies.
- Both Refit and Swift are designed to be practical by implementing them as minor modifications to standard SFT training primitives, facilitating integration with existing LLM fine-tuning pipelines.

## Results

- Swift achieved the highest accuracy in 7 out of **12** multi-turn reasoning experiments and ranked among the top two in **10** out of **12**, consistently outperforming all SFT and DPO-based baselines.
- Both Refit and Swift demonstrated competitive or superior performance across a range of conversation quality metrics (e.g., Accuracy, Reasoning Ability, Pedagogical Value) in multi-turn QA tasks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that increasing training data improves performance and that the core findings are robust across different AI evaluators (GPT-4o and Claude 4 Opus), supporting the generalizability of the methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly weighting policy updates based on full trajectory rewards is more effective for LLM optimization than transforming rewards into binary preferences or selecting only top-performing trajectories for SFT.
> - Eliminating token-level propensity score ratios from the offline RL objective significantly improves numerical stability and practicality when fine-tuning LLMs, which are prone to high variance issues.
> - Standardizing rewards (using context-specific means and standard deviations) effectively reduces gradient variance during policy optimization, leading to empirically superior learned policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a framework designed for agents to learn sequential decision-making in environments with the objective of maximizing long-term rewards. Traditional RL algorithms, such as Q-learning and policy gradients, often necessitate extensive online interaction with the environment. However, many real-world applications face constraints that prevent or complicate such online interactions, including safety concerns, computational costs, and engineering complexities. This challenge motivates the field of offline reinforcement learning (offline RL), where policies are learned from pre-collected datasets of trajectories and associated rewards, rather than through live interaction. Offline RL is particularly relevant for domains where large datasets of interactions are readily available or can be simulated, such as in natural language processing (NLP) tasks like question answering (QA).

In the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), fine-tuning techniques like Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) have become standard for post-training adaptation. Recent works have applied these methods to conversational AI, particularly for learning to ask clarifying questions or improving reasoning in multi-turn QA settings. For instance, STaR-GATE leverages SFT on high-reward trajectories, while StepDPO employs DPO to optimize responses at each conversation step. These approaches often convert the reward signal into a format suitable for SFT or DPO, such as binary preferences or selection of "best" trajectories, potentially leading to information loss from the original reward signal.

This work addresses the problem of applying offline RL to LLMs for conversation optimization, specifically aiming to overcome the limitations of indirect reward optimization inherent in SFT and DPO-based methods. It seeks to provide a practical and stable alternative that directly optimizes for rewards without relying on complex components like token-level propensity scores or auxiliary distributions, which can introduce instability or additional hyperparameters. By recasting offline RL as reward-weighted fine-tuning, the authors position their approach as a direct enhancement to standard LLM training paradigms, offering a more statistically efficient method for leveraging reward signals in offline settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop practical and stable offline RL algorithms for Large Language Models (LLMs) that directly optimize a given reward function for conversation optimization. The authors identify several motivations and specific goals:

1. **Direct Reward Optimization**: Existing state-of-the-art methods for conversation optimization with LLMs, such as those based on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), often do not directly optimize the reward signal. Instead, they transform the RL problem into an SFT or DPO problem, for example, by fine-tuning on only the most rewarding trajectories or by using pairwise preferences. This indirect optimization can result in information loss from the reward signal. The authors aim to design methods that leverage the full reward signal more effectively.

2. **Stability and Practicality**: Traditional offline RL objectives, particularly those involving importance sampling (e.g., in PPO or GRPO), often include ratios of token-level propensity scores. These ratios can become very large when the learned policy deviates significantly from the data logging policy, leading to high variance, numerical instability, and the need for careful hyperparameter tuning (e.g., clipping in PPO). A key motivation is to develop an objective that avoids these propensity score ratios to yield a more stable and practical algorithm suitable for LLM fine-tuning.

3. **Integration with Standard LLM Training**: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is a widely adopted and well-understood technique for post-training LLMs. The authors are motivated to recast the offline RL problem in a way that allows its solution using minor modifications of standard SFT training primitives. This approach aims to reduce the computational and implementation complexity associated with deploying RL for LLMs.

4. **Variance Reduction in Policy Optimization**: The raw reward signal, especially when non-negative and unscaled, can lead to high variance in gradient estimates during policy optimization. This can hinder learning efficiency and policy quality. A specific objective is to investigate the use of standardized rewards to reduce this variance and potentially improve the learned policies.

5. **Comprehensive Evaluation**: The research aims to empirically demonstrate the value of the proposed approaches on a diverse set of multi-turn Question Answering (QA) problems. This involves evaluating the agent's ability to reason about potential answers and ask clarifying questions across various domains, including open-book exams, science topics, conversational text-to-SQL, and mathematical dialogues. The evaluation includes not only the optimized reward but also other metrics like reasoning ability, pedagogical value, and confidence.

In summary, the core motivation is to bridge the gap between robust offline RL theory and practical, stable, and efficient application to LLMs for conversational tasks, offering a direct and statistically efficient way to optimize for rewards without incurring the complexities or information loss of prior methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on recasting offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) as reward-weighted fine-tuning for Large Language Models (LLMs), developing two algorithms: Refit and Swift.

**Problem Formalization:**
The problem of learning multi-turn conversation policies is framed as a generic RL problem. An agent interacts with an environment, taking actions $a_t$ (strings of tokens) conditioned on context $x$ and conversation history $\tau_{t-1}$. The environment responds with observations $y_t$. A conversation is a trajectory $\tau_n = (a_1, y_1, \ldots, a_n, y_n)$. A non-negative reward function $r(x, \tau_n) \geq 0$ measures the quality of the conversation. The agent's policy $\pi(a | x, \tau_{t-1}; \theta)$ is parameterized by $\theta$. The objective is to maximize the expected policy value $V(\theta) = E_{x \sim q, \tau_n \sim \pi(\cdot|x;\theta)}[r(x, \tau_n)]$.

**Addressing Online RL Challenges:**
Directly optimizing $V(\theta)$ via policy gradients is challenging because trajectories need to be resampled with policy changes, and a reward model is required for every sampled trajectory. To address this, the authors resort to offline RL, utilizing a pre-collected dataset $D = \{(x, \tau_n, r)\}$ generated by a data logging policy $\pi_0$. The classical approach of importance sampling (Equation 3) to correct for selection bias is noted, but its high variance, especially when $\pi_0(\tau_n | x)$ is small, is highlighted as a drawback.

#### 3.1. Reward-Weighted Fine-Tuning (Refit)

The core idea of Refit is to maximize a lower bound on the online RL objective $V(\theta)$ that avoids propensity score ratios.
Based on Lemma 1, for any policies $\pi$ and $\pi_0$ and non-negative reward function, the online objective $V(\theta)$ is lower bounded by:
$J(\theta) = E_{x \sim q, \tau_n \sim \pi_0(\cdot|x)}[r(x, \tau_n) \log \pi(\tau_n | x; \theta)] + C_1$
where $C_1$ is a constant independent of $\theta$. Maximizing $J(\theta)$ thus maximizes a lower bound of $V(\theta)$.
By plugging in the definition of trajectory probability $\pi(\tau_n | x; \theta) = \prod_{t=1}^n p(y_t | x, \tau_{t-1}, a_t) \pi(a_t | x, \tau_{t-1}; \theta)$, the objective simplifies to:
$J(\theta) = E_{x \sim q, \tau_n \sim \pi_0(\cdot|x)}[r(x, \tau_n) \sum_{t=1}^n \log \pi(a_t | x, \tau_{t-1}; \theta)] + C$
where $C$ is another constant independent of $\theta$.
This objective (Equation 5) shows that maximizing $J(\theta)$ is equivalent to maximizing the sum of log-probabilities of actions, weighted by the trajectory reward $r(x, \tau_n)$. This can be viewed as "reward-weighted fine-tuning," where each action in a trajectory contributes to the likelihood proportionally to the trajectory's overall reward.
The key differentiators from PPO, GRPO, and Q-SFT are:
- **Absence of Token-Level Propensity Score Ratios**: Refit does not involve these ratios, which can cause numerical instability.
- **No Token-Level Advantage Model**: Refit does not require a token-level reward or advantage model.
Refit is implemented by iterative gradient ascent on this objective (Algorithm 1), approximating the gradient using samples from the logged dataset $D$. It is algebraically equivalent to fine-tuning on $n$ SFT data points, each weighted by the same reward, and can be implemented by modifying SFT in libraries like TRL.

#### 3.2. Standardized Reward-Weighted Fine-Tuning (Swift)

Swift extends Refit by incorporating standardized rewards to address the high empirical variance that can arise from unscaled reward signals.
Lemma 2 states that if a policy $\theta^*$ maximizes the expected reward for each context $x$ jointly, it also maximizes the expected standardized reward $E_{x \sim q, \tau_n \sim \pi(\cdot|x;\theta)}[\tilde{r}(x, \tau_n)]$, where $\tilde{r}(x, \tau_n) = (r(x, \tau_n) - \mu(x))/\sigma(x)$.
Since standardized rewards can be negative, Lemma 1's lower bound derivation does not directly apply. Lemma 3 establishes that the difference between the online objective with standardized rewards and its offline counterpart $J(\theta) = E_{x \sim q, \tau_n \sim \pi_0(\cdot|x)}[\tilde{r}(x, \tau_n) \log \pi(\tau_n | x; \theta)]$ (Equation 9) is bounded. While not a strict lower bound, it indicates that optimizing this objective still aligns with the goal, with error proportional to the gap in Lemma 1.
The implementation of Swift follows Algorithm 1, but with standardized rewards. The logged dataset $D = \{(x, \tau_n, \tilde{r})\}$ is generated by:
1. Sampling context $x$.
2. Sampling $m$ trajectories $\tau_{n,i}$ under $\pi_0$ and computing their rewards $r(x, \tau_{n,i})$.
3. Estimating context-specific mean reward $\hat{\mu}(x)$ and standard deviation $\hat{\sigma}(x)$ from these $m$ trajectories.
4. Standardizing all rewards for context $x$ as $\tilde{r}(x, \tau_{n,i}) = (r(x, \tau_{n,i}) - \hat{\mu}(x))/\hat{\sigma}(x)$.
This process is repeated to fill $D$. The standardization cost is stated to be negligible.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets**: 6 QA benchmarks (OpenBookQA, ARC, SciQA, MMLU, CoSQL, MathDial), converted to QA-style conversational datasets.
- **Tasks**: Multi-turn conversations of length $n=3$ (ablated for longer lengths), in two categories:
 - **Reasoning Experiments**: Teacher asks for problem-solving, deeper thinking, and final answer.
 - **Clarifying-Questions Experiments**: Assistant asks questions, teacher answers, then assistant provides final answer.
- **Modes**: "Thinking" (assistant reasons in `<thinking>` tags) and "Standard" (no tags).
- **Agent**: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Teacher is scripted or a combination of scripting and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.
- **Data Generation**: 500 tasks per dataset, 3 runs with different temperatures for reward standardization and baseline implementation.
- **Reward Model/Evaluation**: GPT-4o judge provides 6 conversation reward metrics: Overall, Accuracy, Reasoning Ability, Comprehensiveness, Pedagogical Value, and Confidence Calibration. The RL algorithms optimize the overall reward, rescaled to.
- **Baselines**:
 1. **Base**: Original Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct policy.
 2. **STaR-GATE**: SFT on most rewarding trajectories.
 3. **STaR-GATE-D**: STaR-GATE with distillation.
 4. **StepDPO**: DPO applied at each step, generating alternative responses.
 5. **DPO**: DPO on final winning/losing responses to answer the original question directly.
- **Training**: All RL methods optimize the same LLM agent on similar datasets, with comparable training times.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across six distinct QA datasets (ARC, MMLU, OpenBookQA, SciQA, CoSQL, MathDial) in both "thinking" and "standard" modes for multi-turn reasoning, and for "clarifying-questions" on OpenBookQA and SciQA.

#### 4.1. Reasoning Experiments (Thinking and Standard Modes)

- **Overall Performance**: Swift demonstrated strong performance, achieving the highest accuracy in 7 out of 12 reasoning experiments and ranking among the top two methods in 10 out of 12. Refit also performed well, ranking among the best two methods in 9 out of 12 experiments, though it was significantly outperformed by Swift in thinking OpenBookQA, standard MMLU, and standard CoSQL.
- **Reward Metrics**: Swift consistently delivered high scores across all five reward metrics (R Overall, R Accuracy, R Reasoning, R Comprehensive, R Pedagogic, R Confidence), often ranking in the top two in 9 out of 12 experiments, despite optimizing only the overall reward. Refit also showed competitive performance in these metrics.
- **Thinking Mode Adoption**: In "thinking" mode, both Swift and Refit successfully induced the LLM to utilize the `<thinking>` tags for internal reasoning with high percentages (typically 96-99%), indicating effective instruction following.
- **Comparison to Baselines**:
 - **Base (Original Policy)**: Consistently the worst-performing baseline, highlighting the value of applying learning methods.
 - **STaR-GATE and STaR-GATE-D**: These baselines, which leverage SFT on high-reward trajectories, were generally the best among the baselines. However, Swift and Refit often surpassed them, suggesting that directly weighting updates by actual rewards is more effective than thresholding for high rewards or using distillation.
 - **DPO and StepDPO**: These methods typically performed worse than Swift, Refit, and STaR-GATE variants. The authors attribute this to their indirect utilization of the reward signal (e.g., reducing it to binary preferences or optimizing per-step), leading to information loss compared to the direct reward optimization of Refit and Swift.
- **Refit vs. Swift**: Swift generally outperformed Refit, particularly in experiments where Refit struggled. This outcome aligns with the hypothesis that standardizing rewards reduces variance in policy optimization, leading to improved learned policies, even though adaptive optimizers used in Refit might partially mitigate issues with unscaled rewards.

#### 4.2. Clarifying-Questions Experiments

- On OpenBookQA and SciQA datasets, both Refit and Swift achieved higher accuracies than all baselines.
- While their conversation reward metrics were competitive, they did not consistently attain the highest scores in this category compared to some baselines.
- A notable observation was a significant drop in answer accuracies compared to the reasoning experiments, suggesting that in these specific benchmarks, the value of direct reasoning is higher than that of asking clarifying questions.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Conversation Length**: Increasing conversation length (n = 4, 6, 8, 10 on OpenBookQA) generally led to lower accuracy, indicating increased task difficulty with more turns.
- **Dataset Size**: Ablation on the ARC dataset showed that increasing the number of training trajectories (from 1000 to 2000) for Refit resulted in improvements in both accuracy and language quality metrics, confirming the benefit of more training data.
- **Evaluator Robustness**: An ablation study using a Claude 4 Opus judge (instead of GPT-4o) on the ARC dataset confirmed the main findings, with Swift and Refit still ranking as the best two methods and demonstrating higher language quality scores than baselines. This suggests that the reported gains are not solely dependent on the specific GPT-4o evaluation.

In summary, the main findings indicate that the proposed reward-weighted fine-tuning approaches, particularly Swift, offer substantial improvements in accuracy and various conversation quality metrics for multi-turn QA tasks compared to existing SFT and DPO-based offline RL methods for LLMs. This is attributed to their direct utilization of the reward signal and avoidance of certain complexities that can hinder stability and efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of offline reinforcement learning with Large Language Models, particularly for conversational AI. The core significance lies in offering a practical, stable, and theoretically grounded approach to directly optimize LLM policies based on explicit reward signals, circumventing several limitations of prior methods.

**Direct Reward Optimization and Statistical Efficiency:** The development of Refit and Swift provides a mechanism for LLMs to learn directly from rewards without the information loss often associated with converting reward signals into forms suitable for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or direct preference optimization (DPO). By formulating the problem as reward-weighted fine-tuning, the approach allows for a more granular and efficient use of the reward signal, which is empirically shown to yield "major gains" in optimized rewards and language quality. This directness can lead to policies that more accurately reflect the desired objective functions.

**Stability and Practicality for LLMs:** A key impact is the elimination of token-level propensity score ratios from the optimization objective in Refit. These ratios are a known source of numerical instability and high variance in many offline RL algorithms (e.g., PPO, GRPO), requiring careful clipping and hyperparameter tuning. By avoiding them, Refit offers a more stable and practical algorithm for LLM fine-tuning, making RL-based policy optimization more accessible and robust for practitioners. The implementation as a minor modification of SFT further lowers the technical barrier for adoption.

**Variance Reduction and Performance Improvement:** Swift's introduction of standardized rewards is important for enhancing learning efficiency. Policy gradient methods are often sensitive to the scale and variance of rewards. By standardizing rewards per context, Swift helps to normalize these variations, leading to more stable gradient estimates and, as empirically demonstrated, often superior learned policies compared to Refit. This is particularly relevant in diverse conversational settings where reward scales might vary.

**Generality and Broad Applicability:** The proposed algorithms, Refit and Swift, are presented as general-purpose offline RL methods. While demonstrated extensively on multi-turn Question Answering (QA) for reasoning and clarifying questions, their theoretical foundation suggests applicability to a wider range of domains beyond QA, where sequential decision-making with LLMs and explicit reward signals are involved. This opens avenues for improving LLM behavior in areas such as dialogue systems, content generation, and interactive agents.

**Benchmarking and Data Generation:** The creation of 500 multi-turn conversation tasks across six diverse QA benchmarks, along with detailed instructions for reproduction (including prompts and examples), provides valuable resources for the research community. This contributes to the development of standardized evaluation protocols and facilitates future research in conversational AI and offline RL for LLMs.

**Limitations and Future Directions:** The authors acknowledge limitations, including the higher computational cost of RL compared to pure supervised learning, although their reduction to SFT partially mitigates this. They also note the critical dependence on the quality of the logged dataset and the reliance on an AI judge (GPT-4o, with ablation for Claude 4 Opus) instead of human evaluation. These points highlight areas for future work, such as exploring more computationally efficient data generation strategies, investigating techniques for improving logged dataset quality, and conducting human evaluations to validate AI judge metrics.

In conclusion, this work offers a refined and more direct approach to offline RL for LLMs, demonstrating improved performance in conversational tasks. Its practical implementation via reward-weighted fine-tuning positions it as a significant step towards more effective and stable optimization of LLM behavior in complex interactive environments.
