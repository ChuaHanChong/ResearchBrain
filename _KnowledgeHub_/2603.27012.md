# UMI-Underwater: Learning Underwater Manipulation without Underwater Teleoperation

> [!summary] Summary
> UMI-Underwater presents a system for learning underwater robotic manipulation by combining a self-supervised underwater data collection pipeline with a zero-shot, depth-based affordance transfer approach from on-land human demonstrations. The method achieved an 85% success rate on in-distribution grasping and demonstrated robustness to out-of-distribution visual changes and generalization to novel objects, reaching 75% success on previously unseen items.

## Problem

- Underwater environments cause severe perceptual degradation and hydrodynamic disturbances, making traditional visuomotor policies brittle and unreliable.
- Collecting diverse, high-quality underwater training data is costly, time-consuming, and heavily relies on human teleoperation.
- A significant domain gap between land and water prevents direct transfer of learned manipulation skills from terrestrial to aquatic settings.

## Method

- Developed an autonomous, ==self-supervised data collection pipeline== with heuristic visuo-servoing and recovery mechanisms to efficiently gather successful underwater grasp demonstrations.
- Leveraged a zero-shot, ==depth-based affordance predictor==, pretrained exclusively on diverse on-land human demonstrations collected via a portable **UMI-Aquatic** interface, to bridge the land-to-water perception gap.
- Implemented a modular perception-control stack where a goal-conditioned affordance model identifies 'where to grasp' using depth inputs, guiding an ==affordance-conditioned diffusion policy== for 'how to control'.

## Results

- Achieved an **85%** success rate on in-distribution grasping tasks in multi-object scenes, surpassing an RGB-only baseline which reached **65%**.
- Maintained **80%** success in out-of-distribution visual generalization tasks (unseen backgrounds), while RGB-based policies exhibited catastrophic failure (**0%** success).
- Demonstrated strong generalization to novel objects, achieving **75%** success on items seen only in on-land demonstrations, outperforming an RGB-only policy's **50%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Autonomous, self-supervised data collection can substantially reduce the human burden and cost associated with acquiring large-scale underwater manipulation datasets.
> - Depth-based affordance representations are robust to severe underwater perceptual shifts, enabling effective zero-shot transfer of manipulation knowledge from land to water without underwater model fine-tuning.
> - A modular architecture separating goal localization from control, where an affordance model guides a visuomotor policy, enhances robustness and generalizability in challenging underwater environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Underwater robotic manipulation presents significant challenges compared to terrestrial environments, primarily due to severe perceptual degradation and variability (e.g., wavelength-dependent attenuation, scattering, turbidity, rapidly changing illumination), as well as hydrodynamic disturbances and vehicle-object coupling. These factors contribute to the brittleness of end-to-end visuomotor policies and amplify the cost of collecting sufficient underwater training data, making robust autonomy difficult.

Historically, autonomous underwater manipulation has focused on intervention missions like inspection, maintenance, and recovery. Early efforts emphasized reliable mechatronic integration and robust control for free-floating platforms, such as SAUVIM-style systems and the TRIDENT framework. More advanced systems, like DexROV, have explored semi-autonomous intervention and remote supervision, while principled control architectures have been developed to manage competing objectives. A complementary paradigm, human-robot haptic collaboration, exemplified by systems like Ocean One and OceanOneK, utilizes avatar telepresence for dexterous manipulation under supervisory control. Despite these advancements, many practical underwater manipulation systems remain teleoperation-centric due to the expense and time involved in collecting high-quality underwater demonstrations. Learning from demonstration has been applied to assist or partially automate intervention, and recent work like AquaBot has shown that visuomotor policies can be trained and improved through iterative self-learning.

A central challenge across learning-based manipulation is the acquisition of diverse, high-quality demonstration data. This burden is exacerbated in underwater settings. To address this, two main lines of research have emerged in broader robotics:
1. **Autonomous and self-supervised on-robot data collection**: Robots autonomously execute trials and use automatically obtained success signals for learning, reducing manual labeling and teleoperation time. Examples include systems that learn grasping from extensive self-supervised interaction.
2. **Portable handheld interfaces and cross-embodiment transfer (UMI family)**: These interfaces aim to make data collection economical, portable, and robot-agnostic. The Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) allows in-the-wild data collection with handheld grippers and transfers learned policies to robots without requiring robots at collection sites. Subsequent UMI-style systems have extended this concept to various embodiments, sensing modalities, and dataset scales.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing two complementary bottlenecks in underwater manipulation. Firstly, it seeks to replace expert teleoperation with an autonomous, self-supervised data collection pipeline specifically designed for underwater environments, thereby scaling data acquisition. Secondly, it explores how transferable affordance representations, inspired by UMI-style systems, can improve generalization across the challenging land-to-water domain gap. The novel aspect is the use of a depth-based affordance representation for zero-shot transfer, which is hypothesized to be robust to the significant perceptual differences encountered underwater.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties and high costs associated with developing robust robotic manipulation capabilities in underwater environments. The authors identify several key challenges that necessitate a novel approach:

1. **Degraded and Variable Underwater Perception**: Underwater imagery is severely affected by phenomena such as wavelength-dependent attenuation, scattering, backscatter, turbidity, and rapidly changing illumination (caustics). These factors lead to substantial appearance shifts, making traditional visuomotor policies brittle and unreliable under distribution shifts.
2. **High Cost and Burden of Data Collection**: Collecting diverse, high-quality underwater demonstrations for learning-based manipulation is time-consuming, expensive, and often relies on human teleoperation. This presents a significant practical bottleneck for scaling up underwater robotic autonomy.
3. **Domain Gap Between Land and Water**: Knowledge acquired in controlled terrestrial environments typically does not transfer effectively to underwater settings due to the drastic changes in visual appearance and physical dynamics. This necessitates specialized approaches for underwater learning or robust domain adaptation strategies.

To address these motivations, the research establishes the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a Self-Supervised Underwater Data Collection Pipeline**: The goal is to create an autonomous system that can collect successful underwater grasp demonstrations without human teleoperation. This pipeline should incorporate heuristic control and recovery behaviors to improve data efficiency and automatically filter successful episodes. This aims to reduce the human burden and cost associated with acquiring large datasets in underwater environments.
2. **Enable Zero-Shot Land-to-Water Grasp Knowledge Transfer**: The objective is to leverage readily available on-land human demonstrations to inform underwater grasping policies. This involves developing a representation that can bridge the significant perception gap between land and water, allowing knowledge learned on land to be applied directly underwater without requiring mixed training or fine-tuning on underwater data for the initial knowledge transfer.
3. **Improve Robustness to Perceptual Variation**: The system aims to be robust to severe lighting and color shifts, as well as background variability, which are common in underwater environments. This objective guides the selection of visual representations less sensitive to these changes.
4. **Enhance Generalization to Novel Objects and Environments**: The research seeks to enable underwater robots to generalize grasping capabilities to objects not seen during underwater training and to operate effectively in environments with unseen visual characteristics (e.g., different backgrounds). This is crucial for expanding the applicability of underwater manipulation systems.
5. **Separate Goal Localization from Control**: By learning a goal-conditioned affordance predictor (identifying "where to grasp") and an affordance-conditioned visuomotor policy (executing "how to grasp"), the system aims to create a more modular and robust architecture, where the policy can be guided by task-relevant spatial cues.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The UMI-Underwater system integrates autonomous, self-supervised data collection with a zero-shot, depth-based affordance predictor to address the challenges of underwater manipulation.

**Experimental Setup:**
The system operates within an above-ground swimming pool (2x4m, 1m deep). The platform is a tethered underwater ROV equipped with a forward-facing RGB camera, a wide-FOV RGB camera, and a 1-DoF gripper. Camera latency from the integrated front camera is reported to be reduced (below 100 ms). An external waterproof camera is rigidly attached for a complementary wide-FOV viewpoint. For safety and monitoring, four fixed external cameras at the pool corners provide real-time 3D localization, fused with the onboard IMU for 6-DoF pose estimation; this pose is not provided to the learned policy. During operation, vehicle pitch is constrained to a 10-degree downward angle to stabilize the camera view and simplify grasping geometry.

**Data Collection:**
1. **Self-Supervised Underwater Data Collection:** A heuristic visuo-servoing PD controller autonomously generates grasp trajectories. Each episode follows a sequence:
 - **Reset and Initialization:** Vehicle is placed at a shallow depth with a random initial pose.
 - **Object Selection and Tracking:** A real-time segmentation model (SAM2-tiny) identifies object masks. The object closest to the image center is selected and tracked.
 - **Staged Approach:** The ROV performs a sequence of movements:
 - **Yaw Alignment:** Yaw motion to center the target horizontally.
 - **Forward Approach:** Forward motion until the target reaches a reference horizontal line in the lower image portion.
 - **Depth Adjustment:** Vertical motion until the target is within a predefined grasping region in the upper image portion.
 - **Close-Range Approach and Grasp:** A monocular depth estimator (Depth Anything V2) predicts distance. When the object is within gripper reach, the gripper closes.
 - **Drag Verification:** After gripper closure, a short 3-second retreat motion is executed. If the object remains in the gripper, the grasp is labeled as a success.
 - **Failure Recovery Mechanisms:**
 - **Regrasp:** After a failed grasp, the vehicle backs up, applies a small lateral offset, reopens the gripper, and retries.
 - **Overshoot:** If the target approaches the image margin, the vehicle performs a larger retreat to re-acquire the object before restarting alignment.
 This pipeline collected 536 grasp demonstrations (233 successful) over approximately 15 hours of autonomous runtime.
2. **On-Land Demonstrations with UMI-Aquatic:** A portable handheld gripper interface, UMI-Aquatic, was developed, building on the Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI). It integrates an iPhone camera, mounted to match the underwater camera's viewpoint, and AprilTags on the gripper for robust gripper-state tracking. This setup facilitates rapid collection of diverse on-land grasping demonstrations. 800 demonstrations were collected across 6 object types and 8 backgrounds in about 2.2 hours of active demonstrating.

**Goal-Conditioned Data Preprocessing and Training:**
The system separates the task into "where to grasp" and "how to control."
1. **Affordance Predictor (Where to grasp):** A goal-conditioned affordance predictor outputs a dense grasp heatmap. To mitigate the land-to-water domain gap (lighting, color), this model operates on depth inputs, derived from a monocular depth estimator, rather than RGB.
 - **Training Tuples:** Composed of current depth (D_t), goal depth (D_goal), and a sparse per-pixel affordance target (H_t).
 - **Goal Source:** The goal depth image (D_goal) is derived from an on-land UMI-Aquatic RGB image, allowing cross-domain goal matching.
 - **Automatic Affordance Supervision:** For on-land UMI-Aquatic data, AprilTag tracking identifies gripper closure timing. Point tracking (CoTracker3) backtracks the gripper-object contact pixel to earlier frames, generating ground truth affordance targets.
 - **Land-to-Water Pretraining (Zero-shot):** The affordance model is trained exclusively on the diverse on-land UMI-Aquatic dataset. A "plane-at-depth" warping technique aligns iPhone camera frames to the underwater camera geometry, reducing geometric domain mismatch. No mixed training or fine-tuning is performed for underwater deployment of the affordance model.
 - **Architecture:** A U-Net (depth 4, base channels 64) takes a two-channel input (current depth, goal depth) and outputs per-pixel logits representing grasp locations. Training uses a weighted BCE-with-logits objective, AdamW optimizer, and depth-specific augmentations.

2. **Affordance-Conditioned Visuomotor Policy (How to control):** A diffusion policy is trained to generate control actions, conditioned on multimodal inputs.
 - **Policy Inputs:** Predicted affordance heatmap (from the UMI-Aquatic-pretrained model), monocular depth map, and low-dimensional robot status (compass, pitch, vehicle depth).
 - **Architecture:** A diffusion policy models a 16-step action horizon (yaw, forward/backward, up/down, left/right, open, close) which is executed in a receding-horizon manner. Observations are encoded by a pretrained vision backbone (ViT-B/16 CLIP) into a global latent condition, and a 1D UNet denoiser predicts the action sequence.
 - **Training:** Behavior cloning on the successful underwater demonstrations (233 episodes) using a standard diffusion objective.
 - **Inference:** The full perception-control stack runs online at 10 Hz, with policy inference updates at 1 Hz, using an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU and AMD Ryzen 9800X3D CPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation was conducted in an above-ground swimming pool on a pick-and-drag grasping task. Three objects were placed in front of the robot, with the target specified by an on-land goal image. The main challenge involved selecting the correct target among distractors under appearance variation and cross-domain goal specification. Success rate (%) was the primary metric.

**Evaluation Settings:**
1. **In-distribution (ID):** Seen underwater objects (rocks, seagrass, toy ducks) under the training pool background.
2. **OOD Visual Generalization (Background Shift):** Seen underwater objects under unseen pool background wallpapers.
3. **OOD Novel-Object Generalization (UMI Objects):** Novel objects (pitcher with handle, can, power drill), seen only in the on-land dataset, evaluated in the pool under the training pool background (zero-shot transfer).

**Methods Compared:**
- **DP + Aff + Depth (ours):** Diffusion policy conditioned on UMI-Aquatic-pretrained affordance heatmap, monocular depth, and proprioception.
- **DP + RGB:** Diffusion policy conditioned on raw RGB and proprioception (goal-agnostic, implicitly targets the object closest to the image center).
- **DP + RGB + Aff + Depth:** Ablation; diffusion policy conditioned on RGB, UMI-Aquatic-pretrained affordance heatmap, monocular depth, and proprioception.

**Results:**

- **In-Distribution (ID) Performance (Seen Objects + Training Pool Background):**
 - DP + Aff + Depth (ours): **85%** success.
 - DP + RGB: **65%** success.
 - **Finding:** The proposed method demonstrated improved performance in the ID setting. The primary advantage of DP + Aff + Depth was its ability to mitigate target confusion in multi-object scenes due to the explicit spatial goal signal provided by the goal-conditioned affordance heatmap, which the RGB-only baseline lacked.

- **OOD Visual Generalization (Seen Objects + Unseen Backgrounds):**
 - DP + Aff + Depth (ours): **80%** success.
 - DP + RGB: **0%** success.
 - DP + RGB + Aff + Depth: **0%** success.
 - **Finding:** RGB-based policies exhibited catastrophic failure under background shifts. The training background was visually simple, while the unseen wallpapers introduced strong textures and color statistics outside the training distribution. Even adding RGB to an otherwise robust DP + Aff + Depth policy (DP + RGB + Aff + Depth) resulted in 0% success, indicating that raw RGB inputs can degrade robustness under severe appearance shift. Conversely, DP + Aff + Depth maintained high robustness, suggesting that depth-based affordances and depth cues are less sensitive to background appearance changes, enabling reliable target selection.

- **OOD Novel-Object Generalization (Unseen Objects in Pool + Training Pool Background, Zero-Shot):**
 - DP + Aff + Depth (ours): **75%** success.
 - DP + RGB: **50%** success.
 - **Finding:** On novel objects, DP + Aff + Depth substantially outperformed the RGB-only baseline. The RGB-only policy achieved a non-trivial 50% success rate, attributed to the consistent background and some geometric similarities between novel and seen objects. However, the affordance-conditioned policy demonstrated greater robustness, indicating that on-land UMI-pretrained affordances provided a stronger inductive bias for identifying graspable regions on previously unseen objects.

**Observed Failure Mode:**
A recurring failure mode identified was "overshoot-induced target switch." When the robot overshot during an approach, the camera viewpoint could shift, causing the intended target object to move partially out of view or become severely misaligned. In these instances, the affordance model occasionally assigned high confidence to a different salient region (often a distractor object), leading the policy to grasp the wrong object. This also affected recovery strategies, as the system would then attempt to re-approach the newly identified, incorrect target.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for underwater manipulation that addresses critical limitations in data collection and generalization, potentially impacting various underwater robotic applications.

The **significance** of this work lies in several areas:

1. **Reduced Human Burden in Data Collection**: The development of a self-supervised underwater data collection pipeline that autonomously gathers successful grasp demonstrations is a key contribution. By integrating heuristic controllers, recovery behaviors, and automatic success filtering, the system substantially reduces reliance on expensive and time-consuming underwater teleoperation, thereby accelerating data acquisition for learning-based policies.
2. **Effective Cross-Domain Knowledge Transfer**: The work demonstrates a method for zero-shot land-to-water transfer of grasp knowledge. By leveraging a depth-based affordance representation trained on readily available on-land human demonstrations collected with UMI-Aquatic, the system bridges the significant visual domain gap between terrestrial and underwater environments. This eliminates the need for mixed training datasets or fine-tuning the affordance model underwater, which simplifies deployment and expands the utility of on-land data.
3. **Enhanced Robustness to Perceptual Degradation**: The experimental results show that the depth-based affordance approach, coupled with depth-conditioned control, maintains high performance even under severe visual distribution shifts, such as unseen background wallpapers, where RGB-only methods fail. This robustness is critical for reliable operation in challenging and unpredictable underwater conditions.
4. **Improved Generalization to Novel Objects**: The ability to generalize to novel objects seen only in the on-land dataset, without further underwater policy fine-tuning, indicates a stronger inductive bias for identifying graspable regions. This extends the applicability of underwater manipulation systems to a wider variety of items.
5. **Modular System Design**: The separation of goal localization (via an affordance model) from control (via a visuomotor policy) provides a modular and interpretable architecture. This design allows the policy to be explicitly guided by task-relevant spatial cues, contributing to its robustness.

The **potential impact** of UMI-Underwater is broad, particularly for applications requiring autonomous intervention in marine environments:

- **Ecological Sampling and Monitoring**: Robots equipped with this technology could autonomously collect biological samples or deploy sensors with greater efficiency and less human intervention.
- **Underwater Infrastructure Inspection and Maintenance**: The ability to grasp and manipulate objects robustly can facilitate autonomous inspection, repair, or debris removal tasks on submerged structures (e.g., pipelines, offshore platforms).
- **Debris Removal**: Autonomous systems capable of identifying and grasping diverse underwater debris could contribute to ocean clean-up efforts.
- **Search and Recovery**: Improved manipulation capabilities could enhance the recovery of objects from the seabed.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The authors acknowledge limitations, including the reliance on predicted depth which, while robust to domain shifts, might discard valuable color and texture cues. Future work could explore fusing appearance information with depth while maintaining domain robustness (e.g., via domain adaptation or uncertainty-aware conditioning). Another limitation is the simple PID controller, which can cause overshoot and lead to target-switching failures. Implementing dynamics-aware controllers like Model Predictive Control (MPC) could mitigate these issues. Expanding the diversity of underwater demonstrations, object categories, and extending to longer-horizon tasks would further enhance generalization and unlock more complex underwater manipulation capabilities.
