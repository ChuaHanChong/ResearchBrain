# Direction Matters: Learning Force Direction Enables Sim-to-Real Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Zhejiang University developed a framework that enables effective sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation by learning desired contact force directions in simulation. The approach achieved an overall success rate of 91% across diverse tasks, outperforming baselines and reducing force-related failures through adaptive compliance.

## Problem

- Policies trained in simulation for contact-rich tasks often fail in the real world due to discrepancies in physical dynamics and contact models (the "sim-to-real dynamics gap").
- Learning directly in the real world for contact-rich tasks is costly, time-consuming, and carries safety risks, limiting scalability.
- Existing sim-to-real methods often rely on position-only policies with fixed, isotropic compliant controllers, leading to "blind compliance" that passively accommodates errors rather than intelligently managing force interactions.

## Method

- A policy is trained purely in simulation to predict end-effector poses, gripper commands, contact states, and crucially, the desired *normal force direction* as a dynamics-invariant signal.
- A force-aware admittance controller is deployed in the real world, dynamically integrating the policy's predictions with a manually tuned target normal force magnitude to provide adaptive compliance.
- The controller selectively regulates forces and stiffness along task-relevant directions, enabling normal force regulation for stable contact and tangent stiffening to overcome resistive forces, based on the predicted contact state and force direction.

## Results

- The method achieved an overall success rate of 91% across four diverse contact-rich tasks (Microwave Opening, Peg-in-Hole, Whiteboard Wiping, Door Opening), significantly outperforming the best baseline (E2VLA+M-A) at 67%.
- It substantially reduced both "Insufficient Force" and "Excessive Force" failures compared to baselines, demonstrating the ability to apply appropriate forces dynamically and avoid safety violations.
- The approach yielded higher-quality task completion, such as an average peg insertion depth of 25.0 mm compared to 23.6 mm for the best baseline, and consistently maintained stable contact forces during interactions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The *direction* of the desired contact force is primarily constrained by task geometry and remains robust across the sim-to-real gap, unlike force *magnitudes* which are highly sensitive to simulation inaccuracies.
> - Decoupling the prediction of force direction (from simulation) from the application of force magnitude (via a simple, real-world scalar tune) effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap for contact-rich tasks.
> - Intelligent, adaptive compliance, achieved by dynamically adjusting control parameters based on predicted task-relevant force directions and contact states, significantly improves robustness and precision compared to fixed, isotropic compliance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is continuously striving for greater autonomy and versatility, especially in tasks requiring physical interaction with the environment. A significant bottleneck in achieving this has been the "sim-to-real gap," particularly for contact-rich manipulation tasks. Policies trained purely in simulation often fail when deployed in the real world due to discrepancies in physical dynamics, contact models, and material properties. This paper directly addresses this fundamental challenge.

Current research broadly falls into two paradigms for contact-rich manipulation:
1. **Real-World Learning:** This approach bypasses the sim-to-real gap by training policies directly on real-world data, often through imitation learning from human demonstrations or real-world reinforcement learning (RL). While effective, these methods are notoriously costly, time-consuming, and carry inherent safety risks, severely limiting their scalability and widespread adoption.
2. **Sim-to-Real Transfer with Position-Only Policies:** This paradigm leverages the scalability of simulation by training policies to predict only end-effector poses. These policies are then combined with compliant controllers in the real world. However, a major limitation here is "blind compliance" – the controllers often use fixed, isotropic stiffness settings, passively accommodating errors rather than intelligently managing force interactions aligned with task intent. This can lead to limited task effectiveness and brittleness under varied contact conditions.

Other sim-to-real efforts include:
- **System Identification and Dynamics Parameter Alignment:** These methods attempt to calibrate simulators to match real-world dynamics. This can improve fidelity but is often task- and object-specific, still requiring substantial real-world interaction or tuning.
- **Hybrid Sim-to-Real Methods:** These approaches augment simulation training with limited real-world data through techniques like residual learning or policy finetuning. While effective, they compromise the scalability and safety benefits of pure simulation.
- **Dynamics Randomization:** This involves training policies with randomized physical parameters in simulation to improve robustness to real-world variations. While promising for zero-shot transfer, it requires careful engineering of randomization distributions and rewards, and RL itself is data-inefficient.

This paper positions itself strategically within this landscape by offering a novel solution that seeks to combine the best aspects of existing methods while mitigating their drawbacks. It aims to harness the scalability, safety, and cost-effectiveness of *pure simulation training* while enabling *intelligent, task-aware adaptive compliance* in the real world. Unlike methods that try to align dynamics or use real-world data, this work identifies and leverages a *dynamics-invariant signal* (force direction) that can reliably bridge the sim-to-real gap. By doing so, it proposes a powerful alternative to the "blind compliance" issue prevalent in current sim-to-real position-only approaches, moving towards more sophisticated and robust contact-rich manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable reliable and effective **sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation tasks** by facilitating **intelligent, task-aware adaptive compliance** in real-world robot execution, solely through policies trained on simulation data.

The motivation for this work stems from a critical limitation in current robotic learning paradigms for contact-rich tasks:
- **The Sim-to-Real Dynamics Gap:** Policies trained in simulation for contact-rich tasks often overfit to the inaccuracies of simulated contact dynamics. When deployed in the real world, this mismatch leads to failures, unsafe interactions, and even hardware damage.
- **Limitations of Real-World Learning:** While bypassing the dynamics gap, learning directly in the real world is expensive, time-consuming, and inherently risky, hindering scalability.
- **The Problem of "Blind Compliance":** Existing sim-to-real methods often rely on position-only policies combined with fixed, isotropic compliant controllers. This "blind compliance" passively accommodates errors rather than actively managing physical interactions according to task requirements, leading to suboptimal performance and a lack of robustness.

The authors pose a fundamental question: "Can we harness the scalability of simulation while enabling intelligent, task-aware compliance in the real world?" Their key insight is that while force *magnitudes* are highly sensitive to simulation inaccuracies (dynamics gap), the *direction* of the desired contact force is primarily constrained by task geometry and remains robust across the sim-to-real gap.

Based on this insight, the key objectives are:
- To develop a **novel framework** that utilizes pure simulation data and requires only lightweight manual tuning in the real world to address complex contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- To formulate a **policy action space** that predicts contact state and desired contact force direction, identifying these as dynamics-invariant signals that encode high-level task geometry.
- To design a **force-aware admittance controller** that dynamically integrates these policy predictions with a human-specified target force magnitude to achieve adaptive, task-aligned compliance.
- To provide a **theoretical analysis** demonstrating the stability and robustness of the proposed controller to disturbances.
- To conduct **extensive real-world experiments** on a diverse set of contact-rich tasks to validate the superiority and robustness of their approach compared to strong baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, "Direction Matters," tackles sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich manipulation by decoupling force direction from magnitude, leveraging pure simulation data, and integrating predictions with a specialized force-aware controller.

**A. Policy Formulation and Learning:**
The core idea is to train a policy in simulation to act as a high-level Finite State Machine (FSM). This FSM predicts not only end-effector poses but, crucially, **contact state** and **normal force direction**.
- **Action Space:** The policy predicts a sequence of future actions `{A_{t+k}}` for a horizon `h`. Each action `A_t` is a tuple:
 - `Reference Pose and Gripper Command (X_d_t ∈ R^10)`: End-effector position (3), 6D rotation (6), and gripper command (1).
 - `Normal Force Direction (n_t ∈ R^3)`: A 3D vector representing the desired normal force direction.
 - `Contact State (c_t ∈ {0, 1})`: A binary indicator for contact with the object.
 Notably, the policy *does not* predict force magnitudes or tangent force directions directly, as these are dynamics-dependent. The tangent force direction is derived from the predicted reference position and normal force direction in the controller.
- **Demonstration Generation:** To train this policy, expert FSMs are constructed in simulation using privileged state information.
 - **Expert FSM Logic:** The FSM switches strategies based on contact state. In "Free Motion," it generates pose trajectories toward key poses. In "Contact Interaction," it follows human-designed kinematic rules tailored to task geometry (e.g., linear descent for peg-in-hole, zigzag for wiping, circular for articulated objects).
 - **Simplified Controller in Sim:** For data collection, a high-stiffness pose-tracking controller is used throughout, as safety constraints are absent in simulation.
 - **Supervision Signals:** Reference pose is supervised using the end-effector pose at `t+1` (decoupled from dynamics). Gripper command is from the expert. Normal force direction is derived from the contact manifold normal at `t+1`. Contact state is directly from the FSM's phase segmentation.
 - **Simulation Environment Fidelity:** High-fidelity 3D assets (scanned or CAD) and explicit modeling of interaction logic (e.g., dynamic transparency for ink, conditional force fields for door latches) are used to minimize sim-real discrepancies in visual, geometric, and causal aspects.
 - **Randomization:** Extensive randomization is applied to non-dynamic aspects (initial state, visual conditions) to improve transfer robustness, but *not* to dynamic parameters, reinforcing the dynamics-invariant policy design.
- **Network Architecture and Training:** The policy is instantiated using **E2VLA**, a Vision-Language-Action model known for strong 3D geometric reasoning.
 - **Architecture Modification:** E2VLA's diffusion action expert's input and output projection layers are extended to accommodate the new action dimensions (normal force direction, contact state).
 - **Loss Function:** A combined L1 loss is used for the predicted reference pose/gripper command, normal force direction, and contact state.
 - **Finetuning Strategy:** Projection weights for original action dimensions are inherited from a pre-trained model, while new dimensions are initialized with small magnitudes, preserving pre-training benefits.

**B. Force-Aware Admittance Control (Real-World Deployment):**
The policy's predictions are used to configure a Cartesian admittance controller in the real world, providing adaptive compliance.
- **Admittance Law:** `M¨x_r + Dẋ_r + K(x_r − x_cmd) = F_ext − F_cmd`. Base parameters (mass `m`, damping `d`, stiffness `k`) are low-stiffness and overdamped, fixed across tasks.
- **Normal Force Regulation:**
 - When the policy predicts `c=0` (no contact), `F_cmd` is zero, resulting in standard compliant pose tracking.
 - When `c=1` (contact), `F_cmd` is applied along the predicted normal direction `n` (`F_cmd = f*n`). The scalar `f` is derived from a **manually tuned target normal force magnitude (`f_H`)** (constant per contact phase) and compensation for existing spring/damping forces. This ensures the robot maintains stable contact with the desired force, effectively providing damping control along the normal direction. For tasks where contact is maintained by the gripper, normal force regulation is disabled.
- **Tangent Stiffening:** For tasks requiring overcoming resistive forces, the translational stiffness along the tangent direction (`t`) is increased. The direction `t` is derived from the projected motion direction onto the plane orthogonal to `n`. This is a task-dependent binary parameter, and the scaling factor (4x in experiments) is also constant.
- The controller thus selectively regulates forces and stiffness along task-relevant directions, providing adaptive and intelligent compliance.

**C. Stability Analysis:**
Theoretical analysis is provided to guarantee the controller's safety and robustness:
- **Proposition 1 (Contact - Disturbance-Free):** The normal-direction dynamics is asymptotically stable, converging to the equilibrium point where the external force equals the target force `f_H`.
- **Proposition 2 (Contact Loss - Disturbance):** If contact is lost, the system is asymptotically stable in velocity, driving the end-effector towards the environment to re-establish contact.
- **Proposition 3 (During Contact - Disturbance):** The system is input-to-state stable (ISS) with respect to bounded disturbances, meaning states remain bounded in the presence of disturbances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed framework was validated through extensive real-world experiments on four diverse contact-rich tasks: Microwave Opening (MO), Peg-in-Hole (PH), Whiteboard Wiping (WW), and Door Opening (DO).

- **Superior Success Rates and Robustness:**
 - The proposed method achieved an **overall success rate of 91%**, significantly outperforming the best baseline (E2VLA+M-A) at 67%.
 - For tasks like Microwave Opening and Door Opening (resistance-heavy), our method achieved 100% and 90% success rates respectively without disturbance, and crucially maintained 100% and 80% with disturbances. Baselines showed a clear trade-off: higher stiffness improved nominal success but drastically reduced robustness under disturbances.
 - For contact-maintenance tasks like Peg-in-Hole and Whiteboard Wiping, our method showed robust performance (80% and 96% overall success), while pure stiffness-based baselines struggled to reliably regulate force.

- **Reduced Force-Related Failures:**
 - Failure analysis categorized issues into "Insufficient Force," "Excessive Force," and "Others." Baselines suffered predominantly from force-related failures (insufficient force to complete tasks or excessive force triggering safety stops).
 - Our method significantly reduced both "Insufficient Force" and "Excessive Force" failures, demonstrating its ability to apply appropriate forces dynamically. "Other" failures (geometric/grasping errors) remained comparable, primarily due to visual out-of-distribution inputs.

- **Higher Task Completion Quality:**
 - Beyond mere success, our approach consistently yielded higher-quality task completion. For Peg-in-Hole, it achieved an average insertion depth of 25.0 mm, compared to 23.6 mm for the best baseline. For Whiteboard Wiping, the remaining ink length was reduced to 0.8 cm, matching the best baseline but with significantly higher success rates. This indicates more precise and consistent contact interactions.

- **Ablation Study (Whiteboard Wiping):**
 - **Effectiveness of Finetuning:** Finetuning a pre-trained VLA model (E2VLA) improved performance over training from scratch, confirming the benefit of leveraging prior knowledge.
 - **Effectiveness of Force-aware Controller:** A policy trained from scratch *but combined with the force-aware controller* significantly outperformed finetuned pre-trained models using *standard admittance controllers*. This highlights the critical role of the intelligent controller and force-related constraints over large-scale pose-only pre-training alone for contact-rich tasks.
 - **Effectiveness of Normal Force Regulation:** Removing normal force regulation led to a substantial drop in success rate, proving its necessity for stable contact maintenance.
 - **Effectiveness of Tangent Stiffening:** Without tangent stiffening, task quality (e.g., remaining ink length) significantly degraded due to large tangent tracking errors caused by friction.
 - **Effectiveness of Adaptive Compliance:** The full adaptive compliance framework (normal force regulation + tangent stiffening) resolved the dilemma faced by isotropic compliance baselines (low stiffness for safety but ineffective; high stiffness for effectiveness but brittle).

- **Case Studies:**
 - **Contact Force Stabilization:** Visualizations for whiteboard wiping showed our method's normal contact force converging stably to the target (4N) immediately upon contact, and robustly adapting to height disturbances without losing contact or triggering safety stops. Baselines exhibited fluctuations, contact loss, or safety violations.
 - **Interaction with Tangent Stiffening:** In microwave opening, the method adaptively increased tangent stiffness to overcome the snap-lock resistance, with the stiffening direction dynamically adjusting as the door opened. This demonstrated intelligent force application ensuring task completion while maintaining bounded forces under disturbances, unlike baselines that either failed or triggered safety stops.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly significant advancement in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving complex physical interactions. Its potential impact is multi-faceted:

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The most direct and impactful contribution is providing a robust and effective method to overcome the notorious dynamics gap for contact-rich manipulation. By identifying and leveraging force direction as a dynamics-invariant signal, the paper unlocks the potential of scalable simulation data for a class of tasks previously hampered by simulation inaccuracies. This is a critical step towards more general-purpose and robust robotic systems.

- **Enabling Intelligent, Adaptive Compliance:** The framework moves beyond the limitations of "blind compliance" by allowing robots to actively infer and apply task-relevant forces and stiffness profiles. This adaptive compliance makes robotic interactions more intelligent, precise, and robust to environmental variations and disturbances, leading to higher success rates and quality of execution. This is crucial for real-world deployment in unstructured environments.

- **Reduced Cost and Risk in Robot Training:** By successfully enabling policy training *purely in simulation* with only "lightweight manual tuning" (a single scalar per contact state) in the real world, the method drastically reduces the economic cost, time investment, and safety risks associated with real-world data collection and experimentation for complex manipulation skills. This scalability is a cornerstone for accelerating robotics research and development.

- **Enhanced Robot Capabilities:** Robots equipped with this framework could perform a wider array of delicate, precise, and forceful manipulation tasks that are common in assembly, manufacturing, service robotics, and even surgical applications. Examples include inserting components with tight tolerances, wiping/cleaning surfaces, operating articulated objects like doors and drawers, and other tasks requiring fine control of interaction forces.

- **Theoretical Grounding for Robust Control:** The provided theoretical stability analysis of the force-aware admittance controller adds rigor to the practical effectiveness, offering guarantees for system behavior during contact, contact loss, and under disturbances. This is vital for safety-critical applications.

- **Impact on Large-Scale Robot Learning:** The integration with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models like E2VLA suggests a pathway for scaling these techniques to even more complex tasks, potentially allowing high-level instructions to be translated into sophisticated, force-aware physical interactions. This could significantly influence the design of future general-purpose robotic agents.

While the current formulation focuses on translational forces, the authors acknowledge this limitation and suggest extending it to torques for rotational tasks as future work. Similarly, addressing the slight degradation in position accuracy when introducing force-related predictions within VLA models presents an exciting research challenge. Nevertheless, "Direction Matters" offers a practical, theoretically-sound, and empirically validated approach that significantly pushes the boundaries of sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich robotic manipulation, paving the way for more capable and adaptable robots in real-world scenarios.
