# CoRI: Communication of Robot Intent for Physical Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and Honda Research Institute USA introduced CoRI, a pipeline that generates unscripted, contextually-grounded natural language explanations of robot intent for physical human-robot interaction. The system leverages vision-language models and large language models to interpret robot motion plans and visual observations, communicating high-level goals, detailed motion aspects, and required user cooperation. A user study showed CoRI significantly improved user comprehension and achieved high semantic accuracy in conveying robot intent compared to baseline methods.

## Problem

- Non-expert users struggle to interpret autonomous robot motions during physically assistive tasks, leading to uncertainty, discomfort, and diminished control.
- Existing robot intent communication methods are often specialized (e.g., visual projections, AR), require specific hardware, are task-specific, or rely on limited scripted verbalizations.
- Developing unscripted, natural language communication for complex, physical manipulation tasks remains challenging, with prior free-form verbalization primarily focused on robot navigation.

## Method

- The **CoRI** pipeline integrates visual perception (RGB images) and 3D motion plans (trajectories) to generate natural language explanations of robot intent.
- An '==Interaction-based Trajectory Encoding==' stage processes images (with de-identified human landmarks) and segments trajectories based on gripper state, forces, or pauses, then visually overlays dynamic information (start/end points, velocity, force) onto image observations.
- A '==Language-model Querying==' stage uses a ==Vision-Language Model== (GPT-4o) and a ==Large Language Model== (o3-mini) to interpret encoded visual data and trajectory details, generating concise, jargon-free verbal communication for overall intent, motion specifics, and user cooperation, synchronized with robot motion.

## Results

- A user study (**16 participants**) across three assistive tasks (bathing, shaving, feeding) on two robot platforms showed CoRI received significantly higher median scores for motion comprehension (L1, L2) and communication quality (L3-L6) compared to scripted baseline and no-communication strategies (**p < 0.01** for most comparisons).
- CoRI communications achieved a high mean semantic entailment probability of approximately **0.95** when compared to detailed ground-truth descriptions of robot intent, demonstrating consistency and accuracy without introducing contradictory information.
- The approach effectively communicated both high-level task goals (L4) and necessary user actions (L6), areas where the scripted baseline performed less effectively, achieving **p < 0.001** for both.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rich, contextually-grounded, and user-directed verbal communication of robot intent can be generated from only RGB images and 3D spatial waypoint trajectories, without prior task-specific knowledge.
> - Segmenting trajectories based on interaction events and visually encoding dynamic information onto environmental images allows VLMs to build a detailed and accurate understanding of robot actions.
> - Proactively communicating overall robot intention, detailed motion aspects (position, shape, velocity, force), and required human cooperation in natural language significantly enhances user comprehension and interpretability in pHRI.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of human-robot interaction (HRI) increasingly emphasizes clear communication to foster transparency, interpretability, and trust, particularly in physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) scenarios such as assistive robotics. Robots performing tasks like bathing, feeding, or dressing require humans, often non-experts, to understand their autonomous actions. Traditional methods for communicating robot intent frequently involve specialized modalities, such as visual projections, onboard indicator lights, augmented reality displays, or implicit communication through robot gestures and legible motions. While these methods provide various forms of intent visualization, they often require specific hardware, can be task-specific, or rely on visual attention from the human.

Verbal communication offers a universally understandable and adaptable alternative. However, existing verbal communication strategies often rely on scripted sentences, which have limited applicability across diverse tasks and lack the flexibility to adapt to dynamic environmental contexts. More free-form verbalization has primarily focused on robot navigation, where environments are more easily mapped semantically. Developing unscripted, natural language communication for complex manipulation tasks, especially those involving physical contact, remains a challenge.

The emergence of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) has provided new tools for robotics research, being applied in areas such as reward function generation, planning, and low-level action generation. Some work integrates LLMs into socially assistive robots for conversational interaction or issuing commands. However, these applications generally focus on user-initiated interactions or operational task guidance within specific domains.

CoRI distinguishes itself by proposing a task- and robot-agnostic pipeline that proactively generates explanatory natural language communication of robot intent for pHRI. Unlike previous approaches, CoRI does not require specialized visualization hardware or task-specific programming. It focuses on deriving high-level intentions and desired user behavior directly from visual perception and motion plans, without prior knowledge of the robot's morphology or the specific task. This approach moves beyond scripted responses or navigation-centric verbalization, aiming to provide comprehensive, contextually-grounded explanations of upcoming physical interactions, thereby enhancing general user understanding and transparency in diverse assistive scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the inherent ambiguity and difficulty non-expert users often experience in interpreting the autonomous motions of robots during physically assistive tasks. When robots engage in direct physical contact with humans (e.g., during bathing, feeding, or shaving), a lack of clear communication can lead to uncertainty, discomfort, and a diminished sense of control for the human user. This can impede the adoption and effectiveness of assistive robotic technologies. Current solutions, such as expert-oriented interfaces for trajectory analysis, are inaccessible to general users, while many existing communication methods are limited in scope or modality. Natural language communication is identified as a universally understandable medium that can immediately bridge this interpretability gap.

Against this background, the key objectives of the CoRI (Communication of Robot Intent) pipeline are to enable any robot to communicate its intentions to a user in natural language, specifically focusing on three aspects:

1. **The overall intention of the robot**: This refers to the high-level, task-specific goal the robot aims to achieve. The system is designed to infer this goal without relying on pre-programmed task knowledge.
2. **All aspects of the robot's motion**: This includes detailed information about the planned trajectory, such as starting and ending positions, the shape of the path, and critical dynamic properties like velocity and force profiles. The communication is intended to be intuitive, using references to the robot's surroundings and comparative language rather than numerical values, making it accessible to non-technical users.
3. **Required user cooperation**: Many assistive tasks are collaborative, necessitating specific actions or behaviors from the human user for successful task completion (e.g., holding an arm steady, opening the mouth). A crucial objective is to clearly convey these necessary human actions.

To achieve these objectives, CoRI operates under specific assumptions:
- A human is present within the robot's visual field.
- The only available inputs for communication synthesis are an RGB image observation of the environment (potentially including a wrist camera image for tool details) and a planned 3D spatial waypoint trajectory. This trajectory includes information on position, gripper status, velocity magnitude, and intended force.
- The robot's end-effector is captured by its camera for the majority of the planned motion.

The work aims to demonstrate that, given these inputs and without prior task-specific knowledge, it is possible to generate rich, context-grounded, user-directed verbal communication that encapsulates these three facets of robot intent, thereby enhancing transparency and interpretability in pHRI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CoRI pipeline integrates visual perception with motion planning information to generate natural language communication of robot intent. It comprises two main stages: Interaction-based Trajectory Encoding and Language-model Querying.

#### 3.1. Interaction-based Trajectory Encoding

This initial stage processes the raw inputs—an image observation (`o`) and a 3D spatial trajectory (`τ`)—to create a visually rich representation suitable for interpretation by large vision-language models.

- **Body Landmark Detection and De-identification**: The pipeline first identifies human body poses within the robot's image observation using MediaPipe. To ensure privacy, the facial area of the detected human is blurred. The detected body and facial landmarks are then annotated directly onto the image, serving as spatial references for subsequent reasoning. This annotated and de-identified image is denoted as `o_a`. An optional wrist camera image (`o_w`) can be included if the robot is holding a tool not clearly visible in `o_a`.

- **Trajectory Segmentation**: To provide clear and concise communication, the continuous 3D trajectory (`τ`) is segmented into distinct phases based on meaningful interaction events. These events are defined by three criteria:
 1. **Gripper state change**: Opening or closing of the gripper, indicating an intended object manipulation.
 2. **Presence of end-effector forces**: Onset or termination of sustained non-zero force, signifying planned physical contact with the human or environment.
 3. **Robot pause**: A standstill of the robot end-effector lasting more than 2 seconds, potentially indicating a waiting period for user action.
 This segmentation ensures that each resultant trajectory segment (`τ_k`) corresponds to a coherent phase of interaction, allowing for individualized visualization and verbal description.

- **Visual Representation of Planned Trajectory**: For each trajectory segment, its 3D waypoints are projected into the 2D pixel space of the annotated image `o_a`. A whitened version of `o_a` serves as the base for this overlay, enhancing the visibility of the trajectory. The overlay visually encodes dynamic information:
 - The **starting waypoint** of a segment is marked with a red square.
 - The **ending waypoint** is marked with a blue square.
 - **Intermediate waypoints** are represented by green circles, with the shade of green indicating velocity (darker for slower, brighter for faster).
 - Consecutive waypoints are connected by lines. The color of these lines represents the **amount of force** planned for that movement, mapped along a gradient from cyan (low force) to purple (high force).
 Two visual representations are generated for each segment: a full overlay image (`o_τ_k`) and a cropped version (`o'_τ_k`) zoomed in on the trajectory, allowing the VLM to reason at both global and local scales.

#### 3.2. Language-model Querying

This stage utilizes the encoded visual information and textual trajectory data to generate natural language communication. GPT-4o is employed as the Vision-Language Model (VLM) for interpretation, and o3-mini as the reasoning Large Language Model (LLM) for final communication generation, without fine-tuning.

- **Environment Comprehension Stage**: CoRI first queries the VLM with `o_a` (and `o_w` if applicable) to establish environmental context. The VLM is prompted to describe the visible environment, identify any object held by the robot's gripper (if closed at the start of the trajectory), and infer its potential function within the context.

- **Trajectory Comprehension Stage**: For each segmented trajectory (`τ_k`), the VLM receives the segment's overlay images (`o_τ_k` and `o'_τ_k`) along with textual descriptions of its waypoints, position, velocity, and force profiles. Crucially, the positions of the closest body landmarks to the segment's start and end points are also provided textually. The VLM is then asked a series of structured, specific questions about the segment's motion characteristics (e.g., start/end positions, trajectory shape, proximity to landmarks, velocity changes, presence and extent of forces). For the final segment, the VLM is additionally prompted to infer the overall intention of the entire trajectory and any necessary user cooperation. This questioning strategy guides the VLM to incrementally build a detailed understanding of each segment and the overall task.

- **Communication Generation Stage**: The textual outputs extracted from the VLM responses—including the environment summary, per-segment summaries, overall intention, and inferred user cooperation—are then fed to the reasoning LLM (o3-mini). The LLM is tasked with generating a concise, user-directed, jargon-free natural language statement for each trajectory segment. These statements combine the overall intention (primarily in the first segment), motion details (position, shape, velocity, force changes using qualitative descriptions), and explicit instructions for human cooperation, if required. The robot's speech is synchronized with its motion: the statement for the first segment is spoken before motion begins, while for subsequent segments, the speech starts halfway into its duration before the corresponding movement, ensuring the user receives information slightly ahead of the action it describes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated CoRI's effectiveness through a user study involving three assistive tasks on two different robot platforms.

#### 4.1. Tasks and Robotic Systems

Three distinct physical human-robot interaction tasks were designed to demonstrate CoRI's versatility:
- **Simulated Bathing**: Performed by a Stretch 3 mobile manipulator, involving guiding a dry washcloth on a user's arm.
- **Simulated Shaving**: Also performed by the Stretch 3, using a surgical clipper with a plastic shaver head across a user's arm.
- **Feeding**: Performed by an xArm 7 tabletop manipulator, bringing a spoonful of food to a user.

For each task, two different trajectories were planned to emphasize varying communication aspects (e.g., changes in velocity and force for bathing, trajectory shape for shaving, and required human behavior for feeding). The robot's speech was synchronized such that statements for the first segment were spoken before motion, while for subsequent segments, speech commenced mid-way through its duration before the corresponding movement, allowing for proactive communication.

#### 4.2. User Study

A user study involving 16 participants (7 female, 9 male, ages 18–49) was conducted. Each participant experienced all three tasks, both trajectories per task, and three communication strategies:
1. **CoRI**: The proposed method.
2. **Baseline Scripted Communication**: A simple, parameterizable approach stating "I’m moving towards your <LANDMARK NAME>," which is common in prior research.
3. **No Communication**: The robot performs actions silently.
The order of tasks and communication strategies was counterbalanced across participants. Following each trial, participants completed a Likert scale questionnaire (1-7 scale) with six items:
- L1 & L2: Assessed motion comprehension ("I felt confident predicting the robot’s next action," "I understood what the robot was going to do.").
- L3: Evaluated consistency between actions and communication ("The robot’s actions matched its communications.").
- L4-L6: Specifically targeted communication quality regarding overall intention, motion aspects, and user cooperation ("The robot correctly communicated its intentions," "The robot’s communications fully captured its actions in all notable aspects," "The robot clearly communicated what I should do in the interaction.").

#### 4.3. Likert Item Responses

Statistical analysis involved pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for within-subject comparisons.
- **Motion Comprehension (L1, L2)**: CoRI received significantly higher median scores than both the scripted baseline and no-communication strategies (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). CoRI ratings generally clustered near the maximum score of 7, indicating consistent and high user understanding of robot motion, contrasting with lower and more variable scores for the other strategies.
- **Communication-Focused Items (L3-L6)**: CoRI consistently achieved higher median scores than the scripted baseline (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). The most substantial differences were observed for L4 (correct communication of intentions) and L6 (clear communication of user cooperation), both of which showed p < 0.001. This indicates CoRI's effectiveness in conveying task-level intent and required human actions, areas where the context-lacking scripted baseline performed less effectively.

#### 4.4. Entailment within Ground Truth

To objectively assess information completeness and accuracy, each CoRI-generated statement was compared against a detailed, ground-truth paragraph (an "oracle" description of the robot's full intent and motion dynamics) for semantic entailment using a RoBERTa-large model fine-tuned on the Multi-Genre Natural Language Inference (MNLI) dataset. LLM-generated one-sentence summaries of these ground-truth paragraphs served as approximate upper bounds for achievable scores.

- **CoRI Performance**: CoRI-generated statements achieved a high mean entailment probability across all tasks (approx. 0.95, with standard deviations between 0.03 and 0.08). These scores were comparable to those of the oracle summaries (approx. 0.96-0.98), indicating that CoRI communications were largely consistent with the robot's true intent and motion, without introducing contradictory information.
- **Baseline Performance**: The baseline communication strategy exhibited lower mean entailment probabilities (ranging from 0.69 to 0.88) and considerably higher standard deviations (0.21 to 0.40), suggesting less accurate and less consistent information conveyance.

These results collectively indicate that CoRI significantly improves users' understanding of robot intent and motion compared to baseline methods, providing clear, contextually relevant, and semantically accurate natural language explanations for pHRI.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CoRI represents a contribution to the field of human-robot interaction by providing a pipeline for generating natural language communication of robot intent in physical human-robot interaction (pHRI). Its significance stems from its ability to enhance transparency and interpretability for human users, particularly in assistive robotic applications.

**Significance:**
- **Task- and Robot-Agnostic Communication**: CoRI demonstrates the feasibility of generating unscripted, natural language explanations of robot intent that are independent of specific tasks or robot morphologies. This generalizability addresses a limitation of many prior communication methods that are often tailored to specific scenarios or hardware.
- **Comprehensive Intent Conveyance**: The pipeline effectively communicates not only the high-level goals of a robot but also granular details about its planned motion (e.g., trajectory shape, velocity changes, force application) and necessary human cooperation. This multi-faceted communication is crucial for ensuring human understanding and effective collaboration in physically interactive tasks.
- **Contextual Grounding**: By visually encoding 3D motion plans onto 2D image observations and leveraging vision-language models, CoRI grounds its verbal explanations in the immediate environmental context. This contextual awareness enables more intuitive and meaningful communication for users.
- **Empirical Validation**: The user study results provide quantitative evidence that CoRI significantly improves human users' comprehension of robot actions and intentions compared to baseline strategies, including "no communication" and simple scripted responses. The high entailment probabilities further confirm the semantic accuracy and completeness of the generated statements.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced User Trust and Safety**: Clear and proactive communication of robot intent can reduce uncertainty and anxiety for human users, potentially increasing their trust and perceived safety during physical interactions with robots. This is particularly critical in sensitive assistive tasks where physical contact is involved.
- **Broader Adoption of Assistive Robotics**: By making robot behavior more interpretable and predictable for non-expert users, CoRI could facilitate the wider acceptance and deployment of assistive robots in homes, healthcare, and other human-centric environments.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration**: When humans clearly understand what a robot intends to do and what their role in the interaction is, collaboration can become more efficient and harmonious, leading to better task outcomes.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: CoRI lays groundwork for further research in adaptive and bidirectional human-robot communication. Its modular design and reliance on large foundation models could inspire extensions to more complex interaction scenarios.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that indicate areas for future work. The current evaluation primarily focuses on information completeness and accuracy, rather than psychological effects such as perceived safety, comfort, or user trust, which are critical for widespread deployment. The task diversity in the study, while covering three tasks, could be expanded to a broader range of pHRI scenarios. CoRI is currently designed for preplanned trajectories and does not support real-time, closed-loop controllers or policies due to latency associated with VLM and LLM queries. Extending this framework to real-time control, potentially through local language models or parallel simulations, is an identified area for investigation. The current system provides unidirectional communication from robot to human; enabling natural language communication from humans to robots to modify motions is ongoing work. Finally, CoRI assumes a relatively stationary user; significant user movements that alter the intended interaction would require re-planning and re-generating communication, potentially disrupting the interaction. Addressing these limitations could further mature the technology for robust real-world applications.
