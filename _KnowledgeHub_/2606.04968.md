# Potential-Guided Flow Matching for Vision-Language-Action Policy Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> ForesightFlow fine-tunes Vision-Language-Action policies using mixed-quality robot data by integrating action chunks with learned success potentials, enabling self-guidance without a separate critic network. The method improves policy performance on simulation and real-world manipulation tasks while reducing training computational costs by 38% compared to a critic-based baseline.

## Problem

- Existing imitation learning methods struggle with mixed-quality robot data, leading to either imitation of failures (full Behavior Cloning) or underutilization of useful partial successes (filtered Behavior Cloning).
- Offline Reinforcement Learning methods typically require a large, separate critic network to estimate action values, introducing substantial computational overhead and complexity.
- Directly integrating action and value learning can lead to conflicting supervision requirements, making it difficult for the value function to accurately calibrate across all outcomes, including failures.

## Method

- Augments the Conditional ==Flow Matching== (CFM) state with a success-potential vector, making potential an additional generated dimension of the same flow endpoint, without a separate critic network.
- Introduces stage-level potential targets and an efficient one-step boundary estimator for advantage calculation, avoiding costly Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) integration.
- Employs a decoupled advantage-weighted flow matching loss, applying weights only to action coordinates while uniformly weighting potential velocity loss.
- Utilizes self-guided best-of-K inference at test time, selecting actions based on the internally generated potential scores for improved long-horizon execution.

## Results

- Achieved an average normalized task score of 0.46 on BEHAVIOR-1K simulation tasks, matching the strongest separate-critic baseline (IDQL) in average success rate (**39.6%**).
- Demonstrated improved real-world performance with an average success rate of **35.4%** across five bimanual tasks, outperforming Filtered Behavior Cloning by 10.8 percentage points and IDQL by 2.8 percentage points.
- Reduced training computational cost by **38%** (178 vs. 287 GPU hours) and significantly lowered additional parameter count (approximately 1K vs. 500M) compared to critic-based methods like IDQL.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A generative flow policy can intrinsically provide action quality 'foresight' by augmenting its output with learned success potentials, eliminating the need for a separate critic network.
> - Decoupling advantage weighting between action and potential velocities is crucial to prevent 'value hallucination' and ensure accurate potential calibration while guiding action generation towards high-advantage behaviors.
> - An efficient one-step boundary estimator for advantage computation can significantly reduce training time without compromising the fidelity of candidate rankings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control, particularly with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, has seen advancements with the training of large models as conditional generative models for action chunks. These models combine capabilities such as language parsing, high-dimensional observation fusion, and the generation of temporally extended actions for manipulation tasks. Flow-matching policies, in particular, offer a combination of supervised-training stability with expressive continuous action distributions and flexible sampling mechanisms.

A significant challenge in deploying these VLA policies is the collection of diverse, mixed-quality experience. This experience encompasses successful demonstrations, partially completed tasks, recoverable mistakes, and outright failures. Traditional imitation learning methods struggle with this heterogeneity: full behavior cloning (BC) may lead to imitating failures, while filtered BC, which discards unsuccessful trajectories, can waste useful sub-trajectories or partial successes. Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods, designed to learn from such varied datasets, typically introduce additional complexity and computational overhead through the requirement of a separate critic network to evaluate action quality.

This paper addresses the limitation of existing approaches by proposing a method that integrates the "foresight" or value estimation directly into the generative policy itself, rather than relying on a separate critic. This positions the work at the intersection of generative VLA models and offline policy improvement, aiming to enhance learning from mixed-quality data while maintaining computational efficiency. It builds upon the foundation of flow-matching for generative modeling and advantage-weighted regression for policy improvement, but seeks to circumvent the challenges associated with detached critic networks and naive application of weighting schemes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable a VLA generative policy to perform self-guidance, effectively providing its own "foresight" or assessment of action quality, a function typically performed by an external critic in offline reinforcement learning. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Effective Utilization of Mixed-Quality Experience:** Deployed robots inevitably generate a dataset comprising diverse outcomes, from complete successes to various forms of failures. The authors aim to develop a policy improvement method that can leverage all this mixed-quality data. Current methods often fall short: full behavior cloning (BC) indiscriminately imitates all actions, including suboptimal ones, leading to performance degradation on tasks requiring precision. Filtered BC, while avoiding imitation of outright failures, discards potentially useful partial successes or recoverable mistakes, thereby underutilizing collected data.
2. **Reducing Computational Overhead and Complexity of Separate Critics:** Offline RL methods, such as those that use diffusion policies, frequently incorporate a large, separate critic network to estimate action values or advantages. This separate critic introduces significant computational costs during training (e.g., pre-training, actor-critic fine-tuning) and adds complexity to the model architecture and inference pipeline. The authors are motivated to achieve policy improvement comparable to or exceeding critic-based methods without this additional burden.
3. **Addressing Conflicting Supervision Requirements:** A core challenge identified by the authors is that action learning and potential learning require distinct forms of supervision. For action updates, it is beneficial to emphasize high-advantage behavior to guide the policy towards successful trajectories. However, for a potential function to be accurately calibrated across all outcomes, it must be trained to correctly identify and assign low potential to failures, even overconfident ones. Naively applying the same advantage weights to both action and potential coordinates can suppress corrective gradients for overconfident failures, leading to "value hallucination" where the model incorrectly assigns high potential to poor actions.
4. **Improving Efficiency of Advantage Estimation:** Standard methods for calculating advantage often involve computationally expensive processes, such as integrating the learned flow's Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) for every training example. This repeated integration can significantly increase training time. The authors seek a more efficient method for estimating baselines for advantage computation, ideally requiring minimal additional computational steps.
5. **Enhancing Long-Horizon Execution with Self-Guidance:** For complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks, selecting the single best action chunk at each step can be challenging. Leveraging multiple candidate actions (best-of-K inference) combined with a reliable internal scoring mechanism could improve performance by allowing the policy to choose more promising paths. The motivation is to integrate this scoring directly into the generative process for efficient self-guided sampling.

In summary, the paper aims to develop ForesightFlow, a self-guided flow-matching policy that can effectively learn from mixed-quality data, provide intrinsic action scoring without a separate critic, overcome the challenges of conflicting supervision, and do so with improved computational efficiency and enhanced performance on complex VLA tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ForesightFlow fine-tunes a VLA flow policy from mixed-quality data by integrating four key components: an augmented action-potential endpoint, stage-level success-potential targets, decoupled advantage-weighted flow matching, and self-guided best-of-K inference. The approach leverages Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) as its generative backbone, which learns a vector field to transport a simple prior to a context-conditioned data distribution.

1. **Action-Potential Flow State:**
- Instead of modeling only action chunks `a`, ForesightFlow augments the flow endpoint `x` with a success-potential vector `s`. This `s` vector is temporally aligned with the action chunk `a`, meaning `x = [a; s]`.
- The potential coordinate `s` is not a separate network but an additional generated dimension of the same flow endpoint. Each element `s_k` in the vector estimates the conditional likelihood that the corresponding action segment will lead to a successful task trajectory based on the dataset distribution.
- For inference, the potential vector is aggregated into a chunk-level score `Q_hat(c, a)` by temporal averaging, which serves as an intrinsic score for candidate actions.

2. **Stage-Level Potential Targets:**
- To provide finer-grained supervision than sparse binary episode outcomes, ForesightFlow assigns stage-level targets to each training action chunk.
- A binary label `y_i,t` is defined for each chunk `(i, t)`: `1` if the semantic stage is completed, `0` otherwise.
- The potential target `s_1,i,t` for the flow endpoint is a broadcast vector (repeating `y_i,t` across the chunk horizon). This ensures positive supervision for successful sub-trajectories even within a failed overall episode, while maintaining negative supervision for calibration.
- An advantage `A_i,t` is computed as `y_i,t - V_hat_i,t`, where `V_hat_i,t` is a baseline estimate of the chunk's success potential.

3. **One-Step Foresight for Advantage Estimation:**
- To efficiently compute the advantage baseline without integrating the flow ODE for every training example, ForesightFlow employs a boundary estimator derived from the CFM conditional-mean identity.
- At `σ = 0`, the population-optimal velocity `v*(x_0, 0, c)` is shown to be `E[x_1 | c] - x_0`.
- This implies that a single Euler prediction from the noise endpoint `x_0` can estimate the conditional data mean `x_hat_1 = x_0 + sg(v_θ(x_0, 0, c))`, where `sg` denotes a stop-gradient operation.
- The success-potential component `s_hat_i,t` from `x_hat_1` is then averaged to form the action-independent context baseline `V_hat_i,t`. This makes advantage computation computationally comparable to standard supervised flow matching.

4. **Decoupled Advantage-Weighted Flow Matching:**
- This is a core innovation to address the conflicting supervision problem.
- An exponentiated advantage-weighted regression (AWR) weight `w_i,t = min(M, exp(A_i,t / τ))` is calculated.
- The key is that this weight `w_i,t` is **only applied to the action coordinates** (`v^(a)_θ - u^(a)` term) of the flow-matching loss.
- The potential velocity loss (`v^(s)_θ - u^(s)`) remains **uniformly weighted** (i.e., with a weight of 1) across all samples, including failures.
- This decoupling ensures that:
 - Action updates are selectively biased towards high-advantage behaviors.
 - Potential updates receive unmasked corrective gradients from all samples, particularly from low-quality or failed examples, which is crucial for preventing "value hallucination" (where overconfident failures are incorrectly assigned high potential due to down-weighting).

5. **Self-Guided Inference:**
- At test time, the trained flow model uses its internally generated potential to guide action selection.
- Given a context `c_t`, `K` independent noise samples are drawn, and the learned flow is integrated to obtain `K` candidate endpoints `x^(k) = [a^(k); s^(k)]`.
- The candidate action `a_t*` with the highest aggregated potential score (`1/H * sum(s_j^(k))`) is selected for execution.
- This method performs best-of-K selection without requiring a separate critic network, as the scoring is inherent to the generative policy.

The authors use the π0.5 architecture for simulation tasks and π0 for real-world tasks, modifying the output layer of the Action Expert or the token projection dimension to accommodate the augmented action-potential flow state and decoupled heads.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ForesightFlow focuses on its ability to improve VLA policies from mixed-quality data without a separate critic, assessed across simulation (BEHAVIOR-1K) and real-world bimanual tasks.

1. **Simulation Performance on BEHAVIOR-1K Tasks:**
- ForesightFlow achieved the best average normalized task score (0.46) across five long-horizon manipulation tasks.
- It matched the strongest separate-critic baseline, IDQL, in average success rate (39.6% for ForesightFlow vs. 39.0% for IDQL).
- ForesightFlow outperformed full Behavior Cloning (BC) and Filtered BC, which suffered from imitating failures (e.g., BC's 0% SR on Spray) or discarding useful sub-trajectories.
- It also surpassed FQL, a flow-aware offline RL method, demonstrating that integrating the quality signal directly into the generative flow can be more effective than distilling it into a one-step student in this high-dimensional action-chunk setting.
- Specifically, ForesightFlow showed improvements over baselines on "Turning on Radio," "Picking up Trash," and "Wash a Baseball Cap."

2. **Real-World Robot Performance:**
- ForesightFlow demonstrated improved performance in real-world bimanual manipulation, achieving the best average score (0.62) and success rate (35.4%) across five tasks.
- It outperformed Filtered BC by 10.8 percentage points and IDQL by 2.8 percentage points in average success rate in real-world scenarios.
- Notable gains were observed on "Cube-Stack" (precise placements) and "Transfer-Food" (multi-stage progress), indicating the benefit of calibrated ranking for partial progress.

3. **Computational Efficiency:**
- ForesightFlow achieved IDQL-level performance with a significant reduction in training compute. It required 178 GPU hours compared to IDQL's 287 GPU hours, representing a 38% reduction.
- The parameter count difference was substantial: ForesightFlow added only a approximately 1K-parameter progress projection head, while IDQL used an approximately 500M-parameter separate critic.
- At K=5 candidates, ForesightFlow also exhibited faster inference latency (155 ms) compared to IDQL (183 ms), due to the joint generation and scoring of candidates within the same flow.

4. **Ablation Studies:**
- **Decoupled Weighting Prevents Value Hallucination:** A comparison between coupled and decoupled AWR (ForesightFlow) on "Turning on Radio" showed that coupled AWR assigned high potentials to explicit failures (e.g., knocking over the radio). Decoupled training, by contrast, lowered these predictions for failures while maintaining high scores for successful segments, demonstrating better calibration. This contributed to better performance in later stages of complex tasks.
- **One-Step Foresight Preserves Useful Rankings:** The one-step boundary estimator (NFE=1) demonstrated efficiency gains (8.4 hours vs. 11 hours for NFE=20 for 5,000 steps). For ranking fidelity, compared to a high-NFE=100 reference, NFE=1 showed high Kendall τ values (mean 0.834), high mean top-1 agreement (87.2%), and high mean MC gain ratio (96.8%), indicating that it largely preserved candidate rankings. Trajectory curvature analysis in the success-potential subspace showed near-linearity (median κ ≤ 0.039), supporting the approximation.
- **Self-Guided Sampling Improves Long-Horizon Performance:** Comparing K=1 and K=5 candidate ranking, ForesightFlow's average success rate improved by 5.0 percentage points (from 34.6% to 39.6%) with best-of-K sampling. This improvement was most pronounced on multi-stage tasks. IDQL's performance remained largely flat under the same best-of-K protocol. This suggests that the internal coupling of candidate generation and potential prediction in ForesightFlow provides a valuable signal for improving long-horizon execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper, ForesightFlow, introduces a self-guided flow-matching framework that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic control and VLA policy development:

1. **Integration of Foresight into Generative Policies:** The primary significance lies in demonstrating that a generative flow policy can itself provide the "foresight" or value estimation typically assigned to a separate critic. By augmenting action chunks with learned success potentials, ForesightFlow enables the same model to propose and score candidate actions, eliminating the need for an external value function. This represents a conceptual shift towards more unified and intrinsically capable VLA models.

2. **Efficient Policy Improvement from Mixed-Quality Data:** ForesightFlow provides an effective method for leveraging mixed-quality robot experience, which is a common and challenging aspect of real-world robot deployment. It avoids the pitfalls of naive behavior cloning (imitating failures) and filtered behavior cloning (discarding useful partial trajectories), allowing for more comprehensive learning from diverse datasets. This is crucial for developing robust policies that can learn from continuous interaction and self-correction.

3. **Reduced Computational Overhead and Complexity:** By integrating the scoring mechanism directly into the flow policy, ForesightFlow substantially reduces training compute and the number of parameters compared to offline RL methods that rely on large, separate critic networks. The demonstrated 38% reduction in GPU hours and the significantly lower additional parameter count (approximately 1K vs. 500M for IDQL) highlight a practical advantage for scaling VLA policies and making them more accessible for research and application. This efficiency also extends to inference, where joint generation and scoring lead to faster best-of-K candidate selection.

4. **Novel Decoupled Training Objective:** The introduction of decoupled advantage-weighted flow matching is a methodological advance. By applying advantage weights only to action velocities and training potential velocities uniformly, the method effectively addresses the "value hallucination" problem. This ensures that the potential function remains calibrated by learning from failures, while the action generation is still selectively guided towards high-advantage behaviors. This decoupled approach is critical for the reliability of the integrated scoring mechanism.

5. **Efficient Advantage Estimation:** The derivation and empirical validation of a one-step boundary estimator for conditional flow matching provide an efficient way to compute AWR baselines. This approximation significantly speeds up the training process without substantially compromising the fidelity of candidate rankings, demonstrating a practical trade-off that benefits training scalability.

6. **Enhanced Long-Horizon Task Performance:** The ability of self-guided sampling (best-of-K inference) to improve long-horizon execution, particularly on multi-stage tasks, underscores the practical utility of the learned potential. By allowing the policy to internally rank and select the most promising action chunks, ForesightFlow demonstrates a mechanism for more robust and successful completion of complex tasks.

The potential impact includes fostering the development of more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems that can continually learn and refine their skills from their own experience, including mistakes. Future work outlined by the authors, such as scaling to larger multi-task datasets, automatically inferring progress signals, and adapting to online settings, suggests avenues for further impact. However, the reliance on stage labels and challenges with very long-horizon credit assignment remain limitations, alongside general safety considerations for deploying more capable robot policies.
