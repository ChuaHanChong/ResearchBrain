# Mean Flows for One-step Generative Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> MeanFlow, developed by researchers at CMU and MIT, introduces a generative modeling framework based on learning "average velocity" to enable efficient one-step image generation. The model achieves an FID of 3.43 with a single function evaluation on ImageNet 256x256, demonstrating improved performance over prior one-step methods and approaching the quality of multi-step diffusion models.

## Problem

- Existing generative models like diffusion and flow matching require many function evaluations (NFEs) for sampling, leading to slow and computationally expensive inference.
- Prior one-step generative models often rely on heuristic consistency constraints, pre-training/distillation from multi-step models, or complex discretization curricula, limiting their stability and generality.
- There was a need for a principled, standalone one-step generative model that could rival the quality of multi-step models without their computational burden.

## Method

- **MeanFlow** redefines the target for generative modeling from instantaneous velocity to "==average velocity==," which directly represents displacement over a finite time interval.
- It introduces the "MeanFlow Identity," a mathematically derived relationship between average and instantaneous velocities, enabling a principled training objective for a neural network to model average velocity.
- One-step sampling is achieved by directly applying the learned average velocity from noise to data (x = noise - u), requiring only a single function evaluation, and Classifier-Free Guidance is integrated directly into the training target.

## Results

- MeanFlow achieves a 1-NFE FID of 3.43 on ImageNet 256x256, outperforming prior one-step models by a 50-**70%** relative improvement.
- With 2 NFEs, MeanFlow reaches an FID of 2.20, becoming competitive with state-of-the-art multi-step diffusion models that typically require hundreds of NFEs.
- The model is trainable from scratch without distillation or curriculum learning, and its performance scales effectively with model size and training duration; Classifier-Free Guidance is seamlessly integrated, improving generation quality without incurring additional sampling steps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling "average velocity" instead of instantaneous velocity provides a principled and effective theoretical foundation for one-step continuous-time generative models, moving beyond heuristic consistency constraints.
> - High-fidelity image generation is achievable with a single function evaluation (1-NFE) when models are trained with a properly defined average velocity objective, significantly closing the quality gap with multi-step models.
> - A direct, self-contained training approach for one-step models, free from distillation, pre-training, or complex curriculum learning, is feasible and leads to strong performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Generative modeling is a cornerstone of modern artificial intelligence, aiming to learn the underlying data distribution to generate new, realistic samples. Over the past decade, two paradigms have emerged as highly successful: Diffusion Models and Flow Matching models.

- **Diffusion Models**: These models progressively add noise to data and then learn to reverse this process, effectively transforming noise into data. This involves solving stochastic differential equations (SDEs), which can be reformulated as probability flow ordinary differential equations (ODEs). While highly effective in generating high-quality images, traditional diffusion models often require hundreds or thousands of "function evaluations" (NFEs) to generate a single sample, making inference computationally expensive and slow.

- **Flow Matching Models**: Closely related to diffusion models, Flow Matching focuses on directly learning the velocity fields that transport a simple prior distribution (e.g., Gaussian noise) to the complex data distribution along a continuous path. They offer an intuitive and conceptually simpler framework compared to SDE-based diffusion models.

The primary limitation of both traditional diffusion and Flow Matching models for practical applications has been their iterative sampling process, requiring numerous steps (NFEs). This has spurred significant research into **few-step, and in particular, one-step generative models**. The motivation is clear: faster inference without compromising generation quality.

Prior work in few-step generation has largely followed two main avenues:
1. **Distillation:** Pre-training a many-step model and then distilling its knowledge into a smaller, faster few-step model.
2. **Training from scratch with consistency constraints:** Pioneered by **Consistency Models (CMs)**, these models aim to learn a mapping directly from noise to data in one step by imposing a "consistency" property: all points along a trajectory should map to the same data point (or a consistent representation of it). While promising, CMs often require an explicit "discretization curriculum" for stable training and typically rely on heuristics to define the consistency constraint. Other recent works like Shortcut Models and Inductive Moment Matching (IMM) also employ self-consistency losses between different time intervals.

This paper, "Mean Flows," squarely fits into the latter category: **developing a standalone, one-step generative model trained from scratch**. However, it differentiates itself by moving away from heuristic consistency constraints. Instead, MeanFlow introduces a **principled, mathematically derived framework** based on the notion of "average velocity" and its intrinsic relationship with "instantaneous velocity." By establishing a fundamental identity between these two concepts, MeanFlow provides a more robust and theoretically grounded approach to learning one-step mappings, without needing distillation, pre-training, or curriculum learning. The work aims to substantially narrow the performance gap between one-step models and their multi-step predecessors, an ambitious goal given the state-of-the-art of multi-step diffusion models like DiT and SiT.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations behind the MeanFlow model are articulated clearly in the paper's abstract and introduction:

1. **Develop a Principled and Effective Framework for One-step Generative Modeling:** The primary goal is to establish a new theoretical foundation for one-step generation that is mathematically sound, contrasting with prior approaches that often rely on empirical heuristics or consistency constraints imposed on network behavior without clear grounding in the underlying generative process.

2. **Introduce the Concept of Average Velocity:** The authors identify a gap in existing Flow Matching literature, which primarily models instantaneous velocity. They propose "average velocity" as a more suitable concept for one-step generation, as it directly relates displacement over a time interval to a single velocity value, naturally lending itself to large step sizes.

3. **Derive a Well-Defined Identity Between Average and Instantaneous Velocities:** A key motivation is to formalize the relationship between their newly introduced average velocity and the conventional instantaneous velocity. This "MeanFlow Identity" (Eq. 6) is derived from first principles (the definition of average velocity as an integral of instantaneous velocity). This identity serves as the principled basis for guiding neural network training.

4. **Overcome Limitations of Existing One-step Models:**
 - **Training Instability and Curriculum Learning:** Prior consistency models often suffer from training instability and require a "discretization curriculum" (progressively constraining the time domain) for stable learning. MeanFlow aims to remove this dependency by providing a direct, well-defined target field whose optimal solution is inherently stable.
 - **Reliance on Pre-training or Distillation:** Many fast sampling methods rely on distilling knowledge from larger, pre-trained multi-step models. MeanFlow's objective is to be a "self-contained" model, trainable from scratch without any such dependencies.
 - **Heuristic Constraints:** Instead of imposing consistency constraints as an ad-hoc property of the network, MeanFlow's training is guided by an intrinsic, definitional relationship between average and instantaneous velocities, making the framework more robust and principled.

5. **Substantially Narrow the Performance Gap with Multi-step Models:** The ultimate empirical motivation is to achieve generation quality with a single function evaluation (1-NFE) that rivals or even surpasses the quality of state-of-the-art multi-step diffusion models that typically require hundreds of NFEs. This would make generative models significantly more practical for real-world applications where inference speed is crucial.

By addressing these motivations, MeanFlow seeks to establish a new paradigm for efficient, high-quality generative modeling that is both theoretically sound and empirically superior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MeanFlow's methodology centers on redefining the target quantity for generative modeling from instantaneous velocity to average velocity, coupled with a novel training objective derived from a fundamental mathematical identity.

#### 3.1. Core Concepts: Instantaneous vs. Average Velocity

- **Instantaneous Velocity (v):** In Flow Matching, a neural network is trained to model the instantaneous velocity field $v(z_t, t)$, which represents the tangent direction of the flow path at a given point $z_t$ at time $t$. Samples are generated by integrating this velocity field over time via ODE solvers.
- **Average Velocity (u):** MeanFlow introduces the concept of average velocity, $u(z_t, r, t)$, defined as the displacement between two time steps $r$ and $t$ (obtained by integrating the instantaneous velocity) divided by the time interval $(t-r)$. Formally:
 $u(z_t, r, t) = \frac{1}{t-r} \int_{r}^{t} v(z_\tau, \tau)d\tau$
 This average velocity directly represents the overall direction and magnitude of movement over a finite interval, making it inherently more suitable for one-step generation.

#### 3.2. The MeanFlow Identity

The key insight of MeanFlow is deriving a tractable training target for $u$ without requiring explicit integration. This is achieved by differentiating the definitional equation of average velocity with respect to $t$:
$(t-r)u(z_t, r, t) = \int_{r}^{t} v(z_\tau, \tau)d\tau$
Differentiating both sides with respect to $t$ (using the product rule on the left and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on the right), and expanding the total derivative of $u$ ($d/dt u = (dz_t/dt) \partial_z u + \partial_t u = v(z_t, t) \partial_z u + \partial_t u$), leads to the "MeanFlow Identity":
$u(z_t, r, t) = v(z_t, t) - (t-r) \left(v(z_t, t) \partial_z u + \partial_t u\right)$
This identity is crucial because it provides a relationship where the average velocity $u$ (the quantity to be learned) is expressed in terms of the instantaneous velocity $v$ (which can be derived from the data and prior) and partial derivatives of $u$ itself. The authors show that this identity is both necessary and sufficient for the definition of average velocity.

#### 3.3. Training Objective

A neural network $u_\theta$ is parameterized to directly model the average velocity field $u(z_t, r, t)$. The training objective encourages $u_\theta$ to satisfy the MeanFlow Identity:
$L(\theta) = E \left[ \left\| u_\theta(z_t, r, t) - \text{sg}(u_{\text{tgt}}) \right\|^2_2 \right]$
where the target $u_{\text{tgt}}$ is given by:
$u_{\text{tgt}} = v_t - (t-r) \left(v_t \partial_z u_\theta + \partial_t u_\theta \right)$
Here, $v_t = \epsilon - x$ is the conditional instantaneous velocity from Flow Matching. A `stop-gradient (sg)` operation is applied to $u_{\text{tgt}}$ to prevent "double backpropagation" through the Jacobian-vector product (JVP) terms, ensuring efficient first-order optimization.
The `jvp` operation (Jacobian-vector product) efficiently computes the total derivative $(v_t \partial_z u_\theta + \partial_t u_\theta)$, which essentially involves a single backward pass, comparable in cost to standard backpropagation.

#### 3.4. Sampling

One of the main advantages of MeanFlow is its simple and direct sampling process. For 1-step sampling from a prior (e.g., $z_1 = \epsilon$) to data ($z_0 = x$), the definition of average velocity (displacement = interval $\times$ average velocity) is directly used:
$x = \epsilon - u_\theta(\epsilon, r=0, t=1)$
This single function evaluation directly transforms noise to data, fulfilling the 1-NFE objective. Few-step sampling is also straightforward by applying this equation iteratively over smaller intervals.

#### 3.5. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) Integration

MeanFlow naturally incorporates CFG without incurring additional NFEs at sampling time. Instead of applying CFG as an external linear combination during inference (which typically doubles NFEs), MeanFlow treats the CFG-guided velocity as a property of the underlying ground-truth fields. A new ground-truth instantaneous velocity field $v_{\text{cfg}}$ is defined as a weighted combination of class-conditional and class-unconditional instantaneous velocities. The MeanFlow Identity is then applied to this $v_{\text{cfg}}$ to derive a corresponding average velocity field $u_{\text{cfg}}$. The neural network $u_\theta$ directly learns to approximate this $u_{\text{cfg}}$. During sampling, the learned $u_\theta$ already embodies the guidance, requiring only a single NFE. The paper also introduces an "improved CFG" formulation with a mixing scale $\kappa$ (Appendix B.1) to allow the target to include both class-conditional and unconditional components, similar to the random dropping used in standard CFG.

#### 3.6. Design Decisions

- **Loss Metrics:** While the default is squared L2, the paper explores adaptive loss weighting $w=1/(\|\Delta\|^2_2+c)^p$, where $\Delta$ is the regression error and $p=1-\gamma$. They found $p=1.0$ (corresponding to $\gamma=0$) to be optimal, outperforming Pseudo-Huber loss ($p=0.5$) and standard L2 ($p=0$).
- **Sampling Time Steps (r,t):** The time steps $r$ and $t$ are sampled from distributions (uniform or logit-normal), with a certain proportion where $r=t$. Logit-normal distributions were found to perform best.
- **Conditioning on (r,t):** Positional embeddings are used for $r$ and $t$. Conditioning on $(t, t-r)$ (time and interval) showed the best empirical performance.

This comprehensive methodology provides a principled, efficient, and effective approach to one-step generative modeling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The MeanFlow model demonstrates remarkably strong empirical performance, particularly in 1-NFE (one-step) generation, significantly advancing the state-of-the-art in this domain.

#### 4.1. ImageNet 256×256 Generation

- **State-of-the-Art 1-NFE Performance:** MeanFlow achieves an FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) of **3.43** with a single function evaluation (1-NFE) on ImageNet 256×256, trained from scratch. This is a substantial improvement over previous state-of-the-art one-step diffusion/flow models:
 - **iCT-XL/2:** 34.24 FID (1-NFE)
 - **Shortcut-XL/2:** 10.60 FID (1-NFE)
 - This represents a **50% to 70% relative improvement** in FID, effectively redefining the performance standard for one-step models.

- **Closing the Gap with Multi-step Models:**
 - MeanFlow achieves an FID of **2.93** (and **2.20** with enhanced training, "MeanFlow-XL/2+") for 2-NFE generation.
 - This is highly competitive with leading *multi-step* diffusion/flow models that require significantly more NFEs:
 - **DiT-XL/2:** 2.27 FID (250×2 NFE)
 - **SiT-XL/2:** 2.06 FID (250×2 NFE)
 The results strongly suggest that few-step (and even one-step) diffusion/flow models can indeed rival their many-step predecessors in terms of quality.

- **Scalability:** MeanFlow models demonstrate promising scalability (Figure 4). Larger model sizes (from B/2 to XL/2) and longer training durations (from 80 to 240 epochs) consistently reduce FID scores, indicating that the approach benefits from increased computational resources, similar to transformer-based diffusion models.

- **Self-contained Training:** A significant finding is that MeanFlow achieves these results while being **trained entirely from scratch**, without any pre-training, distillation, or the curriculum learning strategies often required by previous one-step methods like Consistency Models.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies (ImageNet 256×256)

The ablation studies confirm the crucial elements of the MeanFlow framework:
- **Ratio of r≠t:** A non-zero ratio of $r \neq t$ (i.e., enabling the "average velocity" concept) is critical. Setting the ratio to 0% (equivalent to standard Flow Matching) yields very poor results (328.91 FID for 1-NFE), while 25% to 100% ratios significantly improve performance.
- **JVP Computation:** Correct computation of the Jacobian-vector product (specifically, the tangent $(v,0,1)$ for $\partial_z u, \partial_r u, \partial_t u$) is essential for meaningful results, validating the MeanFlow Identity.
- **Conditioning on (r,t):** Different positional embedding strategies for $(r,t)$ all yield reasonable 1-NFE results, with conditioning on $(t, t-r)$ performing slightly better.
- **Time Samplers:** Logit-normal distributions for sampling $(r,t)$ are more effective than uniform sampling, consistent with prior work on Flow Matching.
- **Loss Metrics:** Adaptive loss weighting with $p=1.0$ performs best, though $p=0.5$ (Pseudo-Huber) is also competitive, and even standard L2 loss ($p=0$) yields meaningful (though suboptimal) results.
- **Guidance Scale (CFG):** Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) substantially improves generation quality, reducing FID from 61.06 (without CFG) to 15.53 (with $\omega=3.0$), while notably maintaining 1-NFE sampling. The improved CFG formulation with $\kappa$ further enhances quality (e.g., 18.63 FID for $\kappa=0.9$ vs. 20.15 for $\kappa=0.0$ at effective $\omega'=2.0$).

#### 4.3. CIFAR-10 Comparisons

On unconditional CIFAR-10 (32×32), MeanFlow achieves an FID of **2.92** with 1-NFE sampling. This is competitive with existing one-step methods on this dataset (e.g., iCT 2.83, sCT 2.97, IMM 3.20), particularly notable given that MeanFlow operates without any EDM-style preconditioner, unlike its competitors.

In summary, the main findings highlight MeanFlow as a robust, scalable, and principled generative modeling framework that sets a new benchmark for one-step generation quality, drastically reducing the gap with computationally intensive multi-step models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MeanFlow model represents a significant theoretical and empirical advancement in the field of generative AI, with several profound implications and potential impacts:

1. **Principled Foundation for One-step Generative Modeling:** By introducing the concept of "average velocity" and deriving the "MeanFlow Identity" from first principles, the paper provides a much-needed theoretical underpinning for one-step generation. This moves beyond empirical heuristics and consistency constraints, offering a more robust and mathematically sound framework. This principled approach is likely to inspire further theoretical development in the field.

2. **Bridging the Gap Between One-step and Multi-step Models:** The most striking empirical contribution is MeanFlow's ability to achieve FID scores (3.43 for 1-NFE, 2.20 for 2-NFE) that are comparable to, or even surpass, those of state-of-the-art multi-step diffusion models (e.g., DiT, SiT) that require hundreds of function evaluations. This effectively demonstrates that high-quality image generation is achievable with vastly reduced inference costs, which has been a major challenge.

3. **Enhanced Practicality and Accessibility:**
 - **Faster Inference:** The drastic reduction in NFEs (from hundreds to just one or two) directly translates to significantly faster generation times. This makes generative models more practical for real-time applications, interactive content creation, and scenarios with limited computational resources, enabling broader adoption in areas like creative tools, gaming, and robotics.
 - **Reduced Training Complexity:** The fact that MeanFlow can be trained "from scratch" without relying on pre-training, distillation, or complex curriculum learning simplifies the training pipeline. This lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and developers, allowing them to train powerful one-step generative models more easily and cost-effectively.

4. **Novel Integration of Classifier-Free Guidance:** The method's natural integration of CFG without increasing sampling NFEs (maintaining 1-NFE even with guidance) is a notable engineering and conceptual triumph. This ensures that the benefits of guided generation (e.g., improved quality, class-conditional control) can be fully realized without sacrificing the speed advantage of one-step models.

5. **Inspiration for Future Research:** The paper explicitly hopes to "motivate future research to revisit the foundations of these powerful models." By demonstrating the efficacy of conceptualizing generative processes through average velocities, it opens new avenues for exploring alternative perspectives on continuous-time generative models, potentially leading to further innovations in model design, training stability, and performance.

6. **Broader Scientific Connections:** The conclusion draws a compelling parallel between MeanFlow's approach and multi-scale simulation problems in physics. This suggests that the principles behind MeanFlow, focusing on characterizing underlying quantities at coarsened levels of granularity, might have implications beyond generative AI, potentially bridging research in machine learning, numerical simulation, and dynamical systems across scientific disciplines.

In essence, MeanFlow not only delivers a new benchmark for efficient generative modeling but also offers a fresh, principled perspective that could reshape how we conceptualize and build powerful generative AI systems in the future.
