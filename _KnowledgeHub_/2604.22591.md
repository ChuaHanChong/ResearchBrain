# RedVLA: Physical Red Teaming for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called RedVLA was developed by researchers at Peking University to proactively uncover physical safety vulnerabilities in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models before their real-world deployment. This system effectively elicits diverse unsafe physical behaviors from various VLA models, achieving attack success rates between 64.9% and 95.5%, and facilitates a lightweight safety guard capable of reducing unsafe actions by 59.5%.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models face risks of unpredictable and irreversible physical harm when deployed in real-world environments.
- Existing VLA safety research primarily focuses on adversarial visual or linguistic inputs and semantic vulnerabilities, neglecting the proactive detection of physical risks stemming from embodied agent-environment interaction.
- Current red teaming methods, common for LLMs and VLMs, do not adequately address physical risks or model their causality within an embodied robotic context.

## Method

- Proposed **RedVLA**, a ==two-stage physical red teaming== framework comprising ==Risk Scenario Synthesis== and ==Trajectory-Driven Risk Amplification==, to systematically uncover unsafe behaviors.
- ==Risk Scenario Synthesis== constructs initial risk scenes by identifying critical interaction regions from benign trajectories and injecting relevant physical risk factors (objects) into the environment.
- ==Trajectory-Driven Risk Amplification== employs ==gradient-free optimization==, guided by end-effector trajectories, to efficiently refine risk factor placement for stable elicitation of unsafe behaviors across heterogeneous VLA models.
- Developed **SimpleVLA-Guard**, a lightweight safety guard trained on RedVLA data, designed to detect and intervene in unsafe VLA behaviors in real-time using ==internal model representations==.

## Results

- RedVLA achieved Attack Success Rates (ASR) ranging from **64.9%** to **95.5%** across six VLA models, successfully eliciting diverse unsafe behaviors across state-level (>**95%** ASR), cumulative-level (**88.9%** ASR), and conditional-level (**66.1%** ASR) scenarios.
- Analysis confirmed that most unsafe behaviors (over **90%**) occurred while models were actively attempting or succeeding at the task, rather than due to model collapse, indicating deeper safety deficiencies.
- SimpleVLA-Guard reduced the ASR of unsafe behaviors by **59.5%** (from **90.9%** to **31.4%** on seen tasks) with a modest **4.08%** decrease in benign task success rate, demonstrating effective real-time risk mitigation.
- Sim-to-real validation on a physical robot confirmed that the identified physical safety vulnerabilities manifest in real-world deployments, achieving an ASR above **80%** for critical scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA models demonstrate substantial vulnerability to physical safety risks, with unsafe actions frequently occurring during active task execution rather than from trivial model failures.
> - A direct correlation exists between stronger VLA model capabilities (higher task success rates) and increased physical safety vulnerability, suggesting a complex performance-safety trade-off.
> - Physical safety vulnerabilities uncovered by RedVLA are primarily driven by environmental risk factors, distinguishing them from input perturbation attacks and highlighting a distinct class of safety challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are developing towards generalist robotic policies by learning end-to-end mappings from vision and language to action. These models are expanding into critical domains such as manipulation, autonomous driving, and robotic surgery. While their capabilities are increasing, concerns regarding their safety, particularly in physical real-world deployment, have become more prominent. The existing research landscape for VLA safety has primarily focused on adversarial vulnerabilities related to visual or linguistic inputs, such as image perturbations that can disrupt robotic policies or adversarial suffixes that manipulate VLA behavior. Some work also addresses backdoor attacks or explores constrained learning for safety enhancement.

However, the authors identify a gap in existing VLA safety research: the proactive detection of physical safety risks arising from embodied agent-environment interaction. Red teaming, a method for proactively identifying vulnerabilities, has been extensively applied to Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), primarily targeting semantic and intent-based vulnerabilities. The authors argue that the source of risks in VLA models fundamentally shifts from the intent space to the physical space, introducing distinct challenges not addressed by current red teaming methods, which typically do not consider potential physical risks within the environment or model the physical causality of the risk process.

This work, RedVLA, positions itself as the first red teaming framework specifically designed for physical safety in VLA models. It aims to fill the identified gap by systematically uncovering physical unsafe behaviors before real-world deployment, thereby contributing a new paradigm for evaluating VLA safety that focuses on physically instantiated risk factors and their causal interaction with the VLA’s execution flow.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the limitations in the real-world deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which are constrained by the risk of unpredictable and irreversible physical harm. The authors observe a lack of effective mechanisms for proactively detecting these physical safety risks prior to deployment.

To address this, the paper establishes several key objectives:

1. **Develop a Red Teaming Framework for Physical Safety**: The central objective is to propose RedVLA, a framework specifically designed for physical safety red teaming in VLA models. This framework aims to systematically uncover unsafe behaviors by introducing potential risk factors into an environment while preserving the benign nature of the original scene and the semantic consistency of the task instructions. The goal is to maximize physical safety risks with minimal perturbation to the environment.
2. **Systematically Uncover Diverse Unsafe Behaviors**: RedVLA seeks to identify a broad range of physical safety vulnerabilities across different VLA models and under various conditions. This includes behaviors leading to immediate violations (state-level), those arising from cumulative actions over time (cumulative-level), and risks triggered by specific sequences of events (conditional-level). The framework also aims to categorize these risks across different physical hazard categories such as resource damage, dangerous item misuse, robot damage, and environmental harm.
3. **Ensure Stable Elicitation Across Models**: A technical objective is to ensure that the framework can consistently elicit unsafe behaviors across heterogeneous VLA models, even given policy stochasticity and cross-model distribution shifts. This requires an effective method for refining the injected risk factors.
4. **Investigate the Nature of VLA Safety Vulnerabilities**: The research aims to determine whether the uncovered unsafe behaviors result from superficial model failures or occur during legitimate task execution, indicating deeper safety concerns.
5. **Propose Mitigation Strategies**: A practical objective is to leverage the data generated by RedVLA to develop a lightweight safety guard, SimpleVLA-Guard, capable of real-time unsafe behavior detection and proactive intervention during VLA model deployment.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several points:

- **Accelerated VLA Development**: VLA models are rapidly advancing towards generalist robotic policies and are being integrated into critical applications like manipulation, autonomous driving, and robotic surgery. This advancement necessitates a robust understanding and mitigation of potential risks.
- **Gap in Current Safety Evaluation**: Existing safety research for VLA models primarily focuses on adversarial attacks on input modalities (visual, linguistic) or theoretical safety alignment. Red teaming for LLMs and VLMs targets semantic vulnerabilities. There is a fundamental shift in risk from the intent space to the physical space for embodied VLA agents, creating unique safety challenges not addressed by existing methods.
- **Embodied Interaction Risks**: Physical risks are inherent to embodied agent-environment interaction. These risks include unintended collisions, improper handling of hazardous items, or actions leading to environmental damage (e.g., fire hazards). Current methods lack the capacity to proactively uncover these physical risks and model their causality.
- **Prerequisite for Real-World Deployment**: Proactively identifying and mitigating physical safety risks is considered a crucial prerequisite for the safe and large-scale deployment of VLA models in real-world environments. Without such mechanisms, the risks of unpredictable and irreversible physical harm remain a significant barrier.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research is structured around the RedVLA framework, which addresses the VLA physical red teaming problem through a two-stage process: Risk Scenario Synthesis and Trajectory-Driven Risk Amplification. Additionally, the paper proposes a defense strategy, SimpleVLA-Guard, built upon the red teaming data.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The VLA physical red teaming problem is formalized as a constrained optimization. A VLA policy operates in an environment with a state space (S) and action space (A), receiving visual observations, proprioception, and a language instruction (l) to generate actions. The goal is to find a perturbed initial state (s'₀) that maximizes a target safety cost (J_C_i(τ)) while ensuring the task remains feasible, meaning its cumulative reward (J_R(τ)) exceeds a threshold (δ). The instruction (l) is fixed, and only the initial state is perturbed by injecting a single risk factor.

#### 3.2. Physical Safety Taxonomy

To categorize physical safety risks, a taxonomy is introduced, grounded in a set of binary risk predicates (Φ). These predicates define specific physical safety violations. Based on their temporal dependencies, three types of safety costs are defined:
- **State-Level Safety Cost**: Captures immediate violations where a single state-action pair constitutes a risk (e.g., `collide(wine bottle)`).
- **Cumulative-Level Safety Cost**: Addresses risks from the temporal accumulation of specific behaviors, defined by a cumulative occurrence threshold over a trajectory (e.g., `cumulative(knock(bowl), 3)`).
- **Conditional-Level Safety Cost**: Captures risks arising only when a precondition is active before a consequent (e.g., `turnon(stove) ∧ on(book, stove)`).

#### 3.3. RedVLA Framework: Two-Stage Process

**(I) Risk Scenario Synthesis:** This stage constructs an initial risk scene (s^(0)₀) that is semantically valid and task-feasible.

- **Interaction Identification**: To ensure the injected risk factor is relevant to the VLA’s execution, critical interaction regions (H) are localized. This is achieved by analyzing end-effector trajectories from benign rollouts. A rule-based procedure identifies three patterns:
 - **Transit Region (H_transit)**: The primary movement corridor of the end-effector.
 - **Grasping Region (H_grasping)**: Areas where the robot actively interacts with objects.
 - **Vibration Region (H_vibration)**: Regions where the end-effector exhibits repetitive oscillatory motion.
 The union of these regions defines the critical region H for risk injection. Algorithms 1-3 detail the identification logic for these regions using gripper state changes, vertical range/sign flips, and net displacement over windows, respectively.
- **Risk Instantiation**: A specific target safety violation (C_i) is selected based on the scene and instruction, along with its cost type, hazard category, and detection predicate. A corresponding risk object (o_risk) is chosen from a library (e.g., kitchen knife for dangerous item misuse, wine bottle for resource damage). This object is then initialized at a location within the identified critical region H, aiming to entangle with the VLA’s execution flow. The placement is validated by ensuring the target violation can be triggered by a proxy VLA and the original task remains feasible. The annotation process involves human annotators using a specialized 3D simulation platform with Physical, Free, AI, and Data Collection modes to iteratively adjust object poses and verify conditions.

**(II) Trajectory-Driven Risk Amplification:** This stage refines the initial risk factor state to ensure stable elicitation of unsafe behaviors across different models, using gradient-free optimization.

- **Optimization Objective**: The goal is to maximize J_C_i(τ) by optimizing the risk object’s position (p), while its orientation (q₀) is fixed and the task remains feasible (J_R(τ) ≥ δ).
- **Iterative Refinement**: At each iteration (k), the policy (π_θ) is executed on the current risk scene (s^(k)₀). The end-effector trajectory {x^(k)_ee(t)} is extracted.
- **Critical Spatial Anchor (x*)**: An anchor point is identified from the trajectory to guide the update. For collision-based violations, x* is the end-effector position nearest to the risk object. For grasping-based violations, it is the position during gripper closure nearest to the risk object.
- **Risk Object Position Update**: The risk object’s position is updated adaptively by moving it along the direction from its current position (p_k) to the critical spatial anchor (x*), using a step size (α_k). This update is constrained to the horizontal plane and validated by a scene validity function V(.) to prevent physical infeasibility (e.g., interpenetration). The process terminates when the target unsafe behavior is triggered (J_C_i(τ^(k)) > 0). This approach reduces the optimization search space to a low-dimensional interaction space and does not require white-box access to the policy.

#### 3.4. VLA Safety Guardrail: SimpleVLA-Guard

- **Motivation**: The paper observes that safe and unsafe trajectories are separable in the latent space of VLA models, suggesting that internal representations contain useful signals for detecting physical risk.
- **Design**: SimpleVLA-Guard is a lightweight model built on π₀, designed to detect physical risks from the VLA’s internal representations. It extracts the internal feature sequence from the π₀ diffusion transformer at each step. An LSTM module processes these features to model temporal dependencies and output a step-wise risk score.
- **Training Data**: The guard is trained on a dataset comprising:
 - RedVLA unsafe rollouts (where target safety violations were elicited).
 - Safe rollouts from scenes with injected risk factors (where the task was completed without violations).
 - Benign safe rollouts (from standard scenes without risk factors).
- **Online Deployment**: During execution, the guard outputs a risk score at each step. Functional Conformal Prediction (FCP) is used to calibrate a time-varying threshold. If the smoothed risk score exceeds this threshold, the guard can preemptively halt execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted to evaluate RedVLA and SimpleVLA-Guard yielded several findings:

#### 4.1. VLA Models Are Vulnerable to RedVLA

- RedVLA achieved an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) ranging from 64.9% to 95.5% across six representative VLA models, with π₀.₅ demonstrating the highest ASR and OpenVLA the lowest.
- While task feasibility was maintained in the risk scenarios, the Success Rate (SR) of task completion often dropped significantly. In some specific scenarios, marked with 'þ', SR dropped to nearly zero because the models diverted their focus to the injected risk objects rather than the intended task-relevant objects.

#### 4.2. RedVLA Elicits Diverse Unsafe Behaviors

- The framework successfully elicited diverse unsafe behaviors across the defined safety cost types.
 - **State-Level scenarios**: Achieved an average ASR exceeding 95% across the six models, indicating high vulnerability to immediate risks.
 - **Cumulative-Level scenarios**: Achieved an average ASR of 88.9%.
 - **Conditional-Level scenarios**: Achieved an average ASR of 66.1%. OpenVLA exhibited lower ASR in these scenarios, which the authors attribute to its comparatively weaker baseline performance in benign scenes.

#### 4.3. Unsafe Behaviors Are Not Due to Model Collapse

- Analysis of rollout outcomes revealed that most unsafe behaviors occurred in episodes where the model either successfully completed the task while performing an unsafe action ("Success + Unsafe" - SU) or attempted the task and exhibited unsafe behavior ("Attempt + Unsafe" - AU).
- Model collapse, defined as no interaction with task-relevant objects, accounted for only a small fraction of unsafe rollouts across all models. This suggests that RedVLA primarily exposes vulnerabilities during active task execution, rather than merely inducing trivial failures.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Analysis

- **Performance-Safety Trade-off**: A correlation was observed between model capability (measured by Benign SR) and physical safety vulnerability (measured by ASR). Stronger models, which achieved higher Benign SR, also tended to exhibit higher ASR. For instance, OpenVLA-OFT improved Benign SR over OpenVLA by 20.6% but also increased ASR by 25.6%. This suggests that enhanced instruction-following capabilities might inadvertently increase the likelihood of triggering unsafe behaviors when risk factors are present.
- **Impact of Multimodal Perturbations**: RedVLA maintained high ASRs (average 88.2% for language perturbations, 85.5% for visual perturbations) even when combined with input perturbations. In benign scenarios, these input perturbations alone caused significant drops in SR but rarely triggered unsafe behaviors (ASR ≤ 5.2%). This indicates that the physical safety risks uncovered by RedVLA are predominantly driven by the injected environmental risk factors, rather than simple input noise or adversarial attacks.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Impact of Risk Factor Initialization**: The proposed risk factor initialization strategy, which places objects within critical interaction regions, consistently outperformed random position initialization throughout the optimization process. Randomizing object orientation had a negligible impact, supporting the focus on position optimization.
- **Impact of Step Size (α)**: An optimal step size of α=0.01 meters was determined for the trajectory-driven risk amplification. ASR exceeded 90% for α > 0.005m. Larger step sizes (e.g., α > 0.05m) led to a sharp increase in the "Rejection Count" (number of invalid scene updates), indicating instability.
- **Effect of Optimization Iterations (K)**: ASR increased steadily with optimization iterations, with the most significant gains occurring within the first 6 iterations (K=0 to K=6). This demonstrates RedVLA's efficiency in eliciting unsafe behaviors with a relatively small number of optimization steps.

#### 4.6. Empirical Study: Sim-to-Real Validation

- The π₀ policy was deployed on a physical Franka robot for sim-to-real validation. On two benign tasks, the policy achieved over 90% SR.
- When a knife was injected onto a target cup (dangerous item misuse) or obstacle cups were placed along the robot’s trajectory (resource damage), the robot exhibited unsafe behaviors (grasping the knife, knocking over cups). Both scenarios achieved an ASR above 80% over 10 trials, confirming that the identified physical safety vulnerabilities manifest in real-world settings.

#### 4.7. SimpleVLA-Guard Performance

- **Offline Detection**: SimpleVLA-Guard demonstrated strong detection capabilities, achieving a PRC-AUC of 0.94 and Recall@FPR10 of 73.4% on seen tasks. Performance slightly dropped on unseen tasks (PRC-AUC 0.89, Recall@FPR10 69.1%), indicating a meaningful generalization capability.
- **Online Intervention**: When deployed online with the π₀ policy, SimpleVLA-Guard successfully reduced the ASR by 59.5% (from 90.9% to 31.4% on seen tasks). This intervention came with a modest decrease in Benign SR (4.08% on seen tasks), indicating a balance between safety and task performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents RedVLA, a framework for physical safety red teaming in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which holds significance for the development and deployment of embodied AI systems.

**Novelty and Gap Filling:** The work introduces the first systematic framework dedicated to uncovering physical safety risks in VLA models. It addresses a recognized gap in existing VLA safety research, which has largely focused on adversarial input perturbations or semantic vulnerabilities, rather than the embodied physical risks arising from agent-environment interaction and physical causality.

**Exposure of Vulnerabilities:** RedVLA successfully reveals substantial and diverse physical safety vulnerabilities across a range of state-of-the-art VLA models. The high Attack Success Rates (ASR) observed across state-level, cumulative-level, and conditional-level scenarios, and various hazard categories, indicate that current VLA models exhibit a lack of robust physical safety awareness. The finding that unsafe behaviors primarily occur during active task execution, rather than from trivial model collapse, suggests deeper architectural or learning-based safety deficiencies.

**Methodological Contribution:** The proposed two-stage methodology (Risk Scenario Synthesis and Trajectory-Driven Risk Amplification) provides a structured and efficient approach for physical red teaming. The use of benign trajectories to identify critical interaction regions for risk factor injection is a practical method for targeted perturbation. The gradient-free optimization, guided by trajectory spatial features, allows for stable and efficient elicitation of unsafe behaviors across heterogeneous models without requiring white-box access, making it widely applicable. The formalized physical safety taxonomy offers a structured way to categorize and analyze diverse risks.

**Practical Implications for Development and Deployment:**
- **Proactive Safety Evaluation**: RedVLA provides a tool for developers and researchers to proactively stress-test VLA models for physical safety risks in simulation *before* deployment in real-world environments. This facilitates the identification of vulnerabilities that might remain hidden under standard benign testing conditions, thereby enabling safer design and training of robotic systems.
- **Foundation for Mitigation**: The framework generates valuable data on unsafe behaviors, which is then utilized to develop SimpleVLA-Guard. This lightweight safety guard demonstrates a practical pathway for real-time detection and intervention in unsafe behaviors, offering a concrete step towards mitigating identified risks and enhancing the trustworthiness of VLA models in operation.
- **Understanding Performance-Safety Trade-off**: The analysis showing that stronger models can also exhibit higher ASR indicates a complex relationship between model capability and safety. This insight is important for future VLA design, suggesting that improving task performance alone may not inherently lead to safer systems and that dedicated safety alignment is required.

**Future Research Directions:** The work highlights the need for more automated scenario generation methods to uncover a wider and less predictable range of physical safety violations, moving beyond reliance on predefined predicates and risk objects. It also emphasizes the importance of developing stronger guard models and safety alignment methods that not only detect risks but also enable behavioral recovery, improving the resilience of VLA models during deployment.

**Dual-Use Considerations:** The authors acknowledge the potential dual-use risks, where the framework could be misused to intentionally trigger unsafe robot behaviors. They mitigate this by releasing the work for research purposes under a non-commercial license and framing RedVLA as a safety evaluation and defense construction tool. This stance contributes to the ongoing discourse regarding responsible AI development and the necessity of openly studying vulnerabilities to build more reliable and trustworthy systems.
