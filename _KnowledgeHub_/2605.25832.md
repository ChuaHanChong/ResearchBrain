# When Search Becomes Memory: Turning Robot Design Trials into Transferable Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> A self-evolving language agent, AUTO-ROBOTIST, transforms robot design evaluations into a transferable skill library for automated morphology optimization. The system demonstrated a 1.47x average convergence speedup in cold-start searches and an average +1.55 endpoint fitness improvement in cross-scale design transfers, effectively converting costly trials into explicit, reusable design principles.

## Problem

- Traditional automated robot design systems often discard valuable trial data, operating as "memoryless" processes despite costly physical or simulated evaluations.
- This leads to inefficient exploration in vast combinatorial design spaces, as insights into design successes and failures are not systematically accumulated or abstracted.
- Existing LLM-based design approaches primarily act as proposal generators and do not distill explicit, transferable design principles from iterative search processes.

## Method

- Developed **AUTO-ROBOTIST**, a self-evolving language agent that systematically converts robot design trial data into a hierarchical, natural-language skill library.
- The system employs a continuous loop of skill-conditioned proposal generation by an LLM, followed by physical simulation feedback, and then library maintenance operations (ADD, DIAGNOSE, MERGE).
- Skills are represented at three levels: L1 (abstract archetypes), L2 (evidence-grounded positive/negative rules), and L3 (raw evaluation observations) to facilitate explicit knowledge acquisition and transfer.

## Results

- AUTO-ROBOTIST achieved an average **1.47x** convergence speedup and maintained a **70%** average lead fraction compared to a Genetic Algorithm in cold-start 5x5 robot design.
- The system demonstrated superior performance in cross-scale transfer (5x5 to 10x10), yielding an average endpoint fitness improvement of +1.55 over the GA baseline.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical contribution of L2 rules, L3 observations, and active library maintenance (DIAGNOSE, MERGE) to the agent's performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language agents can effectively distill costly robot design evaluations into transferable, abstract design principles, moving beyond mere artifact generation.
> - A hierarchical and explicit skill library, grounded in simulator feedback, facilitates the acquisition of relational design knowledge that is both auditable and transferable across different design scales.
> - Continuous maintenance of this skill library, through operations like skill addition, rule diagnosis, and redundancy merging, is essential for sharpening and stabilizing the learned design theory.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research operates at the intersection of automated robot design, evolutionary computation, and artificial intelligence agents, particularly focusing on the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to complex engineering problems.

Traditionally, automated body-controller co-design has been explored through various evolutionary algorithms, quality diversity methods, learned encodings, Bayesian optimization, and reinforcement learning policies. Early work in this domain includes evolving virtual creatures and voxel-based soft robots, with platforms like EVOGYM providing controlled benchmarks for discrete voxel bodies and reinforcement learning controllers. While these methods have advanced the discovery of high-performing robot morphologies and controllers, the experience gained from costly evaluations is typically compressed into a final population or a single elite design, largely forgetting the underlying reasons for success or failure.

Concurrently, the capabilities of Large Language Models have expanded, leading to their increasing use in various optimization and decision-making tasks. In robotics and design, LLMs have been applied to propose candidate programs, optimize language objectives, synthesize rewards, and reflect on optimization outcomes. More specifically for robot morphology, LLMs have been utilized in LLM-aided evolutionary search, diversity reflection, and in selecting or co-optimizing morphology from language descriptions or preferences. These applications primarily leverage LLMs as powerful proposal generators.

A separate but related thread in AI research focuses on extending agent capabilities through memory, reflection, and the acquisition of reusable skills. This includes curriculum learning, environment design for robust transfer, modular meta-learning, and the induction of program libraries. Recent LLM agent architectures have also explored long-term memory systems, dynamic memory organization for retrieval and update, and reflective memory for revising past experiences.

The work presented by Wang et al. bridges these distinct areas by addressing a critical limitation in existing LLM-based design systems and traditional evolutionary computation: the lack of transferable, explicit knowledge acquisition from search traces. While previous work has focused on making optimizers or representations stronger, or on using LLMs for generating proposals, this paper investigates how a language agent can *learn what to remember* from expensive physical evaluations and convert that experience into reusable, auditable design principles. It deviates from implicit memory (like a population of elites) or raw logs by proposing a structured, abstract, and evidence-grounded natural-language skill library, which can then be explicitly used to guide future design tasks, even across different scales. This positions AUTO-ROBOTIST as a contribution towards making LLM agents more effective in scientific and engineering domains that involve iterative experimentation and costly evaluations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether a language agent can transition from raw robot-design search experience to the acquisition of transferable, explicit robot-design skills. This objective is driven by a fundamental inefficiency in current automated design paradigms.

The core motivation stems from the observation that traditional morphology search, whether using classical evolutionary algorithms or recent LLM-driven proposal generators, largely operates as a "memoryless" process. Each evaluated morphology, representing an expensive experiment about support, contact, actuation, stability, and failure, is typically used only to inform the next population generation or to identify a single best artifact. The rich evidence underlying these trials—why certain designs succeeded, why others failed, and what structural principles govern performance—is effectively discarded. This stands in contrast to human designers, who, after sufficient trials, abstract isolated blueprints into general principles, such as effective load-bearing frames or common failure modes.

The authors highlight the severity of this "forgetting" in combinatorial design spaces. For instance, a 10x10 robot in EVOGYM, with 5 component choices per cell, presents a design space of 5^100 possibilities, a number far exceeding the capacity for exhaustive search. In such vast spaces, losing insights about successful and unsuccessful designs represents a significant waste of computational and time resources.

Therefore, the research is motivated by the need for a more epistemically efficient design process. A useful design memory, as conceptualized by the authors, must possess specific attributes:
- **Abstraction for Transfer**: It must be abstract enough to generalize and transfer across varying resolutions (e.g., 5x5 to 10x10 grid sizes) and different tasks. This implies a focus on relational rules rather than absolute coordinates or pixel-level details.
- **Evidence Grounding**: The memory must be empirically tied to actual evaluated bodies and observed fitness changes, ensuring that the learned principles are not speculative but simulator-validated.
- **Editability and Revisability**: The learned knowledge should not be static but capable of being updated, refined, or contradicted as new evidence emerges from subsequent evaluations.

By framing memory as a "revisable design theory," the authors aim to move beyond LLMs merely generating better proposals in a single search run. Instead, they seek to develop an LLM agent that actively learns, distills, and accumulates auditable design principles that can genuinely improve future design efforts by providing a foundational knowledge base, thereby converting expensive search traces into lasting, transferable skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AUTO-ROBOTIST is presented as a self-evolving language agent that systematically converts robot morphology search traces into a transferable skill library. The methodology integrates LLM-driven proposal generation with continuous library maintenance, all grounded in simulator feedback.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The research focuses on morphology search for modular soft robots within the EVOGYM benchmark. A robot body is defined as an *n* x *n* matrix, where each cell contains one of five voxel types (empty, rigid connector, soft connector, horizontal actuator, vertical actuator). A feasible body must be a single connected component and contain at least one actuator. The objective for a given task *T* is to find the body *d* that maximizes fitness *f(d, T)* after training a PPO controller, under a fixed budget *B* of body evaluations. The key departure is to treat each evaluation not just as a fitness query, but as evidence for building a mutable skill library *S_t*.

#### 3.2. Core AUTO-ROBOTIST Loop

The agent operates in generations, maintaining an evaluated population *P_t* and a skill library *S_t*. Each generation involves:
1. **Skill-Conditioned Proposal**:
 - The agent retrieves skills from *S_t* relevant to the current task and scale.
 - Skills are scored by a smoothed usefulness estimate (*w_s*), calculated from the parent-relative fitness gains of attributed observations. Skills with consistent positive gains are weighted higher.
 - A skill is sampled based on these weights. The LLM then receives the task description, scale, an elite parent body, the sampled skill's archetype (L1), and its relevant rules (L2).
 - The LLM generates proposals by editing the parent body towards the retrieved skill, following positive rules and avoiding negative ones.
 - In parallel, a portion of new designs are generated via a Genetic Algorithm (GA) mutation path to ensure exploration and act as a fallback for invalid LLM outputs.
2. **Validation and Evaluation**:
 - Proposed child bodies undergo validity checks (legal voxels, connectivity, actuator presence). Invalid outputs are repaired or replaced by GA mutations.
 - Valid children are evaluated in the EVOGYM simulator with PPO-trained controllers to obtain fitness and parent-relative gain.
3. **Library Maintenance**: After evaluation, the simulator evidence is used to update the skill library through three operations:
 - **ADD**: Observations that do not match existing skills are collected. Periodically, the LLM analyzes this "unassigned evidence pool" to identify recurring structural patterns and create new L1 archetypes, preventing one-skill-per-anecdote growth.
 - **DIAGNOSE**: For skills receiving new evidence, the LLM updates existing L2 rules or proposes new ones. If a child targeted a specific rule, its gain updates that rule's running mean. Rule-free observations are analyzed by the LLM to decide if they support, contradict, or form new positive/negative rules. Negative rules are considered crucial for capturing failure modes.
 - **MERGE**: The agent clusters skills based on L1 identity and task family. Redundant skills describing the same structural principle are consolidated, preserving rules and observations from absorbed skills to maintain the audit trail.

#### 3.3. Skill Representation

A skill *s* in the library *S* is a three-level hierarchical record:
- **L1 (Archetype)**: A compact natural-language structural concept (e.g., "portal-frame") with an applicability condition. This provides the primary retrieval mechanism.
- **L2 (Rules)**: A set of natural-language rules, categorized as positive (tend to improve fitness) or negative (describe failure modes). Each rule includes a structural description, supporting evidence, and a running mean of parent-relative fitness gain. These rules use morphology-level relations (e.g., "support a central actuator column") rather than absolute coordinates.
- **L3 (Observations)**: Raw records of evaluated child and parent bodies, task, scale, fitness, gain, validity, and proposal attribution. This level provides the empirical grounding for L1 and L2.

This structure is designed for both stability (L1 for retrieval) and accountability (L3 for auditability), allowing the LLM's explicit domain memory to evolve while its parameters remain fixed.

#### 3.4. Cross-Scale Transfer

To facilitate transfer (e.g., from 5x5 to 10x10), the target run imports the source skill library. Imported skills retain their L1 archetypes and L2 rules but discard L3 observations (raw source-scale data). This allows them to act as priors, guiding initial exploration in the new scale. As new observations are generated, they contribute to the unassigned evidence pool, enabling the formation of native target-scale skills. This approach emphasizes the transfer of relational principles, as a 5x5 rule like "rigid side frame for vertical actuators" can guide a 10x10 design without direct voxel upsampling.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

GPT-5.5 serves as the LLM backbone for all generative and reflective operations (PROPOSE, ADD, DIAGNOSE, MERGE) with a temperature of 1.0. Each generation comprises 25 designs: 15 from skill-conditioned LLM proposals and 10 from GA mutation. The LLM mutation range is controlled (1-3 voxels for 5x5, 1-10 for 10x10). A fixed protocol for PPO controller training (5.12x10^5 environment steps) is used for all morphology evaluations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across seven EVOGYM tasks (Walker, BridgeWalker, Balancer, Carrier, Climber, Jumper, Pusher) to assess the agent's ability to discover skills in cold-start scenarios and transfer them across design scales.

#### 4.1. Cold-Start Discovery (5x5 Search)

In cold-start 5x5 search, where the skill library begins empty, AUTO-ROBOTIST demonstrated performance comparable to or exceeding the Genetic Algorithm (GA) baseline across all seven tasks (Table 1, Figure 4 top row).
- **Overall Performance**: AUTO-ROBOTIST achieved a 1.47x average convergence speedup and maintained a 70% average lead fraction over the GA. This indicates that the agent reaches GA's final fitness faster and leads for a significant portion of the search budget.
- **Task-Specific Gains**: The most pronounced endpoint fitness gains for AUTO-ROBOTIST were observed in tasks requiring complex interactions, such as Carrier (+0.74), Jumper (+0.94), and Pusher (+1.42). These tasks often involve persistent contact, specific load paths, or coordinated impulses, where precise structural principles are crucial.
- **Search Trajectory Improvement**: While Climber showed a tied endpoint fitness, AUTO-ROBOTIST led for 86% of the budget, suggesting an improved search trajectory, particularly in early exploration phases.
- **Skill Formation Evidence**: A detailed trace of a Carrier run (Figure 3) illustrated how the agent progressively formed specific structural hypotheses (e.g., "completing a lower active base," "strengthening central webbing") from evaluation evidence, transforming a weak random seed into a high-performing, load-bearing robot. This visually confirmed that the agent distills reusable rules during the cold-start process.

#### 4.2. Cross-Scale Transfer (5x5 → 10x10 Transfer)

The study then evaluated the transferability of skills learned at the 5x5 scale to a larger 10x10 design space (Table 2, Figure 4 bottom row).
- **Superiority over GA**: Reference-conditioned AUTO-ROBOTIST consistently outperformed the GA baseline on all seven 10x10 tasks, achieving an average endpoint fitness improvement of +1.55. It maintained a lead fraction of at least 90% on six tasks.
- **Significant Transfer Gains**: The largest transfer gain was observed in Carrier (+5.71), followed by Walker and BridgeWalker, indicating that principles related to stable frames, leg structures, and compliant contact pads generalize effectively across scales. Pusher was the only task where AUTO-ROBOTIST's speedup (S=0.97) was slightly below GA, suggesting a quality-over-earliness trade-off.
- **Behavioral Differences in Rollouts**: Qualitative analysis of rollouts (Figure 5) revealed that GA-designed elites often contained sufficient material but struggled with maintaining task-critical relations (e.g., contact breakage, support bending). In contrast, AUTO-ROBOTIST's designs more frequently preserved functional motifs, leading to more effective locomotion, higher jumps, or greater object displacement.
- **Rule Transfer vs. Copying**:
 - **Ineffectiveness of Direct Upsampling**: A negative control demonstrated that simple 2x2 voxel tiling (upsampling) of 5x5 elites to 10x10 designs often destroyed functional performance, especially for tasks like Carrier and Pusher (Table 2, Figure 6). This highlights that merely scaling geometry is insufficient.
 - **Isolation of Skill Transfer**: The "skill-only" transfer variant (without providing the 5x5 reference body to the LLM) still outperformed GA on Walker, Carrier, and Jumper. This finding indicates that the learned library encodes functional, relational information beyond the visual appearance of a specific 5x5 elite.
 - **Optimal Transfer**: The best transfer performance was achieved when both the learned skills and a 5x5 reference design were provided, suggesting that skills define *what* relations should hold, while the reference can suggest *where* to instantiate them.

#### 4.3. Anatomy of a Learned Skill Library (Walker Example)

Inspection of the Walker 5x5 library (Figure 7) showed that the memory stabilized into five distinct L1 archetypes (e.g., "lower-bridge," "portal-frame," "twin-spine-frame") and accumulated 12 positive and 17 negative L2 rules. The sampling weights remained distributed, implying that the MERGE operation effectively reduced redundancy without collapsing the library into a single template. This qualitative analysis confirmed that the library stores abstract, relational principles (e.g., support paths, recurring traps) that can be re-instantiated at different scales.

#### 4.4. Component Ablations on Pusher

An ablation study on the Pusher task (Figure 8) investigated the contribution of different AUTO-ROBOTIST mechanisms:
- **GA Mutation (A3 Pure LLM)**: Removing GA mutation resulted in a 28.6% fitness drop, indicating that GA provides valuable structural exploration that the skill-conditioned LLM proposals alone do not fully cover.
- **L2 Rules and L3 Observations (A4 No L2+L3)**: Disabling access to L2 rules and L3 observations led to a 23.0% fitness drop, confirming that these levels are crucial for sharpening L1 archetypes into actionable guidance and grounding LLM interpretations in concrete evidence.
- **Library Maintenance (A1 No Diagnose, A2 No Merge)**: Skipping DIAGNOSE (no L2 rule formation) or MERGE (allowing L1 duplicates) resulted in 17.8% and 16.4% fitness drops, respectively. This highlights the material contribution of active library maintenance to performance, especially in long-horizon tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the domain of automated robot design and the application of Large Language Models in scientific and engineering contexts.

**Methodological Innovation**: The central contribution is the reframing of LLM-based robot design from merely generating high-fitness bodies to actively converting costly search experience into transferable design knowledge. The introduction of AUTO-ROBOTIST, a self-evolving LLM agent that constructs and maintains an explicit, natural-language skill library, represents a novel methodology. This library, structured with archetypes, evidence-grounded rules, and supporting observations, makes the design memory inspectable and auditable, moving beyond implicit knowledge embedded in populations or opaque model parameters.

**Improved Search Efficiency and Transferability**: The empirical results demonstrate that this methodology offers practical benefits:
- **Cold-Start Performance**: AUTO-ROBOTIST significantly improves convergence speed and lead fraction in cold-start 5x5 searches, showing that useful design knowledge can be acquired and leveraged within a single design run. This reduces the time and resources required to find effective designs.
- **Cross-Scale Transfer**: The ability to transfer learned skills from 5x5 to 10x10 design spaces, outperforming traditional genetic algorithms on all tested tasks, highlights the power of abstract, relational design principles. This suggests that expensive evaluations performed in a smaller, more constrained design space can yield reusable knowledge for larger, more complex problems. This transfer is shown to be driven by the understanding of morphological relations rather than simple visual imitation or direct upsampling, which often fails.

**Broader Impact on LLM Agents in Science/Engineering**: AUTO-ROBOTIST suggests a paradigm shift for how LLM agents can be deployed in domains involving expensive physical or simulated experimentation. Instead of merely acting as intelligent proposal generators, these agents can be designed to learn *what to remember* from their interactions, distill complex outcomes into auditable principles, and continuously refine their domain knowledge. This capacity to convert ephemeral evaluation data into persistent, reusable, and inspectable design principles has potential implications for accelerating discovery and optimization in various scientific and engineering fields beyond robotics, such as materials science, drug discovery, and architecture, where iterative design and costly evaluations are common.

**Auditability and Explainability**: By explicitly storing design rules with supporting evidence, the system enhances the auditability of LLM-assisted design decisions. Users can inspect which evaluations led to a particular rule or archetype, fostering greater trust and understanding in automated design processes, which is crucial for real-world deployment.

**Limitations and Future Directions**: While significant, the work acknowledges limitations, including its focus on 2D voxel morphology in a simulated environment with a fixed component alphabet and PPO evaluation protocol. The skills are simulator-grounded and may not directly translate to 3D robots, hardware deployment, or environments with unmodeled dynamics without further validation. However, these limitations frame important future research directions, such as extending the framework to 3D and hardware-facing morphologies, developing explicit rule-conflict resolution mechanisms, and integrating uncertainty-aware skill scoring. The core thesis that expensive physical evaluations should yield grounded, reusable abstractions remains relevant and impactful for advancing autonomous agents in research and development.
