# Zeva: In-Context Causal Learning for Generalizable Embodied Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Zeva enables a frozen embodied policy to learn in-context from its own physical interactions. A Causal Transition Encoder turns each executed action and its induced visual state change into a phase token plus causal interaction signal, stored in dual-timescale memory (short-window trace plus persistent cross-attempt memory) and retrieved as a causal prompt. Success rises across repeated attempts without any gradient updates.

## Problem

- Pretraining alone cannot cover real-world physical diversity: novel geometries, contact dynamics, and execution errors make similar actions yield very different outcomes at deployment.
- Test-time training methods convert deployment history into fast weights or memory via gradient updates, leaving action-to-state-change structure implicit and adapting far slower than a single attempt.
- In-context robot learners condition on demonstrations for task generalization, and memory-augmented policies store perceptual or semantic history, neither preserving action-effect causality across attempts.

## Method

- ==Causal Interaction Extraction==: a gated-recurrent ==Causal Transition Encoder== fuses visual latents, action encodings, and observed effects into a state projected into a ==phase token== and ==causal interaction signal==, trained with ==effect-prediction==, ==task-contrastive==, and ==phase-progression losses==.
- ==Dual-timescale Causal Memory==: a ==Brief Interaction Trace== holds a sliding window of recent signals reset per attempt, while ==Persistent Interaction Memory== accumulates units across attempts using ==similarity-based merging== over both phase and dynamics.
- ==In-Context Policy Injection==: phase-conditioned top-K retrieval builds a ==Causal Prompt== from task token, phase token, and projected memory streams, conditioning the diffusion path's full-attention blocks of the frozen policy.

## Results

- Best average success on RoboCasa365-Atomic5 at **76.8%**, exceeding Fast-WAM by **4.4** points, and best real-world ChemLab-Evo averages at all three levels (**83.3%**, **70.0%**, **7.5%**), with long-horizon process score **57.32** versus **44.73**.
- Cumulative success on Atomic5 scales from **26%** to **45%** to **70%** and plateaus at **73%** over four evolution milestones; ChemLab-Evo atomic tasks rise similarly, for instance **30%** to **80%** on Pour Water.
- Ablations: removing persistent memory costs **10-20** points, removing the brief trace **15-30** points, both **35-45** points; nearest cross-task signal substitution retains **95%** and **80%** success versus **55%** and **45%** for random signals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Storing the action-to-state-change relation, not just perceptual history, is what lets memory correct behavior across attempt boundaries where observation-side history alone cannot.
> - Splitting memory by timescale matters: within-attempt context proved more load-bearing than cross-attempt memory, yet the two contribute complementarily and are weakest when both are absent.
> - Causal interaction signals cluster by physical effect rather than object or viewpoint, so experience transfers across tasks; gains still plateau since experience comes only from task-driven attempts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI aims to develop intelligent agents capable of performing complex manipulation tasks in physical environments. A significant challenge in this domain is achieving generalizable manipulation, particularly when robots encounter novel physical conditions not present during pretraining. Existing approaches, such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World-Action Models (WAMs), have advanced robot control by leveraging large-scale pretraining datasets. These models, including OpenVLA, $\pi^0$, RT-1, RT-2, and Octo, typically learn observation-to-action mappings directly from demonstrations or absorb physical priors from large-scale video data. While effective in controlled settings, their performance often degrades during real-world deployment due to unforeseen variations in object properties, contact dynamics, visual cues, and execution errors. The capability ceiling of these models is largely determined by their pretraining corpus, and their fixed weights after deployment limit their adaptability.

A separate line of research focuses on memory-augmented robot policies to handle long-horizon dependencies. Examples include MemoryVLA, MEM, DIM-WAM, and MemoryWAM. These methods typically store observational or semantic-level historical fragments. However, they often do not explicitly capture the action-state-change relationship crucial for adjusting actions in response to failed attempts.

Another relevant area is deployment-time adaptation and experience-driven self-improvement. Approaches like $\pi^{*}_{0.6}$ (RECAP) and VLA-RL update policies through reinforcement learning, but these gradient-based optimizations are generally too slow for adaptation between consecutive attempts and can be susceptible to capability collapse. Faster adaptation methods, such as RoboTTT and WAM-TTT, update lightweight fast weights or memory modules using test-time gradients. However, their adaptation latency may still be too long for individual attempts, and WAM-TTT specifically relies on human demonstration videos for updates, limiting autonomous exploration. In-context robot learners like GEN-1.5 and Skild S1 maintain fixed model weights but infer new tasks from a few demonstrations, primarily targeting task generalization rather than on-the-fly interaction learning.

Zeva distinguishes itself by proposing "In-Context Causal Learning (ICCL)" where a *frozen* policy learns directly from its own physical interaction experiences in real-time, without requiring any gradient updates or parameter modifications. This approach addresses the limitations of fixed-weight foundation models and slow adaptation methods by extracting explicit causal relationships between actions and state changes, storing this knowledge in a dual-timescale memory, and injecting it as context into the policy. This allows for continuous self-evolution and adaptation to dynamic real-world conditions during deployment, effectively extending the policy's capabilities beyond its pretraining.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable generalizable embodied manipulation by allowing robots to learn from their own physical interactions on the fly during real-world deployment. The motivation stems from the observed brittleness of existing embodied foundation models when deployed in physical environments. Pretraining alone, even with large datasets, cannot fully account for the vast diversity of physical conditions a robot will encounter, such as novel object geometries, varying contact dynamics, visual occlusions, and execution inaccuracies. These discrepancies cause similar actions to yield significantly different outcomes, hindering reliable performance.

The authors argue that for robust generalization, robots must explicitly learn the causal relationships between their actions and the resulting changes in the environment's state. This learned causality should then inform subsequent actions, allowing the robot to adapt to previously unseen conditions. Unlike prior adaptation methods that involve gradient-based parameter updates, Zeva aims for a system where the core policy model remains frozen throughout deployment. This design choice is motivated by the desire for fast, stable adaptation that avoids the computational cost and potential instability of test-time training, while also distinguishing it from approaches that rely solely on external demonstrations for in-context learning.

Specifically, the key objectives are:
- **Formulate In-Context Causal Learning (ICCL):** To define a framework where a frozen robot policy can learn from its own physical interaction experiences without test-time weight updates, specifically by inferring the causal structure of action-induced state changes within a single episode.
- **Develop a gradient-free self-evolution mechanism:** To design a system that continuously improves the robot's success rate as it accumulates interaction experience, without modifying the underlying policy parameters.
- **Enable on-the-fly adaptation:** To create a mechanism that allows the policy to adapt to diverse real-world physical conditions across repeated self-attempts within an episode.
- **Facilitate cross-task knowledge transfer:** To demonstrate that the acquired interaction experience can generalize and be applied across different manipulation tasks.
- **Achieve competitive performance:** To demonstrate that this approach can achieve or exceed the performance of leading VLA and WAM models in both simulation and real-world manipulation benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Zeva implements In-Context Causal Learning (ICCL) through three main components: Causal Interaction Extraction, Dual-timescale Causal Memory, and In-Context Policy Injection. The overall framework operates with a frozen foundation policy, augmenting its capabilities through an external, evolving memory system. The foundation policy itself leverages a two-path architecture, including an autoregressive path for multimodal context encoding and a diffusion path for continuous action generation, similar to Cosmos3's rectified-flow action-generation head.

**A. Problem Formulation: In-Context Causal Learning (ICCL)**
ICCL is framed as an objective to maximize success rate on unseen tasks by minimizing the discrepancy between predicted and actual causal effects. The policy $\pi$ uses a causal context $M_{r-1}$ derived from previous interactions to guide action generation. A "causal interaction unit" $\xi_\tau$ is defined as a function $\phi(s_\tau, u_\tau, \Delta s_\tau)$ that encodes the current state, executed action, and resulting state change. This context $M$ acts as a "causal prompt" to guide the frozen policy, enabling implicit system identification and adaptation to novel physical dynamics.

**B. Part 1: Causal Interaction Extraction**
The Causal Transition Encoder (CTE) is responsible for converting raw interactions into a latent causal space.
- **Interaction Recurrence:** At each timestep $\tau$, visual latents $s_\tau$, action encodings $u_\tau$, and observed effects $d_\tau$ are integrated into a Causal Interaction State $B_\tau$ using a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). This state is initialized with task instruction $g$ and initial state $s_0$.
- **Projection:** From $B_\tau$, two components are projected: a Phase Token $p_\tau$, representing task progress, and a Causal Interaction Signal $e_\tau$, characterizing environmental dynamics. Together, $(p_\tau, e_\tau)$ form the causal interaction unit $\xi_\tau$.
- **Learning Objectives:** The CTE is optimized with a multi-objective loss to ensure $e_\tau$ captures physical causality:
 - **Causal Effect Prediction ($L_{effect}$):** Decodes $e_\tau$ and candidate actions to predict visual state changes, grounding the signal in physical effects.
 - **Task Identity Clustering ($L_{task}$):** A supervised contrastive loss that clusters episodes of the same task, fostering a task-consistent interaction space.
 - **Phase Progression ($L_{phase}$):** Ensures $p_\tau$ monotonically captures task execution progress.

**C. Part 2: Dual-timescale Causal Memory**
Zeva employs two memory streams to organize the extracted causal interaction units $(p_\tau, e_\tau)$, supporting adaptation within and across attempts of an episode.
- **Brief Interaction Trace (BIT):** $H^{brief}_\tau$ stores a sliding window of the most recent $K_{brief}$ causal interaction signals $\{e_j\}$. This provides immediate local context regarding current action efficacy and is reset at the start of each new attempt.
- **Persistent Interaction Memory (PIM):** $H^{pim}_\tau$ accumulates causal units $\xi=(p, e)$ across multiple attempts of the *same* episode. To manage scalability, it uses similarity-based merging. For a new unit $\xi_\tau$, the most similar existing entry $\xi^*$ in PIM is found based on a combined similarity score $S_{merge}$ (weighted sum of phase and signal similarity). If $S_{merge}$ exceeds a threshold $\tau_{merge}$, the new unit is merged with $\xi^*$; otherwise, it is added as a new entry. PIM is cleared at the beginning of each new episode.

**D. Part 3: In-Context Policy Injection**
The final stage transforms the stored interaction experiences into a structured causal prompt for the frozen foundation model.
- **Phase-Conditioned Retrieval:** The current phase token $p_\tau$ is used to query PIM, retrieving the top-K most relevant interaction signals $R_\tau$ whose associated phases match the current task progress.
- **Causal Prompt Construction:** A Memory Context Encoder $F_{mem}$ fuses the task token $g$, current phase token $p_\tau$, projected BIT ($P_{proj}(H^{brief}_\tau)$), and projected retrieved PIM signals ($P_{proj}(R_\tau)$) into the final Causal Prompt $M_\tau$.
- **Policy Conditioning:** This $M_\tau$ is injected into the full-attention blocks of the diffusion path within the foundation policy's transformer layers. This allows the frozen policy to adapt its action strategy $\pi(a_\tau | o_\tau, M_\tau)$ based on the historical success or failure captured in the memory, without any parameter updates.

**E. Inference**
During real-world deployment, all neural network parameters of the CTE, Memory Context Encoder, and foundation policy remain frozen. The robot updates its BIT and PIM online based on its interactions. The evolving causal prompt $M_\tau$ dynamically guides the frozen foundation policy's decision-making.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Zeva's performance was evaluated across both simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, focusing on benchmark success rates, post-deployment in-context scaling, and the utility of its causal memory and cross-task generalization capabilities.

**A. Benchmark Performance:**
- **Real-World ChemLab-Evo:** Zeva achieved the highest average success rate (SR) across all three complexity levels (Atomic, Short-sequence, Complex) of the ChemLab-Evo benchmark using an ARX manipulator. It improved upon the strongest baselines by 6.6, 5.0, and 5.0 percentage points for atomic, short-sequence, and complex tasks, respectively, culminating in an 83.3% average SR for Level 1, 70.0% for Level 2, and 7.5% for Level 3 tasks. For long-horizon tasks, Zeva also achieved the highest process scores, with 47.50% on Balance Weighing and 67.14% on Extraction, surpassing baselines by 8.75 and 8.57 points, respectively.
- **Simulation RoboCasa365-Atomic5:** Zeva achieved a macro-average success rate of 76.8%, outperforming Fast-WAM by 4.4 percentage points and other VLAs/WAMs like LingBot-VA, Xiaomi-Robotics-1, $\tau_0$-WM, and Cosmos3-Nano.

**B. Ablation Studies:**
Ablations on five ChemLab-Evo tasks demonstrated the complementary roles of the dual-timescale memory components.
- Removing the Persistent Interaction Memory (PIM) led to a 10-20 percentage point reduction in success rate.
- Removing the Brief Interaction Trace (BIT) resulted in a larger decrease of 15-30 percentage points, with notable drops on Pour Water and Titration.
- Disabling both BIT and PIM yielded the weakest performance on every task, indicating that both components are crucial for Zeva's performance.

**C. Post-Deployment In-Context Scaling:**
- **Self-Evolution:** Zeva demonstrated a consistent increase in cumulative success rate (CSR@K) as it accumulated interaction experience over repeated attempts on fixed episodes.
 - On RoboCasa365-Atomic5, the pooled CSR rose from 26% at Evolve 1 to 73% by Evolve 4 across 100 task-configuration pairs.
 - On ChemLab-Evo, CSR increased monotonically for all three atomic tasks (e.g., Pick Up Test Tube from 65% to 100%, Place Beaker from 25% to 70%, Pour Water from 30% to 80%). This indicates a self-improving capability without policy parameter updates.
- **One-Shot Human Demonstration Warm-Up:** A single human teleoperation demonstration could "warm-start" Zeva's PIM, leading to improved performance.
 - For the Balance Weighing task, human-guided initialization enabled Zeva to successfully reproduce complex stages like approach, grasp, transfer, and placement, whereas a baseline without warm-up failed.
 - Quantitatively, human warm-up improved or matched SR at every evolution milestone on the three atomic ChemLab-Evo tasks, with gains up to 20 percentage points on Place Beaker and 15 points on Pour Water in early stages of self-evolution.

**D. Cross-Task Generalization of Causal Interaction Signals:**
- **Cross-Task Transfer:** Replacing task-local causal interaction signals with functionally similar signals retrieved from other tasks (nearest cross-task match) showed strong generalization. For instance, on Pick Up Test Tube, SR decreased only from 100% to 95%, and on Pour Water, it remained 80%. This significantly outperformed random cross-task signal replacement, which reduced SR to 55% and 45% respectively.
- **Cross-Task Retrieval Analysis:** t-SNE visualization of CTE interaction embeddings across ChemLab-Evo tasks demonstrated that the full CTE produced compact, task-specific clusters with clear separation, whereas removing the effect stream increased within-task dispersion and inter-task mixing. This suggests that the causal interaction signals effectively capture action-induced state changes independent of specific task appearance, enabling retrieval of functionally equivalent physical effects across different objects, viewpoints, and instructions (e.g., pouring transitions retrieving other container-tilting actions).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Zeva represents a development in the field of embodied AI by introducing In-Context Causal Learning (ICCL) as a mechanism for generalizable embodied manipulation. Its core contribution is enabling a frozen robot policy to learn and adapt from its own physical interaction experiences *without gradient updates* during real-world deployment. This addresses a critical limitation of existing embodied foundation models, which often struggle with unseen physical conditions due to their fixed pretraining knowledge.

The significance of Zeva's approach lies in several aspects:
- **Enhanced Generalizability and Robustness:** By learning explicit causal relationships between actions and state changes and storing them in a dual-timescale memory (BIT for immediate context, PIM for persistent cross-attempt knowledge), Zeva allows robots to adapt dynamically to diverse and unpredictable real-world environments. This enhances the generalizability of policies to novel conditions and improves robustness against execution errors or unforeseen object properties.
- **Self-Evolution Capability:** The demonstration of consistent success rate improvement over repeated attempts (self-evolution) without parameter updates indicates a scalable pathway for robots to autonomously refine their skills and overcome initial failures by accumulating experience. This contrasts with slower, gradient-based adaptation methods and moves towards more autonomous learning in deployment.
- **Gradient-Free Adaptation:** The ability to perform adaptation without modifying model parameters offers benefits in terms of computational efficiency, stability (avoiding catastrophic forgetting or unstable updates), and practical deployment, as it eliminates the need for on-device retraining or complex gradient computation.
- **Cross-Task Knowledge Transfer:** The evidence of causal interaction signals generalizing across tasks suggests that Zeva can build a reusable library of physical insights. This enables a robot to leverage experience from one task to inform its behavior in functionally similar situations in other, potentially novel, tasks, facilitating more efficient learning and reducing the need for extensive task-specific demonstrations.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** The successful one-shot warm-up from human demonstrations highlights Zeva's potential for intuitive human-robot interaction. A single human input can rapidly prime the robot's memory, allowing it to quickly grasp a new skill or adapt to specific conditions before continuing autonomous self-improvement.

Potential impacts include:
- **Broader Real-World Applications:** Zeva's capabilities could accelerate the deployment of robots in less structured and more dynamic environments, such as smart factories, homes, or chemical laboratories, where adaptability to unforeseen conditions is crucial.
- **Reduced Training Costs:** By enabling on-the-fly learning from interaction, Zeva may reduce the need for extremely large and diverse pretraining datasets that aim to cover every possible scenario, as robots can learn during deployment.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a basis for future work on causality-driven exploration, where robots actively seek out informative interactions to reduce uncertainty and acquire experience more efficiently, moving beyond passive learning from task execution attempts. This could lead to more intelligent and proactive learning agents.
