# Zero-WAM: In-Context World-Action Modeling from Human Videos for Open-Ended Task Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> Zero-WAM is a causal video-action model that executes unseen manipulation tasks by conditioning on an in-context human demonstration video instead of language. An automatic pipeline converts task-sampled robot trajectories into semantically matched human videos (HumanGen: 74.2K pairs, 8.6K tasks), and an in-context future chunk prediction loss blocks shortcut learning. It reaches 47.0% average success on seven unseen RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, 29.5 points over the strongest video-action baseline.

## Problem

- Prior VLA and video-action policies specify tasks through language, which underspecifies spatial constraints, intermediate states and temporal structure, and gives no visual evidence of intended scene evolution.
- Task-rich paired human-robot data is scarce and manually collected; existing paired datasets span at most a few hundred task categories on one or few embodiments.
- Policies trained on seen tasks learn shortcuts, predicting the next chunk from robot history and text alone, so in-context video guidance gets ignored at test time.

## Method

- In-context human video generation pipeline: a VLM parses task semantics, an image editor synthesizes the first human frame, a video model generates the demo, VLM scoring filters quality, forming **HumanGen**.
- **Zero-WAM**, a ==Mixture-of-Transformers== causal policy from Wan-2.2-TI2V-5B: human video prepended as prefix memory with a height-axis ==RoPE offset==; action Transformer decodes inverse dynamics from the predicted next robot video chunk via ==flow matching==.
- ==In-Context Future Chunk Prediction (IFP)== supervises four strided future video chunks from fused current-representation features only, dropped at inference; plus task-balanced resampling of public corpora into over 6,000 tasks.

## Results

- On seven unseen RoboTwin 2.0 tasks Zero-WAM averaged **46.95%** success against **17.45%** for LingBot-VA and **10.98%** for WAN-Action, winning on every individual task.
- Ablations: removing the future-chunk objective cut the average from **46.95%** to **28.55%**; the text-only task-balanced variant still hit **39.44%**, beating LingBot-VA by **21.99** points.
- Real bimanual Franka trials: **53.3%** versus **43.3%** on container placement, **33.3%** versus **10.0%** on three-object sequencing, **16.7%** versus **0.0%** on two-table-leg insertion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Demonstration video is a richer task interface than language: visual state changes convey spatial targets and ordering constraints that text descriptions specify only awkwardly.
> - Auxiliary long-horizon prediction losses can force a model to actually use its context; teacher-forced next-step training otherwise silently learns history-based shortcuts.
> - Rebalancing pretraining corpora by task rather than trajectory count buys large cross-task gains cheaply, though absolute unseen-task success still stays below 50%.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of zero-shot cross-task generalization in robotic manipulation, where a robotic policy must execute tasks not encountered during its training phase. This problem is fundamental for developing general-purpose robots capable of operating in diverse, open-ended environments.

Current approaches in robot learning, particularly vision-language-action (VLA) models and video-action models, largely rely on language as the primary task interface. However, the paper identifies a limitation in language-based task specification for manipulation: language can underspecify crucial details such as spatial constraints, intermediate states, and temporal evolution, and it lacks direct visual evidence of desired scene changes.

Inspired by in-context learning (ICL) in large language models (LLMs), which allows models to solve novel problems by specifying them in the input context without parameter updates, this research proposes adapting a similar paradigm to robotics. The core premise is that human demonstration videos can serve as a natural and rich in-context specification for manipulation tasks, providing direct visual cues about the intended task evolution that language often cannot convey. While human videos offer visual references for task inference, they do not directly provide executable robot actions, necessitating a policy that can translate observed human dynamics into robot behavior across different embodiments.

The paper identifies two main challenges in leveraging human videos at scale for this purpose:
1. **Data Scarcity**: Large-scale, task-rich datasets that pair human video demonstrations with corresponding robot trajectories (including executable actions) are difficult and expensive to collect manually.
2. **Shortcut Learning**: Policies trained on a limited set of seen tasks may learn to rely on local robot history or language instructions, potentially ignoring the in-context human video. This can lead to poor generalization when encountering unseen tasks where the human video is the critical source of information.

Within the broader context of related work, this research builds upon world action models (WAMs), which predict future visual states and executable actions, shifting the generalization challenge from direct action generalization to unseen video generation. Unlike some WAM approaches that require test-time adaptation or additional data (e.g., WAM-TTT, RoboTTT), Zero-WAM aims for direct zero-shot generalization without such requirements. Furthermore, while other research has utilized human video data for learning visual representations or as task prompts, this work differentiates itself by developing a scalable, automatic pipeline for generating semantically matched human-robot paired data, thereby addressing the gap in providing broad task coverage with grounded robot actions for in-context human video instructions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable zero-shot cross-task generalization in robotic manipulation. This means a robot should be able to perform novel manipulation tasks it has never been explicitly trained on, relying solely on information provided at deployment without any model parameter updates or additional robot data collection for the new task.

The motivations and specific objectives driving this work are as follows:

- **Bridging the Gap from LLM In-Context Learning to Robotics:** The success of in-context learning (ICL) in large language models (LLMs) for generalizing to new problems by specifying them in context motivated the authors to apply this paradigm to robotic manipulation. The goal is to move beyond fixed policy parameters for specific tasks towards a more flexible system that can infer and execute new tasks from a deployment-time context.
- **Leveraging Human Videos as Natural Task Specifications:** Recognizing the limitations of language in fully specifying complex manipulation tasks (due to spatial constraints, temporal structures, and the absence of direct visual cues), the authors propose human demonstration videos as a more natural and visually rich form of task instruction. A key motivation is that human videos directly present desired visual state changes and their temporal evolution, providing concrete visual evidence that can guide the robot's understanding of an unseen task.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity for Human-Robot ICL:** A significant barrier to using human videos for robot learning is the lack of large-scale, task-rich datasets that pair human demonstrations with semantically corresponding robot trajectories that include executable actions. The motivation is to overcome this data bottleneck by developing an automatic and scalable method for generating such paired data, rather than relying on costly and time-consuming manual collection. This led to the objective of creating the **HumanGen** dataset.
- **Counteracting Shortcut Learning in Policy Training:** When a policy is trained on a finite set of tasks, it can develop "shortcuts," learning to perform tasks using only local robot history or language instructions, potentially ignoring the human video instruction. This becomes problematic during zero-shot generalization when the human video is critical for specifying an unseen task. The motivation here is to design a training mechanism that compels the policy to utilize the in-context human video effectively. This led to the development of the **In-Context Future Chunk Prediction (IFP)** objective.
- **Developing a Unified, Causal Video-Action Model:** The research aims to develop a single causal policy, **Zero-WAM**, that can flexibly interpret and act upon both language instructions and human video demonstrations as task specifications. This unification seeks to provide a versatile interface for open-ended task generalization.
- **Demonstrating Real-World Generalization:** Beyond simulation, a crucial objective is to validate the effectiveness of Zero-WAM in real-world robotic manipulation. This involves demonstrating its ability to generalize to unseen task configurations involving multi-object scenes, long-horizon manipulation, and fine-grained insertion tasks without requiring new robot data or parameter updates.

In summary, the key motivation is to unlock open-ended task generalization for robots by enabling them to learn new skills from readily available human video demonstrations through an in-context learning paradigm, supported by scalable data generation and robust training techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Zero-WAM is designed as a causal video-action model, building upon the framework of the LingBot-VA series, which itself converts a text/image-to-video generation model (Wan-2.2-TI2V-5B) into a causal video-action policy. The model's core function is to autoregressively predict future robot video chunks and their corresponding executable action chunks. This joint prediction is factorized into an initial video prediction followed by an action decoding step. The architecture employs a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) design, where a video Transformer and an action Transformer have separate parameters but interact via shared attention layers. Both video and action generation utilize a flow matching objective, learning a velocity field between a clean observation and Gaussian noise.

The methodology of Zero-WAM is characterized by three main components: data curation, human video as task specification, and the in-context future chunk prediction (IFP) objective.

#### 3.1. Data Curation

Addressing the scarcity of task-rich human-robot paired data, the authors developed a two-pronged data curation strategy:

- **Task-Diverse Video-Action (VA) Data:** This dataset is curated from existing public robotic VA pre-training datasets (AgiBot, InternData-A1, Open-X-Embodiment, RoboCOIN, RoboMIND). To overcome the issue of training data being dominated by repeated tele-operation trajectories from a few tasks, each source dataset is re-partitioned into distinct tasks (defined by the combination of manipulation action and object). Trajectories are then sampled at the task level, ensuring a balanced distribution of diverse manipulation dynamics. This process yields over 6,000 tasks and approximately 400,000 robot trajectories per training epoch, forming a task-balanced corpus for autoregressive robotic video-action pre-training.

- **In-Context Human Video Generation Pipeline:** This pipeline automatically converts task-sampled robot trajectories into semantically matched human manipulation videos, overcoming the cost of manual collection.
 1. **Task Sampling:** Robot videos with executable action annotations are sampled at the task level from the task-diverse robot pre-training corpus.
 2. **Task Analysis (VLM):** A Vision-Language Model (VLM, e.g., Gemini 3.1 Pro or Qwen3.6-Plus) analyzes the sampled robot video to extract task-level information (task name, initial/final object states, state changes). It also generates an image-editing prompt to transform the first robot video frame.
 3. **Initial Human Observation Generation (Image Editing Model):** An image editing model (e.g., Nano Banana 2 or Qwen-Image-2.0) uses the first robot frame and the VLM-generated prompt to create the initial human observation image. This step intentionally injects visual variations (background, viewpoint, environment style, object instance/placement) while preserving task semantics to enhance robustness.
 4. **Human Video Synthesis (VLM + Video Generation Model):** The VLM processes the edited human observation and extracted object-state information to produce a video-generation prompt describing how human hands should manipulate objects to complete the task. This prompt is then fed to a video generation model (e.g., Wan 2.7 or Kling AI 3.0) to synthesize the human manipulation video.
 5. **Quality Control (VLM):** The VLM evaluates each generated human video for task semantic preservation and physical plausibility.
 6. **ICL Pairing:** Qualified human videos are paired with their original robot trajectories to form human-robot In-Context Learning (ICL) samples.

This pipeline results in the **HumanGen** dataset, a collection of 74.2K human-robot ICL pairs across 8.6K tasks. HumanGen is structured into subsets: Pre-train ICL (External, from public datasets), Pre-train ICL (In-house), Simulation ICL (for RoboTwin tasks), and Real-world ICL (for Franka robot tasks), each with varying degrees of visual alignment emphasis.

#### 3.2. Human Video as Task Specification

For training, human video demonstrations serve as an in-context task specification.
- During training, the human video (`h`) is prepended to the robot trajectory's token sequence, serving as prefix memory.
- The video Transformer predicts the next robot video chunk (`x_{i+1}`) conditioned on the human video, past robot video and action history, and language instruction (`[h, x_≤i], a_≤i, ℓ`).
- The action Transformer predicts the next robot action chunk (`a_{i+1}`) conditioned on the robot history, the *predicted* next robot video chunk, and the language instruction (`x_≤i, a_≤i, x_{i+1}, ℓ`). It does not directly attend to the human video, assuming its task semantics are already encoded in the predicted robot video.
- To prevent confusion between human and robot video representations that share the same VAE encoder and latent space, a **RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) offset** is applied to human video latents along the height axis, effectively placing them outside the coordinate range of robot video latents.

#### 3.3. In-Context Future Chunk Prediction (IFP) Objective

To ensure that Zero-WAM utilizes the human video prompt rather than relying on shortcuts from robot history or language, the IFP objective is introduced as an auxiliary training-only component.
- **Problem:** During teacher-forcing training, the model can often predict the next robot video chunk from recent robot history, circumventing the need to learn from the human video.
- **Solution:** IFP supervises the prediction of multiple strided future robot video chunks (K=4, stride s=2) from the *current robot-video representation* (`ϕ_{i+1}`) produced by the main video Transformer.
- **Mechanism:** K auxiliary IFP modules, each a Transformer layer initialized from the last video Transformer layer, are added. Each module `G_k` predicts its target future chunk `x_{j_k}`.
- **Key Conditioning:** The IFP modules are explicitly *not* directly conditioned on the human video prompt (`h`). Their only access to the human video's task information is indirectly through `ϕ_{i+1}`, which is the fused, multi-layer hidden representation of the current robot video chunk *after* the main video Transformer has processed `h`. This forces the main video branch to encode the long-term task evolution from `h` into the robot-video representation itself.

#### 3.4. Training and Inference

- **Training Data Mixture:** Zero-WAM is trained on a mixture of Task-diverse VA data and HumanGen data, drawn with specific sampling weights. Language instructions can be dropped out with a certain probability, and for ICL samples, the human video latent can also be dropped, along with an increased language dropout rate, to reduce reliance on language when human video is present.
- **Training Objective:** Combines flow-matching losses for video prediction and action prediction, augmented with the IFP loss for HumanGen samples.
- **Inference:** IFP modules are removed. Zero-WAM supports a language-only mode (conditioning on `ℓ`) and an ICL mode (conditioning on `{h, ℓ}` with `ℓ` being optional). In ICL mode, human video tokens are cached as prefix memory, and ICL classifier-free guidance is used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Zero-WAM encompassed both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, focusing on zero-shot cross-task generalization. Ablation studies were also conducted to ascertain the contribution of specific methodological components.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments (RoboTwin 2.0)

- **Setting:** Evaluation was performed on seven unseen bimanual manipulation tasks within the RoboTwin 2.0 simulation environment. These tasks were explicitly held out from the 43 tasks used for post-training, ensuring a true zero-shot cross-task generalization setup. The unseen tasks included diverse challenges such as unseen-object manipulation, articulated-object manipulation, and long-horizon tasks (e.g., "place object on scale," "stamp seal," "open microwave," "stack blocks three").
- **Baselines:** Zero-WAM was compared against two video-action baselines:
 - **WAN-Action:** A direct adaptation of Wan-2.2 (the base model for Zero-WAM) to the MoT causal video-action framework, trained only on the seen RoboTwin tasks with language instructions.
 - **LingBot-VA:** A leading video-action model, which benefits from existing robotic video-action pre-training, also post-trained on the seen RoboTwin tasks with language instructions.
- **Results:**
 - Zero-WAM achieved an average success rate of **46.95%** across the seven unseen tasks.
 - LingBot-VA obtained an average success rate of **17.45%**.
 - WAN-Action achieved an average success rate of **10.98%**.
 - This represents an absolute improvement of **29.50 percentage points** over LingBot-VA and **35.97 percentage points** over WAN-Action.
 - Zero-WAM consistently outperformed both baselines on all seven tasks. For instance, on "open microwave," Zero-WAM achieved 59.00% success compared to LingBot-VA's 29.33%. For "stamp seal," Zero-WAM reached 47.00% versus LingBot-VA's 3.67%.
 - Notably, for the "stack blocks three" task, a challenging long-horizon task, Zero-WAM was the only method to achieve a non-zero success rate (9.00%), while both baselines failed. These results indicate that the integration of human video instructions and task-balanced robotic pre-training significantly enhances a model's capacity for executing unseen task dynamics.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

- **Setting:** Zero-WAM was evaluated on a real bimanual Franka robot across three task families: object-to-container placement, three-object sequential manipulation, and two-table-leg insertion. These tasks involved unseen task configurations, multi-object scenes, long-horizon manipulation, and precision requirements. A small set of seen-task robot demonstrations were used to adapt the policy to the robot's kinematics, but the evaluated configurations were unseen. Zero-WAM operated using human video instructions, while LingBot-VA used detailed textual descriptions.
- **Results:**
 - **Object-to-container placement:** Zero-WAM achieved **53.3%** success, outperforming LingBot-VA at 43.3%.
 - **Three-object sequential manipulation:** For this long-horizon task, Zero-WAM reached **33.3%** success, substantially higher than LingBot-VA's 10.0%.
 - **Two-table-leg insertion:** Zero-WAM achieved **16.7%** success on unseen insertion configurations, while LingBot-VA recorded 0.0% success.
 - Qualitative observations also suggested successful transfer to objects and bases with unseen colors and types, indicating fine-grained task specifications from human video instructions were more reliably followed.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablations were conducted in RoboTwin 2.0 to isolate the impact of different components:

- **Effect of In-Context Human Video Prompts:**
 - A Zero-WAM variant trained without large-scale pre-training but with ICL human videos on seen RoboTwin tasks achieved an average success rate of **36.36%**. This significantly outperformed WAN-Action (10.98%) and LingBot-VA (17.45%), demonstrating that human video ICL provides substantial task information beyond text-only conditioning, even with limited training data. However, this variant still registered 0% on "stack blocks three," indicating that small-scale ICL training alone is insufficient for complex long-horizon tasks.

- **Effect of In-Context Future Chunk Prediction (IFP):**
 - The full Zero-WAM, including IFP, achieved **46.95%** average success. Without IFP (Zero-WAM w/o IFP), the average success rate dropped to **28.55%**.
 - IFP led to notable improvements on tasks like "open microwave" and "stamp seal." Crucially, IFP enabled Zero-WAM to achieve a non-zero success rate of **9.00%** on "stack blocks three," breaking the 0% barrier observed in other variants and highlighting its role in eliciting human-video-following capabilities for long-term task evolution.

- **Effect of Task-Balanced Robotic Data:**
 - A text-only Zero-WAM variant (with human video condition masked during pre-training, isolating the effect of task-balanced data) achieved a **39.44%** seven-task average. This significantly surpassed LingBot-VA's 17.45% average, indicating that the task-level re-partitioning and balanced sampling strategy in Task-diverse VA data curation substantially improve cross-task generalization, even without the explicit human-video conditioning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Zero-WAM as a causal video-action model that makes contributions toward zero-shot cross-task generalization in robotic manipulation. Its significance and potential impact stem from its integrated approach to addressing key challenges in the field.

The primary significance lies in the formulation of zero-shot robotic task generalization as an in-context world-action modeling problem. By demonstrating that a single causal policy can effectively utilize both language instructions and human video demonstrations as task specifications, the work offers a more flexible and comprehensive interface for robot control compared to approaches that rely solely on language. This multi-modal conditioning allows the policy to draw on richer visual cues when textual instructions are insufficient or ambiguous.

A notable impact is the development of a **scalable in-context human video generation pipeline**. This pipeline automatically converts existing robot trajectories into semantically matched human videos, creating the **HumanGen** dataset. This method mitigates the traditional bottleneck of acquiring large-scale, paired human-robot demonstration data, which is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming through manual collection. By enabling the generation of 74.2K human-robot ICL pairs across 8.6K tasks, HumanGen substantially broadens the task coverage available for training, which is crucial for achieving generalization. The strategic inclusion of visual diversity in generated human videos (variations in background, viewpoint, object instances) encourages the model to learn task semantics that are invariant to such changes, enhancing robustness.

The introduction of the **In-Context Future Chunk Prediction (IFP) objective** addresses a critical issue of shortcut learning in robot policies. By compelling the model to extract and encode longer-term task evolution from the in-context human video into its current robot-video representation, IFP ensures that the policy actively utilizes the guidance provided by the human demonstration. This is particularly important for unseen tasks where relying on past robot experience is insufficient.

The experimental results, showing substantial improvements in average success rates across unseen simulated tasks (46.95% for Zero-WAM vs. 17.45% for the strongest baseline) and effective generalization to complex, unseen configurations in real-world scenarios, provide evidence for the practical viability of the proposed system. The ability to perform long-horizon and fine-grained manipulation tasks in the real world without task-specific robot data or parameter updates highlights Zero-WAM's potential for reducing the data requirements for deploying new robot skills.

Beyond its immediate technical contributions, this work has the potential to influence future research directions. It offers a practical framework for bridging the gap between abundant human video data and comparatively scarce robot trajectories. By focusing on semantic alignment rather than exact motion copying, the approach could allow robots to acquire a broad understanding of manipulation tasks from human experience, requiring less robot-specific action supervision. This could accelerate the development of generalist robot policies capable of operating in increasingly complex and unstructured real-world environments, moving beyond stationary tabletop manipulation to areas like mobile manipulation and substantially longer-horizon tasks.
