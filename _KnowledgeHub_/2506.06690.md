# SpikePingpong: Spike Vision-based Fast-Slow Pingpong Robot System

> [!summary] Summary
> Peking University and BAAI developed SpikePingpong, a robot system that integrates spike vision with a "Fast-Slow" architecture and imitation learning for precise table tennis play. The system achieved a 12.34 mm Mean Absolute Error in ball interception, a 92% success rate for 30 cm target zones, and a 78% success rate for 100 sequential returns, outperforming human players and baseline robotic systems.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with high-speed object manipulation tasks, such as table tennis, which require millisecond-level perception and precise control under dynamic uncertainties.
- Existing robotic table tennis systems often rely on simplified physics models, struggle with real-world complexities like ball spin, or face a significant sim-to-real gap.
- Traditional vision systems have limitations like motion blur and latency, hindering accurate real-time ball trajectory prediction and precise striking for strategic placement.

## Method

- A "==Fast-Slow==" system perception framework combines rapid physics-based prediction (System 1) with refined neural error correction using high-frequency spike camera data (System 2) to predict ball trajectories.
- An "Imitation-based Motion Planning And Control Technology (IMPACT)" module learns optimal robot arm striking policies from expert demonstrations, enabling strategic ball placement beyond simple interception.
- The system decomposes the table tennis task into distinct interception and striking phases, utilizing an ABB IRB-120 6-DOF robotic arm and a multi-camera setup for vision.

## Results

- The Fast-Slow system achieved a Mean Absolute Error of **12.34** mm and an RMSE of **13.85** mm for ball hittable position prediction, significantly improving over physics-only (MAE **44.13** mm) and RNN-based methods.
- SpikePingpong demonstrated an average **92%** success rate within 30 cm target zones and **70%** within 20 cm high-precision zones for single-target returns, surpassing human average (**53%** at 30 cm) and baselines.
- The system achieved a **78%** success rate for 100 sequential returns to randomly ordered targets and showed robustness, maintaining **74%** success in out-of-distribution launcher positions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating rapid, physics-based trajectory prediction with a learning-based neural calibrator (Fast-Slow architecture) effectively compensates for real-world physical effects and sensor noise, improving contact precision.
> - Imitation learning allows robots to acquire complex, strategic manipulation skills like targeted ball placement for competitive gameplay, rather than just basic interception.
> - High-frequency spike cameras provide critical, ground-truth data for training the neural error correction system, which then operates as a lightweight predictor without real-time spike feedback during deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Current research in robot learning has made progress in manipulation tasks, predominantly focusing on static or slow-moving objects. These scenarios often involve simpler dynamics and predictable object behaviors. However, real-world environments frequently present dynamic challenges involving high-speed objects that necessitate rapid perception and precise control, such as catching falling items, intercepting projectiles, or navigating crowded spaces. These high-speed tasks demand millisecond-level decision-making and robust handling of dynamic uncertainties.

Table tennis serves as a benchmark for developing such capabilities, embodying Moravec’s paradox by presenting a task that is simple for humans yet profoundly challenging for robotics. This domain requires the integration of high-speed perception, predictive modeling, and precise motor control, encompassing millisecond-scale perception and prediction, precise manipulation under temporal constraints, and real-time strategic planning.

Existing approaches to robotic table tennis can be broadly categorized into control-based and learning-based methods. Control-based approaches typically rely on precise physical modeling and predefined motion planning. While mathematically rigorous and computationally efficient, they often struggle with real-world complexities due to requirements for exact calibration and limited adaptability to varying ball trajectories or disturbances. Learning-based approaches, including reinforcement learning and imitation learning, offer theoretical adaptability but frequently face a "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation perform poorly in physical systems. This gap is particularly evident in table tennis, where subtle factors like ball spin and contact dynamics significantly influence performance. Furthermore, many existing methods depend on expensive, high-precision hardware vision systems that may still contend with the rapid temporal dynamics necessary for accurate trajectory prediction.

This work addresses these limitations by drawing inspiration from Kahneman’s dual-system theory, which posits that fast, intuitive processing (System 1) complements slower, deliberate reasoning (System 2). The research proposes developing more robust perception architectures capable of handling high-speed dynamics while maintaining accuracy. By integrating spike-based vision with imitation learning, the SpikePingpong system aims to enhance robotic capabilities in time-critical manipulation tasks by providing both rapid responses and refined precision without relying solely on high-cost, high-precision conventional vision hardware or complex physical modeling for all scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance robotic capabilities in controlling high-speed objects within dynamic environments, using table tennis as a specific, challenging testbed. The motivation stems from two fundamental challenges inherent in this task:

1. **High-Precision Vision for Trajectory Prediction:** The system must accurately predict ball trajectories under complex dynamics, including air resistance, ball spin, and varying bounce characteristics. This requires a vision system capable of capturing rapid motion with exceptional temporal resolution and processing speed. Traditional vision systems often face limitations such as motion blur and latency, which hinder precise real-time prediction.
2. **Intelligent Control Strategies for Precise Striking:** The robot needs intelligent control strategies to ensure precise ball striking, enabling it to hit the ball accurately to designated target regions on the opponent's side of the table. This involves dynamic motion planning and real-time adjustment of the robot arm's trajectory and paddle orientation.

To address these challenges, the authors are motivated by Kahneman's dual-system theory. This cognitive framework suggests that combining rapid, intuitive processing with slower, more deliberate reasoning could lead to more robust perception architectures. Applied to robotics, this implies a system where initial rapid estimations are subsequently refined by a more analytical, error-correcting mechanism.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives for the SpikePingpong system are:
- To design and implement a comprehensive robotic table tennis system that systematically handles high-speed dynamic manipulation through task-specific decomposition.
- To develop a "Fast-Slow" system perception framework that integrates conventional camera data for rapid preliminary trajectory prediction (System 1) with high-frequency spike camera data for refined trajectory analysis and neural error correction (System 2). This aims to compensate for physical model inaccuracies caused by environmental variables and spin dynamics without relying on complex physical modeling.
- To introduce an "Imitation-based Motion Planning And Control Technology (IMPACT)" module. This module is designed to learn optimal robotic arm striking policies through demonstration-based learning, enabling strategic ball placement beyond simple interception.
- To conduct extensive experimental evaluations to demonstrate superior performance in terms of contact precision, tactical spatial control (both single and sequential tasks), and robustness under out-of-distribution scenarios and human interaction, validating the effectiveness of the integrated approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SpikePingpong system integrates spike-based vision with imitation learning, decomposing the robotic table tennis task into two primary phases: interception and striking.

#### 3.1. Fast-Slow System Ball Interception Framework

This framework is designed for precise trajectory prediction and consists of two interconnected systems:

- **System 1: Rapid Ball Detection and Physics-Based Prediction**
 - **Ball Detection:** Employs YOLOv4-tiny for computational efficiency, achieving detection frequencies up to 150 Hz. The model is pre-trained on public datasets (Roboflow, TT2, Ping Pong Detection) and then fine-tuned with domain-specific data using a two-phase approach (initial training, then weighted fine-tuning on misdetected samples). Detected 2D pixel coordinates from an RGB-D camera (Intel RealSense D455) are transformed into 3D world coordinates using calibrated camera parameters.
 - **Physics-Based Trajectory Prediction:** Uses an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) filter to obtain reliable estimates of the ball's current 3D position (x, y, z) and velocity (vx, vy, vz). This filter combines a ballistic dynamics model (gravity, coefficient of restitution for bounces) with real-time observations, using different mixing constants (α) for different axes (αx=0.15, αy=0.15, αz=0.25). A fixed-length history queue (10 frames) handles initial velocity calculation and data interruptions. These state estimates are input into a physics model to predict the hittable position (x_hit, y_hit, z_hit) and velocity (v_hit_x, v_hit_y, v_hit_z) at a predefined hitting plane. The model accounts for direct trajectories and rebound trajectories by calculating rebound time and post-rebound velocities, and classifies balls as unhittable if they fall outside the robot's operational workspace.

- **System 2: Spike-Oriented Neural Improvement Calibrator**
 - This learning-based layer refines System 1's predictions by compensating for real-world physical effects (e.g., air resistance, ball spin, sensor noise) that simplified physics models do not capture. It learns to predict the systematic discrepancy between System 1's theoretical hittable position and the actual optimal interception position.
 - **Data Collection:** An extensive dataset is generated by systematically recording the ball's 3D position and velocity, System 1's predicted hittable position, and the robotic arm's corresponding joint angles (computed via inverse kinematics) used to position the paddle. A high-frequency spike camera (Spike M1K40-H2-Gen3, 20 kHz) captures images of the actual ball-paddle interaction at contact. The pixel distance between the observed ball position and the paddle center in these images provides the ground-truth spatial deviation for training. A detailed pixel-to-real-world conversion process using paddle dimensions is established.
 - **Network Architecture:** The neural network takes three inputs: historical position vectors (K frames), historical velocity vectors (K frames), and System 1's predicted hittable position. Each modality is processed by dedicated Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). The concatenated features are fed into a Transformer encoder to capture temporal dependencies. A regression head with fully connected layers outputs a 2D deviation vector (Δx, Δz) to correct the hittable position.
 - **Training Objective:** The model is optimized using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function to minimize the difference between the predicted and ground-truth deviation vectors. Once trained, System 2 operates as a lightweight neural predictor without requiring real-time spike camera feedback during deployment.

#### 3.2. IMPACT: Imitation-based Motion Planning And Control Technology

This module enables strategic ball striking for targeted gameplay beyond mere interception.

- **Data Collection:** After the Fast-Slow system ensures precise ball-paddle contact, data for IMPACT is collected. Incoming ball trajectories and optimal hitting positions are estimated by the Fast-Slow system. Inverse kinematics compute the corresponding robot joint configurations. To generate diverse striking behaviors, random angular perturbations are applied to three critical robot joints (Axis 3 (shoulder), Axis 5 (elbow), Axis 6 (wrist)) before executing the stroke. Only successful trials where the ball returns to the opponent's side are retained, recording the perturbed joint angles and resulting landing positions, labeled by specific landing regions (A, B, C, D). An Intel RealSense L515 camera records landing positions for this purpose.
- **Network Architecture:** A Transformer-based neural network processes three input modalities: ball trajectory sequences, robot joint configurations (based on initial interception), and desired landing region specifications (one-hot encoded). MLPs encode each modality into token representations, which are concatenated and processed by a Transformer encoder. The network outputs optimal joint angle adjustments for precise ball placement control.
- **Training Objective:** The model is trained using the MSE loss function, minimizing the difference between predicted and ground-truth joint adjustments.

#### 3.3. System Integration and Implementation Details

The complete system includes:
- **Coordinate System:** Handles spatial transformations between world, camera, robot, and racket coordinates using ArUco marker calibration.
- **Control System:** Operates at multiple frequencies: Fast-Slow system processes trajectory data at 60Hz and converts predictions to joint configurations at 20kHz, while IMPACT operates at 2.4kHz for striking adjustments, with commands transmitted to an EGM controller at 250Hz.
- **Robot System:** An ABB IRB-120 6-DOF robotic arm equipped with a standard table tennis racket.
- **Training Details:** Both System 2 and IMPACT are trained for 2000 epochs using Adam optimizer with cosine annealing. System 2 uses a batch size of 32 with 10 consecutive frames, and IMPACT uses a batch size of 4. Inputs are normalized. Training is performed on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted provide a comprehensive evaluation of the SpikePingpong system's performance across various aspects of table tennis.

**Dataset:** The system was trained on a dataset generated using an automated launcher with randomized ball dynamics. This dataset comprises 1,000 samples of trajectory and ground-truth contact deviation data (synchronously captured via RGB-D and spike cameras) and 2,000 expert return demonstrations mapping ball trajectories to robot joint configurations for targeted placement.

**Baselines:** The system was benchmarked against ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) and Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023), with both baselines optimized using state-based inputs (instead of raw images) and accelerated samplers for fair comparison.

#### 4.1. System Precision and Efficiency

- **Ball Hittable Position Prediction Error:** The Fast-Slow system achieved superior contact precision. System 1 + System 2 (Ours) recorded an Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 12.34 mm and a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 13.85 mm for deviation prediction. This represents a significant improvement compared to System 1 Only (MAE 44.13 mm, RMSE 50.62 mm) and an RNN-based method (MAE 22.80 mm, RMSE 23.73 mm). Visualizations from the spike camera showed minimal spatial separation between the ball and racket centers at contact.
- **Computational Performance:** SpikePingpong demonstrated an average inference time of 0.407 ms, which is notably faster than Diffusion Policy (25.18 ms) and ACT (7.15 ms). This millisecond-level responsiveness is crucial for real-time robotic arm execution.

#### 4.2. Tactical Spatial Control (Single-Target Return Accuracy)

The system was evaluated for its ability to target four distinct regions (A, B, C, D) on the opponent's side of the table at two precision thresholds (30 cm and 20 cm radius).
- SpikePingpong achieved an average success rate of 92% within the 30 cm accuracy zone and 70% within the more challenging 20 cm high-precision zone, with consistent performance across all target regions.
- This performance substantially exceeded the human average (53% at 30 cm) and all baseline methods (Diffusion Policy (w/o vision) achieved 6% at 30 cm; ACT (w/o vision) achieved 19% at 30 cm).

#### 4.3. Tactical Spatial Control (Sequential Target Execution Performance)

To assess sustained strategic gameplay, the system was tasked with 100 consecutive returns to randomly ordered targets across the four regions (25 targets per region), using a 30 cm precision threshold.
- SpikePingpong achieved an overall sequence success rate of 78%.
- This significantly outperformed the human baseline of 45%. The system demonstrated balanced performance across all court regions, even under the stricter 20 cm precision thresholds.

#### 4.4. Robustness and Out-of-Distribution Generalization

- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization (Launcher Relocation):** When the ball launcher was physically moved to unseen off-center positions, fundamentally altering trajectory distributions, SpikePingpong maintained an average success rate of 74% for 30 cm targets (compared to 92% in-distribution). This suggests the policy learned robust underlying models rather than merely memorizing training patterns.
- **Adaptation and Generalization to Human Demonstrations:**
 - After fine-tuning on a small dataset of 100 demonstrations from a single human player (Person A), the system achieved a 47% success rate for 30 cm targets when tested against the same player.
 - In a zero-shot setting against a new, unseen human player (Person B), the fine-tuned model achieved a 31% success rate for 30 cm targets. This indicates a capability to generalize to human-like dynamics without specific retraining for new individuals.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

Ablation experiments confirmed the contribution of the Fast-Slow system architecture.
- For single-target returns, the full SpikePingpong system (Fast-Slow + IMPACT) achieved 92% accuracy, a 25 percentage point improvement over an RNN-based prediction method combined with IMPACT (67%).
- For sequential target execution, SpikePingpong achieved a 78% success rate, compared to 52% for the RNN-based method.
- These improvements are attributed to the Fast-Slow system's ability to account for ball-racket interaction sensitivity, where precise contact positioning significantly influences trajectories.

#### 4.6. Ultra-High Precision Evaluation (Appendix F.1)

- Additional experiments with 10 cm radius targets (1.5% of reachable table area) yielded an average success rate of 31% ± 3% for SpikePingpong, outperforming a previous approach (HYSR, 8%). This further validates the system's precision capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents SpikePingpong as a robotic table tennis system that integrates spike vision with a novel Fast-Slow architecture and an imitation learning-based control module. The research demonstrates several advancements and holds significant potential impact:

1. **Advancement in High-Speed Object Manipulation:** The system effectively addresses the fundamental challenge of controlling high-speed objects in dynamic environments, a critical area where traditional robot learning approaches often struggle. By achieving high precision and responsiveness in table tennis, the work contributes to extending robotic capabilities to tasks requiring rapid, millisecond-level decision-making.

2. **Novel Perception Architecture:** The Fast-Slow system architecture, inspired by Kahneman’s dual-system theory, offers a robust framework for perception. It combines the speed of physics-based prediction (System 1) with the accuracy of neural calibration using high-frequency spike camera data (System 2). This innovative integration enables precise trajectory prediction by correcting for real-world physical effects like air resistance and ball spin, which are often difficult to model explicitly. This approach provides a blueprint for developing robust perception in other time-critical applications.

3. **Enhanced Control for Strategic Task Execution:** The IMPACT (Imitation-based Motion Planning And Control Technology) module allows the robot to learn strategic ball striking behaviors. By leveraging imitation learning from collected demonstrations, the system moves beyond mere interception to executing sophisticated tactical gameplay, enabling precise targeting to specific regions on the opponent's court. This capability is a step towards more intelligent and competitive robotic agents.

4. **Superior Performance and Generalization:** Experimental results demonstrate the system's ability to achieve high success rates (92% for 30 cm zones, 70% for 20 cm precision, and 31% for ultra-high 10 cm precision) and execute complex sequential tasks (78% success rate), significantly outperforming human averages and established baselines. Furthermore, the system exhibits robustness to out-of-distribution trajectories from relocated launchers and promising generalization capabilities when adapting to human-generated trajectories. This indicates that the learned policies are not merely memorizing patterns but capturing generalizable features of dynamics and control.

5. **Broad Applicability of Core Competencies:** The core competencies developed through this research—including high-speed object tracking, precision manipulation, and adaptive control—have direct transferability to various other domains. These include industrial automation (e.g., assembly with fast-moving parts), medical robotics (e.g., high-precision surgical tasks), and aerospace trajectory interception systems (e.g., missile defense or drone interaction).

6. **Ethical and Reproducibility Considerations:** The authors explicitly address ethical considerations, stating that data collection focused solely on ball trajectories and paddle movements without personally identifiable information. They also provide comprehensive details for reproducibility, including hardware specifications, data collection protocols, network architectures, and training procedures, with code planned for public release.

Overall, SpikePingpong advances the state-of-the-art in robotic control for dynamic, high-speed tasks, showcasing the potential of integrating advanced vision systems with cognitive-inspired architectures and imitation learning for practical and impactful applications.
