# MARSHAL: Incentivizing Multi-Agent Reasoning via Self-Play with Strategic LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> MARSHAL is an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework that trains Large Language Models using self-play in strategic games to foster multi-agent reasoning abilities. This approach yields LLM agents with enhanced strategic skills, demonstrating up to a 10% performance gain on benchmarks like AIME within cooperative multi-agent systems.

## Problem

- Applying reinforcement learning to multi-turn, multi-agent LLM scenarios faces challenges with long-horizon credit assignment, where the impact of individual actions is hard to attribute over many steps.
- Heterogeneous agent roles and asymmetric information in multi-agent environments lead to high variance in advantage estimation, destabilizing the training process for LLMs.
- Existing LLM-based multi-agent systems primarily focus on interaction frameworks rather than intrinsically training LLMs for enhanced multi-agent reasoning capabilities.

## Method

- **MARSHAL** is an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework that builds on ==Group-Relative Policy== Optimization (GRPO), leveraging self-play across a diverse set of six competitive and cooperative strategic games.
- It introduces a 'turn-level advantage estimator' that uses a 'sum-then-normalize' approach on Monte Carlo returns to provide stable, fine-grained credit assignment over long interaction horizons.
- The framework incorporates 'agent-specific advantage normalization' by partitioning trajectories based on player roles, enabling independent advantage estimation relative to each role's average outcome, which stabilizes training in heterogeneous environments.

## Results

- MARSHAL agents demonstrate strong strategic ability in training games and generalize effectively to more complex, held-out games (e.g., a generalist model achieved a **28.7%** improvement on Leduc Hold'em and **22.9%** on Simple Hanabi over baselines).
- The acquired skills generalize to downstream multi-agent systems, with the generalist MARSHAL model achieving an average of **3.51%** gain in the competitive MAD framework and up to a **10.00%** gain on AIME in the cooperative AutoGen framework.
- Qualitative analysis shows MARSHAL significantly reduced 'Inter-Agent Misalignment' failures (**11.5%** reduction) in the MAD framework, indicating improved listening and task focus among agents, and the framework successfully scaled to Qwen3-**8B** models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-play is critical for developing robust and generalizable multi-agent policies, as training against fixed opponents leads to overfitting and strategic mode collapse.
> - Both turn-level advantage estimation and agent-specific advantage normalization are essential for effective multi-turn, multi-agent learning, providing stable credit assignment and preventing performance degradation.
> - Training with MARSHAL cultivates emergent multi-agent reasoning patterns in LLMs, including role-aware strategy adaptation and intent recognition (Theory of Mind), which generalize to out-of-domain tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to significant advancements across various domains, including question answering and code generation. While reinforcement learning (RL) has proven effective in enhancing the reasoning abilities of individual LLMs, its application to multi-turn, multi-agent scenarios remains less explored. This area is considered a critical frontier for advancing artificial intelligence, as many real-world applications inherently involve multiple agents interacting over extended horizons, such as negotiation, strategic gameplay, and collaborative development.

Existing LLM-based multi-agent systems, such as AutoGen, CAMEL, MetaGPT, ChatDev, and Multi-Agent Debate (MAD), primarily focus on designing frameworks and workflows for interactions among pre-trained or fixed LLMs. In contrast, this work takes a complementary approach by aiming to *train* LLMs to intrinsically enhance their multi-agent reasoning capabilities, thereby creating more robust foundation models that can be integrated into such frameworks.

Within the field of reinforcement learning for LLMs, most successful applications, including RL from human feedback (RLHF) and RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR), typically address single-turn or single-agent tasks. Extending these methods to multi-turn, multi-agent environments introduces challenges related to long-horizon credit assignment and managing advantage estimation variance across diverse agent roles. Concurrent work, such as MT-GRPO, also addresses turn-level credit assignment, but MARSHAL proposes a distinct "sum-then-normalize" approach to overcome specific limitations related to variable trajectory lengths and the focus on immediate versus long-term rewards.

The concept of self-play, which has enabled superhuman performance in complex games like Go and StarCraft II, has also begun to be applied to LLMs. While some research utilizes self-play for LLM alignment or to improve strategic ability in specific games, papers like SPAG and SPIRAL are particularly relevant as they also train LLMs via self-play for generalization to reasoning tasks. However, these often focus on zero-sum games and primarily evaluate generalization in single-agent contexts. MARSHAL distinguishes itself by considering both cooperative and competitive games and demonstrating generalizable improvements in multi-agent systems, further investigating the necessity of role-specific advantage normalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop LLMs that can effectively cooperate and compete within multi-agent systems (MASs), which is posited as a crucial step toward more advanced artificial intelligence. The motivation stems from two significant challenges encountered when extending reinforcement learning to multi-turn, multi-agent scenarios:

1. **Long-horizon credit assignment**: In multi-turn interactions, a sequence of actions can each yield immediate rewards, but the ultimate outcome often depends on the collective effect of these actions over a long horizon, resulting in sparse final rewards. Accurately attributing the contribution of each individual action in such a sequence is inherently more complex than in standard single-turn settings. This makes it difficult for an agent to learn which specific actions led to success or failure.
2. **Agent-specific advantage estimation**: Multi-agent training environments often feature heterogeneous game roles, asymmetric information, and varying payoff scales. This heterogeneity introduces considerable variance in advantage estimation, which can destabilize the training process and hinder robust policy updates, as a single normalization approach may not be appropriate for all agents or roles.

To address these challenges, the authors introduce MARSHAL, an end-to-end RL framework designed to incentivize multi-agent reasoning through self-play in both cooperative and competitive strategic games. The specific objectives include:
- Developing and integrating novel techniques to enable fine-grained credit assignment and stabilize multi-agent training.
- Evaluating the effectiveness of MARSHAL agents in developing strategic abilities across a diverse range of games.
- Demonstrating that the capabilities acquired through self-play in games can generalize to improve the performance of LLMs when integrated into established multi-agent systems on general reasoning benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MARSHAL is an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework that builds upon Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to enhance multi-agent reasoning in LLMs through self-play.

**Foundation with GRPO and its Limitations:**
GRPO is an RL method that eliminates the need for a critic model by estimating advantages based on the relative reward of responses within a group, thus assigning equal advantage to all tokens in a single-turn sequence. A direct generalization of GRPO to multi-turn, multi-agent scenarios would treat all trajectories from a game as a group, applying the same terminal reward for advantage estimation across all tokens and turns. However, this approach faces limitations in fine-grained credit assignment over long horizons and managing heterogeneous agent roles.

**Key Technical Contributions:**
MARSHAL introduces two modifications to address these limitations:

1. **Turn-level advantage estimator**: To improve credit assignment across long trajectories, MARSHAL deviates from a simple terminal reward approach. Instead of normalizing turn-level rewards turn-by-turn (which can lead to instability due to varying trajectory lengths), it first calculates the standard Monte Carlo return (cumulative reward) from each turn onwards (`R_i_k`). These cumulative returns are then normalized by subtracting their empirical mean across the group (`A_i_k,t = R_i_k - mean(R)`). This method is functionally equivalent to Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) with a discount factor (`γ`) and GAE lambda (`λ`) both set to 1, where the value function is approximated by the empirical mean of batch returns. This "sum-then-normalize" approach is designed to provide a more stable and accurate learning signal for multi-turn decision-making.

2. **Agent-specific advantage normalization**: Recognizing that expected returns can vary significantly based on a player's role in multi-agent games, MARSHAL partitions trajectories into sub-groups based on the player index (role). The turn-level advantage estimator is then applied independently within each player-specific sub-group. This ensures that the advantage for a given action is calculated relative to the average outcome for that specific role (`A_p,i_k,t = R_p,i_k - mean(R_p)`). This agent-specific normalization is intended to provide a more accurate and stable credit assignment in multi-agent settings, preventing a single baseline from obscuring role-specific learning signals.

**Game Environments:**
To foster comprehensive multi-agent reasoning, MARSHAL utilizes a diverse set of six two-player strategic games, divided into training and held-out testing sets for out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation:
- **Training Games:**
 - **Perfect-information, competitive:** Tic-Tac-Toe (for deterministic planning and role adaptation).
 - **Imperfect-information, competitive:** Kuhn Poker (for robust reasoning under uncertainty).
 - **Imperfect-information, cooperative:** Mini Hanabi (for social intelligence, intent recognition, and Theory of Mind).
- **Testing Games (for OOD evaluation):**
 - **Perfect-information, competitive:** Connect Four (more complex state space than Tic-Tac-Toe).
 - **Imperfect-information, competitive:** Leduc Hold’em (more sophisticated poker variant).
 - **Imperfect-information, cooperative:** Simple Hanabi (more challenging cooperative variant).

**Reward Design:**
The learning signal comprises three components to ensure stable training:
- **Intrinsic game rewards**: The primary signal, normalized to a maximum of 4 chips across games for multi-game training. Examples include +/-1 for win/loss/draw in Tic-Tac-Toe, chips won/lost in Kuhn Poker, and +1 per successfully played card in Mini Hanabi.
- **Format reward**: A small positive reward (+0.05) for validly formatted actions and a large negative penalty (-10.0) for invalid formats, which terminates the game.
- **Response length penalty**: A turn-level penalty for verbosity, scaling linearly for responses exceeding a minimum length (`l_min = 11`) up to a maximum threshold (`l_max = 2048`), with a scaling coefficient (`α = 0.5`). This encourages concise and efficient reasoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MARSHAL involved training Qwen3-4B models (specialist agents for individual games and a generalist agent for all training games) and comparing them against the base Qwen3-4B model and a SPIRAL baseline.

**Strategic Ability:**
MARSHAL agents demonstrated strong strategic ability across the evaluated games. Specialist agents mastered their training domains, outperforming baselines. Critically, these agents also generalized effectively to more complex, held-out games (e.g., Tic-Tac-Toe specialist to Connect Four). The generalist model, trained on all environments, achieved high performance across the entire suite of games, including a 28.7% improvement on Leduc Hold’em and 22.9% on Simple Hanabi over baselines. Observations also indicated cross-category skill generalization, such as a Tic-Tac-Toe specialist showing improvement in the OOD Mini Hanabi, suggesting the cultivation of foundational turn-based planning skills.

**Generalization to Multi-Agent Systems (MASs):**
The skills acquired through game-play generalized to practical, out-of-domain challenges in zero-shot settings on various math and QA benchmarks.
- **Single-agent setting**: MARSHAL models achieved improvements over the Qwen-4B baseline on several math benchmarks, performing comparably to SPIRAL.
- **Competitive MAD framework**: Agents trained on competitive games showed improved performance. The generalist agent achieved an average of 3.51% gain over the original Qwen3-4B across all benchmarks.
- **Cooperative AutoGen framework**: Agents trained for cooperation (Hanabi specialist and generalist) excelled, with the generalist model achieving a 7.57% gain on GPQA-Diamond in MAD and a 10.00% gain on AIME in AutoGen.
These results indicate that MARSHAL fosters distinct, generalizable skills for both competition and collaboration, which translate to improved performance in downstream multi-agent applications. The generalist model proved to be the most robust.

**Reasoning Pattern and Failure Mode Analysis:**
Qualitative analysis of reasoning traces revealed emergent multi-agent reasoning patterns:
- **Role-aware strategy**: Competitive-trained agents explicitly identified their role (e.g., "second-move player" in Tic-Tac-Toe, "negative side assistant" in MAD) and adapted their strategy accordingly.
- **Intent recognition (Theory of Mind)**: Cooperative-trained agents (e.g., Hanabi specialist) learned to interpret teammates' actions as communications with hidden intent, a skill that generalized to inferring collaborator uncertainty in the AutoGen framework from subtle cues (e.g., a missing 'TERMINATE' token).
Quantitative failure mode analysis on GPQA-Diamond within the MAD framework showed that MARSHAL significantly reduced "Inter-Agent Misalignment" failures (11.5% reduction), primarily due to reductions in "Task Derailment" and "Ignored Other Agent’s Input." This suggests that MARSHAL agents actively listen to peers and maintain task focus.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Self-play vs. fixed opponent**: Training agents against fixed, expert opponents resulted in overfitting, leading to significant performance degradation on OOD games and instances of strategic mode collapse (e.g., Kuhn Poker specialist failing on non-poker games). This validates that the adaptive curriculum provided by self-play is crucial for developing robust, generalizable policies.
- **Advantage estimation design**:
 - **Removing the turn-level advantage estimator**: Reverting to a coarse, trajectory-level reward signal caused a notable performance drop, especially in long-horizon games like Tic-Tac-Toe, confirming the importance of fine-grained credit assignment.
 - **Removing agent-specific advantage normalization**: This also degraded performance, particularly in competitive games where player roles have distinct return distributions (e.g., Tic-Tac-Toe and Kuhn Poker specialists). The effect was milder in cooperative games like Hanabi, where player experiences are more symmetric. Both components of MARSHAL's advantage estimation were found to be critical for effective learning in complex multi-turn, multi-agent environments.

**Scalability to Larger Models:**
The framework demonstrated scalability when extended to the Qwen3-8B model. The MARSHAL-8B generalist agent showed consistent improvements in strategic ability and generalized effectively to held-out games (e.g., Leduc Hold’em performance increased from 7.26% to 53.89%). It also consistently outperformed the base Qwen3-8B model in both MAD (average 2.60% improvement) and AutoGen (average 3.90% improvement) frameworks.

**Comparison with SPIRAL and MT-GRPO:**
Further analysis confirmed the superiority of MARSHAL's algorithmic design over SPIRAL's RAE in cooperative settings and MAS generalization. Additionally, MARSHAL's "sum-then-normalize" approach proved more robust than MT-GRPO's turn-by-turn normalization, particularly in variable-length, multi-turn strategic games and their downstream generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of MARSHAL contributes to the field of AI by providing a framework that enables Large Language Models to acquire and generalize multi-agent reasoning capabilities.
- **Scalable Training Paradigm**: The work establishes self-play in strategic games as a scalable approach for developing LLM agents with generalizable multi-agent reasoning. This method offers a promising avenue for training complex behaviors without extensive human annotation.
- **Enhanced LLM Abilities**: MARSHAL-trained agents demonstrate improved strategic abilities, including role awareness and intent recognition (elements of Theory of Mind). These skills directly translate into enhanced performance within both competitive and cooperative multi-agent systems, suggesting a path toward more intelligent and adaptable LLMs.
- **Algorithmic Advancements**: The introduction of the turn-level advantage estimator and agent-specific advantage normalization addresses key challenges in multi-turn, multi-agent reinforcement learning, providing more stable and effective credit assignment mechanisms for LLMs operating in complex interaction environments.
- **Foundation for Advanced Intelligence**: By cultivating fundamental skills necessary for multi-agent interaction, MARSHAL contributes to building more sophisticated LLM agents capable of handling complex real-world scenarios such as negotiation, collaborative problem-solving, and strategic decision-making in dynamic environments.

Potential future directions include scaling the approach to N-player environments, which would introduce further challenges related to non-stationarity and population diversity, and applying the training paradigm to more practical "social sandboxes" beyond classic games, such as simulated collaborative research or software engineering tasks.
