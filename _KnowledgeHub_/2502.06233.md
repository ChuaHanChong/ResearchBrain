# Confidence Improves Self-Consistency in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google Research and The Hebrew University of Jerusalem developed Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency (CISC), a decoding strategy that integrates LLM-generated confidence scores into a weighted majority vote to enhance the efficiency of self-consistency. This method reduces the computational cost by over 40% on average, requiring fewer reasoning paths to match or exceed the accuracy of traditional self-consistency across various LLMs and benchmarks.

## Problem

- Standard Self-Consistency (SC) decoding for LLMs, while effective for complex reasoning, incurs high computational costs by requiring the generation of numerous lengthy reasoning paths.
- The debate on LLMs' ability to reliably self-assess the correctness of their outputs lacked robust empirical validation, particularly for discerning correct vs. incorrect responses to the same question.
- Traditional confidence metrics (e.g., ECE, Brier Score) assess calibration across different questions but not the model's ability to discriminate between varying outputs for a single question, which is crucial for self-correction.

## Method

- Proposed **CISC** (==Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency==), a decoding strategy that uses a weighted majority vote for sampled reasoning paths, where weights are derived from LLM-generated confidence scores.
- Utilized a ==two-step prompting== approach to efficiently extract confidence scores (e.g., ==P(True)==, verbal confidence, response probability) from LLMs with minimal computational overhead.
- Implemented ==Softmax normalization== with a tunable temperature parameter on confidence scores to balance between answer frequency and confidence in the final aggregation.

## Results

- **CISC** consistently outperformed standard Self-Consistency across **9** LLMs and **4** reasoning benchmarks, either by achieving higher accuracy or significantly reducing computational cost.
- CISC delivered an average computational cost reduction of over **40%** in required reasoning paths, with the P(True) confidence method achieving up to a **46%** reduction for matching 10-sample SC accuracy.
- The proposed Within-Question Discrimination (WQD) metric perfectly correlated with CISC's performance, indicating its superior ability to evaluate confidence for distinguishing correct from incorrect responses for the same question.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models exhibit an intrinsic ability to effectively self-assess the correctness and quality of their own generated reasoning paths.
> - The P(True) method, which extracts the model's probability of asserting correctness, consistently proves to be the most effective and efficient approach for obtaining confidence scores in a self-correction context.
> - Traditional confidence metrics are inadequate for evaluating self-correction; the novel Within-Question Discrimination (WQD) metric accurately predicts the efficacy of confidence-informed decoding strategies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses several critical and evolving areas within the field of large language models (LLMs). Firstly, it builds upon the success of **reasoning strategies for LLMs**, specifically focusing on "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022) and "Self-Consistency" (SC) decoding (Wang et al., 2022). Self-consistency, which samples multiple reasoning paths and selects the most frequent answer, has been shown to significantly improve LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks. However, its high computational cost—requiring the generation of numerous lengthy reasoning paths—has been a major practical hurdle. This paper positions itself as a solution to this efficiency problem, aiming to make advanced reasoning more accessible and scalable.

Secondly, the work contributes to the ongoing **debate regarding LLMs' self-assessment capabilities**. There is a vibrant discussion in the AI community about whether LLMs truly "know what they know" and can reliably judge the correctness or quality of their own outputs (e.g., Kadavath et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024a; Stechly et al., 2024). This paper provides empirical evidence supporting the positive side of this debate by demonstrating that LLM-generated confidence scores can indeed be effectively leveraged to discern correct from incorrect reasoning paths, even for the same question. It validates an intrinsic self-assessment ability beyond mere surface-level understanding.

Thirdly, it innovates in the area of **confidence evaluation metrics**. Traditional metrics for assessing model confidence, such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE) or Brier Score, primarily evaluate how well confidence aligns with correctness *across different questions*. The authors argue that for tasks like improving self-consistency, a different kind of confidence—one that can distinguish correct and incorrect answers *to the same question*—is needed. By introducing the "Within-Question Discrimination" (WQD) metric, the paper pushes the boundaries of how model confidence is understood and evaluated, highlighting a crucial distinction for self-correction mechanisms.

Finally, the paper connects to the broader theme of **improving LLM efficiency**. As LLMs become larger and more powerful, their computational demands for both training and inference are escalating. Techniques that reduce inference costs, especially for sophisticated decoding strategies like self-consistency, are highly valuable. This research provides a practical, lightweight extension to self-consistency, contributing to the growing body of work aimed at making LLMs more deployable and sustainable. It differentiates itself from other efficiency-focused methods by maintaining parallel sampling and minimal overhead, avoiding the common trade-offs between throughput and latency that plague many proposed optimizations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the recognized strength and simultaneous weakness of the Self-Consistency (SC) decoding strategy in Large Language Models (LLMs). While SC significantly boosts LLMs' performance on complex reasoning tasks by aggregating diverse reasoning paths, its Achilles' heel is its **computational expense**. Generating a large number of potentially long reasoning paths to ensure the correct answer emerges as the most frequent one consumes substantial computational resources.

Against this backdrop, the authors identified a promising avenue: recent findings suggesting that LLMs possess an inherent ability to judge the correctness of their own outputs. This led to the central hypothesis that if an LLM could effectively review and assign confidence scores to its generated reasoning paths, the efficiency of self-consistency could be dramatically improved.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this study are multifaceted:

1. **To introduce Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency (CISC):** The core objective is to propose and formalize a novel decoding strategy, CISC, which integrates model-generated confidence scores into a weighted majority vote for answer selection, aiming to reduce the sample size required for high accuracy.
2. **To empirically validate CISC's effectiveness:** Conduct a comprehensive comparison of CISC against standard self-consistency across a wide array of LLMs (nine models), diverse reasoning datasets (four benchmarks), and various confidence extraction methods. The goal is to demonstrate that CISC consistently outperforms SC in terms of efficiency (cost reduction) and/or accuracy.
3. **To investigate the utility of different confidence extraction methods:** Evaluate how different techniques for obtaining confidence scores from LLMs—such as response probability, verbal confidence, and P(True)—impact CISC's performance, and identify the most effective method.
4. **To address limitations in traditional confidence evaluation:** Propose a new metric, "Within-Question Discrimination (WQD)," specifically designed to assess an LLM's ability to distinguish between correct and incorrect responses *for the same question*. This objective aims to show that existing, calibration-focused metrics are insufficient for evaluating confidence in this specific context and that WQD is a more predictive measure for CISC's success.
5. **To contribute to the debate on LLM self-assessment:** Provide robust empirical evidence, supported by both quantitative analysis (CISC performance, WQD scores) and qualitative human evaluation, that LLMs are indeed capable of effectively self-assessing the quality and correctness of their own reasoning processes.
6. **To open-source the implementation:** Make CISC publicly available to foster further research and adoption within the LLM community.

Ultimately, the overarching goal is to enhance the practicality and scalability of advanced LLM reasoning by making self-consistency more computationally efficient, while simultaneously deepening the understanding of LLMs' intrinsic self-assessment capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency (CISC)**, a decoding strategy designed to improve the efficiency of conventional self-consistency by incorporating model-derived confidence scores. The methodology involves several interconnected components:

#### 3.1. CISC Mechanism

CISC operates by performing a **weighted majority vote** on sampled reasoning paths. For each input question, the model generates multiple responses, each comprising a reasoning path ($r_i$) and a final answer ($a_i$).
1. **Confidence Extraction:** A self-assessed confidence score ($c_i$) is derived for each $(r_i, a_i)$ pair.
2. **Confidence Normalization:** These scores are then normalized using a Softmax function: $\tilde{c}_i = \frac{\exp(c_i/T)}{\sum_{j=1}^m \exp(c_j/T)}$, where $T$ is a tunable temperature hyper-parameter. This temperature controls the balance between answer frequency and confidence scores, with high $T$ reducing CISC to vanilla self-consistency and low $T$ prioritizing high-confidence responses.
3. **Aggregation:** The final answer ($\hat{a}_{CISC}$) is chosen through a confidence-weighted majority vote: $\hat{a}_{CISC} = \arg \max_a \sum_{i=1}^m \mathbf{1}[a_i = a] \cdot \tilde{c}_i$.

#### 3.2. Confidence Extraction Methods

Three distinct methods were used to obtain confidence scores from the LLMs:
- **Response Probability:** The length-normalized probability of the model generating the entire reasoning path and answer $(r, a)$.
- **Verbal Confidence:** The model is explicitly prompted to rate its confidence in its generated output, either as a binary (0 or 1) or on a scale (0-100).
- **P(True):** The probability the model assigns to the token "1" when prompted for a binary confidence rating (e.g., "Proposed confidence: (1)").

To ensure computational efficiency and prevent the confidence prompt from influencing the generated reasoning path, a **two-step prompting procedure** was employed for verbal and P(True) methods. The model first generates $(r, a)$, then a short confidence extraction prompt is appended, and the generation continues for just one token. This leverages prefix caching, making the confidence extraction step negligible in terms of computational overhead.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Models:** Nine instruction-tuned open-weight LLMs from Gemma2 (2B, 9B, 27B), Qwen2.5 (3B, 14B, 72B), and Mistral (8B, 22B, 123B) families were used to ensure broad applicability.
- **Datasets:** Four large reasoning benchmarks covering diverse tasks: GSM8K (grade-school math), MATH (challenging math), MMLU-Pro (general knowledge and reasoning), and Big-Bench-Hard (BBH) (variety of challenging reasoning tasks).
- **Metrics:**
 - **% Cost Reduction:** The percentage of samples saved by CISC to achieve the same accuracy as self-consistency.
 - **% Accuracy Improvement:** The relative accuracy gain of CISC over self-consistency using the same number of samples.
- **Temperature Scaling:** The temperature parameter $T$ for Softmax normalization was tuned separately for each model and confidence method using a 10% held-out validation set, aggregated across all datasets.
- **Bootstrap Sampling:** To accurately compare performance across different sample budgets (1 to 30 responses), 500 sets of $b$ paths were drawn for each question from 30 initial samples, and accuracy was averaged across these bootstrap sets.

#### 3.4. Within-Question Confidence Evaluation (WQD)

To address the shortcomings of traditional confidence metrics for CISC's specific needs, the authors introduced the WQD metric. WQD measures the fraction of cases where a correct response has a higher confidence score than an incorrect response, specifically *for the same question*, when considering pairs of responses where one is correct and the other is incorrect. This metric aims to directly quantify the discriminative power of confidence scores within a single problem context.

#### 3.5. Qualitative Analysis

Human evaluators (NLP PhD students) reviewed a subset of LLM responses from MMLU-Pro, specifically focusing on cases where CISC and SC differed. They identified "low-quality indicators" in the reasoning paths, which are logical patterns that reduce confidence in the response's correctness. These human assessments were then correlated with the model's self-assigned confidence scores to further validate the LLM's self-assessment capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded compelling results, strongly supporting the efficacy of Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency (CISC) and providing new insights into LLM self-assessment:

1. **CISC Consistently Outperforms Self-Consistency (SC):** Across virtually all nine LLMs, four datasets, and all examined confidence extraction methods, CISC demonstrated superior performance compared to standard self-consistency. This improvement manifested as either higher accuracy for the same computational budget or significantly reduced computational cost to achieve comparable accuracy.

2. **Significant Computational Cost Reduction:** CISC achieved an average **cost reduction of over 40%** in the number of reasoning paths required to match self-consistency's accuracy. In many configurations, especially with the P(True) method, CISC using only 10 samples surpassed the accuracy of 30-sample self-consistency. Some instances even showed cost reductions exceeding 67%, indicating that self-consistency could not reach CISC's accuracy even with 30 samples.

3. **P(True) is the Most Effective Confidence Method for CISC:** Among the tested confidence extraction methods, P(True) consistently yielded the best results for CISC. It achieved an average Cost Reduction of 41% and 46% with budgets of 5 and 10 responses, respectively, significantly outperforming Verbal Confidence (e.g., 30% reduction for Verbal 0-100 with 10 responses) and Response Probability (31% reduction).

4. **Confidence Normalization is Crucial for CISC's Performance:** The study demonstrated that incorporating a softmax normalization step with a carefully tuned temperature parameter significantly enhances CISC's performance across all confidence methods. CISC with normalization consistently outperformed its unnormalized counterpart, highlighting the importance of scaling confidence values appropriately for the weighted majority vote. Without proper temperature scaling, softmax normalization could even degrade performance (e.g., Verbal 0-100 with Softmax T=1).

5. **Within-Question Discrimination (WQD) is a Superior Confidence Metric for CISC:** A surprising finding was that traditional, calibration-focused confidence metrics like Expected Calibration Error (ECE-t) and Brier Score (Brier-t) were poor predictors of CISC's success. Verbal confidence methods, despite often being considered well-calibrated, performed worse for CISC. In contrast, the newly proposed WQD metric, designed to measure a model's ability to distinguish correct from incorrect answers *for the same question*, perfectly correlated with CISC's performance across all confidence methods. This validates WQD as a more relevant evaluation tool for this specific application. The WQD scores for P(True) were consistently higher (62.3%) than for other methods (e.g., Verbal Binary 52.2%), aligning with its superior CISC performance.

6. **Empirical Evidence for LLM Self-Assessment:** The strong performance gains from CISC, particularly with the P(True) method, and the effectiveness of the WQD metric collectively provide compelling quantitative evidence that LLMs possess an intrinsic ability to effectively judge the correctness of their own outputs and reasoning paths. This contributes significantly to the ongoing debate about LLM self-verification capabilities. Furthermore, the analysis showed a fine-grained self-assessment mechanism, where even small differences in confidence scores reflected significant variations in the probability of a response being correct.

7. **Qualitative Analysis Corroborates Self-Assessment:** A human evaluation confirmed a strong correlation between model confidence scores and human assessments of reasoning path quality. 67% of responses flagged as "low-confidence" by the model were also identified by human evaluators as containing "low-quality indicators" in their reasoning. Specific indicators like providing an answer not among options or incomplete calculations were strongly associated with low model confidence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Confidence-Informed Self-Consistency (CISC) and the accompanying research carry substantial significance and potential impact across both the practical application and theoretical understanding of Large Language Models.

#### 5.1. Practical Impact: Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability of LLM Reasoning

The most immediate and tangible impact of CISC is its ability to significantly **reduce the computational cost of LLM reasoning tasks** that rely on self-consistency. By cutting the required number of reasoning paths by over 40% on average—and in some cases by more than 67%—CISC transforms self-consistency from a powerful but often prohibitively expensive technique into a much more practical and scalable solution. This efficiency gain is crucial for:
- **Wider Adoption:** Makes advanced reasoning capabilities accessible to a broader range of applications and users, particularly where latency and computational budget are critical constraints.
- **Real-world Deployment:** Facilitates the deployment of LLMs for complex tasks in production environments, such as code generation, scientific problem-solving, or sophisticated data analysis, where high accuracy and efficiency are paramount.
- **Throughput and Latency:** Unlike many other efficiency-boosting techniques that might increase latency by sequential processing, CISC maintains parallel sampling, thus improving throughput without compromising real-time response requirements.
- **Resource Optimization:** Reduces energy consumption and hardware requirements associated with extensive LLM inference, aligning with sustainability goals in AI.

#### 5.2. Theoretical Impact: Stronger Evidence for LLM Self-Assessment

This work provides robust, multi-faceted empirical evidence supporting the notion that **LLMs can effectively judge the quality and correctness of their own outputs**. This is a significant contribution to the ongoing debate about LLM self-verification and self-correction capabilities. By demonstrating that model-derived confidence scores can reliably distinguish between correct and incorrect reasoning paths *for the same question*, the paper strengthens the argument for an intrinsic metacognitive ability within LLMs. This understanding can inform:
- **Model Design:** Future LLM architectures and training methodologies could explicitly leverage or even enhance these self-assessment capabilities, potentially leading to models that are not only more accurate but also more reliable and trustworthy.
- **Alignment Research:** A deeper understanding of how LLMs evaluate their own performance is critical for research into AI safety and alignment, enabling the development of models that are better at identifying and mitigating their own errors or biases.

#### 5.3. Methodological Impact: Refined Confidence Evaluation

The introduction of the **Within-Question Discrimination (WQD)** metric is a vital methodological advancement. It highlights a critical distinction between traditional confidence calibration (across questions) and the discriminative ability required for self-correction (within a single question). WQD offers a more appropriate and predictive tool for evaluating confidence mechanisms in contexts where models need to choose among their own varied outputs for the same problem. This could lead to:
- **Improved Evaluation Practices:** Researchers and practitioners can use WQD to more accurately assess and compare new confidence extraction methods, ensuring they are optimized for tasks involving self-consistency or similar internal verification processes.
- **Targeted Model Training:** Future work on training LLMs to generate better confidence signals can directly use WQD as an optimization target, leading to more useful confidence scores for self-correction.

#### 5.4. Future Research Avenues

The findings open several promising directions for future research:
- **Integration with Advanced Reasoning Frameworks:** The authors suggest integrating CISC's principles into more complex frameworks like Tree of Thoughts or Graph of Thoughts, potentially leading to even more sophisticated and efficient reasoning.
- **Training for Better Confidence:** Encourages research into training models to produce more accurate intrinsic or verbal confidence signals, which would directly amplify CISC's benefits.
- **Internal State Exploration:** Calls for further exploration of confidence signals derived from the model's internal states, beyond output probabilities, as a potentially richer source of self-assessment information.
- **Ablation Studies:** Further ablations, such as zero-shot vs. few-shot prompting effects on confidence, or different normalization techniques, could further refine CISC's application.

In conclusion, this research not only offers a highly practical and immediately implementable solution for making LLM reasoning more efficient but also significantly advances the theoretical understanding of LLM capabilities and how they can be effectively measured and leveraged. It pushes the field towards more robust, autonomous, and cost-effective AI systems.
