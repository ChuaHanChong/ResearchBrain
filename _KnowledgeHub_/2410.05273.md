# HiRT: Enhancing Robotic Control with Hierarchical Robot Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> HiRT, a hierarchical framework from Tsinghua University and UC Berkeley, enables Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to perform dynamic robotic manipulation tasks by nearly doubling their inference speed to 9.8Hz while improving generalization. It achieves a 75% success rate in real-world dynamic tasks, significantly enhancing the practical applicability of VLA models beyond static environments.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models based on large VLMs suffer from high computational cost and inference latency, leading to low control frequencies.
- These latency issues limit VLA models primarily to quasi-static manipulation tasks, making them unsuitable for dynamic tasks requiring rapid interactions and real-time control.
- There is a need to balance the high-level understanding and generalization capabilities of large VLMs with the practical requirement for high-frequency, real-time action generation in robotics.

## Method

- Introduces **HiRT** (==Hierarchical Robot Transformer==), a hierarchical imitation learning framework inspired by the dual-process theory of human cognition.
- Utilizes an "Understanding Module" (System 2) based on a large VLM (InstructBLIP) for low-frequency, long-term scene understanding and planning, and an "Execution Module" (System 1), a lightweight visual-based policy, for high-frequency, rapid action generation.
- Employs asynchronous operation during inference, where the fast execution module uses cached VLM latents while the VLM updates in parallel at a lower frequency, and trains the policy with randomly sampled past observations to handle time-inconsistent latents.

## Results

- Achieved an inference speed of 9.8Hz, nearly doubling Vanilla-VLA's 4.1Hz, while maintaining comparable or superior success rates in quasi-static tasks (e.g., Metaworld: **76.4%**; Franka-Kitchen: **80.8%**).
- Demonstrated an average **75.0%** success rate in real-world dynamic manipulation tasks, significantly outperforming baselines like Vanilla-VLA (**48.0%**), RT-1* (**18.0%**), and Diffusion Policy (**18.0%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed the importance of VLM-latent conditioning, multiple visual inputs for the execution module, and a training strategy that samples past observations for VLM input to ensure robustness during asynchronous deployment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hierarchical approach effectively integrates powerful but slow large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with fast, lightweight policies, enabling high-frequency control without sacrificing generalization.
> - Asynchronous operation, coupled with a training strategy robust to time-inconsistent VLM guidance, is critical for deploying VLA models in real-time, dynamic robotic environments.
> - Leveraging a VLM's rich visual scene understanding through latent conditioning significantly improves a smaller policy's generalization and multi-task learning capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "HiRT" is situated at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, large pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs), imitation learning, and hierarchical robotic control**.

In recent years, large VLA models, exemplified by works like RT-1, RT-2, and PaLM-E, have emerged as a promising paradigm for robotic control. These models leverage the immense generalization capabilities of pre-trained VLMs by fine-tuning them on extensive robot demonstration data. This approach has shown remarkable success in multi-task generalization and zero-shot/few-shot learning across various static or quasi-static manipulation tasks. The underlying principle is to enable robots to understand high-level language instructions and translate them directly into low-level actions, benefiting from the rich semantic understanding embedded within VLMs.

However, a critical limitation of these state-of-the-art VLA models, which HiRT directly addresses, is their **high computational cost and inference latency**. Relying on VLM backends with billions of parameters means that processing sensory input and generating actions can be slow, leading to low control frequencies. This constraint severely limits their applicability in dynamic tasks, such as manipulating fast-moving objects or interacting in cluttered, time-sensitive environments, where rapid reactions and high-frequency control are paramount. Existing VLA models, therefore, tend to be confined to quasi-static scenarios, where reaction time is less critical.

HiRT positions itself as a solution to this specific bottleneck. Instead of replacing the powerful VLM, it proposes a **hierarchical framework** that intelligently integrates the VLM while mitigating its computational burden. This aligns with the broader trend in AI and robotics to move beyond purely end-to-end monolithic models towards more modular, efficient, and specialized architectures, especially as models scale.

Furthermore, the paper draws inspiration from the **dual-process theory of human cognition** (System 1 for fast, intuitive reactions; System 2 for slow, analytical planning). This theoretical grounding provides a compelling analogy for HiRT's design, where the VLM acts as the "System 2" for high-level understanding and the lightweight policy acts as the "System 1" for real-time execution. This hierarchical approach also connects to long-standing research in hierarchical action planning in robotics, but with a novel integration of modern large vision-language models for grounding. Unlike previous hierarchical methods that often rely on symbolic planning or discrete sub-tasks, HiRT grounds the VLM to a specific robot's physical form in an end-to-end manner, learning continuous intermediate representations for hierarchical task planning.

In essence, HiRT represents a significant step towards making the impressive generalization capabilities of large VLA models practically viable for a wider range of real-world robotic applications, particularly those requiring dynamic interaction, by optimizing for both performance and efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind HiRT stems from the inherent limitations of current Large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models:

1. **High Computational Cost and Inference Latency:** Existing VLA models rely heavily on large VLM backends with billions of parameters (e.g., InstructBLIP, PaLM-E). While these models offer impressive generalization capabilities due to pre-training on vast datasets, their sheer size translates to significant computational demands and slow inference speeds during deployment. This results in low control frequencies and high latency, making them unsuitable for dynamic robotic tasks that require rapid interactions and precise, real-time control.
2. **Limited Applicability to Dynamic Tasks:** Due to the aforementioned latency issues, most successful applications of VLA models have been restricted to quasi-static manipulation tasks, where the environment changes slowly or is entirely static. Deploying these models in dynamic environments, such as manipulating fast-moving objects or operating in cluttered, unpredictable settings, becomes challenging, impairing performance and potentially compromising safety.

Given these limitations, the **key objectives** of the HiRT framework are:

1. **Enable Flexible Frequency and Performance Trade-off:** To design a robotic control framework that can balance the high-level understanding of large VLMs with the need for high-frequency, real-time action generation.
2. **Achieve Real-Time Interaction:** To significantly increase the control frequency of VLA-based robotic systems, thereby enabling effective performance in dynamic tasks that demand rapid responses.
3. **Maintain Generalization Capabilities:** To leverage the powerful generalization and semantic understanding benefits of pre-trained VLMs while overcoming their computational overhead, ensuring that the enhanced speed does not come at the cost of task versatility or zero-shot capabilities.
4. **Improve Performance in Dynamic Manipulation Tasks:** Specifically, to develop a system that can effectively perform tasks involving moving objects or rapidly changing environments, which are challenging for traditional VLA models.

By addressing these objectives, HiRT aims to unlock the full potential of VLA models for practical, real-world robotic deployment beyond static laboratory settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HiRT (Hierarchical Robot Transformer) is a hierarchical imitation learning framework designed to achieve high-frequency robotic control while leveraging the robust generalization capabilities of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs). The core methodology is inspired by the "dual-process theory" of human cognition, distinguishing between a slow, analytical "System 2" (the VLM) and a fast, intuitive "System 1" (a lightweight action policy).

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Architecture

HiRT decomposes the control policy into two primary modules:

- **Understanding Module (System 2):** This module is responsible for long-term scene understanding, task planning, and error correction. It is implemented using a pre-trained large VLM, specifically **InstructBLIP (7B)**. InstructBLIP processes visual information (images) and natural language instructions to extract rich, semantically informed latent features. This module operates at a lower frequency due to its computational demands.
 - **VLM Process:** The VLM takes a visual observation ($\bar{o}_{\hat{t}_k}$) (sampled asynchronously) and a natural language instruction ($l$). It encodes the visual input using a Vision Transformer (ViT), concatenates it with instruction tokens and learnable query tokens, and processes them through a Q-Former. These visual query features then prompt a pre-trained Large Language Model (LLM, e.g., LLaMA). Instead of generating language tokens, HiRT uses a Multi-Attention Pooling (MAP) module to aggregate the informative language embeddings from the LLM's final layer output into a continuous latent representation ($x_{t_k}$). This latent variable captures high-level understanding and common sense knowledge.

- **Execution Module (System 1):** This is a compact, visual-based action policy designed for short-term scene cognition and rapid action generation. It operates at a high frequency, utilizing recent visual observations and guided by the slowly updated latent features from the understanding module. It is a lightweight model based on architectures like EfficientNet or Vision Transformer.
 - **Latent-Conditioned Policy:** The execution module takes recent visual observations ($o_{:t}$) and the latest VLM-generated latent ($z_{\hat{t}_k}$) from a cache. It encodes the image context using a lightweight visual encoder (e.g., EfficientNet for CNN-based, ViT for Transformer-based). A MAP block then aggregates these tokens into the continuous action space.
 - **Conditioning Strategies:** To effectively integrate the VLM's latent guidance into the lightweight policy, HiRT explores three conditioning strategies:
 - **FiLM-Condition (for CNN-based visual encoder):** Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers condition the hidden layers of the convolutional network based on the VLM latent variable. This allows the VLM's high-level understanding to modulate the low-level visual processing.
 - **Condition with Cross-Attention Layers (for Transformer-based visual encoder):** In each self-attention layer of the Transformer, an additional cross-attention layer is inserted. This layer allows the hidden tokens of the visual encoder to attend to a projected version of the VLM latent, effectively injecting high-level context.
 - **Condition with Prefix Tuning:** The VLM latent variable ($x_{t_k}$) is used as a prefix prompt for the MAP block in the action head. This means the action generation is directly conditioned by the VLM's understanding before the final MLP layer outputs the actions.

#### 3.2. Asynchronous Operation and Sampling

A crucial aspect of HiRT is its **asynchronous operation** during inference to achieve speedup:
- At the initial time step (t=0), the VLM processes the initial multi-modal input and caches its latent.
- In subsequent steps, the lightweight execution module rapidly outputs actions using its visual observations and the *most recent* cached latent from the VLM.
- Concurrently, the VLM runs in parallel but at a much lower frequency, periodically updating the cached latent. This allows the overall system to operate at the speed of the fast execution module, avoiding delays caused by the VLM.

To address the potential **misalignment** between the slowly updated latent and the real-time visual observations during asynchronous inference, HiRT employs a specific **training strategy**:
- During training, instead of always using the current observation for the VLM, HiRT **randomly selects a past observation** from the current trajectory context to feed into the VLM. This teaches the latent-conditioned policy to be robust to time-inconsistent latent variables, effectively simulating the asynchronous deployment scenario during training.

#### 3.3. Training Objective

The training objective uses **maximum likelihood imitation learning**. The model is trained to minimize the difference between its predicted actions and expert demonstrations.
- For continuous actions (e.g., end-effector position and pose), an MSE loss is used.
- For discrete actions (e.g., gripper status), binary cross-entropy loss is applied.
- The VLM part (InstructBLIP) is fine-tuned using **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)**, an efficient fine-tuning method that significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters for large models, making it more computationally feasible. The rest of the network (execution module) is fully fine-tuned.

This comprehensive approach combines the strengths of large pre-trained models for semantic understanding with efficient, dedicated modules for real-time action generation, all within a carefully designed asynchronous framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across simulated environments (Metaworld, Franka-Kitchen) and a real-world Panda robot setup demonstrate the significant advantages of HiRT in terms of both inference speed and task success rates, particularly in dynamic scenarios.

#### 4.1. Performance on Quasi-Static Manipulation Tasks

- **Imitation and Zero-Shot Generalization:**
 - In simulated tasks (Metaworld with 20 tasks, Franka-Kitchen with 5 tasks), HiRT achieved the highest success rates (Metaworld: 76.4%; Franka-Kitchen: 80.8%).
 - In real-world quasi-static tasks (4 skills including unseen objects), HiRT demonstrated generalization capabilities comparable to Vanilla-VLA (70.0% average success rate).
 - Compared to RT-1 (which uses language embeddings for conditioning), HiRT (which utilizes VLM latent for conditioning) showed a significant improvement: approximately 20% higher success rate on seen tasks and 30% higher on new task scenarios. This highlights the benefit of VLM's visual scene understanding in guiding the smaller action policy for better generalization.

- **Balancing Performance and Efficiency:**
 - HiRT achieved an inference speed of **9.8Hz**, nearly doubling the speed of Vanilla-VLA (4.1Hz) while maintaining comparable or superior success rates. This empirically validates HiRT's ability to offer a flexible performance-frequency trade-off, significantly enhancing efficiency without sacrificing generalization.
 - Diffusion Policy (DP) and RT-1 operate at higher frequencies (20.1Hz and 4.6Hz respectively for RT-1 in different setups), but generally with lower success rates compared to VLA-based methods like Vanilla-VLA and HiRT. HiRT successfully bridges this gap by achieving VLA-level performance at a much higher frequency.

#### 4.2. Performance on Real-World Dynamic Manipulation Tasks

- This is a critical area where HiRT truly shines, addressing a key limitation of existing VLA models.
- Experiments involved moving target objects at a constant speed (1 cm/s) to simulate realistic dynamic scenarios, including both in-domain ("Seen") and out-of-domain ("Unseen") generalization tests.
- **Time to completion (quasi-static reference):** HiRT achieved the shortest completion time in quasi-static tasks (6.18s), indicating its underlying efficiency.
- **Success Rate in Dynamic Tasks:** HiRT achieved the highest task success rate in dynamic action tests (Average: 75.0%), significantly outperforming baselines:
 - Vanilla-VLA: 48.0%
 - RT-1*: 18.0%
 - Diffusion Policy: 18.0%
- This demonstrates that HiRT's hierarchical design and high execution speed allow it to effectively track and manipulate moving objects, a task that proves challenging for slower, monolithic VLA models. The visualization in Figure 6 clearly shows HiRT successfully tracking and catching a moving block, while a baseline fails due to latency.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies on Implementation of HiRT

- **Importance of VLM-Latent Conditioning:**
 - Removing the extra conditioning layers (FiLM, Cross-Attention, Prefix Tuning) in HiRT-CD resulted in a 20% decrease in success rate in Franka-Kitchen (64.2% vs. 80.8%), underscoring the vital role of effective conditioning in leveraging VLM-latent for multi-task learning and generalization.
- **Impact of Multiple Visual Inputs:**
 - Using multiple consecutive visual inputs for the latent-conditioned policy (as in full HiRT) significantly outperformed using only a single recent image (HiRT-IC, which had 58.8% vs. 80.8% in Franka-Kitchen). While it slightly reduces inference speed, it greatly enhances the policy's understanding of the scene, leading to more accurate actions.
- **Random Sampling in VLM Visual Inputs (Asynchronous Sampling):**
 - Ablation showed that random sampling of past observations for VLM input during training (**HiRT** vs. **HiRT-AS** without random sampling) is crucial for maintaining robustness during asynchronous inference. When VLM operates at a low frequency (e.g., interval 6), HiRT (63.4% seen, 52.5% new) significantly outperforms HiRT-AS (52.2% seen, 50.0% new), preventing a substantial drop in success rate. This validates the training strategy's effectiveness in simulating real-world asynchronous deployment.

In summary, HiRT successfully demonstrates that by decoupling high-level understanding from low-level execution and employing asynchronous operation, it can achieve a superior balance of speed and performance, making VLA models practical for dynamic robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HiRT framework presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic control, particularly concerning the deployment of powerful Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in real-world scenarios. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Performance-Efficiency Gap for VLA Models:** The most immediate and impactful contribution of HiRT is its ability to overcome the major bottleneck of current large VLA models: their high computational cost and slow inference speed. By enabling VLA models to operate at significantly higher control frequencies (nearly doubling that of Vanilla-VLA) while maintaining or even improving generalization performance, HiRT makes these models far more practical for real-world robotic deployment. This directly addresses the long-standing challenge of translating impressive laboratory-based AI capabilities into robust, real-time robotic behavior.

2. **Enabling Dynamic Robotic Manipulation:** Prior VLA models were largely confined to quasi-static tasks due to latency. HiRT's demonstrated success in real-world dynamic manipulation tasks (e.g., tracking and grasping moving objects) is a major breakthrough. This opens up new avenues for applications where robots need to react swiftly to changing environments, such as assembly lines with moving parts, human-robot collaboration scenarios, or manipulation in unstructured and unpredictable settings. The improved success rate from 48% to 75% in dynamic tasks is a compelling testament to its efficacy.

3. **General Framework for Hierarchical Control with Large Models:** The dual-process theory inspiration and the hierarchical design of HiRT provide a valuable blueprint for integrating large foundation models into robotic systems. This approach offers a generalizable methodology for combining the semantic richness and generalization power of VLMs with the low-latency requirements of physical robot control. This paradigm could be extended to other modalities or types of large models, fostering more sophisticated and adaptable robotic intelligence.

4. **Improved Robustness through Asynchronous Training:** The novel asynchronous sampling strategy during training, which accounts for time-inconsistent latent variables, is a subtle yet crucial innovation. By making the low-level policy robust to potentially outdated VLM guidance, HiRT ensures reliable performance in real-world asynchronous deployments, contributing to the overall system's robustness and reliability.

5. **Practical Deployment and Accessibility:** By reducing the inference burden, HiRT potentially lowers the hardware requirements for deploying VLA models on robots. This could make advanced robotic capabilities more accessible, facilitating broader adoption in research and industrial settings where dedicated high-performance computing might be a limiting factor. The use of LoRA for VLM fine-tuning also points to a practical approach for adapting large models efficiently.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge that the current work, while significant, has limitations. The complexity of dynamic tasks tested so far is bounded by data availability. Future research could explore:
- Applying HiRT to even more challenging dynamic tasks, such as grasping high-speed flying objects or rapidly adjusting complex object postures. This would likely require more specialized and extensive dynamic task datasets.
- Adaptive adjustments to certain modules, implying that the current architecture, while effective, might benefit from further dynamic tuning or more sophisticated feedback mechanisms between the hierarchical layers.

In conclusion, HiRT represents a substantial step forward in making VLA models a viable and efficient solution for complex, dynamic robotic control. Its hierarchical design and focus on real-time performance have the potential to significantly expand the applicability of large AI models in robotics, pushing the boundaries of what autonomous systems can achieve in interactive and time-sensitive environments.
