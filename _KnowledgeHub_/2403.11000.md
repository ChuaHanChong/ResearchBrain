# Quantifying the Sim2real Gap for GPS and IMU Sensors

> [!summary] Summary
> VEPD (Velocity Estimation Performance Difference) quantifies the sim2real gap of GPS and IMU sensor models by judging them through a downstream task rather than raw signal comparison. A standard EKF localization package estimates velocity from real and simulated sensor streams; RMSE and Wiener-entropy distributions across tests are compared via Wasserstein distance. Benchmarked on 40 real tests, five GPS noise variants, showing measurement covariance modeling dominates fidelity.

## Problem

- Raw synthetic-versus-real sensor signal comparison, the standard fidelity check, ignores how downstream algorithms consume that data, so it misrepresents actual sim2real impact.
- Prior IMU/GPS noise work estimated measurement covariance matrices against real data but never traced how those discrepancies propagate into downstream robotics task errors.
- Unlike camera and LiDAR, no methodology existed for quantifying the sim2real gap of GPS/IMU sensor simulation, leaving noise models across simulators unvalidated and unranked.

## Method

- Judge-based evaluation: an off-the-shelf ==EKF== localization package processes real and simulated GPS/IMU streams at 35 Hz, so estimator performance difference isolates sensor-model fidelity.
- **VEPD** (Velocity Estimation Performance Difference) metric averages two ==Wasserstein distance== terms between real and sim point sets: velocity RMSE against ground truth, and FFT Wiener-entropy differences capturing error signal signature.
- Five GPS variants built in a ==Chrono== digital twin of a 1/6-scale vehicle: additive Gaussian, bounded random walk, HDOP-only covariance, plus both noise-covariance combinations.

## Results

- Simulated IMU replayed with real GPS scored VEPD **0.0211**, with matched RMSE (0.2004 versus 0.2083) and Wiener entropy (0.0311 versus 0.0272), showing negligible IMU-induced gap.
- Across **150** simulated tests, HDOP-covariance and random-walk models scored best (**0.039**, **0.043**); Gaussian noise without covariance scored **0.155**, improving roughly **eightfold** once covariance was added.
- Rankings stayed largely stable across straight-line, circle, and sine maneuvers; swapping real terrain from grass to inclined concrete shifted VEPD only from **0.0796** to **0.0867** (about **8%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Validate sensor models through the downstream consumer, not raw signal statistics: the estimator reweights inputs, so fidelity only matters where it changes task output.
> - Reported measurement covariance can matter more than the injected noise itself, since a filter that distrusts noisy inputs cancels much of the modeling error.
> - A well-designed sensor-fidelity metric should be invariant to unrelated dynamics and terrain differences, otherwise confounded factors mask the sensor contribution being measured.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and testing of algorithms for autonomous agents heavily rely on simulation environments to reduce the time and cost associated with real-world experimentation. A significant challenge in this process is the "sim2real gap," which describes the discrepancies in algorithm performance when transitioning from simulated to physical environments. This gap can stem from various sources, including inaccuracies in simulated sensor data, vehicle dynamics, or environmental modeling.

Robust and accurate localization is a prerequisite for the safe operation of many autonomous systems, often achieved through the fusion of Global Positioning Systems (GPS) and Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs). While GPS offers global positioning, its accuracy can be compromised by signal obstructions. IMUs provide high-frequency motion data but are susceptible to drift and magnetic interference. State estimators, such as Kalman or particle filters, are commonly used to fuse these sensor inputs and account for their respective uncertainties. However, the development and refinement of these estimators are complex, and their effectiveness in simulation is contingent upon the fidelity of the simulated sensor data. Inaccurate sensor models can lead to state estimation algorithms that perform suboptimally in real-world scenarios, thereby contributing to the sim2real gap.

Prior research has addressed the sim2real gap in various sensor modalities. For optical tactile sensors, imperfections in real-world objects have been identified as a source of the gap, with image augmentation suggested as a mitigation strategy. In LiDAR simulation, a "paired-scenario" method has been proposed to evaluate and improve simulations by creating digital twins of real-world scenarios. For camera simulation, "context-based" approaches and style transfer techniques are common for quantification and bridging the discrepancy.

However, the quantification and mitigation of the sim2real gap, particularly for IMU/GPS sensor simulations in the context of downstream robotics tasks, have received less attention compared to visual sensors. Existing efforts, such as those by Hafez et al. (2012), estimated measurement noise covariance matrices by direct comparison of simulated and real-world data, but did not evaluate the impact of these discrepancies on downstream tasks. Wang et al. (2008) focused on modeling GPS/INS navigation systems but placed less emphasis on model validation and sim2real gap measurement. While GPS/IMU sensors have been modeled across various simulators, a systematic methodology for quantifying their sim2real gap, specifically regarding their impact on downstream robotics tasks, has not been widely established. This paper addresses this identified gap by introducing a methodology to quantitatively measure the sim2real gap for IMU and GPS sensors in velocity estimation applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work arises from the inherent difficulties and limitations associated with developing and validating state estimation algorithms in real-world environments. Real-world testing is characterized by significant engineering effort, high costs, and time-consuming processes. Furthermore, obtaining accurate, noise-free ground truth data in real-world settings to effectively validate and refine these systems is often challenging. Simulation environments offer a controlled, cost-effective alternative for iterative testing and refinement of state estimation algorithms before real-world deployment. However, the utility of simulation is directly dependent on the accuracy of its sensor models. If simulated sensor data does not faithfully represent real-world sensor behavior, the algorithms developed and validated in simulation may exhibit a significant "sim2real gap," leading to unreliable performance in actual applications.

A specific motivation for this study is the observation that direct comparison of raw simulated and real sensor data, a standard technique for evaluating sensor model fidelity, often fails to fully capture the sim2real gap's impact on downstream robotic tasks. Such raw comparisons may overlook how sensor data is ultimately processed and utilized within a robotic system, such as in state estimation. There is a recognized lack of comprehensive methodologies for quantifying the sim2real gap for IMU and GPS sensor simulations, especially within the context of their specific application in downstream robotics tasks like velocity estimation.

Against this background, the key objectives of this research are:

1. **Introduce a novel, application-specific methodology:** To establish a quantitative framework, named Velocity Estimation Performance Difference (VEPD), specifically designed to measure the sim2real gap associated with IMU and GPS sensor models in the context of velocity state estimation. This methodology aims to be sharp enough to pinpoint discrepancies arising solely from sensor models.
2. **Benchmark sensor model performance:** To apply the proposed VEPD methodology to validate five distinct IMU and GPS sensor models against data from 40 real-world experiments. This objective seeks to provide a comprehensive benchmark of the performance characteristics of various sensor models for velocity estimation tasks.
3. **Identify crucial GPS modeling aspects:** Through the benchmarking process, to discern and highlight key features and assumptions within GPS sensor models that contribute significantly to achieving a smaller sim2real gap, particularly concerning velocity estimation.
4. **Disseminate data and code:** To create and make publicly available an open-source dataset and the associated code used in this investigation, thereby contributing to the broader robotics research community and facilitating further research in sensor simulation and sim2real gap mitigation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the Velocity Estimation Performance Difference (VEPD) methodology, a five-component approach designed to evaluate the sim2real gap in GPS/IMU sensor simulations.

**A. Judge:**
The methodology employs the `robot_localization` package, a widely used state estimation tool, as a "judge." This package processes both simulated and real-world IMU and GPS data to produce velocity estimates at 35 Hz. The difference in the performance of this state estimator between simulated and real environments serves as an indirect measure of the fidelity of the simulated sensor models within the specific application of velocity state estimation.

**B. Real-World Experiments:**
Experiments were conducted using the Autonomy Research Testbed (ART) vehicle, a 1/6 scale autonomous vehicle. This platform is equipped with a Wheeltec N-100 IMU operating at 100 Hz and a Sparkfun ZED-F9P GPS-RTK2 operating at 10 Hz, capable of centimeter-level positional accuracy with a base station. An Nvidia Jetson AGX Xavier handles computation and runs a ROS2-based autonomy stack. A total of 40 real-world experiments were performed across diverse environments.

**C. Simulation Environment and Sensor Models:**
The study utilized two simulation environments: Project Chrono, a high-fidelity open-source multi-physics engine suitable for ground vehicles, and AirSim, an open-source simulator built on Unreal Engine.

- **IMU Model:** Both simulators employed an identical Gaussian drift noise model for the gyroscope and accelerometer. This model incorporates additive Gaussian noise and a random walk component to simulate drift (detailed in Appendix IV-A).
- **Chrono GPS Models:**
 - **Ch:Gauss:** An additive Gaussian noise model applied to ground truth latitude/longitude measurements, with the measurement noise covariance matrix set to zero.
 - **Ch:RW:** A random walk noise model that introduces accumulated error in position based on a normal distribution in acceleration, with a mechanism to prevent excessive drift from ground truth. This model also sets the measurement noise covariance matrix to zero.
- **AirSim GPS Model:** This model does not add explicit noise to ground truth latitude/longitude measurements. Instead, it models the Horizontal Dilution Of Precision (HDOP), which is used to compute the measurement noise covariance matrix. HDOP is simulated using a low-pass filter to represent the geometric quality of GPS satellite configuration.
- **Implementation Note:** To ensure consistency and leverage an existing Chrono digital twin, the AirSim GPS and IMU models were implemented within Project Chrono, utilizing its ROS2 bridge for communication. Simulated GPS and IMU data were published at 10 Hz and 100 Hz, respectively, mirroring the real-world setup.

**D. Ground Truth Velocity:**
In simulation, ground truth velocity (`v_sg`) is directly queried from the simulator. For real-world experiments, a high-end commercial SBG Ellipse2-D Inertial Navigation System (INS) provided ground truth velocity (`v_rg`). The accuracy of the INS velocity was validated through straight-line experiments using an RTK-GPS to measure distance, yielding an average error of 0.03 m/s when compared to INS-recorded speed.

**E. Error Metric (VEPD):**
The VEPD metric quantifies the sim2real gap by comparing velocity error signatures. It comprises two main components, each assessed using the Wasserstein distance:

1. **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) Comparison (W1):** For each experiment, RMSE values are calculated for both real (`E_r`) and simulated (`E_s`) scenarios, comparing the state estimator's velocity output against the respective ground truth. `K` experiments generate two sets of `K` RMSE values (A1 and B1), which are then compared using the Wasserstein distance `W1(A1, B1)`. A smaller `W1` indicates closer alignment of velocity estimation accuracy between simulation and reality.
2. **Wiener Entropy Comparison (W2):** To capture the qualitative "noise-like" characteristics of the velocity signals, Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) are applied to the velocity estimates. Wiener entropy `S` is then computed, where a higher value indicates a more uniform (noise-like) energy distribution across the spectrum, and a lower value indicates prominent spectral peaks (tonal signals). The absolute difference between the Wiener entropy of the estimated and ground-truth velocities for both real (`S_r`) and simulated (`S_s`) cases is calculated. These differences are then aggregated over `K` tests into sets (A2 and B2) and compared using the Wasserstein distance `W2(A2, B2)`. A smaller `W2` suggests similar noise patterns in the velocity estimates.

The final VEPD metric is the average of these two Wasserstein distances: `VEPD = (W1 + W2) / 2`. This metric provides a balanced assessment by considering both the accuracy and qualitative characteristics of the velocity signals, moving beyond simple point-by-point comparisons.

**Experimental Design:**
To address the research questions, a dataset was generated using three distinct vehicle trajectories: straight line, half-sine curve, and circle. Each scenario was tested 10 times in both simulated and real-world environments. Real-world tests were conducted on off-road terrain (grass), while simulation tests were performed on flat terrain. A waypoint-based neural network (NN) controller was used for vehicle navigation. The VEPD metric was calculated for each scenario and sensor model variant.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments addressed four specific questions regarding the sim2real gap for IMU and GPS sensors.

**A. Sim2Real gap for the IMU sensor:**
To isolate the IMU sensor's contribution, experiments compared a setup with a real IMU and real GPS against a setup with a simulated IMU and real (played back) GPS, both feeding into the state estimator. The calculated VEPD score for the IMU sensor was 0.0211. This low score suggests that the distributions of RMSEs and Wiener Entropy differences between the real and simulated IMU scenarios were very close. Visual inspection of velocity signatures from a representative test pair also indicated high similarity (Figure 4). This finding implies that the simulated IMU model employed in this study (Gaussian drift noise model) accurately captures the real-world IMU behavior for velocity estimation.

**B. Important aspects for GPS modeling:**
The study investigated five variants of GPS sensor models, fixing the IMU model identified as low-gap. The variants included Chrono's Gaussian noise model (Ch:Gauss), Chrono's Random Walk model (Ch:RW), AirSim's HDOP-based covariance model (AirSim), and combinations of Chrono's noise models with AirSim's covariance modeling (Ch:Gauss+AirSim, Ch:RW+AirSim).

- **Wiener Entropy (Noise Characteristics):** The Ch:RW GPS model demonstrated the most similar Wiener Entropy distribution to the real-world data, indicated by its lowest W2 value (Table I). This suggests that the random walk noise model effectively captures the "noise-like" characteristics of real GPS signals.
- **RMSE (Accuracy):** The AirSim GPS model yielded the smallest difference in velocity error between simulation and reality, as measured by RMSE (W1 value), closely followed by Ch:RW (Table I, Figure 3a).
- **Impact of Measurement Covariance:** The Ch:Gauss model, which initially performed poorly without measurement covariance, improved substantially (8x reduction in W1, W2 values) when AirSim's HDOP-based measurement covariance was incorporated (Ch:Gauss+AirSim vs. Ch:Gauss). This indicates that accurately modeling measurement noise covariance is crucial, as it allows the state estimator to appropriately weight noisy GPS outputs. Conversely, adding measurement covariance slightly worsened the performance of the Ch:RW model, suggesting that its inherent random-walk noise characteristics were already closely aligned with real-world ground truth, and higher covariance values might have, in this specific case, perturbed the estimator's weighting.
- **Impact of Latitude/Longitude Noise:** The AirSim model's strong performance, despite not adding explicit latitude/longitude noise to the ground truth position, is hypothesized to be related to the high precision (3 cm accuracy) of the real-world RTK-GPS used. For such high-precision sensors, minimizing additional simulated latitude/longitude noise might contribute to higher simulation fidelity.

**C. Effect of vehicle maneuver on VEPD scores:**
VEPD scores were calculated for each sensor variant across three distinct dynamic scenarios: straight line, circle, and half-sine curve. While minor variations in ranking occurred across scenarios, the overall trends regarding sensor model performance remained consistent. Ch:RW and AirSim models consistently ranked among the best performers, while the Ch:Gauss model consistently ranked lower (Table II). This suggests that the VEPD metric, and the relative performance of the sensor models, are largely robust to different vehicle maneuvers.

**D. Agnosticism of VEPD to other sim2real differences:**
To assess the VEPD's robustness to non-sensor-related sim2real differences (e.g., vehicle dynamics, environment), the Ch:RW model was tested against real-world data from two different environments: grass and inclined concrete. The VEPD for sim2grass was 0.0796, and for sim2concrete, it was 0.0867, representing an approximate 8% difference. This similarity in VEPD values, despite substantial changes in environment and likely vehicle dynamics (Figure 5), indicates that the VEPD metric remains largely consistent and is not significantly confounded by sim2real differences unrelated to the GPS/IMU sensor modeling itself. This characteristic allows for a more focused evaluation of sensor models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics and autonomous systems by addressing a critical challenge: the quantification of the sim2real gap for fundamental navigation sensors.

**Significance:**
The primary significance lies in the introduction of the Velocity Estimation Performance Difference (VEPD) metric. This novel, application-specific methodology provides a systematic and quantitative approach to evaluate the realism of IMU and GPS sensor simulations, specifically within the context of velocity state estimation tasks for autonomous agents. To the authors' knowledge, this represents the first attempt to quantify the sim2real gap for this specific sensor modality in a downstream robotics application. The VEPD metric is demonstrated to be capable of distinguishing sensor model performance and remains robust to confounding factors arising from other sim2real differences like varied vehicle dynamics or environments.

The comprehensive benchmarking of five GPS sensor model variants provides empirical insights into their performance characteristics. The findings highlight the critical role of measurement noise covariance modeling in achieving realistic GPS simulations, particularly for state estimators. Furthermore, the effectiveness of a random walk-based GPS noise model (Ch:RW) in capturing real-world noise patterns is a notable finding, as is the observation that for high-precision GPS, minimizing simulated noise can improve fidelity.

**Potential Impact:**
The established framework and the VEPD metric have several potential impacts:

- **Improved Sensor Model Development:** The methodology can guide developers in creating more accurate and realistic IMU and GPS sensor models for simulation environments. By providing a clear metric to assess fidelity, it enables targeted refinement of simulation parameters and noise characteristics.
- **Accelerated Algorithm Development:** More realistic sensor simulations reduce the sim2real gap for state estimation algorithms, allowing developers to test and refine their algorithms more effectively in simulation. This can lead to faster development cycles and reduced reliance on expensive and time-consuming real-world testing.
- **Enhanced Autonomous System Reliability:** Algorithms validated with higher-fidelity sensor simulations are likely to perform more robustly and reliably when deployed in physical autonomous agents, contributing to safer operation.
- **Community Resource:** The open-source dataset and code released alongside this paper provide a valuable resource for the robotics community. Researchers can use this data for benchmarking new sensor models, developing advanced sim2real gap mitigation techniques, or for educational purposes.
- **Generalizability:** While focused on GPS/IMU, the core concepts of the VEPD metric, particularly the use of downstream task performance and signal characteristics, could be adapted to quantify the sim2real gap for other sensor modalities in robotics.
- **Future Research Direction:** The study also identifies avenues for future work, such as evaluating VEPD's robustness across different state estimators ("judges") and exploring velocity estimation in GPS-challenged environments, which could further refine and generalize the methodology. Future comparative studies with other popular simulators like CARLA and Gazebo are also planned, which could provide broader insights into their integrated GPS/IMU sensor accuracy.
