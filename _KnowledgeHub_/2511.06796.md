# Human-Level Actuation for Humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> Teragon Research introduces a quantitative framework, the Human-Level Actuation Score (HLAS), for evaluating humanoid robot actuation against human biomechanical capabilities, integrating six physically motivated factors and reproducible measurement protocols. The framework's diagnostic decomposition identifies specific performance bottlenecks, as demonstrated in a synthetic example where a robot's knee and hip actuation proved insufficient for stair climbing.

## Problem

- Humanoid robotics lacks standardized, quantitative definitions and reproducible testing methodologies for "human-level" or "human-like" actuation claims, hindering objective comparison.
- Existing actuator performance specifications (e.g., peak torque) often omit critical operational conditions like posture, angular rate, or thermal state.
- Decades of human biomechanics data on joint moments and powers have not been systematically translated into explicit, robot-testable performance requirements.

## Method

- The paper introduces the **Human-Level** ==Actuation Score (HLAS)==, a comprehensive framework for quantifying robot actuation against human capabilities, incorporating six physically motivated factors.
- It defines Human-Equivalence Envelopes (HEE) to evaluate whether a robot can simultaneously meet human torque and power demands within task-relevant operating bands.
- The framework establishes detailed, reproducible measurement protocols for each HLAS factor, ensuring evaluations are based on continuous-safe operation under realistic thermal and electrical conditions.

## Results

- The framework successfully operationalizes the qualitative concept of "human-level actuation" into a quantitative, measurable, and comparable Human-Level Actuation Score (HLAS).
- Human-Equivalence Envelopes (HEE) effectively enforce the simultaneous delivery of human-level torque and power within specific (q, ω) operating points, preventing claims based on isolated peak specifications.
- A synthetic worked example demonstrated the framework's diagnostic power by identifying critical weaknesses, such as a robot's knee and hip actuation being insufficient for sustained stair climbing, despite adequate performance in other areas like bandwidth.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A holistic assessment of human-level actuation requires simultaneous consideration of torque, power, bandwidth, efficiency, and thermal sustainability, as optimizing one aspect often creates trade-offs in others.
> - Translating human biomechanics into robot requirements demands standardized kinematic foundations, a reference human body for scaling, and task-derived operating bands where humans perform significant positive mechanical work.
> - The hierarchical, decomposable nature of HLAS allows for diagnostic analysis, pinpointing specific actuation bottlenecks at the task, joint, and individual feature level.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics frequently makes claims of "human-level" or "human-like" actuation, yet these claims often lack standardized, quantitative definitions or reproducible testing methodologies. Existing performance specifications, such as peak torque or no-load speeds, are insufficient because they typically fail to specify the operational conditions (e.g., posture, angular rate, duration, thermal state) under which these values apply. This ambiguity hinders objective comparison between different robotic systems and obscures the complex trade-offs inherent in actuator design.

Decades of research in human biomechanics have extensively quantified human joint capabilities, including moments, powers, ranges of motion (ROM), and energetic demands across a wide array of functional tasks like walking, stair climbing, and manipulation. These data, often normalized by body mass, provide a rich reference for establishing human performance envelopes. However, a systematic framework for translating these biological data into explicit, robot-testable requirements has been largely absent.

Actuator and transmission design involves fundamental trade-offs among various performance metrics, such as torque density, bandwidth, efficiency, backdrivability, and thermal capacity. For instance, series elastic actuators (SEAs) offer compliance for force control but can reduce bandwidth, while quasi-direct-drive (QDD) systems prioritize backdrivability and bandwidth at the cost of motor size and thermal stress. The choice of gearbox (e.g., strain-wave, cycloidal, planetary) also significantly impacts efficiency, friction, and reflected inertia. Current evaluation methods often fail to capture how these coupled design choices manifest in sustained, task-relevant performance or interaction quality, leading to designs optimized for isolated peak specifications rather than integrated functional utility. The paper highlights the importance of "interaction quality," drawing from haptics and impedance control theory, which emphasize that factors like torque-mode bandwidth and transmission friction are crucial for stable, responsive human-robot interaction.

This work addresses these gaps by integrating existing biomechanical knowledge, actuator physics, and measurement science into a unified framework. It aims to bridge the qualitative nature of "human-level" claims with a quantitative, auditable, and comparative evaluation methodology, building upon foundational work in human movement science, robotics actuation, and control systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a comprehensive and quantitative framework for evaluating "human-level actuation" in humanoid robots. This framework aims to make such claims measurable, comparable, and grounded in scientific principles.

The motivation for this work arises from several critical limitations in the current state of humanoid robotics:

1. **Lack of Quantitative Grounding for "Human-Level" Claims:** Humanoid robotics platforms frequently assert "human-level" or "human-like" actuation capabilities, but these claims are rarely substantiated with standardized definitions or reproducible tests. Specifications like peak torque or speed often omit crucial context, such as the specific joint posture, angular rate, or duration for which these values apply. This ambiguity makes it challenging to ascertain whether a robot can genuinely deliver the necessary combination of torque, power, and endurance at task-relevant operating points.

2. **Operationalizing Biomechanics Data into Robot Requirements:** Decades of human biomechanics research have accumulated extensive data on joint moments, powers, ranges of motion, and angular velocities across various tasks and populations. This rich body of knowledge, however, has not been systematically translated into explicit, measurable performance criteria for robotic systems. A key motivation is to operationalize this human data into a set of explicit acceptance tests for robot actuators, defining precisely what "human-level" entails for each joint and task. This involves:
 - **Standardizing Kinematics:** Establishing a common reference frame for comparing human and robot joints using International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) conventions for joint coordinate systems, axis directions, and sign conventions.
 - **Reference Body and Scaling:** Adopting a standardized human reference body (e.g., a 75 kg, 1.75 m adult male) and consistent anthropometric scaling conventions to convert mass-normalized human biomechanics data into absolute torque and power requirements for robots.
 - **Task-Derived Operating Bands:** Defining specific regions in joint angle and angular velocity space (q, ω) where humans perform positive mechanical work for particular tasks. This ensures that robot performance is evaluated where it is functionally relevant.

3. **Exposing Actuator Trade-offs:** Current evaluation metrics often fail to reveal the inherent trade-offs in actuator design, such as those between torque density, bandwidth, efficiency, transparency, and thermal capacity. For example, high gearing can increase static torque but degrade backdrivability and torque-mode bandwidth, which are critical for safe, responsive interaction. The paper is motivated to develop a metric that exposes these coupled trade-offs, preventing designs that optimize for isolated specifications rather than holistic, real-world task performance. This includes recognizing that interaction quality, determined by factors like closed-loop torque bandwidth and reflected inertia, is as crucial as nominal torque capability.

4. **Enabling Reproducible Measurement and Benchmarking:** A significant motivation is to establish detailed, reproducible experimental protocols for measuring every input to the proposed scoring system. These protocols aim to ensure that performance claims are based on "continuous-safe" operation (sustained performance under realistic thermal and electrical conditions), thereby preventing claims based on transient burst capabilities.

In summary, the key objectives are to provide a quantitative framework that accurately defines, measures, and compares human-level actuation in humanoids, grounded in biomechanics, while transparently exposing actuator design trade-offs and enabling robust, reproducible benchmarking. This framework is intended to serve as both a design guide for humanoid development and a standardized tool for objective performance comparison.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology proposed for quantifying human-level actuation comprises three core components, supported by detailed measurement and benchmark protocols.

#### 3.1. Kinematic Foundation and DoF Atlas (Section 2, 1.4, 1.8.1)

The first component establishes a standardized kinematic reference. The methodology adopts International Society of Biomechanics (ISB) recommendations for joint coordinate systems across the lower limb, spine, and upper limb, supplemented by established conventions for specific joints (e.g., Grood-Suntay for the knee). This ensures a common anatomical reference frame, where terms like "ankle dorsiflexion" refer to the same axis, origin, and positive direction for both humans and robots.
- **DoF Atlas:** A visual and tabular reference specifies axis directions, signs, and functional ranges of motion (ROM) for every major human joint, including digits.
- **Workspace Definition:** The atlas defines the functional joint-angle ranges (I_func_j,t(a)) over which robot performance is evaluated, ensuring robots are not credited for capabilities in postures humans rarely use.
- **DoF Accounting:** It enumerates rotational and translational degrees of freedom per joint, providing a basis for verifying that a robot implements the necessary axes for a given task.

#### 3.2. Human-Equivalence Envelopes (HEE) (Section 4, 1.5, 1.8.3)

HEE defines per-joint performance requirements by comparing a robot's capabilities against human biomechanical data.
- **Biomechanics Synthesis (Section 3, 1.8.2):** Human joint moment, power, and angular velocity data are systematically reviewed and synthesized from literature. These mass-normalized values (Nm/kg, W/kg) are converted to absolute targets for a reference human (75 kg, 1.75 m adult male) using de Leva anthropometry. This generates human demand fields T_hum_j,t(q, ω) and P_hum_j,t(q, ω) for each joint j and task t.
- **Task-Derived Operating Bands (R_j,t):** From human kinematic and kinetic data, task-specific operating bands are defined as specific (q, ω) regions where humans produce significant positive mechanical work. For example, the ankle push-off band for walking is defined within specific plantarflexion angles and angular rates.
- **Simultaneity Condition:** The HEE metric measures whether a robot can meet human torque and power requirements *simultaneously* at the same (q, ω) point within these operating bands. This condition prevents "gaming" by achieving high torque at low speed and high speed at low torque, without ever performing at a relevant operating point.
- **Positive-Work Weighting:** The assessment is weighted by the positive mechanical work humans produce at each (q, ω) point, focusing credit on functionally important regions.

#### 3.3. Human-Level Actuation Score (HLAS) (Section 4, 1.8.4)

HLAS is a scalar score that aggregates six physically motivated factors:
- **Workspace Coverage (ρ_ROM, d_DoF):** Quantifies the fraction of task-required ROM covered by the robot's safe ROM and whether all necessary degrees of freedom are present and independently actuated.
- **HEE Coverage (h^(w)):** The power-weighted fraction of the operating band where the robot meets simultaneous human torque and power demands.
- **Torque-Mode Bandwidth (b_τ):** Measures the closed-loop responsiveness of the joint to torque commands under task-representative loading, reflecting interaction quality.
- **Task-Weighted Efficiency (η):** Assesses how efficiently electrical power is converted to mechanical power within the operating band, weighted by positive human power.
- **Thermal Sustainability (θ_therm):** Quantifies the joint's ability to sustain required torque plateaus over the task's duty cycle without thermal derating.
- **Aggregation:** These six factors are combined for each joint-task pair (j,t) into a feature vector x_j,t. This vector is then aggregated using pre-registered feature weights (α), joint weights (u_j,t, derived from biomechanical work distribution), and task weights (w_t, reflecting mission importance) to yield a final HLAS between 0 and 1. A human achieves HLAS=1.0 by definition.
- **Diagnostic Decomposition:** The score is designed to be decomposable into task-level, joint-task, and individual feature contributions, enabling detailed diagnosis of strengths and weaknesses.

#### 3.4. Reproducible Measurement and Benchmark Protocols (Section 5 & 6, 1.8.5)

Detailed experimental procedures are provided to gather the inputs for HLAS, ensuring reproducibility and comparability.
- **Continuous-Safe Measurement:** All measurements (torque, power, efficiency, thermal) must reflect sustained capabilities under realistic conditions (e.g., after thermal warm-up, constrained temperature rise < 0.5°C/s, no current saturation).
- **Instrumentation:** Standardized sensors are required (high-resolution encoders, in-line torque transducers or validated observers, DC-bus power meters, temperature sensors).
- **Protocols for Factors:** Specific protocols are outlined for each HLAS factor, including:
 - **ROM/DoF:** Slow sweeps with current limits; chirp tests for cross-axis coupling.
 - **HEE:** Isovelocity sweeps on a discretized (q, ω) grid within R_j,t, ramping torque to find continuous-safe limits.
 - **Bandwidth:** Small-signal sine sweeps in torque mode with task-representative loads to find -3dB crossover frequency.
 - **Efficiency:** Measurement of mechanical and electrical power at sub-thermal loads over the R_j,t grid.
 - **Thermal Sustainability:** Execution of task-specific duty profiles (cadence, dwell times) until thermal steady-state to determine continuous plateau torque.
- **Pre-registration:** To prevent "gaming" of the score, all task weights, joint weights, feature weights, operating bands, and target values for bandwidth and efficiency must be pre-registered before testing.
- **Benchmark Protocols (Section 6):** Complementary task-level benchmarks (e.g., gait, lifting, reaching, hand dexterity) are specified. These trials, performed on the whole robot, validate that joint-level capabilities translate to functional performance and expose system-level integration issues not captured by single-joint dynamometer tests.

This comprehensive approach combines detailed biomechanical and kinematic foundations with robust measurement protocols and a multi-faceted scoring system to provide a quantifiable and diagnostic assessment of humanoid actuation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

This paper primarily introduces a methodological framework and demonstrates its properties and utility through a worked example, rather than presenting new experimental results from a physical robot. The "findings" therefore relate to the capabilities and insights offered by the framework itself.

1. **Quantification of "Human-Level" Claims:** The framework successfully operationalizes the qualitative concept of "human-level actuation" into a quantitative and measurable score (HLAS). This provides a standardized language for describing and comparing robot capabilities.

2. **Human-Equivalence Envelope (HEE) as a Core Metric:** The HEE demonstrates its ability to enforce simultaneous torque and power delivery requirements at specific, task-relevant joint angle and angular velocity (q, ω) operating points. This is a critical finding as it prevents superficial claims of human-level performance based on isolated peak torque or speed values obtained under different, unrelated conditions. By weighting performance by the positive mechanical work humans produce, HEE focuses evaluation on functionally meaningful regions of the joint's operating space.

3. **Comprehensive Actuation Assessment (HLAS Factors):** The Human-Level Actuation Score (HLAS) integrates six physically grounded factors, showing that a holistic assessment requires considering:
 - **Workspace coverage:** ROM and DoF sufficiency.
 - **Delivery capability:** HEE coverage.
 - **Interaction quality:** Torque-mode bandwidth.
 - **Energetic realism:** Task-weighted efficiency.
 - **Sustained performance:** Thermal sustainability.
 This multi-factorial approach reveals that optimizing for one aspect (e.g., peak torque) often compromises others, thereby exposing the inherent trade-offs in actuator design.

4. **Diagnostic Decomposition Capability:** A key result is the hierarchical decomposition of HLAS. The overall score can be broken down into task-level scores (s_t), joint-task scores (s_j,t), and individual feature vector contributions (x_j,t). This diagnostic capability is critical for identifying specific bottlenecks (e.g., a particular joint's thermal limit in a specific task) and guiding design iterations, a substantial improvement over single, aggregated metrics that obscure root causes.

5. **Resistance to Gaming:** The paper demonstrates, through theoretical analysis and the structure of the metrics, that HLAS is difficult to "game." The simultaneity requirement of HEE, the weighting by positive human power, the inclusion of coupled physical factors (bandwidth, thermal), and the continuous-safe measurement protocols collaboratively prevent designs optimized for paper specifications rather than genuine, sustained performance. Formal guardrails (e.g., breadth floors, headroom factors) are proposed to further enhance robustness against gaming.

6. **Worked Example Demonstrating Design Trade-offs:** The synthetic worked example (Section 7) is a crucial "result" for demonstrating the framework's practical application.
 - **Identified Bottleneck:** For the hypothetical robot with an HLAS of 0.636, the primary weakness was identified as the **knee and hip HEE in stair climbing**, scoring very low (approximately 0.085). This specific finding highlights that the robot's actuators, despite potentially adequate peak torque, lacked sufficient continuous torque at the low angular rates required for sustained support during stair ascent. This contrasts with strong performance in other areas like bandwidth and thermal margins.
 - **Relative Strengths:** The robot exhibited relatively stronger performance in walking (s_Walk = 0.671) and reaching (s_Reach = 0.687), primarily driven by adequate ankle positive-power delivery and shoulder/elbow capabilities.
 - **Impact of Design Choices:** The example also showed how a secondary limitation, such as a restricted wrist ROM, contributed to a lower score in manipulation tasks.
 - **Sensitivity Analysis:** Implementing a 10% headroom factor in the HEE test reduced the overall HLAS from 0.636 to 0.515, demonstrating that a significant portion of the robot's performance was near the minimum human requirement threshold, and that the score is sensitive to such design choices and robustness margins.

7. **Reproducible Measurement Protocols:** The detailed, step-by-step measurement protocols (Section 5) for all HLAS inputs, coupled with benchmark protocols (Section 6) for joint-level validation and task-level integration, provide a concrete and auditable methodology for assessing actuator performance. This is a foundational output of the research.

In essence, the main findings demonstrate that the proposed HLAS framework is a robust, transparent, and diagnostic tool capable of translating complex biomechanical and engineering principles into a unified, interpretable score for humanoid actuation. The worked example illustrates how this framework can effectively expose design strengths and critical weaknesses in a robot's actuation system.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the Human-Level Actuation Score (HLAS) framework carries significant implications across several dimensions of humanoid robotics and related fields:

1. **Standardization of "Human-Level" Claims:** HLAS provides a much-needed, quantitative standard for substantiating claims of "human-level" or "human-like" actuation. By moving beyond vague assertions to concrete, measurable metrics grounded in biomechanics, it enables objective comparison of actuation systems across different humanoid platforms and research laboratories. This standardization can foster a more rigorous and transparent research environment.

2. **Guidance for Actuator and Robot Design:** The framework serves as a direct design specification for engineers developing actuators and transmissions. The diagnostic decomposition of HLAS allows designers to pinpoint specific bottlenecks—whether it's insufficient torque in a particular posture-rate band (HEE), low bandwidth, poor efficiency, or inadequate thermal capacity—for specific tasks. This granular feedback can guide iterative design processes, helping engineers make informed trade-offs (e.g., between gear ratio, motor size, and cooling strategies) to optimize performance for target applications.

3. **Enhanced Benchmarking and Progress Tracking:** HLAS establishes a common benchmarking standard that can accelerate humanoid development. Similar to how standardized benchmarks (like FLOPS for computational power) have driven progress in computing, HLAS can provide clear metrics for tracking improvements in actuation capabilities over time. This allows for a clearer understanding of the state-of-the-art and areas requiring further research investment.

4. **Improved Procurement and Acceptance Testing:** For commercial and government entities procuring humanoid robots, HLAS offers a robust tool for defining explicit acceptance criteria. Instead of subjective evaluations, procurement can specify target HLAS values for critical tasks and joints, ensuring that purchased robots meet functional requirements for deployment. The complementary task-level benchmarks further validate that component-level capabilities translate into integrated, real-world performance, thereby reducing integration risks.

5. **Advancement in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) and Safety:** By quantifying metrics like torque-mode bandwidth and backdrivability, HLAS directly informs the design of safe and responsive human-robot interaction systems. Actuators with high bandwidth and low reflected inertia are crucial for compliant contact and stable impedance control, which are essential for collaboration with humans. The framework brings these critical but often overlooked factors into the forefront of performance evaluation.

6. **Structured Priors for Learning and Control:** The biomechanically derived operating bands (R_j,t) and human reference targets provide structured priors for advanced control and learning algorithms. Reinforcement learning agents or trajectory optimizers can use these bands as safe exploration boundaries, prioritize learning in high positive-power regions, or set human-like performance targets, leading to more biologically plausible and efficient robot behaviors.

7. **Transparency and Auditability:** The requirement for detailed measurement protocols, pre-registration of parameters, and the publication of raw data logs, HEE heatmaps, and thermal traces significantly enhances the transparency and auditability of performance claims. This fosters trust in reported results and allows the broader scientific community to independently verify, analyze, and build upon the framework.

8. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper explicitly discusses limitations and outlines future directions, such as extending HLAS to incorporate population variability, negative work and energy regeneration, dynamic tasks, and multi-modal actuation. This positions HLAS as a foundational framework that can be continuously refined and expanded by the robotics community.

In conclusion, HLAS represents a significant step towards a more scientific and engineering-driven assessment of humanoid robot actuation. By providing a clear, reproducible, and diagnostic framework, it promises to accelerate design, improve benchmarking, enhance safety, and foster greater transparency in the development of truly human-level robotic systems.
