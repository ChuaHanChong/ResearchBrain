# COM Kitchens: An Unedited Overhead-view Video Dataset as a Vision-Language Benchmark

> [!summary] Summary
> The COM Kitchens dataset offers 40 hours of unedited, overhead-view cooking videos from 145 diverse home environments, annotated with fine-grained "visual action graphs" that link instructional texts to visual events and object interactions. This work introduces two benchmarks, Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR) and Dense Video Captioning on Overhead-View videos (DVC-OV), revealing a significant domain gap for current vision-language models and demonstrating the utility of structured graph-based supervision for improved video understanding.

## Problem

- Most existing procedural video datasets rely on edited web videos, which do not represent raw, unedited observations needed for robust real-world instructional retrieval.
- Fixed-viewpoint datasets, while offering advantages like consistent object tracking, historically lack environmental diversity due to high collection costs and technical support requirements, leading to a "cold-start problem" for raw-video-based systems.
- Linguistic annotations in many datasets are coarse, describing high-level steps rather than fine-grained actions and object state changes, limiting deep comprehension of complex procedures.

## Method

- Collected COM Kitchens, a new dataset of 145 unedited, overhead-view cooking videos from 70 unique home kitchens using distributed smartphones, enabling diverse data capture at reduced cost.
- Introduced "visual action graphs," a novel fine-grained annotation scheme linking instructional texts to specific visual events, element movements, and bounding boxes via temporal and compositional relationships.
- Proposed two novel benchmark tasks: Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR) to identify recipes and cooking stages from partial, raw video, and Dense Video Captioning on Overhead-View videos (DVC-OV) to analyze the domain gap for unedited fixed-viewpoint content.

## Results

- OnRR experiments showed baseline models (UniVL, CLIP4Clip, X-CLIP) often struggled with feasible recipe retrieval from partial videos, frequently not outperforming random selection, though they demonstrated better performance in identifying the exact cooking stage within a given recipe after fine-tuning.
- DVC-OV revealed a significant domain gap, with zero-shot performance of state-of-the-art models (PDVC, Vid2Seq) being very low (e.g., SODA_c scores around 0.02) compared to their performance on web-based datasets.
- Fine-tuning Vid2Seq with a combined approach of Action Graph as Relation Labels and Attention Supervision (RL+AS) significantly improved DVC-OV performance (SODA_c 0.581, CIDEr 6.195), demonstrating the effectiveness of leveraging structured graph annotations for better temporal alignment and caption generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unedited, fixed-viewpoint video data from diverse home environments can be effectively and affordably collected using modern smartphone technology.
> - Fine-grained "visual action graph" annotations provide a rich, structured representation of procedural knowledge, enabling deeper understanding of dynamic object interactions and state changes.
> - There is a substantial domain gap between models pre-trained on edited web videos and their performance on unedited, overhead-view procedural content, underscoring the need for domain-specific adaptation and structured supervision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of procedural video understanding within the broader vision-language community. Current advancements in this area largely rely on large-scale datasets sourced from the web, such as YouCookII and HowTo100M, or collected through egocentric vision, exemplified by Epic Kitchens and Ego4D. While these datasets have facilitated the development of robust techniques and pre-trained models, they present several limitations. Web videos are often edited and curated, which can obscure the raw, unedited instructional content necessary for certain applications. Egocentric datasets, while capturing real-world interactions, may not provide a comprehensive view of a workstation or minimize occlusion effectively.

A notable gap exists in datasets featuring fixed-viewpoint (FV) observations, which were common before the deep learning era but have received less attention due to the difficulty of finding such unedited footage online. These FV datasets traditionally suffered from a lack of environmental diversity due to high camera setup costs and technical support requirements. This leads to a "cold-start problem" for developing raw-video-based retrieval systems, as sufficient initial data is a prerequisite for their implementation.

Furthermore, existing linguistic annotations in many procedural video datasets tend to be coarse, describing high-level steps rather than fine-grained actions and their effects on objects. Datasets like BioVL2 and VRF have begun to address the need for fine-grained instructions, but often face limitations in scale, task variation, or video duration.

This work aims to address these identified limitations by revisiting and advancing fixed-viewpoint video datasets. It leverages modern hardware to efficiently collect diverse environmental data and introduces a novel, fine-grained annotation scheme. By doing so, it positions itself to enable more detailed comprehension of long procedural contexts and to bridge the domain gap between edited web videos and unedited, overhead-view recordings, thereby enriching the resources available for vision-language research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance procedural video understanding in the vision-language domain by introducing a novel dataset that addresses specific limitations of existing resources. The motivation stems from several identified challenges in current deep learning-based video analysis:

Firstly, a significant portion of research in procedural video understanding relies on web-sourced videos. These videos are often edited, summarized, or specifically produced for demonstration, which means they do not always represent raw, unedited observational data. This characteristic makes it challenging for models trained on such data to robustly query instructional content directly from unedited video observations, a capability desired for practical applications.

Secondly, fixed-viewpoint (FV) video datasets, while offering advantages like consistent object tracking and minimal occlusion from an overhead perspective, have historically been limited in environmental diversity. This limitation is primarily due to the high costs associated with setting up specialized camera equipment and providing in-person technical assistance for data collection in varied settings. The paper identifies a "cold-start problem," where the absence of a sufficiently large and diverse dataset of raw, fixed-viewpoint videos hinders the development of systems that could then encourage users to upload more such content.

Thirdly, there is a recognized need for richer and more structured linguistic annotations. Many existing vision-language datasets provide coarse-grained instructions or narrations that describe overall actions without detailing the specific interactions between objects or the states of ingredients before and after an action. This lack of fine-grained, structured annotation limits the depth of understanding that models can derive from the videos, particularly for complex, multi-step procedures like cooking.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of this work are four-fold:
1. **Dataset Construction**: To introduce COM Kitchens, a new dataset consisting of unedited overhead-view videos of cooking activities. This dataset specifically aims to achieve environmental diversity by distributing modern smartphones equipped with wide-angle lenses to participants, enabling capture of entire cooking counters without requiring in-person assistance, thereby mitigating traditional data collection costs.
2. **Structured Annotation**: To provide a novel form of manual annotation for these unedited videos, termed "visual action graphs." These graphs link instructional texts to specific visual events and element movements through temporal connections and bounding boxes, offering a structured representation of the procedural workflow.
3. **Novel Task Introduction (Online Recipe Retrieval - OnRR)**: To propose the task of Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR). This task is designed to evaluate a system's ability to retrieve corresponding recipes and identify the current cooking stage from partial, raw video observations, thereby simulating practical, real-time smartphone applications.
4. **Novel Domain for Video Captioning (Dense Video Captioning on Overhead-View videos - DVC-OV)**: To establish a new domain for Dense Video Captioning (DVC-OV) on unedited overhead-view videos. This objective is intended to facilitate the analysis of the domain gap between models trained on edited web videos and those required to understand unedited, fixed-viewpoint procedural content. The evaluation aims to assess the capabilities and limitations of current state-of-the-art methods in handling these challenging new tasks and data characteristics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the COM Kitchens dataset, focusing on its collection, annotation, and the two novel benchmark tasks proposed.

#### 3.1. Data Collection

The dataset comprises videos of food preparation based on recipes from the Cookpad Recipe Dataset (CRD), a Japanese home cuisine database. Recipes were selected for an estimated preparation time under 30 minutes, moderate complexity, and avoidance of off-counter facilities (e.g., microwaves).
Data collection involved 110 participants hired between August and October 2021, each recording up to four recipes in their homes. To ensure diverse environments and minimize setup costs, participants used an iPhone 11 Pro fixed to a tripod, recording at 30 frames per second (fps) in Full HD (1920x1080) resolution using the ultra-wide field of view (13mm focal length). Participants received instructional documents and videos covering recording guidelines (e.g., cooking alone, camera placement, privacy exclusion).
Out of 410 recorded videos, 210 were curated, with common rejection reasons including inappropriate camera view (50.5%) and visible faces (24.0%). Ultimately, 145 videos, totaling 40 hours of footage, were manually annotated, with the remaining 85 videos reserved as an unsupervised resource. For experiments, videos were downscaled to 640x480 resolution.

#### 3.2. Grounding Text to Video via Visual Action Graph

A core component of the methodology is the "visual action graph" annotation, which formally delineates intentional actions performed by a person (Action-by-Person, AP) on ingredients into a temporal graph.
Each AP is defined as a tuple `a_k = (w_k, A_k, s_k, f_k)`, where:
- `w_k` is the word sequence of the procedural text (e.g., an instruction like "add the onion and the celery stalk").
- `A_k` is a set of visual actions in the video corresponding to `w_k`, with each `a_k^(r)` representing bounding boxes for the target ingredient *before* the action (`b_k^(r,bef)`), *after* the action (`b_k^(r,aft)`), and optionally the *destination* (`b_k^(r,dest)`) where the ingredient is mixed.
- `s_k` and `f_k` denote the start and finish frames of the AP, determined by the first and last bounding box.
Nodes in the visual action graph are the bounding boxes. Edges track ingredient changes and movements:
- **Intra-action edges**: Automatically connect `b_k^(r,bef)` to `b_k^(r,aft)` and `b_k^(r,dest)` to `b_k^(r,aft)` within the same AP.
- **Inter-action edges**: Manually identified connections from `b_k^(r,aft)` of one AP to `b_k^(r',bef/dest)` of another, tracking ingredients across different steps.
Ingredient names are manually assigned as leaf nodes and connected to the AP that first processes them, allowing for a representation of ingredient composition at each node.

#### 3.3. Annotation Procedure

Annotation was performed by a domain specialist from February to September 2023, requiring approximately 430 hours. The process involved:
1. Reviewing videos and revising procedural instruction texts to match actual participant actions, as deviations from original recipes were common.
2. Identifying and tagging APs within the instructions. All Japanese instructions were translated into English by experts.
3. Delineating start/finish times and bounding boxes (`b_k^(r,bef)`, `b_k^(r,aft)`, `b_k^(r,dest)`) for each action using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT), with videos examined every five frames.
4. Manually assigning inter-action edges, ingredients, and ingredient-action edges to construct the complete visual action graphs. A total of 6,826 bounding boxes and 8,061 relationships were annotated.

#### 3.4. Dataset Statistics

COM Kitchens includes 145 videos from 70 unique kitchens/actors, totaling 40 hours of footage. The average video duration is 16.6 minutes. 80.0% of video frames contain labeled APs, with a 15.0% overlap. The average AP duration is 46.7 seconds.
Linguistically, the dataset contains 949 sentences of fine instructions, averaging 6.5 sentences per video. The average word lengths for recipes and sentences are 87.2 and 13.3, respectively. There are 131 types of APs (2,286 distinct instances, averaging 15.8 per recipe) and 305 types of ingredients (1,187 instances, averaging 8.2 per recipe). Both APs and ingredients exhibit a long-tail distribution.

#### 3.5. Proposed Tasks

Two novel tasks are proposed using the COM Kitchens dataset:
- **Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR)**: This task involves analyzing a partial cooking video (up to Z% completion) to determine the recipe type and the current cooking stage. It is decomposed into:
 - *Feasible Recipe Retrieval*: Identifying recipes that could be followed given the ingredients and actions already performed.
 - *Recipe Stage Identification*: Pinpointing the exact cooking step within a given recipe.
 Experiments used Z values of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% (early, middle, late, full stages). Baselines included UniVL, CLIP4Clip, and X-CLIP, compared against random selection. Recipe texts were shortened (nouns and verbs extracted) to fit model token limits. An "extra recipe resource" from CRD (1,828 candidates) was used to expand retrieval targets and include hard negative examples. Metrics were Recall@K (R@K) and Median Rank (MdR).

- **Dense Video Captioning on Unedited Fixed-viewpoint Videos (DVC-OV)**: This task requires detecting distinct event segments (APs) in a video and generating descriptive captions for each. The challenge lies in the domain gap with traditional DVC targets (web videos).
 Baselines were PDVC and Vid2Seq, evaluated in zero-shot and fine-tuned settings. Two supervision approaches leveraging the action graph were explored during fine-tuning: (i) Action Graph as Relation Labels (RL) using a TablERT-CNN decoder for relation prediction, and (ii) Action Graph as Attention Supervision (AS) by minimizing Kullback-Leibler divergence between self-attention and alignment matrices. A combination of RL+AS was also tested. Metrics included SODA_c, CIDEr, and METEOR.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on COM Kitchens provided insights into the performance of existing state-of-the-art methods on the proposed tasks.

#### 4.1. Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR)

For the OnRR task, the performance varied significantly between the two subtasks:
- **Feasible Recipe Retrieval**: Baseline models (UniVL, CLIP4Clip, X-CLIP) did not consistently outperform random selection in retrieving feasible recipes across different cooking stages (25%, 50%, 75%, 100%). For instance, in the early stage (25%), UniVL achieved an R@1 of 3.4% without fine-tuning, which slightly decreased or remained similar with fine-tuning. X-CLIP's R@1 was 0.0% in the middle stage with fine-tuning. This suggests that simple contrastive learning strategies, as implemented in these models, struggle with the nuanced task of identifying a broad set of suitable recipes from partial, raw video observations. The performance decline in R@K values for feasible recipe retrieval as the cooking stage progressed (e.g., from 991 to 5 feasible recipes from early to full stage) highlights the increasing difficulty of the task.
- **Recipe Stage Identification**: In contrast, all baseline models demonstrated an ability to outperform random selection in identifying the exact cooking stage within a given recipe. Fine-tuning on the COM Kitchens dataset generally improved these models' performance. For example, UniVL's R@1 for early stage identification increased from 6.8% without fine-tuning to 17.2% with fine-tuning. Similarly, X-CLIP achieved an R@1 of 10.3% for the full stage with fine-tuning. This indicates that models can learn to align video segments with corresponding parts of a recipe text when the recipe context is provided. The observed improvement with fine-tuning across models and stages suggests the dataset is suitable for training models to understand the temporal progression of steps within a known recipe.

Overall, these results indicate that the OnRR task, especially the feasible recipe retrieval component, presents a significant challenge for current methods, suggesting limitations in their ability to perform both task categorization and fine-grained stage identification simultaneously from unedited video input.

#### 4.2. Dense Video Captioning on Unedited Fixed-viewpoint Videos (DVC-OV)

The evaluation of Dense Video Captioning (DVC) on COM Kitchens revealed a substantial domain gap:
- **Zero-shot Performance**: The zero-shot performance of both PDVC and Vid2Seq was observed to be very low (e.g., SODA_c scores of 0.022 for PDVC and 0.017 for Vid2Seq), significantly below their reported performance on web-based datasets like YouCookII (which have SODA_c scores around 4.9-7.9). This marked deterioration indicates that models pre-trained on edited web videos do not generalize well to unedited, overhead-view procedural videos without adaptation. The absence of speech information in COM Kitchens, often a crucial modality in web-based procedural video datasets, was identified as a contributing factor to this domain gap.
- **Fine-tuning and Action Graph Supervision**: Fine-tuning Vid2Seq on the COM Kitchens dataset without explicit action graph supervision led to a notable improvement in performance (SODA_c 0.369, CIDEr 2.832, METEOR 0.642). This demonstrates the dataset's capacity to facilitate learning once the models are adapted to its specific characteristics.
 The integration of action graph information during fine-tuning yielded varied results:
 - Using Action Graph as Relation Labels (RL) or as Attention Supervision (AS) individually did not consistently surpass the performance of fine-tuning Vid2Seq without such explicit supervision. For instance, Vid2Seq fine-tuned with RL achieved a SODA_c of 0.211, and with AS, 0.266, both lower than fine-tuning alone (0.369).
 - However, the **combination of RL and AS (RL+AS)** resulted in a substantial performance gain across all metrics (SODA_c 0.581, CIDEr 6.195, METEOR 1.142). This improvement was attributed to a better-aligned attention mechanism. Visualizations indicated that the RL+AS supervision helped align frames more effectively to relevant parts of the video (both relative frames defined by the action graph and surrounding frames), leading to smoother attention distribution. This enhanced alignment is presumed to facilitate both the generation of more accurate recipe texts and the precise determination of event segment boundaries.

These findings suggest that while the COM Kitchens dataset presents a significant challenge for off-the-shelf models, models can be adapted through fine-tuning, and the structured "visual action graph" annotations, particularly when leveraged through combined relational and attention supervision, can further enhance their ability to comprehend and describe complex, unedited procedural videos.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The COM Kitchens dataset and its accompanying benchmarks contribute to the field of vision-language understanding in several ways.

Firstly, the dataset introduces a novel approach to collecting unedited, fixed-viewpoint videos in diverse environments. By leveraging modern smartphone technology and a distributed collection methodology, the work addresses the long-standing challenge of obtaining high-quality, varied fixed-viewpoint data without incurring the high costs associated with traditional setups. This aspect has the potential to make large-scale data collection more accessible and cost-effective for similar research in the future.

Secondly, the provision of "visual action graph" annotations for unedited videos is a significant contribution. This fine-grained, structured representation, which links instructional texts to visual events and object interactions via temporal graphs and bounding boxes, offers a richer form of supervision than typically found in existing datasets. This level of detail enables a more profound understanding of procedural tasks, moving beyond coarse instructions to capture the dynamic changes of ingredients and actions.

Thirdly, the introduction of the Online Recipe Retrieval (OnRR) and Dense Video Captioning on unedited Overhead-View videos (DVC-OV) tasks establishes new benchmarks for the vision-language community. These tasks are designed to evaluate the capabilities of models in scenarios that more closely reflect real-world applications (e.g., real-time assistance during cooking) and to expose the limitations of current state-of-the-art methods when confronted with unedited, long-duration procedural videos. The experimental results confirm a significant domain gap between web-sourced videos and COM Kitchens, highlighting the need for specialized approaches.

The findings from the benchmark experiments have the potential to impact future research directions. The observed difficulties of existing models in the feasible recipe retrieval task suggest that current global alignment and contrastive learning methods may be insufficient for complex, open-ended retrieval problems requiring deep procedural comprehension. The superior performance of models fine-tuned with combined relation and attention supervision from the action graph in DVC-OV demonstrates the value of structured annotations and attention mechanisms in enhancing video understanding. This could lead to further exploration of how to effectively integrate graph-based knowledge into vision-language models.

Potential practical applications stemming from this work include the development of advanced smartphone applications that can provide real-time cooking assistance, offer dynamic recipe recommendations based on ongoing actions, or generate detailed, accessible captions for procedural content. Beyond cooking, the methodology could be extended to other domains requiring fine-grained procedural understanding from fixed-viewpoint observations, such as laboratory experiments or assembly tasks.

Finally, the dataset's characteristics, such as long video durations and spatio-temporal ground truth, make it suitable for investigating complex tasks like episodic memory in procedural domains. The ongoing plan to expand the dataset, both with and without supervision, further indicates its long-term potential as a resource for advancing research in the interpretation of complex video content.
