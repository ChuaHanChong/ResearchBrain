# Certifying Stability of Reinforcement Learning Policies using Generalized Lyapunov Functions

> [!summary] Summary
> This research develops a framework for certifying the stability of reinforcement learning policies by introducing generalized Lyapunov functions that relax the traditional one-step decrease condition to a multi-step, weighted average decrease. This approach enables less conservative stability certificates and the synthesis of policies with larger provably stable regions for nonlinear systems.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies, despite empirical success, lack formal stability guarantees, posing a barrier to their deployment in safety-critical applications.
- Classical Lyapunov stability theory's stringent pointwise decrease condition is challenging to apply to complex nonlinear systems with neural network controllers and often results in conservative certificates.
- Existing safe RL methods primarily address safety during learning or under limited conditions, not providing comprehensive post-hoc stability certificates for final learned policies across a wide operating region.

## Method

- A framework employing ==generalized Lyapunov functions== is developed, relaxing the traditional one-step decrease requirement to a multi-step, weighted average decrease criterion.
- Reinforcement learning (RL) policy value functions are augmented with learned neural network residual terms to construct these generalized Lyapunov candidates.
- The methodology extends to jointly synthesize neural network control policies and generalized stability certificates, maximizing the size of provably stable regions through a combined loss function and formal verification.

## Results

- Generalized Lyapunov functions analytically demonstrated significantly less conservative stability bounds for discounted Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problems, with larger multi-step horizons reducing conservatism.
- Empirical evaluations successfully certified stability for pre-trained reinforcement learning policies on nonlinear control tasks, showing generalized Lyapunov functions consistently satisfy the multi-step decrease condition across tested states.
- Joint synthesis leveraging the generalized framework generated neural network controllers with substantially larger formally verifiable regions of attraction compared to one-step methods, despite incurring longer verification times for higher multi-step horizons.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Relaxing the classical one-step Lyapunov decrease to a multi-step weighted average allows for greater flexibility in certification, accommodating transient non-monotonic behavior often seen in RL value functions.
> - Augmenting discounted optimal value functions with learned neural network residuals provides a practical way to construct generalized Lyapunov candidates for stability analysis in complex systems.
> - Optimized multi-step weights in the generalized condition can strategically distribute the required decrease over time, tolerating initial increases while ensuring overall stability across the horizon.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses one of the most significant open problems in modern control engineering and artificial intelligence: bridging the gap between the empirical success of Reinforcement Learning (RL) in solving complex control tasks and the lack of formal, verifiable stability guarantees for the resulting policies. While RL has demonstrated remarkable capabilities in optimizing long-term performance for highly nonlinear and uncertain systems, the learned policies often operate as "black boxes," making it difficult to predict or assure their behavior, especially in safety-critical domains like autonomous driving, aerospace, or medical robotics.

The broader research landscape can be segmented into several relevant areas:

- **Classical Control Theory (Lyapunov Stability):** Lyapunov theory, dating back to Aleksandr Lyapunov in the late 19th century, provides a powerful and principled framework for certifying the stability of dynamical systems. However, its application to complex, high-dimensional, or learned nonlinear systems is challenging. The core difficulty lies in constructing a suitable Lyapunov function that satisfies the strict pointwise decrease condition (i.e., the function's value must decrease at every time step along any system trajectory).
- **Optimal Control and Value Functions:** In optimal control, value functions represent the optimal long-term cost-to-go. RL algorithms learn approximations of these value functions. While these functions inherently capture system performance over time, they do not naturally satisfy classical Lyapunov conditions due particularly to the use of discount factors in RL, which prioritize immediate rewards and can lead to non-monotonic behavior in the value function over short horizons. Prior work (e.g., Postoyan et al., 2017) has explored augmenting discounted value functions for stability but often yields conservative guarantees.
- **Safe Reinforcement Learning (Safe RL):** This field aims to incorporate safety considerations into RL training. Approaches include learning to switch between stable controllers (Perkins and Barto, 2002), using model-based RL with stability guarantees (Berkenkamp et al., 2017), or penalizing Lyapunov condition violations during training (Hejase and Ozguner, 2023). These methods often focus on ensuring safety *during* the learning process or under specific conditions, rather than providing post-hoc formal certificates of stability for the *final* learned policy across a wide region of operation.
- **Joint Learning of Policies and Lyapunov Functions:** A growing area of research focuses on simultaneously learning a control policy and a Lyapunov function from data. Methods in this domain often frame the problem as maximizing the volume of a certifiable region while satisfying Lyapunov conditions. Techniques range from using formal verification methods like Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) or Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP) (Chang et al., 2019; Dai et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023) to gradient-guided falsification (Yang et al., 2024). These methods, while innovative, typically adhere to the classical one-step Lyapunov decrease condition, which, as the authors argue, can be overly restrictive.

This paper distinguishes itself by introducing the concept of **generalized Lyapunov functions** (building on Fürnsinn et al., 2023, 2025) which relax the traditional stepwise decrease requirement to a **multi-step, weighted average decrease criterion**. This relaxation is critical because it allows for temporary increases in the Lyapunov candidate function over short time horizons, as long as its value decreases on average over a longer, finite horizon. This makes the construction of stability certificates significantly more flexible and applicable to the non-monotonic behaviors often exhibited by RL value functions, thus enabling stability certification for a broader class of learned policies and systems where classical methods fail. By augmenting RL value functions with learned neural network residual terms, the authors offer a pragmatic yet theoretically grounded bridge between classical control and modern learning paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel and effective approach for **certifying the stability of closed-loop systems controlled by Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies**. This overarching goal is driven by the critical need for formal guarantees in deploying RL in real-world, safety-critical applications.

More specifically, the paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a principled method to construct generalized Lyapunov functions for RL policies:** The core idea is to leverage the RL policy's value function and augment it with a learned neural network residual term. The aim is for this composite function to satisfy a relaxed, multi-step Lyapunov decrease condition.
2. **Provide analytical and empirical evidence for the efficacy of generalized Lyapunov functions:** The authors aim to first demonstrate, using the well-understood Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem, that augmenting value functions and applying generalized decrease conditions can lead to less conservative stability certificates compared to classical Lyapunov methods. Subsequently, they seek to empirically validate this approach on complex nonlinear RL benchmarks.
3. **Extend the approach to the joint synthesis of stable neural controllers and certificates:** Beyond post-hoc certification of fixed policies, the paper aims to integrate the generalized Lyapunov framework into a joint learning scheme, enabling the co-design of policies and certificates that explicitly optimize for larger certified regions of attraction.
4. **Open-source the developed methods:** To foster reproducibility and further research in this critical area.

The motivations underpinning these objectives are multifaceted:

- **Addressing the "Black Box" Problem of RL:** Despite RL's impressive empirical performance in complex control tasks, the lack of formal stability guarantees severely limits its deployment in safety-critical systems. Industries demand provable assurances, which RL, in its standard form, cannot provide.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Classical Lyapunov Theory:** Constructing classical Lyapunov functions for complex nonlinear systems, especially those controlled by neural network policies, is notoriously difficult. The strict pointwise decrease condition often leads to conservative results or makes certificate discovery infeasible.
- **Leveraging RL Value Functions:** RL value functions naturally encode long-term costs or rewards. The authors are motivated to explore how these functions can be effectively adapted to serve as (or form the basis of) stability certificates, rather than designing certificates from scratch, which is a harder problem.
- **Enabling More Flexible and Less Conservative Certificates:** The insight from discounted optimal control (e.g., LQR) is that value functions might not strictly decrease at every step due to discounting, yet the system might still be stable. This motivates the introduction of a *generalized* decrease condition that allows temporary increases while ensuring an overall average decrease over a finite horizon, thereby making certificates easier to construct and less conservative.
- **Advancing the State of the Art in Safe and Certified Control:** By offering a more flexible framework for stability certification and joint synthesis, this work aims to significantly contribute to the development of safer and more reliable autonomous systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper progresses from theoretical insights derived from linear systems to practical applications on nonlinear RL benchmarks, ultimately extending to joint policy-certificate synthesis.

#### 3.1. Intuition from Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) Problems

The authors begin by studying the discounted LQR problem to gain insights into how value functions relate to Lyapunov stability.
- **Problem Setup:** They consider a discrete-time linear system with a quadratic stage cost and a discount factor $\gamma \in (0,1)$. The optimal value function is quadratic, $J^*_\gamma(x) = x^\top P_\gamma x$.
- **Observation 1 (Augmentation):** It's known that the discounted value function $J^*_\gamma(x)$ typically does not satisfy classical Lyapunov conditions due to $\gamma$. Building on Postoyan et al. (2017), they show that augmenting $J^*_\gamma(x)$ with a quadratic residual term, $W(x) = \frac{1}{\varpi} x^\top S_0 x$, can form a valid Lyapunov function $V(x) = J^*_\gamma(x) + W(x)$, provided $\gamma$ is sufficiently large and certain Linear Matrix Inequal (LMIs) are satisfied.
- **Observation 2 (Generalized Decrease):** The key innovation is to relax the classical Lyapunov decrease condition to a *generalized Lyapunov condition*. This condition requires the weighted average of the Lyapunov function over $M$ future time steps to decrease relative to the current value (Definition 4.1). They demonstrate analytically (Theorem 4.4) that for LQR, this generalized condition (expressed as a set of LMIs) allows for stability certification with a significantly *lower* bound on the discount factor $\gamma$ compared to classical methods, thus being less conservative. The multi-step weights $\sigma_i$ can be optimized to further improve this bound.

#### 3.2. Stability Certification of Pre-trained Nonlinear RL Policies

Leveraging the intuition from LQR, the authors extend their approach to nonlinear systems controlled by neural network RL policies.
- **Generalized Lyapunov Candidate:** For a pre-trained RL policy $\pi_{RL}$ and its learned value function $J^{\pi_{RL}}_\gamma(x)$, the generalized Lyapunov candidate is defined as $V(x; \theta_1) = J^{\pi_{RL}}_\gamma(x) + \varphi(x; \theta_1)$, where $\varphi$ is a neural network residual. To ensure strict positive definiteness and $V(0)=0$, they modify it to $V(x; \theta_1) := J^{\pi_{RL}}_\gamma(x) - J^{\pi_{RL}}_\gamma(0_n) + |\varphi(x; \theta_1) - \varphi(0_n; \theta_1)| + \beta\|x\|^2$.
- **Learned Multi-step Weights:** They introduce a state-dependent neural network $\sigma(x; \theta_2) \in \mathbb{R}^M_{\ge 0}$ that outputs the non-negative weights $\sigma_i(x)$ for the generalized decrease condition, ensuring $\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \sigma_i(x) \ge 1$. The output of this network is typically scaled by a softmax layer to ensure normalization.
- **Training Objective:** The networks $\varphi(\cdot; \theta_1)$ and $\sigma(\cdot; \theta_2)$ are trained jointly by minimizing a loss function based on the ReLU activation, which penalizes violations of the generalized decrease condition $F(x_k) := \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \sigma_i(x_k; \theta_2) V(x_{k+i}; \theta_1) - (1-\bar{\alpha})V(x_k; \theta_1) \le 0$, where $\bar{\alpha}$ is a user-specified decay parameter. States are sampled from rollouts of the pre-trained RL policy.
- **Evaluation:** The method is evaluated on Gymnasium and DeepMind Control benchmarks (Inverted Pendulum, Cartpole Swingup) by checking the satisfaction of $F(x_k) \le 0$ for a large number of sampled test states.

#### 3.3. Joint Synthesis of Stable Neural Policies and Certificates

The methodology is further extended to jointly learn a control policy $\pi(x; \phi)$ and a generalized Lyapunov certificate $V(x; \theta_1)$.
- **Problem Formulation:** Inspired by previous work (e.g., Yang et al., 2024), the goal is to maximize the volume of a certifiable Lyapunov sublevel set $S = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : V(x) \le \rho\}$ subject to stability constraints.
- **Generalized Condition Integration:** The classical one-step decrease condition is replaced with the multi-step generalized condition (Eq. 18). Importantly, for this joint synthesis, the state-dependent weights $\sigma_i(x)$ are simplified to fixed constants (chosen via grid search for small $M$) due to computational challenges with formal verification of neural-network-parameterized weights.
- **Training Objective:** A combined loss function $L(\theta_1, \phi)$ is used, including:
 - **Stability Loss ($L_{stab}$):** Penalizes violations of the generalized Lyapunov decrease condition and domain constraints using ReLU activations.
 - **Region Loss ($L_{region}$):** Encourages expansion of the certified region by penalizing states outside the desired sublevel set.
 - **Falsification:** Gradient-guided projected gradient descent (PGD) is used to actively search for states that violate the Lyapunov conditions, which are then added to the training set as hard negative examples.
- **Formal Verification:** After training, the synthesized policy-certificate pair is formally verified using the $\alpha, \beta$-CROWN verifier (Wang et al., 2021) to ensure the generalized decrease condition holds within the specified verification domain $X$.

#### 3.4. Computational Aspects and Architectures

- Neural networks (feedforward with LeakyReLU) are used for the residual term $\varphi$ and step-weighting $\sigma$.
- Adam optimizer, learning rate schedulers, and gradient clipping are standard practices.
- Verification times are tracked to assess computational overhead.

This comprehensive approach demonstrates a strong commitment to both theoretical rigor and practical implementation, systematically building from simplified models to complex learning scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings across its analytical and empirical investigations:

#### 4.1. Analytical Benefits for Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)

- **Augmented Value Functions as Generalized Lyapunov Functions:** For the discounted LQR problem, the paper analytically demonstrates that augmenting the optimal value function $J^*_\gamma(x)$ with a quadratic residual term ($W(x) = \frac{1}{\varpi} x^\top S_0 x$) can yield a valid *generalized* Lyapunov function.
- **Less Conservative Stability Bounds:** Critically, by employing the generalized multi-step decrease condition, the authors show that stability can be certified for a broader range of discount factors $\gamma$. In Example 3.3 (a scalar system), classical one-step Lyapunov analysis yields a conservative $\gamma > 0.8090$ for stability. In contrast, the generalized 2-step LMI formulation improves this to $\gamma > 0.6229$ through optimized weights. More importantly, increasing the horizon $M$ progressively pushes the certified $\gamma$ bound closer to the true stability threshold $\gamma^* = 1/3$ (Figure 2), showcasing the significant reduction in conservatism.

#### 4.2. Empirical Certification of Nonlinear RL Policies (Post-hoc)

- **Successful Certification of RL Policies:** The proposed method successfully certifies the stability of various pre-trained RL policies (PPO, SAC, TD-MPC) on standard nonlinear benchmarks: the Inverted Pendulum (Gymnasium) and Cartpole Swingup (DeepMind Control Suite).
- **Generalized Functions Exhibit Non-monotonicity:** Visualizations confirm that the learned generalized Lyapunov functions $V(x)$ can exhibit temporary increases along trajectories (non-monotonic behavior) over individual steps, which is explicitly permitted by the generalized decrease condition, but demonstrate an overall decrease over the multi-step horizon (Figure 3).
- **Consistent Satisfaction of Generalized Decrease:** Quantitative evaluation shows that the generalized decrease condition $F(x_k) \le 0$ is satisfied for 100% of the sampled test states in both the Inverted Pendulum and Cartpole environments across different RL policies (Table 1). This provides strong empirical evidence that valid generalized certificates can be learned for complex neural policies.
- **Concentration of Learned Step-Weights:** Analysis of the learned state-dependent step-weights $\sigma_i(x)$ reveals a consistent pattern: weights tend to concentrate towards the end of the multi-step horizon (80-100% segment) while being lower for initial steps (0-20% segment) (Table 2). This demonstrates that the learning algorithm strategically tolerates initial transients in $V(x)$, relying on a decisive decrease over the full horizon to satisfy the generalized condition. This empirical behavior directly validates the core premise of the generalized Lyapunov framework.

#### 4.3. Joint Synthesis of Stable Neural Policies and Certificates

- **Larger Certified Regions of Attraction (ROAs):** When the generalized multi-step Lyapunov condition is incorporated into a joint policy-certificate synthesis framework, it consistently yields *larger formally verifiable inner approximations of the Region of Attraction (ROA)* compared to approaches using classical one-step Lyapunov conditions. This is demonstrated on three systems: Inverted Pendulum, Path Tracking, and 2D Quadrotor (Figures 7, 8; Table 3a). For example, on the Inverted Pendulum, going from $M=1$ to $M=3$ increases the certified ROA volume from $42.9 \pm 1.2$ to $89.2 \pm 1.2$.
- **Trade-off in Verification Time:** This improvement in ROA volume comes at the cost of increased verification time, as bounding multiple intermediate states and their weighted combinations adds complexity to formal verification tools like $\alpha, \beta$-CROWN (Table 3b). For the 2D Quadrotor, verification time increases from $2209.5 \pm 72.6$ seconds for $M=1$ to $5628.6 \pm 185.9$ seconds for $M=3$.

Overall, the main findings robustly demonstrate that generalized Lyapunov functions offer a more flexible and less conservative framework for stability certification, both for analyzing existing RL policies and for synthesizing new ones with formal guarantees. The empirical results on standard benchmarks confirm the practical applicability and advantages of this theoretical extension.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work holds significant implications for both theoretical control systems and the practical application of reinforcement learning in real-world scenarios.

#### 5.1. Bridging the Theory-Practice Gap in RL Stability

The most profound significance of this research lies in its effective bridging of the gap between the empirical success of reinforcement learning and the rigorous demands of classical control theory regarding stability guarantees. By providing a principled, data-driven approach to construct and verify generalized Lyapunov functions for learned policies, the paper moves beyond mere empirical performance and offers a pathway to formal assurances of system behavior. This is crucial for deploying RL in safety-critical applications where unpredictability is unacceptable.

#### 5.2. Expanding the Applicability of Lyapunov Theory

The introduction and successful application of generalized Lyapunov functions represent a notable advancement in Lyapunov theory itself. By relaxing the stringent pointwise decrease condition to a multi-step, weighted average decrease, the authors make stability certificates easier to construct for complex nonlinear systems, particularly those controlled by neural networks, where classical Lyapunov functions are notoriously difficult to find or prove. This flexibility allows for the certification of systems exhibiting transient non-monotonic behaviors, which is a common characteristic of optimal policies designed under discounting or for tasks requiring intermediate "swing-up" maneuvers.

#### 5.3. Enabling Safer and More Robust Autonomous Systems

Formal stability guarantees are paramount for autonomous systems in domains such as robotics, aerospace, and intelligent transportation. This work directly contributes to the development of safer autonomous systems by:
- **Post-hoc Certification:** Allowing engineers to formally certify the stability of already-trained RL policies before deployment, instilling confidence in their reliability.
- **Joint Synthesis:** Providing a framework to co-design controllers and certificates, leading to policies that are not just performant but also provably stable over a specified region. The ability to certify *larger regions of attraction* implies that these systems can operate reliably from a wider range of initial conditions.

#### 5.4. Driving Further Research in Formal Verification and Control-RL Integration

The paper opens several promising avenues for future research:
- **Optimal Horizon Length (M):** Determining the minimal or optimal certification horizon $M$ for a given system is a non-trivial problem. Future work could explore adaptive or learned $M$.
- **High-Dimensional Systems:** Applying this methodology to more complex, high-dimensional systems (e.g., humanoids, dexterous manipulators) is a natural next step, though it poses significant computational challenges for formal verification.
- **Robust and Stochastic Stability:** Extending the generalized Lyapunov formulation to certify robust stability under bounded perturbations and stochastic disturbances would enhance its applicability to real-world, noisy environments. This would connect to concepts like input-to-state stability and robust Lyapunov theory.
- **Equilibrium Inference:** Relaxing the requirement of knowing the equilibrium state and instead inferring it from data or reward structures would broaden the scope to RL problems where the precise equilibrium is unknown or non-isolated.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The provision of an open-source implementation is highly valuable, lowering the barrier for other researchers and practitioners to build upon this work, fostering collaboration and accelerating progress in the field.

In conclusion, this research is a significant step towards creating verifiable and trustworthy AI-driven control systems. By skillfully combining classical control theory with modern machine learning, it offers a pragmatic yet rigorous solution to a fundamental challenge, with far-reaching implications for the design and deployment of safe and reliable autonomous technologies.
