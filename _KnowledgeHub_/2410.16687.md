# DARE: Diffusion Policy for Autonomous Robot Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> DARE: Diffusion Policy for Autonomous Robot Exploration introduces a generative approach using diffusion models to produce explicit, multi-step exploration paths. The system, trained on ground truth optimal demonstrations, achieves performance on par with state-of-the-art conventional methods in simulated and real-world environments while demonstrating the ability to reason about the structure of unknown areas.

## Problem

- Traditional robot exploration methods are often myopic, lacking foresight to reason about potential structures in unknown areas.
- Existing Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) planners typically generate only the next waypoint, lacking visible long-term planning and facing challenges with sparse reward training.
- Behavior cloning for robotic tasks is limited by the sub-optimality of expert demonstrations, preventing the learned policy from surpassing expert performance.

## Method

- Develops **DARE**, a ==diffusion policy== that generates explicit, multi-step exploration paths in a single inference step for long-term planning.
- Employs a ==graph-based representation== and ==attention-based encoder== to derive a "robot belief feature" that conditions the diffusion policy.
- Trains the diffusion policy via ==supervised learning== on expert demonstrations derived from a novel ==ground truth optimal demonstration planner== which accesses the full environment map.

## Results

- DARE achieves competitive performance, with an average travel distance of **563m** in simulated environments, statistically on par with the state-of-the-art TARE Local (**558m**) and outperforming other baselines including ARiADNE (**579m**).
- The system qualitatively demonstrates an ability to predict unknown environmental structures (e.g., rooms, connections) and integrate this foresight into long-term path planning from partial belief.
- Validated in high-fidelity Gazebo simulation, DARE completed exploration more efficiently than TARE (**678m** vs **714m**), and successfully deployed on a real mobile robot in a cluttered lab environment, demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion models can effectively generate explicit, multi-step long-term paths for robot exploration tasks, enhancing explainability.
> - Training a diffusion policy with ground truth optimal demonstrations allows it to learn to reason about and anticipate the underlying structures of unknown areas.
> - Graph-based belief representations combined with attention networks provide a robust and generalizable input for learning-based exploration policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robot exploration is a fundamental problem in robotics, aiming to efficiently map unknown environments. The field has traditionally been dominated by **conventional methods**, primarily "frontier-based" approaches. These methods, such as Nearest and Utility, rely on reactive, greedy strategies that optimize paths based solely on the robot's current belief (e.g., an occupancy map). More advanced conventional planners, like NBVP and TARE, employ sampling-based strategies to generate non-myopic (long-term) paths, balancing optimization quality with computational efficiency. While effective, these methods typically lack the ability to implicitly reason about the potential structures of unknown areas.

In recent years, **learning-based methods**, particularly those leveraging Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), have emerged as a promising alternative. DRL planners can learn to maximize long-term returns by drawing on past experiences, implicitly predicting future robot beliefs. Early DRL approaches, often based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), were limited to small-scale environments and generally underperformed compared to advanced conventional methods. A significant advancement in this area, pioneered by the authors' previous work (ARiADNE), involved using **graph representations** of the robot's belief coupled with **attention-based networks**. This approach overcame the limitations of CNNs, achieving competitive performance with state-of-the-art conventional planners and demonstrating strong generalizability to real-life applications. However, a common drawback of many DRL-based planners is their tendency to generate only the next waypoint, lacking visible and explainable long-term planning. Furthermore, training DRL in complex environments with sparse reward signals (e.g., long corridors, deadends) can be challenging.

DARE positions itself at the intersection of these advancements, integrating the latest developments in **generative AI, specifically diffusion models**, into robot exploration. Diffusion models, originally prominent in image generation, have recently been adapted for various robotic tasks, including motion planning, visual navigation, and collision avoidance. A key advantage of diffusion models in robotics is their ability to generate high-dimensional output sequences, enabling the synthesis of entire future action sequences rather than just individual actions. This promotes temporal consistency and allows the robot to understand the long-term implications of its actions. DARE specifically builds upon the "diffusion policy" paradigm, which focuses on modeling conditional action distributions, allowing for efficient end-to-end training and real-time deployment.

By leveraging diffusion models, DARE aims to address the limitations of existing learning-based approaches—namely, the lack of explicit long-term path generation and the challenges of DRL training in complex scenarios. The unique aspect of training DARE on "ground truth optimal demonstrations" further distinguishes it, allowing it to learn "better patterns for exploration" by reasoning about the underlying structure of unknown areas, a capability that transcends the limitations of real-time partial map observation. This work represents a significant step towards more intelligent, generalizable, and explainable autonomous exploration systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an **autonomous robot exploration system capable of efficiently exploring and mapping unknown environments as quickly as possible**, generating explicit and explainable long-term exploration paths.

The motivation for proposing DARE stems from several limitations of existing robot exploration planners:

- **Myopic Nature of Conventional Methods:** Traditional frontier-based exploration planners are inherently reactive and optimize paths based only on the robot's current belief of the environment. They lack the foresight to reason about potential structures in unknown areas, leading to suboptimal exploration efficiency in complex scenarios.
- **Lack of Explicit Long-Term Planning in DRL:** While Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) offers the advantage of making reactive decisions and estimating long-term returns by implicitly predicting future robot beliefs, most DRL-based planners typically generate only the next waypoint. This "next-waypoint" approach lacks visible and explainable long-term planning, making it difficult to understand the robot's overall exploration strategy.
- **Challenges in DRL Training:** Training DRL policies for challenging exploration scenarios, such as environments with long corridors or dead-ends, can be difficult due to the sparsity of reward signals. This often leads to unstable or inefficient learning.
- **Sub-optimality of Behavior Cloning:** While supervised learning or behavior cloning can offer more stable training compared to DRL, its performance is inherently limited by the quality of the expert demonstrations. If trained on sub-optimal expert policies, the cloned policy will also be sub-optimal.
- **Limitations of Image-based Encoders:** Using image-based (CNN) encoders for robot belief representation in learning-based exploration policies suffers from several drawbacks, including limited generalizability to varying map sizes, poor capture of long-term dependencies, and a significant sim-to-real gap due to cluttered and noisy real-world maps.

To address these motivations, DARE proposes a novel generative approach leveraging diffusion models trained on expert demonstrations. The key motivations for this specific approach are:
- To **generate explicit long-term paths** for better explainability and more strategic exploration.
- To **achieve improved performance** over state-of-the-art DRL-based planners, especially in challenging environments.
- To **overcome the inherent sub-optimality of behavior cloning** by introducing a novel "ground truth optimal demonstration planner," allowing the model to learn near-optimal exploration patterns.
- To enable the trained planner to **reason about partial belief to recognize potential structures in unknown areas** and integrate this foresight into path planning, much like humans do.
- To utilize **graph-based representations** and attention networks for encoding robot belief, ensuring better generalizability and robustness to real-world complexities compared to image-based methods.

By achieving these objectives, DARE aims to advance autonomous robot exploration towards more efficient, intelligent, and deployable solutions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DARE (Diffusion Policy for Autonomous Robot Exploration) proposes a novel generative approach to robot exploration by leveraging diffusion models trained on expert demonstrations. The core idea is to generate an explicit, multi-step exploration path through a one-time inference, enabling long-term planning. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

**A. Graph-based Exploration Representation:**
- **Motivation:** To efficiently discretize the action space, explicitly represent collision-free connections, and avoid limitations of image-based inputs (size, dependencies, sim-to-real gap).
- **Process:** At each decision step, a collision-free graph $G_t = (V_t, E_t)$ is constructed from the robot's partial 2D occupancy map $B$.
 - **Nodes ($V_t$):** Uniformly distributed in the currently observed free space $B_f$, serving as candidate viewpoints/waypoints. A node $v_t$ is always at the robot's current position.
 - **Edges ($E_t$):** Connect neighboring nodes (up to 5x5) if a collision-free straight-line path exists.
- **Actions:** Defined as selecting one of the 5x5 neighboring nodes, represented as a one-hot encoding.
- **Path Execution:** Planned paths are executed in a receding horizon manner, where the robot executes the first $T_a$ steps of the planned trajectory before replanning.

**B. Observation Encoder (Attention-based Network):**
- **Purpose:** To transform the graph-based representation of the robot's belief into a high-dimensional latent "robot belief feature" that conditions the diffusion policy.
- **Informative Graph ($G'_t$):** An extension of $G_t$, where each node $v'_i$ includes not only coordinates but also:
 - **Utility ($u_i$):** Quantifies information gain (number of observable frontiers).
 - **Guidepost ($b_i$):** Binary signal indicating paths to nodes with non-zero utility.
 - **Occupancy:** Binary signal indicating robot presence.
- **Attention Encoder Architecture:**
 - Projects node features to a $d$-dimension space.
 - Passes features through **6 stacked self-attention layers**. These layers capture complex dependencies between nodes, using an edge mask to enforce graph connectivity constraints (only neighboring node features are accessed).
 - Feeds the output of self-attention (a group of node features) and the "current node feature" (corresponding to the robot's position) to a **cross-attention layer**. This layer merges all node features with the current node feature, encapsulating the robot's comprehensive understanding of the environment.
- **Output:** The final output of the cross-attention layer is the "robot belief feature" in latent space, which serves as input to the diffusion policy.

**C. Diffusion Policy:**
- **Foundation:** Builds upon the Diffusion Policy framework for visuomotor policy learning via action diffusion, adapted for exploration.
- **Generative Process:**
 - Starts with an initial noisy action sequence $A^K_t$ drawn from a Gaussian distribution.
 - Iteratively denoises $A^K_t$ over $K$ steps using a noise-prediction model $\epsilon_\theta$.
 - The denoising process is conditioned on the "robot belief feature" ($O_t$) from the observation encoder.
 - The final output is a denoised action sequence $A^0_t$, representing a sequence of future actions (node selections).
- **Action Sequence Interpretation:**
 - The action sequence $A^0_t$ spans an observation horizon ($T_o$) and a prediction horizon ($T_p$).
 - One-hot encoded actions are mapped to positional changes based on the graph's node resolution.
 - These changes are cumulatively summed with the robot's current location to generate the explicit sequence of future locations, $\psi_{t+1:t+n}$.
- **Training:**
 - Trained using supervised learning (behavior cloning) with Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, minimizing the difference between predicted noise and actual sampled noise.
 - Utilizes a transformer-based noise-prediction network and Square Cosine Noise Scheduler.
- **Collision Avoidance:** A simple collision avoidance algorithm is implemented post-generation to ensure the executed portion ($T_a$) of the planned path is collision-free.

**D. Ground Truth Optimal Demonstration:**
- **Novelty & Motivation:** To overcome the sub-optimality inherent in behavior cloning when using existing exploration planners as experts, DARE introduces a novel expert planner that has access to the *ground truth map* of the environment. This effectively simplifies the exploration problem to a coverage problem, where optimal coverage paths are analogous to optimal exploration paths.
- **Process:**
 - Constructs a "ground truth graph" $G^*(V^*, E^*)$ covering the entire ground truth free area $M_f$.
 - Identifies ground truth frontiers (boundary of unexplored free area $M_f - B_f$).
 - **Sampling-based approach:** Extends a constrained sampling-based planner. Iteratively samples sets of nodes from $G^*$ such that visiting these nodes allows the robot to cover all ground truth frontiers.
 - **TSP Solving:** For each sampled set of nodes, a Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) solver finds the shortest path visiting all selected nodes starting from the robot's position.
 - **Optimal Path Selection:** The process is iterated $k$ times, and the shortest path among all sampled paths is selected as the expert demonstration.
- **Learning Outcome:** By training on these near-optimal paths (which consider the full environment structure), the diffusion model learns useful underlying patterns, enabling it to reason about and incorporate potential structures in unknown areas into its planning, even when only a partial map is available during inference.

**E. Training Details:**
- **Environments:** Simulated dungeon environments (250x250 grid, 100m x 100m) with varying complexity.
- **Dataset:** 4000 expert trajectories generated by the ground truth coverage planner.
- **Parameters:** Planning horizon $T_p=8$, observation horizon $T_o=2$, action horizon $T_a=1$. Denoising steps $K=100$.
- **Optimizer:** AdamW with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$.
- **Hardware & Time:** Trained for 130 epochs over 36 hours using 4 NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs.

This comprehensive methodology allows DARE to combine the strengths of graph-based representation, attention mechanisms, and generative diffusion models, trained with a unique "oracle" expert, to achieve intelligent and efficient autonomous exploration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DARE demonstrates its competitive performance against state-of-the-art conventional and learning-based exploration planners, as well as its practical applicability in both simulations and real-world scenarios.

**A. Comparison with Baselines (Simulated Environments):**
- **Test Setup:** 100 unseen simulated dungeon environments, measuring total travel distance to complete exploration.
- **Baselines:** Conventional (Nearest, Utility, NBVP, TARE Local) and learning-based (ARiADNE). Optimal path from the ground truth coverage planner was also provided as a theoretical lower bound.
- **Quantitative Results (Mean Travel Distance in meters):**
 - Nearest: 652 (±76)
 - Utility: 585 (±79)
 - NBVP: 645 (±109)
 - TARE Local: 558 (±67)
 - ARiADNE: 579 (±82)
 - **DARE (Ours): 563 (±71)**
 - Optimal: 499 (±61)
- **Performance Analysis:**
 - DARE achieves **on-par performance with TARE Local**, which is considered a state-of-the-art conventional planner, showing a travel distance of 563m compared to TARE Local's 558m.
 - DARE **outperforms other conventional methods** (Nearest, Utility, NBVP) and their previous DRL-based work, ARiADNE.
 - A **one-tailed paired t-test** comparing DARE and TARE Local yielded a p-value of 0.16 (much higher than 0.05 significance level). This statistically demonstrates that DARE's performance is **not significantly worse than TARE Local's** in this test set.
 - DARE even **surpassed TARE Local in 54%** of the test environments.
 - The gap to the theoretical "Optimal" path (499m) highlights the inherent challenge of operating with only partial belief, but DARE significantly reduces this gap compared to other sub-optimal methods (12.8% vs. 11.8% for TARE Local).

- **Qualitative Observations (Prediction of Unknown Areas):**
 - A notable and promising finding is DARE's ability to **predict unknown areas based on partial belief**. Examples provided (Figure 3: Rooms, Connections, Deadends) illustrate that DARE can accurately model the underlying structure of unexplored regions and plan efficient paths that anticipate these structures.
 - This capability is attributed to training the diffusion model with ground truth optimal demonstrations, allowing it to learn "useful underlying patterns" by reasoning about dependencies between optimal paths and partial robot beliefs. This signifies a novel advantage over methods that solely rely on observed data.

**B. Gazebo Simulation:**
- **Test Setup:** High-fidelity simulation in a 64m × 85m Gazebo environment.
- **Results:**
 - **DARE:** Completed exploration in **701 seconds** and **678 meters** of travel distance.
 - **TARE:** Completed exploration in **736 seconds** and **714 meters** of travel distance.
- **Performance:** DARE demonstrated **better exploration efficiency** than TARE in this high-fidelity simulation, reinforcing its competitive edge. The per-step planning time for DARE was 1.29s using a single-threading CPU process, indicating its real-time applicability.

**C. Hardware Validation:**
- **Test Setup:** Deployment on an Agilex Scout mini mobile robot equipped with a Leishen 16-line 3D lidar in a cluttered 20m × 30m research lab.
- **Results:** DARE successfully guided the robot to complete the exploration task of the lab (Figure 6).
- **Significance:** This hardware experiment validates DARE's **applicability to real-life scenarios** and demonstrates its ability to bridge the sim-to-real gap, a common challenge in robotics research.

In summary, DARE not only achieves highly competitive quantitative performance in terms of exploration efficiency but also exhibits a novel qualitative ability to infer and incorporate unknown environmental structures into its planning, validated across diverse simulation and real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DARE represents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous robot exploration, offering several key advancements and opening new avenues for research.

**Significance:**

1. **Novel Application of Diffusion Models in Robotics:** DARE is one of the first works to successfully adapt the powerful generative capabilities of diffusion models to the complex problem of long-term path planning for autonomous robot exploration. This demonstrates the versatility and potential of diffusion models beyond their traditional image generation applications.
2. **Explicit Long-Term Path Generation for Explainability:** Unlike many DRL-based planners that provide only the next waypoint, DARE explicitly generates multi-step exploration paths through one-time inference. This enhances the explainability of the learned policy, allowing human operators to better understand and potentially intervene in the robot's high-level exploration strategy.
3. **Enhanced Reasoning about Unknown Areas:** A groundbreaking aspect of DARE is its ability to "reason about the partial belief to recognize the potential structure in some unknown areas and consider these areas during path planning." This foresight, trained by leveraging ground truth optimal demonstrations, allows the robot to make more intelligent, non-myopic decisions, distinguishing it from conventional reactive planners and even many DRL methods that operate solely on observed data.
4. **Overcoming Behavior Cloning Limitations:** By introducing a novel "ground truth optimal demonstration planner," the authors address a core limitation of behavior cloning (supervised learning)—that the trained policy cannot surpass the expert's performance. Training on theoretically optimal paths, even if the expert has access to information unavailable to the robot during deployment, allows DARE to learn fundamental, high-quality exploration patterns.
5. **Competitive Performance with State-of-the-Art:** DARE achieves on-par or even superior performance compared to leading conventional planners (e.g., TARE Local) and prior state-of-the-art DRL approaches (e.g., ARiADNE) in terms of exploration efficiency (travel distance). This demonstrates that the novel diffusion-based approach is not just theoretically interesting but also practically effective.
6. **Strong Generalizability and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The methodology, particularly the graph-based observation encoder, enables DARE to generalize well across different environment sizes and shapes and to handle real-world complexities like cluttered and noisy maps. Its successful deployment in high-fidelity Gazebo simulations and on a real mobile robot validates its practical readiness and robust sim-to-real capabilities.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **More Efficient and Intelligent Exploration:** By incorporating foresight into planning, DARE can lead to significantly more efficient exploration strategies in complex, large-scale environments, reducing the time and resources required to map unknown spaces.
2. **Foundation for Future Research in Generative Robotics:** This work opens up a new research direction for applying generative models like diffusion policies to various robotic planning and control tasks, especially those requiring sequential decision-making and implicit understanding of future states or environmental structures.
3. **Improved Explainability in AI for Robotics:** The explicit path generation contributes to the growing need for more transparent and interpretable AI systems in safety-critical applications like autonomous robotics.
4. **Enabling Robust Autonomous Systems:** DARE's robust performance and sim-to-real transferability make it a promising candidate for deployment in real-world applications such as search and rescue, infrastructure inspection, planetary exploration, or autonomous warehousing.
5. **Bridging the Gap between Planning and Learning:** The approach combines the structured nature of graph-based planning with the adaptive learning capabilities of neural networks and the generative power of diffusion models, offering a hybrid paradigm that could inspire future robotic intelligence systems.

**Future Directions (as identified by authors):**
The authors themselves suggest future work will focus on:
- **Accelerating inference speed:** To enable even more reactive planning in environments with high-frequency map updates.
- **Explicit prediction of unknown structures:** Further pushing the capacity of diffusion models to not just implicitly reason about unknown areas but to explicitly predict their layout, which could be directly leveraged for even better exploration planning.

In conclusion, DARE advances autonomous robot exploration by successfully integrating diffusion policies, offering a powerful framework for intelligent, efficient, and explainable path planning with significant implications for future robotic autonomy.
