# COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences

> [!summary] Summary
> A meta-algorithm called COMAL enables robust Large Language Model alignment by achieving provable last-iterate convergence to the exact Nash equilibrium of the general preference alignment game. This method demonstrated win rates above 60.2% against other algorithms on Llama-3-8B and maintained general model capabilities.

## Problem

- Existing LLM alignment methods (e.g., RLHF, DPO) rely on the Bradley-Terry model, which oversimplifies and fails to capture the complexity and intransitivity of general human preferences.
- Current game-theoretic approaches for LLM alignment often suffer from instability, divergence, or only converge to average iterates or equilibria of regularized/modified games, lacking strong last-iterate guarantees for the true problem.
- Practical deployment of LLMs requires algorithms with provable last-iterate convergence to the true Nash equilibrium, which most existing game-theoretic methods do not provide.

## Method

- **COMAL** is a novel meta-algorithm inspired by the conceptual prox-method, iteratively solving a KL-regularized two-player ==zero-sum alignment game== at each step.
- It dynamically updates a 'reference policy' and adaptively adjusts KL regularization strength, which guides the policy towards the exact Nash equilibrium of the unregularized general preference game.
- The iterative subproblems within COMAL can be solved using various existing preference optimization algorithms, such as DPO, IPO, or INPO, as 'prox operators'.

## Results

- COMAL-aligned Llama-3-**8B**-Instruct models achieved win rates exceeding **60.2%** against all compared baselines, and Qwen2.5-**7B** models surpassed **56.9%** win rates, in controlled evaluations with a general preference oracle.
- The algorithm demonstrated superior training stability and consistent performance progression over many iterations, avoiding the performance degradation or plateaus seen in iterative DPO, IPO, and INPO.
- Models trained with COMAL retained comparable general capabilities on standard academic benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, MMLU) to base models, and showed strong generalizability on GPT-4 based evaluations like AlpacaEval.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - COMAL is the first algorithm to provide provable last-iterate convergence to the exact Nash equilibrium of the unregularized general preference alignment game, even when subproblems are solved approximately.
> - The dynamic updating of the reference policy and adaptive regularization are critical for achieving stable and continuous performance improvement, overcoming the instability and performance degradation observed in other iterative alignment methods.
> - Framing LLM alignment as a convergent meta-algorithm allows for principled integration of existing preference optimization techniques while achieving stronger theoretical guarantees for handling complex, general human preferences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values and preferences is a central challenge in LLM development. Current dominant approaches, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), rely on the Bradley-Terry (BT) model assumption. The BT model postulates that human preferences can be characterized by a single underlying reward function, implying transitivity (if A is preferred over B, and B over C, then A must be preferred over C).

However, the BT model is increasingly recognized as insufficient to capture the complexity and diversity of real-world human preferences. It fails to account for intransitive or cyclic preferences (e.g., A preferred over B, B over C, but C over A), which can arise from varying individual preferences or nuanced contextual factors. Such limitations mean that models aligned solely with BT assumptions might not generalize robustly across diverse user populations or complex tasks.

To address these limitations, recent research has begun exploring LLM alignment under more general preference frameworks. A prominent approach frames the alignment problem as a symmetric two-player zero-sum game. In this game, two LLM policies compete, with payoffs determined by their win rate against each other according to a general preference model. The objective is to find a Nash equilibrium policy, which theoretically guarantees at least a 50% win rate against any other competing policy, thereby offering a more robust notion of alignment.

Despite the theoretical appeal of this game-theoretic formulation, existing algorithms designed to find such Nash equilibrium policies face practical challenges. These methods, often based on Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU) or similar iterative schemes, frequently exhibit instability, diverge, or only converge to Nash equilibria of a *modified* or *regularized* version of the alignment game. This deviation from the original game means they may not maintain the crucial 50% win rate guarantee against arbitrary opponents. Furthermore, many existing theoretical convergence guarantees only hold for the *average* iterate (a mixture of all policies learned during training), which is impractical for deploying large-scale LLMs. Practical applications typically require *last-iterate* convergence, where the final trained policy itself is the desired solution.

COMAL contributes to this landscape by introducing a meta-algorithm specifically designed to overcome these challenges. It aims to achieve stable and theoretically guaranteed last-iterate convergence to the exact Nash equilibrium of the unregularized general preference alignment game, thus providing a principled method for more robust LLM alignment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an algorithm that reliably converges to the Nash equilibrium policy of the alignment game defined under general preferences, thereby guaranteeing at least a 50% win rate against any competing policy. This objective is motivated by several critical shortcomings of existing LLM alignment methodologies:

1. **Limitations of the Bradley-Terry (BT) Model:** Current mainstream alignment methods like RLHF and DPO fundamentally rely on the BT model. This model assumes a transitive preference structure, which simplifies the modeling of human preferences but fails to capture their full complexity. Human preferences can be intransitive, particularly when aggregating diverse viewpoints or evaluating nuanced, multi-faceted responses. For example, a user might prefer response A for its creativity over B, B for its conciseness over C, but C for its detail over A. The BT model cannot represent such cyclic preferences, leading to potentially suboptimal or non-robust alignment in complex scenarios. The paper highlights that even a mixture of two BT models cannot necessarily be parameterized by a single BT model, indicating the model's inherent restrictiveness.

2. **Instability and Non-Convergence in Game-Theoretic Approaches:** Recognizing the limitations of BT, recent work has formulated LLM alignment as a two-player zero-sum game, seeking a Nash equilibrium policy. A Nash equilibrium policy in this context guarantees a certain win rate against any opponent, offering a strong notion of robustness. However, existing self-play algorithms designed to find these Nash policies have demonstrated instability, often diverging or failing to converge to the *exact* Nash equilibrium of the original, unregularized game. Instead, some methods may converge only in expectation (average-iterate convergence) or to an equilibrium of a modified, KL-regularized game. Convergence to a modified game's equilibrium does not necessarily translate to the desired 50% win rate guarantee in the original alignment game.

3. **Lack of Practical Last-Iterate Convergence Guarantees:** For practical deployment of LLMs, the performance of the final, single trained model (the "last iterate") is paramount. Most existing theoretical convergence guarantees for game-theoretic learning algorithms, when they exist, often apply only to the *average* of all policies generated during training. Averaging deep neural network checkpoints is computationally intensive, storage-inefficient, and generally impractical for LLMs. Therefore, an algorithm with provable *last-iterate* convergence to the true Nash equilibrium is essential for practical and reliable LLM alignment.

The motivation for COMAL, therefore, stems from the need for an LLM alignment algorithm that is robust to general human preferences, theoretically sound with strong convergence guarantees, and practically viable for large-scale model training. The authors explicitly pose the question: "Is there an algorithm that converges to the Nash equilibrium policy of the alignment game (Definition 2), thus guaranteeing 50% win-rate against any competing policy?" COMAL is proposed as an answer to this question.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Convergent Meta Alignment Algorithm (COMAL) is a novel meta-algorithm designed to achieve robust LLM alignment with general preferences by finding the Nash equilibrium policy of a two-player zero-sum alignment game.

#### 3.1. Algorithmic Structure (COMAL Meta-Algorithm)

COMAL operates as an iterative meta-algorithm inspired by the conceptual prox-method from convex optimization and game theory. At its core, COMAL iteratively refines LLM policies by solving a regularized two-player zero-sum game at each step, using the previously learned policy as a dynamic reference point. The procedure is structured as follows:

1. **Initialization:** The process begins with an initial policy, $\pi_1$, which also serves as the first reference policy, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
2. **Iterative Refinement (Outer Loop):** For each iteration $t$:
 - **Subproblem Formulation:** A KL-regularized two-player zero-sum game is formulated. The objective of this regularized game, $J_{\tau}(\pi_1, \pi_2, \pi_{\text{ref}})$, is based on the original alignment game objective $J(\pi_1, \pi_2)$ (which measures the win rate) but includes KL-divergence terms that penalize deviation from the current reference policy, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, for the max-player ($\pi_1$) and reward proximity for the min-player ($\pi_2$). This regularization promotes stability and prevents the policy from drifting too far.
 - **Subproblem Solution:** The Nash equilibrium of this *regularized* game, $\pi_{t+1}$, is computed. This step is crucial and can be implemented using various existing preference optimization algorithms, interpreted as computing a "prox operator."
 - **Reference Policy Update:** The reference policy is then updated to the newly computed policy: $\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_{t+1}$. This dynamic updating of the reference policy is a key distinction from other methods that often use a fixed initial policy as a reference.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Foundation and Convergence Guarantees

COMAL's design is based on the conceptual prox-method, a class of algorithms known for their strong convergence properties in solving two-player zero-sum games.

- **Monotonicity:** A core theoretical property demonstrated for COMAL is that the KL divergence from the true Nash equilibrium policy ($\pi^{\star}$) of the original unregularized game to the current policy ($\pi_t$) is monotonically non-increasing (i.e., $KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{t+1}) \leq KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_t)$). This implies that each iteration brings the policy closer to the true Nash equilibrium.
- **Last-Iterate Convergence:** The paper provides theoretical guarantees that COMAL converges to an *exact* Nash equilibrium policy in the last iterate. This is a significant improvement over methods that only guarantee average-iterate convergence or convergence to a regularized Nash equilibrium.
- **Robustness to Approximate Solutions:** Crucially, the theoretical analysis extends to the more practical scenario where the regularized subproblem at each step is solved *approximately* rather than exactly. As long as the approximation error decreases sufficiently fast (e.g., $O(1/t^4)$), last-iterate convergence to the true Nash equilibrium is still guaranteed. This makes COMAL more practical for large-scale LLM training where exact solutions are often computationally prohibitive.

#### 3.3. Practical Implementation and Prox Operator

The step of solving the regularized subgame (finding $\pi_{t+1}$ given $\pi_{\text{ref}}$) can be understood as computing a "prox operator." The authors highlight that a wide range of existing LLM alignment and preference optimization algorithms can be adapted to serve as practical implementations of this prox operator. These include:

- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithms:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).
- **Loss Minimization Algorithms:** Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Iterative Preference Optimization (IPO), Self-Play Preference Optimization (SPPO), REBEL (Reinforcement Learning via Regressing Relative Rewards), Direct Reward Optimization (DRO), and Iterative Nash Policy Optimization (INPO).

The paper specifically details an instantiation of COMAL using INPO as the sub-solver (Algorithm 3). This involves an outer loop of COMAL controlling the reference policy updates, and an inner loop where INPO (Algorithm 4) minimizes a loss function to find the regularized Nash equilibrium for that iteration. The INPO loss function itself is designed to find the prox operator for KL-regularized preferences.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design

The effectiveness of COMAL was evaluated through both synthetic and large-scale LLM experiments.

- **Synthetic Experiments:** A simple 3x3 bandit problem with intransitive preferences ($y_c \succ y_b \succ y_a \succ y_c$) was used. This setting provides a controlled environment to visualize convergence dynamics and identify the unique Nash equilibrium. Algorithms were initialized at a common point, and their trajectories and equilibrium gaps were tracked.
- **LLM-Based Experiments:**
 - **Models:** Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B (with additional experiments on Qwen2-1.5B).
 - **Instruction Set:** 64K instructions from the UltraFeedback dataset for training.
 - **Preference Oracle:** A mixture of two Bradley-Terry reward models (Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2 and ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 for 8B models, Llama-3-OffsetBias-8B for 1.5B model) was used to simulate diverse and potentially non-transitive human preferences. This oracle was also used for evaluation, providing a controlled assessment environment.
 - **Preference Data Generation:** Candidate outputs were sampled, and the oracle selected preferred pairs to create win-loss data.
 - **Baselines:** BASE model (untrained or SFT-tuned), vanilla DPO, vanilla IPO, INPO, and Iterative IPO. Iterative variants also explored different KL regularization strengths.
 - **Training Details:** Training was iterative, dividing UltraFeedback into six subsets, with multiple "training rounds." COMAL updated its reference policy periodically (e.g., every 6 iterations, equivalent to one full pass over UltraFeedback), and adjusted KL regularization strength adaptively.
 - **Evaluation:** Pairwise win rates were computed against all other trained algorithms using the preference oracle on the AlpacaEval benchmark instructions. Additionally, models were evaluated on standard academic benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU, BBH, HumanEval) and general alignment benchmarks (AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard using GPT-4) to assess retention of general capabilities and generalizability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted demonstrate COMAL's effectiveness in achieving stable convergence to robust policies under general preference models, outperforming various baseline methods.

#### 4.1. Synthetic Experiments

- **Convergence to Nash Equilibrium:** In a controlled 3x3 bandit problem with intransitive preferences, COMAL was observed to converge to the unique Nash equilibrium.
- **Comparison with Baselines:** In contrast, standard Mirror Descent (MD) algorithms (including iterative DPO, iterative IPO, and SPPO, which approximate MD) exhibited cyclical behavior and diverged from the Nash equilibrium. The INPO algorithm, while demonstrating last-iterate convergence, converged to a Nash equilibrium of a *regularized* game, resulting in a constant equilibrium gap from the true, unregularized Nash equilibrium. This outcome aligns with COMAL's theoretical guarantees of last-iterate convergence to the exact Nash equilibrium.

#### 4.2. LLM-Based Experiments (Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B)

- **Superior Win Rates:** When applied to Llama-3-8B-Instruct, COMAL achieved a win rate above 60.2% against all compared algorithms. Similarly, with Qwen2.5-7B, it maintained a win rate exceeding 56.9% against all baselines in controlled evaluations using the preference oracle. These results indicate COMAL's ability to learn policies that are consistently preferred over those generated by other state-of-the-art alignment algorithms under a general preference model.
- **Enhanced Training Stability and Performance Progression:**
 - COMAL consistently exhibited steady improvements throughout the training period, even in later stages.
 - In contrast, iterative IPO and INPO often showed performance degradation, particularly after approximately 6 training iterations when applied to Llama-3-8B-Instruct. The authors suggest this instability could be due to the delicate nature of further optimizing extensively pre-trained models.
 - COMAL's strategy of dynamically adjusting the KL-regularization strength and updating the reference policy allowed it to overcome these stability issues and achieve continuous performance gains where baselines faltered.
 - For smaller models like Qwen2-1.5B, COMAL also showed sustained performance improvement over more iterations (up to 42), while other methods like Iter-IPO exhibited rapid increases in output length and performance decline, and INPO variants began to plateau or degrade.
- **Maintenance of General Capabilities (Alignment Tax Mitigation):** Evaluation on standard LLM benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU, BBH, HumanEval) demonstrated that models trained with COMAL retained comparable performance to the base models and other alignment methods. This suggests that COMAL can improve alignment with general preferences without incurring a significant "alignment tax" (degradation of general abilities).
- **Generalizability to GPT-4 Preferences:** While not directly optimized for GPT-4's preferences, COMAL-trained models performed strongly on AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard (which use GPT-4 as an evaluator) compared to baselines. This indicates a degree of generalizability beyond the specific training oracle.

#### 4.3. Discussion on Reference Policy Updates

The empirical results support the theoretical finding that dynamically updating the reference policy is critical for convergence to the true alignment game's Nash equilibrium. COMAL's ability to adjust the KL-regularization strength further contributes to its stability and performance, enabling it to avoid the performance ceilings or instability observed in methods with fixed or frequently updated but unadjusted regularization. Iter-IPO, despite having smaller constraints from an iteratively updated reference, failed to outperform COMAL and suffered from training instability, highlighting that the dynamic adjustment and the meta-algorithmic structure of COMAL are key.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of COMAL represents a significant step forward in the field of Large Language Model alignment, particularly for addressing the complexities of diverse and general human preferences.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Advancements

COMAL is presented as the first algorithm that provides provable last-iterate convergence to the *exact* Nash equilibrium policy of the unregularized general preference alignment game. This is a crucial theoretical contribution, as previous game-theoretic approaches either diverged, converged only in expectation (average-iterate convergence), or converged to the equilibrium of a modified, regularized game, which does not guarantee the desired 50% win rate against all opponents. Furthermore, COMAL's theoretical guarantees hold even when the internal regularized subproblems are solved approximately, enhancing its robustness and practical applicability. This robust theoretical foundation provides a principled basis for designing and analyzing LLM alignment algorithms.

#### 5.2. Practical Utility and Flexibility

The meta-algorithmic design of COMAL offers substantial practical benefits. It is modular and can be integrated with many existing preference optimization methods (e.g., PPO, DPO, IPO, INPO, SPPO, REBEL, DRO) with minimal modifications. This compatibility allows researchers and practitioners to leverage well-established and optimized training pipelines while benefiting from COMAL's convergence properties. The empirical results demonstrate that this integration is not only feasible but also leads to consistent performance improvements and enhanced training stability in large-scale LLM contexts. The dynamic adjustment of regularization and adaptive reference policy updates address common challenges like instability and performance plateaus observed in iterative alignment methods.

#### 5.3. Enhanced LLM Robustness and Performance

By targeting the Nash equilibrium of the general preference alignment game, COMAL aims to produce LLMs that are more robust and universally preferred across a wider range of user preferences, including those that are intransitive or context-dependent. The experimental results, showing COMAL consistently achieving significantly higher win rates against multiple baselines in controlled environments, corroborate this. This improved alignment can lead to LLMs that are more reliable, trustworthy, and satisfy a broader spectrum of human expectations, thereby expanding their utility and user acceptance.

#### 5.4. Mitigation of "Alignment Tax"

The paper's findings indicate that COMAL achieves superior alignment performance without a substantial "alignment tax," meaning it does not significantly degrade the LLM's general capabilities on standard academic benchmarks. This balance between improved alignment and retention of core competencies is vital for deploying highly capable and aligned LLMs.

#### 5.5. Broader Impact on AI Alignment Research

COMAL's success in robustly aligning LLMs with general preferences opens new avenues for research into more sophisticated preference models and learning paradigms. It validates the game-theoretic approach to alignment and provides a strong foundation for future work on designing convergent algorithms for complex multi-agent learning problems in AI. The work suggests that adapting convergent algorithms from game theory and optimization can effectively address practical challenges in machine learning with complex feedback mechanisms.
