# LIBERO-RECOVER: Beyond Task Success Towards Failure Recovery in Robotic Manipulation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> LIBERO-Recover is a failure-recovery benchmark for robotic manipulation, built by collecting naturally occurring failures from six VLA and world-action policies on LIBERO rather than injecting perturbations. It organizes 2,000+ scenarios into four recovery levels (action retry, action adaptation, object state recovery, environmental recovery) with metrics for recovery success, degradation, and consistency, and shows every evaluated model loses over 50% of its success rate after failure.

## Problem

- Existing manipulation benchmarks score one-shot task completion from predefined initial states, so near-100% LIBERO scores may overstate real-world robustness of current policies.
- Robustness extensions of LIBERO manually perturb object poses, appearance, or layout, testing generalization to distribution shift rather than understanding of consequences from genuine execution failures.
- Failure recognition, disrupted-state restoration, and post-failure replanning remain essentially unmeasured, leaving no way to compare policies on recovery rather than execution.

## Method

- **LIBERO-Recover**'s ==three-stage construction pipeline==: run six policies unperturbed on 130 LIBERO subtasks as ==failure generators==, temporally localize deviation with a ==video-analyzing VLM==, then characterize post-failure consequences and difficulty.
- A ==four-level recovery taxonomy== formalized through a ==failure predicate== requiring execution-induced, task-relevant, plan-invalidating state change that remains recoverable by some recovery policy, spanning retry to environmental restoration.
- Three metrics plus an expert dataset: ==Recovery Success Rate== per level, ==Recovery Degradation== across pre/during/post-failure phases, ==Recovery Consistency== as one minus task-level variance, with 3,184 teleoperated recovery trajectories for fine-tuning.

## Results

- All six policies drop over **50%** between standard and recovery evaluation, for example OpenVLA-OFT from **97.6%** to **15.7%** on Spatial; rankings invert, with Wan2-Policy beating GR00T by **14.4%** on LIBERO-100 yet trailing **5.0%** under failure.
- Recovery success falls monotonically with level, with a sharp L2-to-L3 cliff and near-zero L4 on LIBERO-100; averaged degradation reaches **54%**, **82%**, **87%**, and **100%** across Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-100.
- Mixed recovery training lifts OpenVLA-OFT recovery from **20.8%** to **25.4%** but drops standard LIBERO from **97.1%** to **96.6%**; adding the initial frame as temporal context raises it to **26.8%**, including **10.3%** to **24.7%** on LIBERO-100.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Saturated one-shot benchmark scores are poor predictors of robustness; rankings reverse under real execution failures, so recovery should be measured as a separate axis.
> - Current policies handle action-level correction but not state-level recovery, indicating the bottleneck is reasoning over object states and interaction topology, not motor control.
> - Fine-grained closed-loop control and a reference to the intended initial configuration both aid recovery, since deviation must be inferred by comparison, not from the current observation alone.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World Action Model (WAM) technologies have enabled robotic manipulation models to achieve high success rates on established benchmarks, such as LIBERO. These models are designed to interpret visual observations and language instructions to generate sequences of actions, aiming for task completion. However, the prevailing evaluation paradigm in these benchmarks primarily assesses task success from predefined initial states, often under idealized conditions where failures are not explicitly considered or addressed.

The broader research landscape for robot manipulation benchmarks has seen developments aiming to increase realism and challenge. Examples include RLBench and earlier versions of LIBERO, which established large-scale simulation tasks. More recent benchmarks, such as LIBERO-Pro, LIBERO-Plus, LIBERO-X, and LIBERO-Safety, have introduced complexities like diverse task variations, multi-dimensional robustness evaluations, variations in environmental conditions, and safety-critical scenarios. Other contributions like CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin have also pushed towards greater task generalization and scene diversity.

Despite these advancements, the existing benchmarks primarily rely on manually predefined perturbations or challenging conditions. They evaluate a model's ability to complete tasks under varied starting conditions, rather than its capacity to manage and recover from failures that naturally arise during task execution. Real-world robotic deployments inevitably encounter issues such as failed grasps, collisions, or unintended object displacements, which can significantly alter the task state. The capability of a robot to recognize, adapt to, and recover from such unexpected states remains largely unexplored and unmeasured by current evaluation methods.

This work addresses this specific gap by introducing a benchmark designed to evaluate failure recovery. It diverges from previous work by focusing on failures that *naturally occur* during the execution of embodied models, rather than synthetically introducing perturbations. This approach aims to provide a more realistic assessment of model robustness and adaptability in the face of real-world operational challenges, promoting the development of agents capable of handling dynamic and error-prone environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address a perceived critical gap between the high performance reported on existing robotic manipulation benchmarks and the actual reliability required for real-world deployment. The authors argue that while current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World Action Model (WAM) models achieve high success rates on benchmarks like LIBERO, these results can be misleading because they primarily evaluate task completion from predefined, often ideal, initial states. This paradigm implicitly assumes error-free execution.

The key motivations for this work are detailed as follows:

- **Addressing the Reality of Robotic Failures:** In real-world robotic operations, failures are an inevitable part of interaction. These can manifest as unsuccessful grasps, collisions with objects or the environment, or unintended displacement of task-relevant items. A robot designed for practical deployment must possess the capability to not only execute tasks successfully but also to recognize when a failure has occurred, understand its implications for the task state, and execute recovery actions.
- **Limitations of Current Benchmarks:** Existing benchmarks, even those designed to evaluate robustness (e.g., LIBERO-Pro, LIBERO-Plus, LIBERO-X, LIBERO-Safety), typically assess performance under pre-defined perturbations of initial object positions, appearances, or environmental conditions. These setups primarily measure a model's generalization to varied environmental conditions rather than its ability to perceive and respond to dynamic state changes caused by actual execution errors. They often do not require the robot to diagnose a failure, formulate a recovery plan, or restore a disrupted state before resuming the main task.
- **Shifting the Evaluation Paradigm:** The work is motivated by the need to shift the focus of evaluation from merely "Can the robot succeed?" to the more critical question, "Can the robot recover after failure?". This reorientation aims to foster the development of agents that are not just proficient at completing tasks under ideal conditions but are also resilient and adaptive in imperfect, real-world scenarios.
- **Promoting Robust and Generalizable Embodied Agents:** By highlighting the deficiencies of current models in failure recovery, the research seeks to stimulate further development in areas such as state understanding, replanning capabilities, and adaptive control, which are essential for creating truly robust and generalizable embodied agents. The introduction of a dedicated benchmark for failure recovery is intended to serve as a standardized tool for measuring progress in these areas.
- **Understanding Real-World Failure Dynamics:** The authors emphasize that real execution failures induce complex and unstructured states that differ from manually perturbed scenarios. By collecting naturally occurring failures, the benchmark aims to capture the true distribution and characteristics of errors encountered by embodied models, leading to more realistic and pertinent evaluations.

In summary, the core objective is to bridge the gap between idealized benchmark performance and real-world reliability by providing a systematic, data-driven framework for evaluating and promoting failure recovery capabilities in robotic manipulation models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces LIBERO-Recover, a benchmark designed to evaluate failure recovery in robotic manipulation models. The methodology involves a specific problem formulation, a definition of failure states, a taxonomy of recovery difficulty, and a multi-stage scenario construction pipeline.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated by extending the conventional task execution paradigm. In standard evaluation, a policy $\pi_\theta$ aims to generate an action sequence from an initial state $s_0$ to a terminal state $s_T$ that satisfies a goal condition $G(s_T, l)=1$. The proposed framework, however, considers an execution that deviates to a failure state $s_f$, where $G(s_f, l)=0$. A failure state is defined as one where the original task goal is not satisfied, but the task is still recoverable through subsequent interaction. The robot must not merely continue but must generate a new sequence of actions, termed a recovery policy $\pi_{rec}$, to restore the necessary state and resume the original task.

A state $s_f$ is identified as a failure state if:
1. It is induced by a preceding action.
2. The task goal is not satisfied ($G(s_f, l)=0$).
3. Directly continuing the current execution plan is insufficient to complete the task ($\pi_\theta(s_f) \not\Rightarrow G$).
4. It remains recoverable (an $\pi_{rec}$ exists to reach $s_T$ where $G(s_T, l)=1$).

#### 3.2. Failure Taxonomy and Difficulty Levels

Recoverable failures are categorized into four progressive difficulty levels based on the required state reasoning and recovery actions:
- **Level 1: Action Retry (L1):** The failure results in minimal alteration to the task configuration ($s_f \approx s_{expected}$). Recovery primarily involves re-executing the failed action.
- **Level 2: Action Adaptation (L2):** The failure causes a limited change in object configuration ($s_f \neq s_{expected}$), but the object remains usable. Recovery requires adapting the subsequent action to the observed state, rather than simply retrying.
- **Level 3: Object State Recovery (L3):** The failure significantly alters a task-relevant object, making direct task completion impossible. The model must first restore the object's required state before resuming the original task.
- **Level 4: Environmental Recovery (L4):** The failure affects an object or state not directly manipulated by the original task but which subsequently obstructs task execution. Recovery demands reasoning about both task-relevant and task-irrelevant states and their interaction topology, identifying the obstruction, restoring the configuration, and then continuing the original task.

This hierarchy assesses capabilities ranging from basic action repetition to complex environment-level reasoning.

#### 3.3. Recovery Scenario Construction

Unlike benchmarks that rely on manual state perturbations, LIBERO-Recover grounds each scenario in a failure that occurred during embodied model execution. A scenario is defined as a tuple $(I, s_0, \tau_{fail}, s_f, g, r)$, representing the task instruction, initial state, failure trajectory, resulting failure state, original task goal, and required recovery behavior.

The construction pipeline proceeds in three stages:
1. **Task Execution:** Utilizing four LIBERO task suites (130 subtasks), multiple embodied policies (e.g., π0, π0.5, OpenVLA, GR00T, Wan-Policy, Cosmos-Policy) serve as "failure generators." Executions from original initial states generate failure trajectories ($\tau_{fail}$), with simulator states recorded as videos. No manual perturbations or injected failures are involved at this stage.
2. **Failure Localization:** Qwen3.5-27B-Instruct analyzes each execution video and task instruction to temporally pinpoint where execution deviates from the intended progression. Videos are segmented into pre-failure, during-failure, and post-failure stages. Corresponding robot simulation states and object poses are extracted to define the localized failure scenario.
3. **Failure Characterization:** For each candidate failure scene, Qwen3.5-27B-Instruct assesses post-failure consequences, determining the recovery type and difficulty level (L1-L4) based on the defined taxonomy.

The resulting benchmark comprises 2178 recovery scenarios across five evaluation suites, with a naturally imbalanced distribution across difficulty levels, reflecting real-world failure frequencies.

#### 3.4. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics

To facilitate model improvement, 3,184 expert recovery trajectories across 413 failure scenarios were collected by four human teleoperators using a SpaceMouse. These trajectories are intended for model fine-tuning.

Evaluation metrics include:
- **Recovery Success Rate (RSR):** The proportion of failure scenarios where the policy successfully completes the original task after entering a failure state. RSR is reported overall and for each difficulty level (RSR_L1 to RSR_L4).
- **Recovery Degradation (RD):** Quantifies the execution capability lost after a failure, defined as $1 - \frac{M_{post} + \epsilon}{M_{pre} + \epsilon}$, where $M_{pre}$ and $M_{post}$ are pre- and post-failure execution performances. Lower RD indicates better retention of capability.
- **Recovery Consistency (RC):** Measures the stability of recovery across different failure states within the same task. It is defined as $1 - \text{Std}_k(R_k)$, where $R_k$ is the task-level recovery success rate. Higher RC indicates more consistent recovery.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Six representative VLA and WAM models (OpenVLA-OFT, π0-Fast, GR00T-N1.5, π0, Wan2-Policy, Cosmos-Predict2-Policy) are evaluated using their official configurations. Each task is evaluated over 10 trials with minor object-position perturbations, under a time limit set at 1.1 times the average human completion time scaled by task difficulty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the LIBERO-Recover benchmark revealed several key findings regarding the failure recovery capabilities of contemporary robotic manipulation models.

#### 4.1. Multi-level Evaluation

- **Finding 1: Disparity Between Predefined Scenario Success and Real Failure Robustness.** Models exhibiting high performance on standard LIBERO benchmarks demonstrated over 50% performance degradation when faced with naturally occurring failures in LIBERO-Recover. Furthermore, models that outperformed others in standard settings did not consistently maintain this advantage in failure recovery scenarios. For instance, a model outperforming another by a substantial margin on LIBERO-100 could fall behind in corresponding failure recovery tasks. These results indicate that benchmark rankings based on predefined scenarios are not reliable predictors of a model's robustness to real execution failures.
- **Finding 2: Local Correction vs. State Recovery.** All evaluated models achieved higher success rates on L1 (Action Retry) and L2 (Action Adaptation) failures compared to L3 (Object State Recovery) and L4 (Environmental Recovery). Performance consistently declined with increasing recovery difficulty. This suggests that current models are more capable of making local adjustments to their immediate actions based on visual observations (L1/L2). However, they exhibit limitations in reasoning about and actively recovering the underlying task state after significant disruptions, which is required for L3 and L4 failures involving changes in object states, spatial relations, and interaction dependencies. The notable performance drop between L2 and L3 highlights a transition point where models struggle with state-level recovery beyond simple action adaptation.

#### 4.2. Multi-label Evaluation

- **Finding 3: Compounding Degradation from Failures.** Failures were observed to induce compounding distribution shifts that progressively impaired model performance. Across all LIBERO suites, models experienced significant Recovery Degradation (RD), with success rates dropping by 54.0% to 100.0%. Performance continued to decrease from the "during-failure" phase to the "post-failure" phase. This indicates that a failure is not merely a transient event but can lead to a sequence of increasingly unfamiliar states, making subsequent task execution more challenging.
- **Finding 4: World Action Models (WAMs) and Recovery Stability.** WAM policies, such as Cosmos-Predict2-Policy and Wan2-Policy, demonstrated higher Recovery Consistency (RC) compared to conventional VLA models. This observation suggests that WAMs, which explicitly model action-conditioned state transitions, may possess a stronger inductive bias for adapting to unexpected failure states, leading to more stable recovery behaviors. In contrast, VLAs primarily learning action imitation from successful trajectories might be less equipped for robust state transitions.

#### 4.3. Enhancing Model Failure Handling

- **Finding 5: Impact of Action Chunk Size.** Recovery success rates consistently decreased as the action chunk size (the number of actions executed before the next policy update) increased from 4 to 32. This trend was consistent across different policies and LIBERO subsets. Smaller action chunks enable more frequent state feedback and action adjustments, allowing the policy to better track and respond to evolving post-failure states. This indicates that effective failure recovery is sensitive to the temporal granularity of control and benefits from fine-grained closed-loop interaction.
- **Finding 6: Failure-Recovery Transfer Gap.** Training models by combining original LIBERO data with LIBERO-Recover expert data improved performance on LIBERO-Recover scenarios. However, this combined training showed minimal or even negative impact on the models' success rates in standard LIBERO tasks (which can also encounter failures naturally). This suggests a "failure-recovery transfer gap," implying that learning to act from an already failed state is not sufficient to enable a policy to reliably recover from errors it generates during its own ordinary task execution. It highlights the potential need for mechanisms to recognize and respond to self-induced failures online.
- **Finding 7: Benefits of Temporal Context.** Providing the initial frame of a task as temporal context consistently improved recovery performance across models, with more significant gains on challenging tasks. The initial frame serves as a reference point, aiding the policy in identifying state deviations and inferring what aspects of the scene have been disrupted by the failure. This indicates that effective recovery involves not just understanding the current state, but also reasoning about its divergence from an expected or desired state, facilitated by historical context.
- **Finding 8: Concentrated Nature of Failure States.** Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) visualization of training trajectories revealed that LIBERO-Recover data exhibited a more concentrated state distribution with distinct high-density regions, primarily around critical interaction points (e.g., grasping, placing). In contrast, standard LIBERO data covered a more dispersed workspace of successful executions. This difference suggests that recovery data provides targeted information about critical state-transition regions that are underrepresented in conventional successful demonstrations, emphasizing the unique informational value of failure-centric data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LIBERO-Recover benchmark and its associated findings hold several significant implications for the field of robotic manipulation and the development of embodied intelligent agents.

Firstly, this work addresses a critical gap in the evaluation of robotic manipulation models by shifting the focus from idealized task success to real-world robustness under failure conditions. Current benchmarks, despite high reported success rates, may overestimate the readiness of models for deployment in dynamic and unpredictable environments where failures are inevitable. By introducing naturally occurring failures, LIBERO-Recover provides a more realistic and stringent evaluation criterion.

Secondly, the introduction of LIBERO-Recover as the first large-scale benchmark specifically dedicated to embodied failure recovery offers a standardized and comprehensive tool for research. It enables systematic comparison of different robotic policies not just on their ability to complete tasks, but on their resilience and adaptability when things go wrong. This structured approach, with its four levels of recovery difficulty and multi-dimensional evaluation, provides a diagnostic framework to pinpoint specific weaknesses in current models, from basic action retry to complex environmental state recovery.

Thirdly, the main findings provide concrete insights that can guide future research and development in robotic manipulation.
- The observed performance drop between L2 and L3 failures highlights that current models struggle with "state-level recovery" beyond simple action adaptation. This suggests a need for architectural and algorithmic advancements that enable deeper reasoning about object states, spatial relationships, and interaction dependencies, potentially involving more sophisticated scene memory and planning capabilities.
- The finding that World Action Models (WAMs) exhibit more consistent recovery suggests that explicit modeling of action-conditioned state transitions (a core feature of WAMs) might be a promising direction for enhancing failure handling.
- The impact of action chunk size underscores the importance of fine-grained, closed-loop control for effective recovery, indicating that policy architectures should allow for frequent state feedback and rapid action adjustment.
- The "failure-recovery transfer gap" is a particularly noteworthy finding. It indicates that simply exposing a model to failed states during training does not automatically equip it to recover from its *own* online execution errors. This suggests that future research might need to focus on explicit failure detection mechanisms, self-monitoring, and online replanning capabilities.
- The benefit of temporal context (e.g., providing the initial task frame) suggests that models can leverage historical information to better understand the nature of a failure by comparing the current disrupted state to an intended or expected state.

Finally, the project's availability of the benchmark and collected data (including expert recovery trajectories) will catalyze further research. By providing a common ground for evaluation and a rich dataset of real failures, LIBERO-Recover is expected to foster innovation towards more robust, autonomous, and generally capable robotic agents that can operate reliably beyond controlled laboratory settings, moving closer to practical real-world deployment.
