# AirExo-2: Scaling up Generalizable Robotic Imitation Learning with Low-Cost Exoskeletons

> [!summary] Summary
> 

## Problem

- Traditional robotic imitation learning relies on costly, time-consuming teleoperation that requires access to specific robot platforms, limiting its scalability.
- Existing "in-the-wild" human demonstration methods suffer from action inaccuracies (handheld devices) or necessitate expensive robot data fine-tuning due to visual and kinematic "domain gaps."
- A lack of systems capable of scalable, low-cost demonstration collection that can be directly transferred to real robots without further fine-tuning.

## Method

- Development of **AirExo-2**, a low-cost, robust, and kinematically isomorphic exoskeleton system for scalable human demonstration collection in natural environments.
- Implementation of ==demonstration adaptors== that convert raw in-the-wild RGB-D images and human actions into photorealistic pseudo-robot observations and kinematically consistent actions, bridging domain gaps via ==differentiable rendering== and ==ControlNet-guided Stable Diffusion==.
- Proposition of **RISE-2**, a generalizable visuomotor policy that robustly learns from adapted pseudo-robot demonstrations by fusing 3D geometric features from a sparse encoder with 2D semantic features from a dense ==DINOv2== encoder via a ==spatial aligner==.

## Results

- RISE-2, trained solely on AirExo-2 pseudo-robot demonstrations, achieved an average **85.0%** success rate on real robot tasks during zero-shot deployment, with only a small performance drop compared to policies trained on teleoperated data.
- AirExo-2 demonstrated significantly faster task completion times (**5.66 seconds**) and higher action accuracy (**1.737mm** average translation error) compared to existing teleoperation and handheld device methods.
- RISE-2 (DINOv2) showed strong generalization, achieving **85.0%** success rates under novel backgrounds and objects individually, and **62.5%** under combined disturbances in a generalization evaluation, outperforming baseline policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A low-cost, kinematically accurate exoskeleton can provide high-fidelity human demonstrations with superior action accuracy and user-friendliness, significantly boosting data collection efficiency.
> - Advanced visual adaptation techniques, including differentiable rendering and generative models like ControlNet with Stable Diffusion, can effectively transform human-centric data into robot-ready data, enabling zero-shot policy transfer.
> - Fusing 3D spatial (geometric) and 2D semantic features is crucial for developing robust policies that generalize well across varied environments, objects, and disturbances without explicit robot data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalizable robotic imitation learning for real-world applications is a central challenge in robotics. Traditional methods, such as teleoperation, are effective for collecting demonstrations but are often costly, time-consuming, and require access to specific robot platforms, limiting their scalability.

To address these limitations, researchers have explored alternative, lower-cost data sources, including human videos and "in-the-wild" demonstrations. Human video approaches typically involve inferring robot actions from 2D human movements, which can be challenging to convert accurately into 3D robot trajectories and states. In-the-wild demonstrations utilize specialized devices to capture human motion and environmental observations, translating them into robot-relevant data during task execution.

Current in-the-wild demonstration devices fall into two categories:
- **Handheld devices:** These often use end-effectors identical to the robot's and employ in-hand cameras for visual consistency. However, they can suffer from action inaccuracies due to reliance on visual SLAM for pose estimation and limited camera field-of-view, which can hinder depth perception.
- **Whole-body devices:** These systems typically offer more accurate action capture and flexible observations. However, they frequently require subsequent fine-tuning with teleoperated robot data to bridge the "domain gap" – discrepancies between human-centric in-the-wild data and robot-centric data in terms of visual appearance (visual gap) and motion characteristics (kinematic gap).

This research positions itself by addressing the limitations inherent in existing in-the-wild data collection methods. It aims to develop a system that enables scalable, low-cost demonstration collection without requiring robot platforms, while simultaneously bridging the domain gaps effectively so that policies can be trained and deployed on real robots without further fine-tuning. This work seeks to advance the field by providing a more efficient and accessible pipeline for acquiring high-quality, generalizable robotic manipulation skills.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance generalizable robotic imitation learning by enabling efficient and scalable demonstration collection for real-world applications. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and addresses specific challenges within the current research landscape:

1. **Addressing the Cost and Scalability of Traditional Teleoperation:** Current imitation learning pipelines heavily rely on teleoperation to collect robot-centric demonstrations. While effective, this approach is inherently costly due to the requirement of physical robot platforms, extensive infrastructure, and significant human labor. The authors are motivated to find a more economical and scalable alternative that reduces the dependency on expensive robotic hardware during the data collection phase.

2. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing In-the-Wild Demonstration Systems:** Prior attempts at in-the-wild data collection, using devices like handheld grippers or whole-body motion capture systems, present specific shortcomings:
 - **Action Inaccuracies and Observational Limitations:** Handheld devices often rely on visual SLAM for pose estimation, which can introduce inaccuracies. Their limited camera field of view can also restrict comprehensive depth perception, critical for manipulation tasks.
 - **Domain Gap Requiring Robot Data Fine-Tuning:** While whole-body devices capture human motions more accurately, the collected data often exhibits a "kinematic gap" (differences in motion translation between humans and robots) and a "visual gap" (presence of human hands and collection devices in visual data versus the robot itself). This necessitates fine-tuning with additional, costly robot-teleoperated data, undermining the goal of robot-free data collection.

3. **Enabling Direct Transfer of In-the-Wild Demonstrations to Robot Learning:** The core motivation is to develop a system that can efficiently convert human-centric, in-the-wild demonstrations into "pseudo-robot" demonstrations that are directly suitable for training real-world manipulation policies, bypassing the need for any real robot data during training or fine-tuning.

To achieve these, the key objectives are:
- **Develop a Robust, Low-Cost Exoskeleton System (AirExo-2):** Design and construct a physically robust and kinematically accurate exoskeleton that facilitates large-scale, in-the-wild human demonstration collection at a low cost ($600). This system must overcome structural limitations, motion tracking inaccuracies, and operational inconveniences of prior designs.
- **Implement Effective Demonstration Adaptors:** Create a suite of adaptors that can transform the raw in-the-wild observations (RGB-D images) and human actions captured by AirExo-2 into pseudo-robot observations and actions. These adaptors must bridge both the visual and kinematic domain gaps, ensuring the adapted data is photorealistic and kinematically consistent with a real robot's operational environment.
- **Propose a Generalizable Imitation Learning Policy (RISE-2):** Design a robust visuomotor policy capable of effectively learning from the pseudo-robot demonstrations generated by AirExo-2. This policy must demonstrate strong generalization capabilities across varied environments, objects, and disturbances, enabling reliable zero-shot deployment on real robotic platforms.

In summary, the motivation is to make robotic imitation learning more accessible, scalable, and effective by innovating both the hardware for data collection and the software for data adaptation and policy learning, thereby democratizing access to high-quality demonstration data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the AirExo-2 system, comprising both hardware for scalable in-the-wild demonstration collection and software adaptors for converting these demonstrations into pseudo-robot data. Concurrently, it proposes RISE-2, a generalizable imitation learning policy designed to leverage this adapted data.

#### 3.1. AirExo-2 System: Efficiently Collecting and Adapting In-the-Wild Demonstrations

The AirExo-2 system is developed to collect human demonstrations in natural environments and adapt them for robot imitation learning. The methodology consists of hardware design, a calibration process, and demonstration adaptors.

##### 3.1.1. Hardware Design

AirExo-2 is an upgrade to the original AirExo system, focusing on robustness, precision, and usability.
- **Structural Rigidity:** Replaces 3D-printed PLA components with 20x20 European standard aluminum profiles and carbon fiber-reinforced PLA for joints, enhancing durability and structural integrity. Larger bearings are used within joints to further increase rigidity.
- **Kinematic Isomorphism:** Designed with a 1:1 scale and kinematic isomorphism to a robotic arm to minimize discrepancies between human and robot embodiments.
- **Joint Mechanism:** Features hollow rotating discs and side-mounted encoders. This design allows for internal wire routing, preventing stretching and damage, and simplifies encoder maintenance. Joints include angle limits and adjustable friction, enabling precise alignment with robot joint ranges and optimizing user comfort during data collection.
- **Gripper Control:** Incorporates a linear guide mechanism for smoother and more reliable gripper opening and closing, even under clamping forces.
- **Mobile Platform:** Mounted on a mobile aluminum profile stand to ensure base stability during operation, addressing issues of base movement caused by operator body motion. A global Intel RealSense D415 camera is mounted on top for observation capture.
- **Cost-Effectiveness:** The dual-arm platform, excluding cameras, is priced at $600.

##### 3.1.2. Calibration

A two-stage calibration process aligns AirExo-2's kinematics and camera observations with the robot domain.
- **Initial Calibration:** Involves manually adjusting joint zero positions and performing optical camera-to-base calibration using an ArUco marker board. This provides an initial, approximate alignment.
- **Calibration via Differentiable Rendering:** Refines the initial calibration by optimizing parameters (camera-to-base transformation and joint zero-position offsets) using differentiable rendering. A single human play trajectory with varied poses is recorded. SAM-2 is used to manually annotate AirExo-2 masks and depth masks from sampled frames. A differentiable rendering engine (Redner) generates rendered masks and depths, and calibration parameters are optimized to minimize discrepancies between rendered and annotated masks, and rendered and observed depths.

##### 3.1.3. Demonstration Adaptors

These adaptors bridge the visual and kinematic gaps between in-the-wild and robot demonstrations.
- **Operation Space Adaptor:** Projects all collected states and actions into a unified global camera coordinate frame, which serves as a common reference between AirExo-2 and the robot platform.
- **Image Adaptor:** Transforms in-the-wild RGB images into pseudo-robot images.
 1. Uses joint encoder readings and calibration results to render RGB-D and mask images of both AirExo-2 and the target robot.
 2. Merges the rendered AirExo-2 mask with human hand masks (obtained via SAM-2, semi-automatically annotated) to identify human-related regions.
 3. Inpaints these regions in the original in-the-wild image using ProPainter.
 4. Refines the rendered robot image using a ControlNet-guided Stable Diffusion model (trained on platform-specific, task-invariant robot play data) and composites it onto the inpainted image, creating a photorealistic pseudo-robot image.
- **Depth Adaptor:** Transforms in-the-wild depth images into pseudo-robot depth images.
 1. Captures a universal reference depth map of the empty workspace, combined with static object depth from the first frame.
 2. Uses the combined mask from the image adaptor to inpaint human-related regions in the in-the-wild depth map with the reference depth.
 3. Merges the inpainted depth with the rendered robot depth to produce the adapted depth map, ensuring spatial consistency.

#### 3.2. RISE-2: A Generalizable Policy for Learning from In-the-Wild Demonstrations

RISE-2 is an imitation learning policy designed to robustly learn from the adapted pseudo-robot demonstrations by integrating 3D spatial and 2D semantic perception.

##### 3.2.1. Architecture

- **Sparse Encoder (E_s):** Built on MinkowskiEngine (ResNet-like architecture), this encoder processes the depth image and camera intrinsics to extract sparse local geometric features (F_g) and corresponding seed point coordinates (C_g) from the 3D point cloud. It focuses on geometric information by excluding color input.
- **Dense Encoder (E_d):** Utilizes DINOv2 (fine-tuned with LoRA) as a 2D backbone to extract rich semantic features (F_s) from the color image. It also generates corresponding 3D coordinates (C_s) by applying 2D adaptive average pooling to the point cloud derived from the depth map. This component provides scene understanding.
- **Spatial Aligner:** Fuses the geometric features from the sparse encoder and the semantic features from the dense encoder. For each seed point from the sparse encoder (c_i_g ∈ C_g), it identifies M nearest neighbors (N_i) in the 3D coordinates from the dense encoder (C_s). An aligned semantic feature (f_i_s*) for c_i_g is computed via weighted spatial interpolation, using the inverse Euclidean distance to its neighbors. The geometric (f_i_g) and aligned semantic (f_i_s*) features are then concatenated to form the fused feature (f_i). These fused features are aggregated using sparse convolution layers.
- **Action Generator:** A decoder-only transformer processes the high-level sparse representations (point tokens with sparse positional encoding) to generate a latent feature. This feature conditions a CNN-based diffusion head, which then generates action chunks (a sequence of 20 future actions). Actions are represented in the camera frame, comprising absolute translations and 6D rotations, ensuring cross-scene consistency.

##### 3.2.2. Training and Inference

- **Training:** The policy is trained for 60,000 steps on 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a batch size of 240, using the Adam optimizer, a cosine learning rate scheduler, and color jitter augmentation for 20% of color images.
- **Inference:** Achieves an inference speed of 5Hz on an NVIDIA RTX 2060 SUPER GPU, indicating practical real-time deployment capabilities.

This comprehensive methodology, combining specialized hardware with advanced visual adaptation techniques and a robust, generalizable policy, aims to provide an end-to-end solution for scalable robotic imitation learning from human demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results evaluating both the RISE-2 policy and the AirExo-2 system, including its adaptors.

#### 4.1. Policy Evaluation: RISE-2

##### 4.1.1. In-Domain Evaluation

The RISE-2 policy was evaluated on four manipulation tasks (Collect Toys, Lift Plate, Open Lid, Close Lid) using 50 teleoperated demonstrations for training. It was compared against several state-of-the-art imitation policies: ACT, Diffusion Policy, CAGE, RISE, and π₀.
- RISE-2 (DINOv2) achieved an average success rate of 93.8% across the four tasks. This rate was higher than all compared baselines, which ranged from 46.9% (ACT) to 84.4% (RISE-2 (ResNet-18) variant).
- For tasks requiring precise motion execution, such as Lift Plate and Open Lid, RISE-2 demonstrated superior accuracy in predicting fine-grained actions.
- The performance difference between RISE-2 and its predecessor RISE indicated that the use of separate 2D and 3D encoders with spatial feature alignment improved policy effectiveness.
- The variant of RISE-2 utilizing DINOv2 for the dense encoder (RISE-2 (DINOv2)) consistently outperformed the variant using a ResNet-18 backbone (RISE-2 (ResNet-18)), highlighting the contribution of visual foundation models.

##### 4.1.2. Generalization Evaluation

The Collect Toys task was used to assess the generalization ability of policies under varying environmental disturbances (novel backgrounds, novel objects, or both).
- RISE-2 (DINOv2) demonstrated strong generalization, achieving 85.0% success rates under novel backgrounds and novel objects individually, and 62.5% under combined disturbances.
- These generalization rates were higher than those of other baselines, including π₀ (77.5% for novel backgrounds, 82.5% for novel objects, 82.5% for both).
- The results support the architectural choice of integrating separate encoders for 3D geometric and 2D semantic features to enhance policy robustness and generalization.

#### 4.2. System Evaluation: AirExo-2

##### 4.2.1. Learning from Pseudo-Robot Demonstrations

To evaluate the AirExo-2 system, the RISE-2 policy was trained exclusively on pseudo-robot demonstrations collected and adapted by AirExo-2, without any teleoperated robot data.
- The policy achieved an average success rate of 85.0% across the four evaluation tasks during zero-shot deployment on a real robot. This performance was comparable to the 93.75% achieved when trained on teleoperated data, indicating a relatively small performance drop.
- For comparison, the RISE policy trained on AirExo-2 data showed a larger performance drop from its teleoperated-trained counterpart, reinforcing the necessity of a generalizable policy like RISE-2 for this data source.
- These findings indicate that the AirExo-2 system can effectively collect and adapt high-quality human demonstrations suitable for training downstream imitation policies.

##### 4.2.2. Complex Task

The AirExo-2 system and RISE-2 policy were tested on "Serve Steak," a complex, long-horizon, and contact-rich task.
- Trained with 50 in-the-wild AirExo-2 demonstrations, the RISE-2 policy achieved a 50% overall success rate on the task.
- It demonstrated notable performance even in contact-rich phases such as "Scoop Steak" (70% success rate).
- This suggests AirExo-2's capability in capturing accurate, fine-grained actions and RISE-2's ability to execute complex tasks without direct robot demonstrations.

##### 4.2.3. Ablation on Visual Adaptors

An ablation study on the Collect Toys task assessed the impact of the visual adaptors.
- Removing the visual adaptors from the AirExo-2 pipeline significantly reduced RISE-2's performance from 90.0% to 52.5%.
- This result demonstrates the visual gap between in-the-wild and robot demonstrations and validates the adaptors' role in bridging this gap for effective policy transfer.
- RISE-2, even without adaptors, maintained performance comparable to RISE with adaptors, indicating its inherent generalization capabilities across embodiments.

#### 4.3. System Analysis: AirExo-2

##### 4.3.1. User Study

A user study involving 20 participants compared AirExo-2 with end-effector pose teleoperation and joint-space teleoperation on the Collect Toys task.
- AirExo-2 exhibited significantly faster task completion times (5.66 ± 1.978 seconds) compared to joint teleoperation (17.31 ± 5.055 seconds) and end-effector teleoperation (46.06 ± 27.21 seconds).
- Participants rated AirExo-2 highest in terms of intuitiveness and learnability.
- The study indicates that AirExo-2 is user-friendly and efficient for diverse operators, facilitating consistent, high-quality data collection.

##### 4.3.2. Action Accuracy

The accuracy of motion tracking for AirExo-2 was compared with handheld devices like UMI.
- AirExo-2 achieved significantly lower translation errors (1.737 ± 1.713 mm average, 6.134 mm max) across three tracks, compared to UMI (8.855 ± 3.228 mm average, 20.002 mm max).
- This higher accuracy is attributed to AirExo-2's mechanical structure and forward kinematics, which provide more precise motion tracking than visual SLAM-based methods.

##### 4.3.3. Scalability Analysis

The scalability and effectiveness of AirExo-2 data collection were compared with traditional teleoperation.
- AirExo-2 demonstrated higher demonstration throughput for equivalent collection times, leveraging its user-friendliness and efficiency.
- The system offers a significantly lower cost ($0.6k) per unit compared to typical teleoperation platforms (~$60k), enhancing its scalability.
- Policies trained on AirExo-2 demonstrations, collected within the same time frame, outperformed policies trained on teleoperation data, suggesting that adapted pseudo-robot demonstrations are effective for efficient imitation learning.

In conclusion, the findings support the effectiveness of the AirExo-2 system for scalable and accurate in-the-wild demonstration collection, and the RISE-2 policy for robust and generalizable learning from this data, without requiring real robot demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses several challenges in scaling up robotic imitation learning, offering implications for the development and deployment of robotic systems.

#### 5.1. Addressing Cost and Scalability Bottlenecks

The AirExo-2 system offers a low-cost ($600) alternative for collecting high-fidelity human demonstrations compared to traditional teleoperation, which typically requires expensive robot platforms. This cost reduction, coupled with the system's demonstrated efficiency and ease of use in human-in-the-loop data collection, significantly enhances the scalability of obtaining diverse and large-scale demonstration datasets. This approach could democratize access to data collection, enabling more researchers and practitioners to contribute to and benefit from advancements in robotic manipulation.

#### 5.2. Enabling Robot-Free Policy Training

A key contribution is the ability to train generalizable robot policies solely on adapted in-the-wild human demonstrations, eliminating the need for any robot-centric data for initial training or fine-tuning. The demonstration adaptors effectively bridge the visual and kinematic domain gaps, transforming human-centric data into a form directly usable by robot policies. This capability reduces reliance on physical robots, accelerating the development cycle and enabling researchers to iterate on policies without constant access to scarce and costly robot resources. The successful zero-shot deployment of RISE-2 on a real robot platform, even for complex tasks, demonstrates the viability of this paradigm shift.

#### 5.3. Enhancing Policy Generalization

The proposed RISE-2 policy, through its fusion of 3D spatial (geometric) and 2D semantic features (from visual foundation models), demonstrates robust generalization capabilities. Its ability to perform well in-domain and maintain high success rates under novel backgrounds, objects, and combined disturbances is significant. This improved generalization is crucial for deploying robots in varied real-world environments where training data may not cover all possible scenarios. The architectural design of RISE-2, particularly its efficient feature fusion mechanism, provides a foundation for developing more adaptable robotic skills.

#### 5.4. Broadening Applicability and Research Directions

The work opens avenues for transferring human manipulation skills to a wider range of robotic platforms. The adaptability pipeline, particularly the ControlNet-guided image generation, suggests that the system could potentially generalize to different robot embodiments without requiring new exoskeleton designs for each robot, thus enhancing data reusability.

Future research directions identified by the authors, such as integrating novel view synthesis for data augmentation, incorporating in-hand cameras, and extending to dexterous hands or additional sensor modalities (force/torque, tactile, sound), indicate a roadmap for further enhancing the system's capabilities and broadening the scope of transferable skills. Addressing visual overfitting to robot states and improving recovery behaviors are also noted as important areas for developing more robust policies.

Overall, this research represents a step towards making generalizable robotic imitation learning more efficient, cost-effective, and accessible, potentially accelerating the development of capable robotic systems for real-world applications.
