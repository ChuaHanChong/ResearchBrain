# Learning Perceptive Humanoid Locomotion over Challenging Terrain

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Harbin Institute of Technology developed the Humanoid Perception Controller (HPC), an architecture integrating terrain perception with sensor denoising through a learned world model. This framework enabled a humanoid robot to autonomously traverse 2 kilometers of diverse outdoor terrains, including stairs and rough surfaces, while demonstrating superior velocity tracking and maintaining stability even under extreme sensor noise conditions.

## Problem

- Current humanoid locomotion systems lack robust external terrain perception and struggle with unstructured, rapidly changing real-world environments.
- Existing controllers cannot effectively account for erroneous or noisy exteroceptive sensor data, leading to instability on deformable surfaces and in complex scenarios.
- Bridging the 'sim-to-real gap' remains challenging, as simulating the full spectrum of real-world perception failures is difficult, limiting deployment into unpredictable environments.

## Method

- A Humanoid Perception Controller (HPC) is introduced, employing a two-stage teacher-student distillation framework to learn robust locomotion.
- A learned world model with a Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB) is integrated to denoise noisy observations and infer privileged states, enhancing robust state estimation.
- Extensive domain randomization, including a sophisticated terrain perception noise model, is applied during simulation training to improve sim-to-real transfer and noise resilience.

## Results

- The HPC achieved superior simulated performance, exhibiting the lowest linear velocity tracking error (0.182) and angular velocity tracking error (0.237), and successfully navigating the highest mean terrain difficulty level (8.292) compared to baselines.
- The system demonstrated exceptional robustness against sensor noise, retaining 74.3% of its baseline terrain performance even under 200% noise intensity, significantly outperforming other architectures.
- The trained controller was successfully deployed on a physical humanoid robot, autonomously traversing approximately 2 kilometers of varied outdoor terrains, including stairs, cobblestone, and snow, without requiring external intervention.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learned world model with a variational information bottleneck effectively filters noise from exteroceptive perception and robustly estimates the robot's state, crucial for adapting to terrain variability.
> - Teacher-student distillation provides an efficient paradigm for transferring optimal behaviors from an oracle with privileged information to a student policy that operates on noisy, real-world-like observations.
> - Realistic modeling of diverse sensor noise and comprehensive domain randomization are essential for training humanoid policies that generalize effectively from simulation to real-world deployment without fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of navigating diverse and challenging terrains is a grand challenge in robotics, crucial for their deployment in human-centric environments, disaster response, and exploration. This paper positions itself at the forefront of this challenge by addressing a critical gap in current humanoid locomotion capabilities: the robust integration of noisy exteroceptive perception for proactive gait planning and adaptability.

**Current State of Humanoid Locomotion:** Recent years have seen remarkable progress in humanoid locomotion, largely driven by breakthroughs in reinforcement learning. Velocity-tracking controllers have enabled humanoids to achieve robust movement on relatively simple or structured terrains. However, as the authors highlight, these systems often rely heavily on proprioception (internal sensing) and operate with a "blind policy" lacking advanced external terrain perception. This limitation becomes pronounced when encountering rugged, unstructured, or rapidly changing real-world environments, leading to instability or catastrophic failure.

**Human vs. Robot Capabilities:** The paper draws a clear distinction between human and robot capabilities. Humans naturally anticipate upcoming terrain, plan steps in advance, and continuously refine their understanding of the environment and their own state (a "world model") based on accumulated experience. For instance, stepping on deep snow provides immediate tactile feedback that can correct an overestimated terrain height. Current robots, however, struggle to account for erroneous terrain perceptions or noisy sensor data, compromising their reliability on deformable surfaces or in complex scenarios.

**Traditional vs. Learning-Based Approaches:** Traditional robotics approaches for locomotion often rely on high-precision maps and trajectory optimization. While effective in controlled environments, they assume accurate models and noise-free sensor inputs, limiting their scalability and robustness in diverse real-world settings. Reinforcement learning offers a promising alternative by enabling robots to learn complex behaviors directly from experience. The integration of height maps into RL policies, has further enhanced performance, allowing for navigation of stairs and sparse footholds. However, simulating the full spectrum of real-world perception failures (e.g., overgrown vegetation, radar dropouts) remains challenging, leading to a persistent "sim-to-real gap."

**Addressing the Perception-Noise Challenge:** This paper directly confronts the critical issue of noisy exteroceptive perception. While other works have explored learning legged state estimation and inferring privileged information from sensor sequences, the challenge lies in effectively combining this with proactive terrain perception and robust control. The authors specifically aim to create a controller that not only uses external perception but also actively denoises observations and performs robust state estimation using a world model, thereby enhancing adaptability to terrain variability and sensor noise. This work extends the state-of-the-art by proposing a principled framework to integrate these elements, moving towards more intelligent and resilient humanoid navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of existing humanoid locomotion controllers when confronted with challenging, real-world terrains and the inherent noise and inaccuracies of real-world sensors. The authors observe that while humanoids are designed to navigate human-like environments, their current capabilities fall short due to a lack of perceptive foresight and robust error handling.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Integrate External Terrain Perception Robustly:** To enable humanoid robots to proactively plan their gait using exteroceptive sensors (e.g., height maps) for anticipating upcoming terrain, moving beyond reactive, proprioception-only control.
2. **Mitigate Sensor Noise and Handle Perception Errors:** To develop a system that can effectively denoise sensor observations and robustly estimate the robot's state and environment, especially when exteroceptive perception is unreliable or corrupted by noise. This includes addressing issues like overestimated terrain height, sensor gain variations, and transient sensor noise.
3. **Develop a World Model for State Estimation and Denoising:** To incorporate a learned world model that continuously refines the robot's understanding of itself and its environment based on accumulated experience, drawing inspiration from human and animal cognitive processes. This model should infer privileged information from noisy observations.
4. **Bridge the Sim-to-Real Gap for Real-World Deployment:** To train a controller in simulation that is robust enough to be deployed directly onto physical humanoid robots in diverse, unstructured, and challenging outdoor environments without further fine-tuning.
5. **Enhance Adaptability and Reliability:** Ultimately, to significantly improve the humanoid robot's performance, stability, and safety when traversing varied and unpredictable terrains characterized by unreliable estimations.

In essence, the authors aim to equip humanoids with "perceptive" capabilities that allow them to "see" and "understand" their environment, even through noisy data, and use this understanding to plan and execute reliable locomotion, mimicking a more human-like intelligence in navigating complex landscapes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose the **Humanoid Perception Controller (HPC)**, a novel solution based on a **teacher-student distillation framework** that combines terrain perception with sensor denoising. The training process unfolds in two distinct stages:

**Stage 1: Oracle Policy Training**
This stage aims to establish an optimal, high-performing reference policy.
- **Formulation:** The problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and optimized using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Privileged Observation:** The "oracle" policy has access to the *maximum information* available in the simulation, crucially including *noise-free* proprioceptive and exteroceptive measurements (e.g., ground-truth root height, local body position/rotation/velocities, joint states, and a noise-free robot-centric height map). This represents a "privileged state space."
- **Architecture:** The oracle policy uses separate actor and critic networks. A dedicated terrain encoder processes the noise-free height map into spatial features, which are then concatenated with proprioceptive and kinematic states. Temporal modeling is achieved through LSTM layers to maintain hidden states.
- **Reward Formulation:** The reward function is designed with only regularized rewards, intentionally omitting predefined motion references (like periodic ground contact or stylized imitation). This allows the robot to fully explore and develop its own capabilities within safety constraints.

**Stage 2: Student Policy Training**
The student policy is trained to imitate the oracle's actions while simultaneously learning a robust world model to handle noisy, real-world-like observations.
- **Student Observation:** The student only receives noisy observations, including root angular/linear velocities, projected gravity vector, joint states, commanded velocities, and a *corrupted* terrain height map (˜e_t).
- **World Model with Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB):** This is the core component for prediction and denoising.
 - **Architecture:** It employs a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) where an encoder `q_φs(z_t|o_1:t)` maps the history of noisy observations (`o_1:t`) to a Gaussian latent distribution `z_t`. A decoder `p_ψs(s^p_t|z_t)` then reconstructs the *privileged states* (which the oracle observed) from this latent representation.
 - **Objective:** The VAE is optimized using the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) loss. A crucial aspect is the KL-divergence term, weighted by an annealing coefficient `β`. This `β` parameter gradually increases during training, enforcing an information bottleneck that encourages the latent space `z_t` to filter out noise and retain only the most relevant information for both reconstruction and control.
 - **Inference:** During deployment, the VAE decoder is discarded. The locomotion policy directly uses the mean `μ_t` of the latent distribution from the encoder as input, enabling deterministic, noise-filtered control.
- **Imitation from Oracle Policy:** The student policy learns by imitating the teacher's actions using Dataset Aggregation (DAgger). In each iteration, the student executes its current policy in parallel simulated environments, while the oracle's actions (`a_teacher_t`) corresponding to the *privileged states* are recorded. The imitation objective minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the student's actions and the teacher's actions.
- **Loss Formulation:** The student's overall training objective is a multi-task loss combining the imitation loss and the ELBO loss, with a balancing weight `λ` (set to 0.5).
- **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer and enhance robustness, extensive domain randomization is applied. This includes randomizing proprioceptive parameters (angular velocity, joint positions/velocities, friction, payload, gravity, joint damping/stiffness, motor offset). Crucially, a sophisticated **terrain perception noise model** is introduced for the height map: `ˆE_t = α ⊙ E_t + β + ϵ_t`. This model incorporates multiplicative noise (sensor gain variations), additive noise (persistent terrain deformation), and spatially correlated Gaussian process noise (transient sensor noise with adaptable correlation length-scale). This realistic noise modeling is key to training a robust world model.
- **Student Policy Architecture:** The student's locomotion policy receives the latent representation `z_t` from the VAE's encoder as input. It consists of three fully-connected layers with layer normalization.
- **Terrain Configuration and Curriculum:** The robot is trained over a variety of custom-designed challenging terrains (stairs, rough, pyramid slope, boxes, waves) in simulation. A curriculum learning approach is used, starting with easier terrains and increasing difficulty based on performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the Humanoid Perception Controller (HPC) demonstrates its significant advantages in both simulated and real-world scenarios, particularly its superior robustness against sensor noise.

**A. Simulated Locomotion Performance Comparison (Table II):**
The HPC achieves statistically significant improvements across all key performance metrics compared to four baseline methods:
- **Superior Velocity Tracking:** The HPC exhibits the lowest linear velocity tracking error (E_vel = 0.182) and angular velocity tracking error (E_ang = 0.237), outperforming the next best baseline (PPO with Terrain Perception) by 31.3% in linear velocity error.
- **Enhanced Terrain Negotiation:** The HPC achieves the highest mean terrain difficulty level (M_terrain = 8.292), indicating its ability to traverse more challenging terrains. This is a 42.9% improvement over the PPO baseline.
- **Optimal Reward Attainment:** The HPC garners the highest normalized reward score (M_reward = 43.99), reflecting better overall policy performance.
- **Ablation Studies Confirm Architectural Necessity:**
 - Removing the world model ("Humanoid Perception Controller without world model") leads to a 12.1% degradation in terrain performance, highlighting the world model's crucial role in robust state estimation and denoising.
 - Omitting the distillation process ("Humanoid Perception Controller without distillation") results in an 18.9% increase in velocity tracking error, emphasizing the importance of the teacher-student framework for learning optimal actions.
- **Value of Perception:** Transformer-based approaches without exteroceptive sensing (Humanoid Transformer 2*) show severely limited terrain navigation (M_terrain = 3.596), underscoring the necessity of integrated environmental perception.

**B. Sensor Noise Resistance (Table III):**
A comprehensive analysis under varying sensor noise levels (0%, 50%, 100%, 200%) reveals the HPC's exceptional noise resilience:
- **Graceful Degradation:** Even under extreme noise conditions (200% intensity), the HPC retains 74.3% of its baseline terrain performance, a stark contrast to the Transformer architecture, which retains only 40.4%. This demonstrates its robust operating envelope.
- **World Model Efficacy:** The variational information bottleneck within the world model proves increasingly critical with higher noise levels. The performance differential between the full HPC and the ablated version (without world model) expands significantly as noise increases, confirming the world model's effective denoising capability.
- **Distillation Necessity for Robustness:** Pure reconstruction objectives (without distillation) prove insufficient under noise, showing 45.4% higher velocity tracking error at 100% noise compared to the full distilled approach.
- **Temporal Integration:** The world model's temporal integration capacity effectively suppresses noise beyond the immediate receptive field of a simple terrain encoder, contributing to sustained terrain negotiation capability (72.9% retention at 200% noise) compared to the rapid collapse of the PPO baseline (48.3% retention).

**C. Deployment in Real-World Terrains (Fig. 1, Fig. 5):**
The most compelling validation comes from real-world deployment:
- The model successfully traversed approximately 2 kilometers of varied outdoor terrains, including cobblestone, platforms, discrete obstacles, snow, grass, and multi-step stairs, without external intervention.
- It demonstrated strong robustness against real-world terrain complexities and sensor noise, maintaining stability even in the face of perception failures (e.g., radar dropouts, reflection noise).
- A direct comparison on stair ascent (Fig. 5) highlighted the HPC's superior performance. While a PPO baseline failed catastrophically due to overconfident foot placement and compounding state estimation errors, the HPC achieved reliable ascent through conservative foothold selection, safety margins, and full-body stabilization, facilitated by the world model's temporal filtering of real-world sensor artifacts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Humanoid Perception Controller" represents a significant advancement in the field of humanoid locomotion, offering several key contributions and potential impacts:

**1. Enabling Robust Locomotion in Unstructured Environments:** This work directly addresses one of the most persistent challenges in robotics: enabling complex robots like humanoids to reliably navigate unstructured, dynamic, and challenging real-world environments. The successful traversal of 2km of varied outdoor terrain without human intervention is a powerful demonstration of this capability.

**2. Overcoming Sensor Noise Limitations:** Real-world sensors are inherently noisy and imperfect. The proposed framework, particularly the variational information bottleneck within the world model and its integration with comprehensive domain randomization, provides a principled solution for mitigating sensor inaccuracies. This robust noise suppression capability is crucial for moving robotics research from controlled lab settings to unpredictable outdoor deployments.

**3. Advancing Intelligent Control Architectures:** The teacher-student distillation framework combined with a world model for simultaneous denoising, state estimation, and policy learning represents a sophisticated approach to intelligent control. It mimics human cognitive abilities to build and refine an internal model of the environment, leading to more adaptive and resilient robotic behaviors. The concept of "privileged knowledge transfer" from an oracle to a deployable, noise-aware student policy is a powerful paradigm for future robot learning.

**4. Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap Effectively:** Domain randomization, combined with the world model's ability to handle noisy observations, significantly contributes to bridging the sim-to-real gap. By exposing the student policy to a wide array of simulated noise and physical parameter variations, it learns to generalize effectively to the complexities of the real world, reducing the need for extensive real-world data collection or fine-tuning.

**5. Practical Implications for Humanoid Deployment:** The ability of humanoids to reliably navigate challenging terrains with perceived noise opens doors for their deployment in critical applications. This could include:
 - **Logistics and Delivery:** Operating in complex indoor/outdoor environments.
 - **Disaster Response:** Traversing rubble, uneven surfaces, and debris in dangerous zones.
 - **Personal Assistance:** Navigating homes, stairs, and outdoor paths.
 - **Exploration:** Moving through unknown and unpredictable natural environments.
 The demonstrated ability to handle stairs, deep snow, and rough terrain highlights its applicability to many practical scenarios where wheeled or tracked robots might struggle.

**6. Foundation for Future Research:** This work provides a strong foundation for future research in several directions:
 - **Further Enhancing World Models:** Exploring more complex world model architectures or integrating predictive capabilities (forecasting future terrain changes) could further improve proactive planning.
 - **Adaptive Learning in Real-time:** While robust, the current model is trained offline. Future work could investigate how robots can continuously adapt and refine their world models and policies online in deployment.
 - **Multi-Modal Perception Fusion:** Integrating other modalities (e.g., LiDAR, tactile, auditory) more deeply into the world model for an even richer and more robust perception.

**Limitations Acknowledged:** The authors transparently identify a limitation related to the multi-objective learning paradigm, specifically the delicate balance required for the variational bottleneck coefficient and imitation loss weight. This highlights a common challenge in complex deep learning systems where intricate hyperparameter tuning is often necessary to ensure optimal convergence and performance across different objectives. Addressing this limitation could involve more automated hyperparameter search or adaptive loss weighting schemes.

In conclusion, "Learning Perceptive Humanoid Locomotion over Challenging Terrain" marks a significant stride towards creating truly autonomous and robust humanoid robots. By synergizing terrain perception with advanced sensor denoising through a sophisticated teacher-student world model framework, the authors have demonstrated a powerful approach to overcoming critical real-world challenges, paving the way for wider deployment and more impactful applications of humanoid technology.
