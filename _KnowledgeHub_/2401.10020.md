# Self-Rewarding Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Rewarding Language Models introduces an iterative training method where a single language model learns to both generate and evaluate its own responses, leading to improvements in instruction following and self-assessment capabilities. This approach enables models to outperform leading open-source models on instruction-following benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench.

## Problem

- Current LLM alignment methods like RLHF and DPO are limited by the scalability and quality of human-annotated preference data.
- Reliance on human preferences caps LLMs' ability to surpass human-level performance, as they cannot receive 'superhuman feedback'.
- Separate reward models used in RLHF are typically frozen and cannot adapt or improve alongside the evolving capabilities of the LLM.

## Method

- An ==iterative Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)== framework where a single language model acts as both an instruction follower and its own judge.
- The process begins by fine-tuning a base LLM on ==human instruction and evaluation (LLM-as-a-Judge) seed data== to create an initial self-rewarding model.
- In subsequent iterations, the current model generates new prompts, creates multiple candidate responses, and then evaluates these responses using its ==internal judge capability== to form DPO preference pairs for training the next model version.

## Results

- The iterative self-rewarding process progressively improved instruction-following, with the final model achieving a **20.44%** win rate against GPT-4 Turbo on AlpacaEval 2.0 and scoring **7.25** on MT-Bench.
- The model's reward modeling ability also improved iteratively, with pairwise accuracy aligning with human rankings increasing from **78.7%** to **81.7%**.
- The self-rewarding models maintained performance on traditional NLP benchmarks, indicating that the alignment process did not incur a significant 'alignment tax' on general capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large language models (LLMs) can simultaneously improve their instruction-following ability and their capacity to evaluate responses through an iterative self-training process.
> - This self-alignment mechanism allows LLMs to generate their own high-quality feedback, moving beyond the limitations of static human or fixed AI feedback sources.
> - Explicitly training the LLM to act as a judge with a custom, additive scoring prompt is crucial for developing its evaluation capabilities, which then further enhances its instruction-following.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models has rapidly advanced, yet aligning these powerful models with human values and intentions remains a significant challenge. The prevailing approaches for LLM alignment are primarily Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF):** This standard paradigm involves training a separate "reward model" on human preference data (e.g., humans ranking responses). This frozen reward model then provides a scalar feedback signal to train the LLM via reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The limitation here is the scalability and quality of human-annotated data, which can be expensive and potentially bottlenecked by human biases or performance ceilings. Moreover, the reward model, once trained, is typically fixed and cannot improve alongside the LLM.
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** DPO simplifies the process by directly optimizing the LLM based on human preference pairs, bypassing the explicit training of a separate reward model. While more efficient, DPO still relies heavily on the availability and quality of human preference data, facing similar scalability challenges.

Recent advancements have explored **Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF)**, where other powerful LLMs (acting as judges) generate synthetic feedback to train a reward model, which then guides the LLM. Constitutional AI is a notable example, using an LLM to provide feedback based on a set of principles. However, even in RLAIF, the LLM-as-a-Judge or the resultant reward model is often a separate, fixed entity.

This paper, "Self-Rewarding Language Models," innovates by proposing a **self-improving reward model** that is intrinsically part of the same LLM being trained. Instead of a separate, frozen reward model or a distinct LLM-as-a-Judge, the *same* language model learns to both generate responses *and* evaluate them, iteratively improving both capabilities. This concept bridges RLAIF with the idea of **self-instruction and data augmentation**, where LLMs generate their own training data (e.g., Self-Instruct, Alpaca). Previous works have used LLM-as-a-Judge for data curation or as a fixed component, but not in a dynamically improving, integrated loop with the core instruction-following LLM.

The work is closely related to **Iterative DPO**, as seen in Xu et al., which showed the benefits of iterative preference training but still relied on a fixed, external reward model. "Self-Rewarding Language Models" takes this a step further by internalizing and improving the reward mechanism itself, addressing the fundamental bottleneck of static feedback sources (human or fixed AI). This places it at the forefront of research into truly autonomous and continually improving AI systems, aiming to transcend human-level performance by generating "superhuman feedback."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a paradigm for **continual and self-driven improvement of large language models**, specifically in their instruction-following ability and their capacity to evaluate responses. The authors posit a critical challenge with current LLM alignment methods:

1. **Bottleneck by Human Performance:** Existing methods like RLHF and DPO rely on human preferences. This inherently limits the models' ability to surpass human-level performance, as their training signal is capped by the quality and quantity of human feedback. To achieve "superhuman agents," the authors argue, future models will require "superhuman feedback."
2. **Frozen Reward Models:** In methods that utilize a separate reward model (like RLHF), this model is trained once and then frozen. It cannot adapt or improve alongside the LLM's evolving capabilities, potentially becoming a limiting factor for higher performance gains.

Motivated by these limitations, the paper aims to:
- Propose **Self-Rewarding Language Models**, where the LLM itself acts as its own judge, providing rewards for its own generated responses.
- Demonstrate that through iterative training, the LLM can not only **improve its instruction-following abilities** but also **enhance its capacity to provide high-quality self-rewards**. This creates a "virtuous circle" where a better judge helps train a better instruction follower, and a better instruction follower might, in turn, become a better judge.
- Show that this self-alignment mechanism can lead to models that **outperform leading proprietary and open-source models** on instruction-following benchmarks, even when starting from a relatively small seed dataset.

Essentially, the core motivation is to break free from the reliance on external, static feedback sources and enable LLMs to autonomously generate increasingly sophisticated training signals for their own continuous improvement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Self-Rewarding Language Models" approach is built upon an **iterative Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) framework** where the language model dynamically generates and evaluates its own training data. This process aims to integrate both instruction following and reward modeling capabilities within a single model.

The overall procedure involves training a sequence of models, $M_1, \ldots, M_T$, where each successive model $M_t$ is trained using augmented data created by the previous model $M_{t-1}$.

#### 3.1. Initialization

The process begins with a base pretrained LLM ($M_0$), which is then fine-tuned on two types of human-annotated seed data to create $M_1$:
- **Instruction Fine-Tuning (IFT) Seed Data:** A small set of human-authored (instruction prompt, response) examples for general instruction following. The authors use 3,200 high-quality, English, first-turn conversational examples from the Open Assistant dataset. This forms the basis for the model's initial instruction-following ability.
- **Evaluation Fine-Tuning (EFT) Seed Data:** A set of (evaluation instruction prompt, evaluation result response) examples designed to teach the model how to act as an LLM-as-a-Judge. This data is also derived from the Open Assistant dataset, where human-ranked responses are converted into an evaluation format. The target responses include chain-of-thought reasoning and a final score (out of 5). A crucial aspect is the custom, additive 5-point scoring prompt (Figure 2 in the paper) that explicitly guides the LLM on how to evaluate responses, covering criteria like relevance, coverage, usefulness, clarity, and expertise.

#### 3.2. Self-Instruction Creation (AI Feedback Generation)

For each iteration $t > 1$, the current model $M_{t-1}$ is used to generate new training data:
1. **Generate New Prompts:** New prompts are generated using few-shot prompting, sampling from the original seed IFT data, similar to the Self-Instruct approach. While the main experiments used a fixed model (Llama 2-Chat 70B) for prompt generation for efficiency, the authors show in Appendix A.5 that the self-rewarding models can also generate these prompts.
2. **Generate Candidate Responses:** For each newly generated prompt, the current model $M_{t-1}$ generates $N=4$ diverse candidate responses using sampling (e.g., temperature, p-sampling).
3. **Evaluate Candidate Responses (LLM-as-a-Judge):** The critical step: the *same* model $M_{t-1}$ then uses its LLM-as-a-Judge capability (trained via EFT data) to evaluate its own candidate responses. It assigns a score (0-5) to each response, along with a justification, using the specially designed additive scoring prompt. This self-evaluation generates an "AI Feedback" (AIF) dataset.

#### 3.3. Instruction Following Training (DPO with AIF)

The self-created AIF data is then used to train the next iteration of the model:
- **Preference Pair Construction:** From the $N$ evaluated candidate responses for each prompt, preference pairs (winning response $y_w$, losing response $y_l$) are constructed. The response with the highest score is chosen as the "winning" response, and the one with the lowest score as the "losing" response. If scores are tied, the pair is discarded.
- **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** These preference pairs are then used to train the next model $M_t$ via DPO. DPO directly optimizes the language model to prefer the winning response over the losing one, without requiring an explicit reward model to be trained.

**Overall Self-Alignment Algorithm (Model Sequence):**
- **$M_0$:** Base pretrained LLM (Llama 2 70B).
- **$M_1$:** $M_0$ fine-tuned using Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on the combined IFT + EFT seed data. This model forms the initial instruction follower and self-judge.
- **$M_2$:** $M_1$ further trained using DPO on the AIFT data generated by $M_1$.
- **$M_3$:** $M_2$ further trained using DPO on the AIFT data generated by $M_2$.

This iterative loop enables the model to continually refine both its instruction-following prowess and its self-assessment capabilities, creating a potentially self-improving system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated compelling improvements across several metrics and axes, validating the core hypothesis of self-rewarding models.

#### 4.1. Instruction Following Ability

- **Initial Training ($M_1$):** Adding EFT (evaluation fine-tuning) data to IFT (instruction fine-tuning) data for $M_1$ did not negatively impact instruction following performance compared to IFT alone. This confirms that the model can learn to be a judge without compromising its primary role as an instruction follower.
- **Iterative Gains ($M_2$ and $M_3$):**
 - $M_2$ significantly outperformed $M_1$ and the SFT baseline in head-to-head GPT-4 evaluations (55.5% wins vs. 11.7% for $M_1$).
 - $M_3$ showed further, substantial gains over $M_2$ (47.7% wins vs. 12.5% for $M_2$) and the SFT baseline (62.5% wins vs. 9.8%).
- **AlpacaEval 2.0 Leaderboard Performance:** $M_3$ achieved a 20.44% win rate against GPT-4 Turbo, outperforming notable models like Claude 2, Gemini Pro, and even GPT-4 0613. This is significant given that $M_3$ started from a relatively small seed dataset, unlike many proprietary models that leverage vast amounts of alignment data or distillation from stronger models.
- **Fine-grained Analysis (AlpacaEval):** Self-Rewarding models improved win rates across most instruction categories, complexities, and expected response lengths. However, gains were less pronounced for mathematics and logical reasoning tasks, suggesting the approach primarily helps models better utilize existing knowledge rather than learn new reasoning skills, possibly due to the nature of the Open Assistant seed data.
- **MT-Bench Results:** Similar iterative improvements were observed on MT-Bench, with $M_3$ scoring 7.25 (out of 10), up from 6.78 for $M_1$. Improvements were stronger in humanities, STEM, roleplay, writing, and extraction categories, again with smaller gains in math and code, consistent with AlpacaEval findings.
- **Human Evaluation:** Human judges largely corroborated the GPT-4 evaluations, confirming that later iterations of self-rewarding models demonstrated progressively better head-to-head performance against the SFT baseline.
- **NLP Benchmarks:** The models generally maintained performance on traditional NLP benchmarks, avoiding the "alignment tax" (performance regressions on other tasks) often seen during RLHF fine-tuning. This suggests the self-rewarding process is mostly additive without sacrificing general capabilities.
- **Response Length:** An interesting observation was the increase in average response length across iterations ($M_1$: 1092 tokens, $M_2$: 1552 tokens, $M_3$: 2552 tokens), which is known to correlate with perceived quality in some evaluations.

#### 4.2. Reward Modeling Ability

- **EFT Impact:** Training with EFT data significantly improved the model's LLM-as-a-Judge capability, with pairwise accuracy aligning with human rankings increasing from 65.1% (IFT only) to 78.7% (IFT+EFT). This highlights the importance of explicitly teaching the model evaluation skills.
- **Self-Training Improvement:** Crucially, the reward modeling ability itself improved with each iteration of self-training. $M_2$ (trained by $M_1$'s rewards) showed better reward modeling performance than $M_1$, with pairwise accuracy rising from 78.7% to 80.4%. $M_3$ further improved this to 81.7%. This demonstrates that the model becomes a better judge of its own generations over time, even without additional explicit EFT data. The authors hypothesize this is due to improved general instruction following benefiting the LLM-as-a-Judge task.
- **LLM-as-a-Judge Prompt Importance:** The custom additive scoring prompt developed for this work was found to be significantly more effective than other prompt formats, demonstrating the sensitivity of LLM-as-a-Judge performance to prompt design.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Self-Rewarding Language Models" paper introduces a highly significant paradigm shift in LLM alignment, with profound potential implications:

1. **Breaking the Human Feedback Bottleneck:** The most significant contribution is demonstrating a viable path to transcend the limitations of human preferences. By enabling the model to generate and improve its own feedback, it opens the door to achieving "superhuman feedback" for training, potentially leading to AI agents that surpass human capabilities in various domains. This is a crucial step towards truly autonomous AI improvement.
2. **Virtuous Circle of Improvement:** The finding that both instruction-following ability *and* reward-modeling ability improve simultaneously is powerful. This "virtuous circle" suggests a mechanism for continuous self-refinement, where a better judge can provide more refined signals to train a better instruction follower, and vice-versa. This iterative self-improvement loop could unlock sustained progress beyond what static datasets can offer.
3. **Reduced Reliance on Costly Human Annotation:** By generating its own preference data, the method significantly reduces the need for expensive and time-consuming human labeling, making the alignment process more scalable and accessible. This could democratize access to advanced alignment techniques.
4. **Integrated Capabilities:** Combining the instruction-following and reward-modeling abilities within a single model is an elegant architectural choice. It streamlines the training process and suggests a more unified cognitive architecture for future intelligent agents.
5. **Foundation for Continual Learning:** This work lays a foundation for continual learning in LLMs, where models can adapt and improve over long periods without requiring constant human intervention or new external datasets. While the paper acknowledges potential saturation, it offers a strong conceptual framework for overcoming the "frozen model" problem.
6. **Potential for Safety Enhancement:** As the model's reward modeling ability improves, it raises the intriguing possibility of self-improving safety alignment. Future iterations could potentially learn to identify and mitigate more subtle and complex safety risks than earlier versions, provided appropriate safety-focused evaluation prompts are incorporated into the self-rewarding loop.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations, including the need for further evaluation regarding safety, understanding the "scaling laws" of iterative training (e.g., how many iterations before saturation), and deeper analysis of the observed correlation between increased response length and perceived quality. The possibility of "reward hacking" (where the model learns to game its own reward function without truly improving quality) is also a crucial area for future research.

Despite these open questions, "Self-Rewarding Language Models" presents a compelling vision for future AI systems that can learn to critically evaluate and improve themselves, moving closer to the goal of robust and continually evolving intelligence.
