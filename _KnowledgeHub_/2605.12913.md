# Revisiting DAgger in the Era of LLM-Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> A post-training recipe that brings classical DAgger to multi-turn LLM agents. During rollouts, student and teacher take turns acting, and the chance of a teacher turn shrinks as training goes on. The teacher labels every visited state, and the student trains on those labels with cross-entropy. On SWE-bench Verified, 4B and 8B students reach 27.3% and 29.8%, beating SFT, GRPO and on-policy distillation.

## Problem

- Supervised fine-tuning on teacher trajectories trains only on states the expert visits, so one early student mistake causes covariate shift that compounds over long multi-turn agent horizons.
- Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards trains on the student's own rollouts but gets only one sparse outcome reward per trajectory, is costly due to group sampling, and collapses without diversity.
- On-policy distillation adds dense teacher supervision but stalls at cold start, because weak students' rollouts fail early, and it needs teacher logits, which black-box models like GPT or Gemini do not expose.

## Method

- **DAgger**-style ==turn-level mixture rollout==: at each turn an independent coin flip with probability beta decides whether teacher or student acts, and beta decays toward zero over training iterations.
- ==AggreVaTe==-style ==trajectory-level rollout==: the student acts for a sampled prefix length, then the teacher completes the episode, and the scheduled prefix length grows so data shifts toward on-policy states.
- ==Teacher-labeled cross-entropy== objective: the teacher action is queried at every visited state regardless of who executed, and a unified framework places this alongside SFT, RL and ==on-policy distillation==.

## Results

- On SWE-bench Verified the DAgger-style variant reaches **27.3%** at 4B and **29.8%** at 8B, beating on-policy distillation by **3.9** and **3.6** points and SFT by **4.4** and **6.4**.
- The 4B agent beats R2E-Gym-7B (**19.0%**) by **8.3** points, and the 8B agent beats SWE-Gym-32B (**20.6%**) by **9.2**. At 3K samples, DAgger hits **20%** versus distillation's **13%**.
- Under student rollouts, reverse KL to the teacher stays near **0.10** while SFT's rebounds to about **0.126**, a roughly **20%** reduction. Syntax and runtime failures fall from SFT's **20.3%** to **15.9%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Old imitation-learning theory still pays off for LLM agents. Correcting the training state distribution directly addresses compounding long-horizon errors that neither SFT nor sparse-reward RL handles well.
> - Letting the teacher take turns early solves on-policy cold start. Trajectories still reach productive states while the student is weak, which gives better sample efficiency than pure student rollouts.
> - Teacher labels only need sampled actions, not logits, so DAgger-style training works with black-box API teachers. Its leftover failures move toward context overflow and localization rather than malformed behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed as interactive agents that operate over extended horizons, engaging in multi-turn interactions with environments. Such agentic applications include software-engineering agents, web-browsing agents, and general-purpose tool-using assistants. The development of effective post-training algorithms for these agents is a current area of research.

Existing post-training methodologies for LLM agents each exhibit structural limitations when applied to long-horizon, multi-turn tasks:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: While providing dense, token-level supervision by imitating teacher trajectories, SFT trains the policy exclusively on expert-generated states. This approach leads to covariate shift, where early errors by the student policy during deployment can cause significant divergence in the state distribution from the training data, degrading performance.
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**: This method addresses distribution mismatch by training on the student's own rollouts. However, it typically suffers from sparse credit assignment, providing only a single outcome-level reward for an entire trajectory. RL is also computationally intensive and can be unstable if samples lack diversity in correctness.
- **On-Policy Distillation (OPD)**: A more recent approach that combines on-policy state coverage with dense token-level supervision from a stronger teacher. OPD aims to match student token probabilities over self-rollout trajectories to the teacher model. However, it faces a cold-start bottleneck: early rollouts from weak student policies often fail prematurely in long-horizon tasks, leading the teacher to supervise unsuccessful prefixes rather than productive trajectories. Additionally, OPD requires access to the teacher model's logits, which is not feasible for black-box LLMs.

This paper addresses these collective limitations by revisiting Dataset Aggregation (DAgger), a classical imitation learning algorithm. DAgger is designed to reduce covariate shift in sequential decision-making by gradually supervising the student using states visited by the student itself. The work adapts DAgger for multi-turn LLM agents to provide dense feedback, on-policy state coverage, and early access to successful trajectories, thereby aiming to overcome the specific shortcomings of SFT, RLVR, and OPD.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an efficient post-training algorithm for multi-turn LLM agents that simultaneously leverages dense feedback, ensures on-policy state coverage, and facilitates access to successful trajectories from the outset of training. The motivation stems from the distinct limitations of prevailing post-training paradigms:

- **Addressing Covariate Shift:** Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is efficient in providing dense supervision but trains models on expert-generated trajectories. When a student agent is deployed, its own actions can lead to states not encountered during training, causing a mismatch between training and deployment state distributions—known as covariate shift. This can lead to compounding errors, where small early mistakes derail an entire long-horizon interaction. The paper aims to mitigate this by exposing the model to realistic states likely to be encountered during its own deployment.
- **Providing Dense, Action-Level Feedback:** Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) trains agents on their own on-policy rollouts, which helps with covariate shift. However, RLVR typically provides only sparse, outcome-level rewards (e.g., success or failure at the end of a task). This sparsity makes credit assignment difficult over long, multi-turn interactions, hindering effective learning, especially for intermediate reasoning steps or tool invocations. The research seeks to integrate rich, turn-level feedback.
- **Overcoming the Cold-Start Bottleneck and Improving Sample Efficiency:** On-Policy Distillation (OPD) attempts to combine on-policy learning with dense teacher supervision. However, in the early stages of training, a weak student policy may generate many unproductive or failed trajectories. The teacher then supervises these unhelpful sequences, leading to inefficient learning and a "cold-start" problem where the model struggles to generate successful rollouts to learn from. The paper aims to provide a mechanism that ensures access to productive trajectories even with a nascent student policy and to reduce the computational cost associated with extensive unproductive exploration.
- **Compatibility with Black-Box Teacher Models:** Some advanced LLMs are available only as black-box APIs, meaning their internal logits are inaccessible. Existing methods like OPD often require these logits for distillation, limiting their applicability. The proposed approach aims to function by only querying the teacher for actions, making it compatible with black-box models.

The specific context of software-engineering (SWE) tasks, where agents must navigate complex codebases, search files, localize bugs, edit code, and submit patches over many turns, serves as a motivating application. In this domain, minor early mistakes can quickly lead to states unrecoverable by the student, making it an appropriate testbed for an algorithm designed to handle long-horizon dependencies and error recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves adapting the Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) principle for post-training multi-turn LLM agents. The core idea is to collect trajectories by interleaving student and teacher policies, then train the student on these trajectories using supervised labels provided by the teacher at every visited state.

#### 3.1. Multi-Turn LM-Agent Setting

The task is formalized as an agent interacting with an environment over a sequence of turns, given an initial prompt. At each turn *t*, the policy *π* observes the interaction history *sₜ* (current state) and samples an action *aₜ*. The environment executes *aₜ* and returns an observation *oₜ*. This continues until a "finish" action or a maximum turn budget *Tₘₐₓ* is reached, forming a trajectory *τ*. A verifier then assigns a binary success signal *R(τ, x)*. The policy is denoted as *π(· | sₜ)*, with *πₑ* for the teacher and *πᵧ* for the student.

#### 3.2. DAgger for Multi-Turn LM Agents

The paper proposes two distinct rollout strategies for collecting data, both designed to integrate student and teacher sampling to expose the model to deployment-accurate states:

- **Rollout with Stochastic Policy Mixture (DAgger-style Rollout):**
 - In iteration *i*, a mixing parameter *βᵢ ∈* is defined, which is linearly decayed towards 0 across iterations (e.g., from 1.0 down to 0.6).
 - For each turn *t* in a trajectory, a binary indicator *bₜ* is sampled. If *bₜ = 1* (with probability *βᵢ*), the action *aₜ* is sampled from the teacher policy *πₑ(· | sₜ)*. If *bₜ = 0* (with probability *1 - βᵢ*), the action *aₜ* is sampled from the student policy *πᵧ(· | sₜ)*.
 - This implements a turn-level mixture where the executor for each turn is independently selected.

- **AggreVaTe-style Rollout:**
 - In iteration *i*, a distribution *ρᵢ* over possible student-prefix lengths *κ ∈ {0..., Tₘₐₓ}* is defined.
 - For each trajectory, a single student-prefix length *κ* is sampled from *ρᵢ*.
 - The student policy *πᵧ* maintains control for the first *κ* timesteps.
 - After timestep *κ*, the teacher policy *πₑ* takes over to complete the remainder of the trajectory.
 - The distribution *ρᵢ* is scheduled so that student prefixes gradually grow over training, shifting the rollouts towards the on-policy distribution.

**Data Collection and Training Objective:**
For both rollout strategies, at every visited state *sₜ* during trajectory generation, the expert action *ãₜ* is queried from the teacher policy *πₑ(· | sₜ)*, irrespective of whether the student or teacher executed the action for that turn. These pairs *{(sₜ, ãₜ)}* constitute the data batch *B(τ)* used for training.

The student model *πᵧ* is then optimized using a standard cross-entropy loss *Lᵢ(θ)*. This loss aims to minimize the discrepancy between the student's predicted actions and the expert-provided labels *ãₜ* at each token, given the observed state *sₜ*. Transitions with shared prefixes are packed for computational efficiency with a loss mask.

#### 3.3. A Unified Perspective on Post-Training Algorithms

The paper frames various post-training algorithms (SFT, RL, OPD, DAgger) under a unified objective:
*θᵢ₊₁* = arg max*θ* *E*s∼sg(*p_s*),a∼sg(*p_a*) [*sg(w(s,a))* log *πᵧ(a|s)]* − *λΩᵢ(θ)*.
This formulation highlights three key differentiating components:
- **Context Distribution (*p_s*)**: Where the states (*s*) for training are sampled from.
- **Label Distribution (*p_a*)**: Where the target actions (*a*) for supervision are derived from.
- **Scoring Function (*w(s,a)*)**: A weight assigned to each sample, reflecting its importance.

- **SFT/Behavior Cloning:** Uses the expert's state distribution (*d*ₚₑ) and expert actions (*πₑ*) with uniform weighting (*w(s,a)* ≡ 1).
- **RL (Policy Gradient):** Uses the student's state distribution (*d*ₚᵧ) and student's actions (*πᵧ*), weighted by an advantage function (*A(s,a)*).
- **OPD:** Uses the student's state distribution (*d*ₚᵧ) and student's actions (*πᵧ*), weighted by a log-likelihood ratio relative to the teacher (*-log (πᵧ(a|s) / πₑ(a|s)) *).
- **DAgger-style (Ours):** Uses a mixture-induced state distribution (*d*ₜᵤᵣₙᵢ) and expert labels (*πₑ*) with uniform weighting (*w(s,a)* ≡ 1).
- **AggreVaTe-style (Ours):** Uses a trajectory-mixture induced state distribution (*d*ₜᵣₐⱼᵢ) and expert labels (*πₑ*) with uniform weighting (*w(s,a)* ≡ 1).

This unified view emphasizes that DAgger-style approaches bridge paradigms by interpolating between student and teacher state distributions while retaining the expert as the label source, providing dense, information-rich feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results on software-engineering (SWE) tasks using Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (4B) and Qwen3-8B (8B) as student models, and Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct (30B) as the teacher. The OpenHands scaffold is used for all agent interactions. Evaluations are performed on SWE-Gym Holdout (in-domain) and SWE-Bench Verified (out-of-domain) benchmarks.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness on Task Resolution

- **Quantitative Performance (Table 3):** DAgger-style training consistently surpassed prior post-training methods (SFT, GRPO, On-Policy Distillation) across both student scales and benchmarks.
 - For the 4B student model, DAgger-style achieved 27.3% on SWE-Bench Verified, a +3.9 percentage point improvement over On-Policy Distillation (OPD), which was the strongest non-DAgger baseline. This performance level exceeded representative published 8B SWE agents (e.g., +17.9 points over SkyRL-Agent-8B-v0 and +8.3 points over R2E-Gym-7B-Agent).
 - For the 8B student model, DAgger-style achieved 29.8% on SWE-Bench Verified, a +3.6 percentage point gain over OPD. This result surpassed SWE-Gym-32B by +9.2 points and narrowed the gap to stronger 32B-scale agents (trailing by 4.6 and 6.8 points from R2E-Gym-32B-Agent and SWE-Dev-32B, respectively).
 - The AggreVaTe-style variant also showed improvements over SFT and GRPO, maintaining competitiveness with OPD.

#### 4.2. Sample Scaling and Training Stability

- **Training Trajectory (Figure 1):** Teacher-interleaved training methods (DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style) produced more stable and consistently improving scaling trajectories compared to SFT and OPD, particularly in the cold-start phase (e.g., at 3K effective samples).
 - At 3K effective samples, DAgger-style achieved 12% on SWE-Gym Holdout and 20% on SWE-Bench Verified-100, outperforming OPD's 9% and 13% respectively. AggreVaTe-style showed similar early advantages.
 - At larger budgets, DAgger-style continued to improve, reaching 17% and 26%, while SFT's performance on SWE-Bench Verified-100 peaked and then declined (from 20% to 19%), suggesting limitations in its asymptotic scaling due to covariate shift.

#### 4.3. Policy Divergence under Student-Induced Rollouts

- **Covariate Shift Mitigation (Figure 2):** The analysis of policy divergence (average token-level reverse KL between student and teacher on student-visited contexts) indicated that teacher-interleaved training reduced deployment-time covariate shift.
 - SFT exhibited a clear covariate-shift pattern, with its reverse KL initially dropping but then rebounding as training progressed.
 - Both DAgger-style and AggreVaTe-style training maintained a lower and more stable reverse KL (around 0.10) after an initial drop, representing approximately a 20% reduction relative to SFT at the largest sample budget.
 - Compared to OPD, DAgger-style methods achieved this reduction earlier and more stably, supporting the hypotheses about mitigating cold-start issues and distribution mismatch.

#### 4.4. Qualitative Failure Analysis

- **Behavioral Changes (Table 4):** A qualitative analysis of unresolved trajectories on SWE-Bench Verified, using an LLM-as-a-judge protocol, revealed shifts in agent failure modes.
 - DAgger-style training resulted in the highest task resolution rate (27.3%) and a high patch submission rate (97.9%), while also reducing the rate of syntax or runtime errors in submitted patches (15.9%) compared to SFT (20.3%). This suggests an improvement in the quality of submitted solutions.
 - For DAgger-style, no-submission failures were predominantly due to context overflow (57.5%), rather than issues like lack of tool use, repetitive loops, or budget exhaustion. This indicates that the agent is making progress on tasks but is limited by its context window, suggesting an elevation in the difficulty of remaining challenges.
 - AggreVaTe-style training achieved the lowest "wrong file" error rate (15.7%), implying that longer teacher continuations might aid in improving repository localization.
 - Overall, mixture-policy training shifted agent failures away from passive or malformed behaviors towards more advanced long-horizon limitations, such as localization and context management.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research demonstrates that revisiting DAgger principles for multi-turn LLM agents can yield substantial advancements in their training and performance.

**Significance:**
- **Effective Problem Resolution:** The DAgger-style approach effectively addresses the limitations of existing post-training methods. It mitigates covariate shift, a known issue with SFT, by continuously training on states likely to be encountered by the student during deployment. It provides dense, turn-level supervision, overcoming the sparse reward problem common in RLVR. Furthermore, by incorporating teacher-guided recovery, it addresses the cold-start bottleneck and improves sample efficiency compared to OPD.
- **Performance Benchmarking:** The empirical results demonstrate that DAgger-style training enables smaller models (4B and 8B parameters) to achieve performance levels that surpass or closely approach significantly larger published SWE-agent systems (e.g., outperforming 32B models with an 8B agent). This suggests improved efficiency in terms of model scale required for complex tasks.
- **Training Robustness:** The method contributes to more stable training trajectories and a sustained reduction in policy divergence, indicating that the learned policies remain robust and aligned with expert behavior across their own rollouts, even as training progresses.
- **Behavioral Improvement:** Beyond aggregate task resolution, DAgger-style training leads to qualitatively improved long-horizon agent behaviors, such as better search strategies, more effective code editing, and enhanced recovery from intermediate errors. The shift in failure modes towards higher-level limitations (like context management) suggests a fundamental improvement in agent capabilities.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancements in LLM Agent Training:** The findings highlight explicit state-distribution correction as a powerful ingredient for post-training LLM agents, suggesting a promising direction for developing more capable tool-using agents in complex, long-horizon environments.
- **Software Engineering Automation:** More capable and reliable software-engineering agents could significantly assist developers in tasks such as bug localization, code repair, and maintenance of open-source projects, potentially reducing development costs and accelerating software cycles.
- **Broader Agentic Applications:** The principles of teacher-interleaved state-distribution correction could extend to other interactive, long-horizon domains, including web navigation, data analysis, and scientific computing, improving the dependability of AI assistants in various technical workflows.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
- **Domain Specificity:** The experiments are focused on software-engineering agents within the OpenHands scaffold. Further research is needed to validate the transferability of these gains to other long-horizon agentic domains.
- **Teacher Dependency:** The method relies on a strong teacher policy to provide action labels. Its effectiveness and practicality may depend on the quality, availability, and inference cost of such a teacher.
- **Residual Challenges:** The qualitative analysis indicates that remaining failures are increasingly dominated by long-context limitations, such as context overflow. This suggests that further advancements in memory, retrieval mechanisms, or context management may be necessary for continued performance gains.

**Potential Negative Societal Impacts (as acknowledged by the authors):**
- **Misuse and Security Risks:** More capable code-editing agents could potentially be misused for malicious purposes, such as automating harmful software modifications, discovering exploitable vulnerabilities, or scaling malicious development workflows.
- **Quality and Safety Concerns:** Even in benign contexts, agents might introduce subtle bugs, insecure code, or incorrect patches if deployed without adequate human review and rigorous testing.
- **Lowered Barrier to Automation:** By enabling smaller models to perform complex software tasks, the method could lower the barrier to both beneficial and harmful automation.

The authors emphasize the importance of safeguards for practical deployments, including sandboxed execution, restricted tool access, human review for code changes, and rigorous testing.
