# DSD: Learning Diverse and Reusable Motor Skills via Diffusion Skill Discovery

> [!summary] Summary
> Diffusion Skill Discovery (DSD) learns a latent-conditioned humanoid skill policy by maximizing state-skill mutual information, using a diffusion model trained on policy rollouts to estimate state-entropy gradients via denoising error, a von Mises-Fisher skill encoder for skill consistency, and an adversarial motion prior for naturalness. It discovers backflips and large jumps unaided, and outperforms ASE, APS, DADS and METRA variants on root diversity, motion FID and most downstream tasks.

## Problem

- Mutual-information skill discovery needs the marginal state entropy, which is intractable for high-dimensional humanoids, so methods like DIAYN optimize latent-space surrogates yielding skills with only minor variations.
- Direct state-entropy estimators, such as particle-based nearest-neighbor distances or skill-conditioned dynamics prediction, become unreliable in high-dimensional state spaces, producing coarse coverage and functionally narrow skills.
- The closest diffusion-based explorer, ExDM, learns no reusable skill latents, ignores motion naturalness, and was shown only in simple low-dimensional domains, not humanoid control.

## Method

- Diffusion marginal-entropy reward: a Transformer ==diffusion model== trained on replay-buffer rollouts over 10-step state histories scores novelty as summed ==noise-prediction error== at fixed timesteps 8, 15, 22, pushing policies toward rarely visited states.
- Conditional-entropy skill encoder: a ==von Mises-Fisher== reward on a unit-hypersphere latent space, following ASE, makes each latent produce a consistent, predictable behavior, combined with an ==Adversarial Motion Prior== discriminator for naturalness.
- **Jitter-Induced Diversity Suppression (JIDS)** low-pass filters only diffusion and encoder inputs (==1 Hz Butterworth==, ==Slerp== for rotations) so jitter is not rewarded as diversity; skills are reused via ==hierarchical== or ==zero-shot trajectory-pool== latent selection.

## Results

- On Reallusion, DSD reaches FID **1.64** vs ASE **2.47** and root diversity **0.130** vs **0.113**, with lowest FID and highest root diversity on all three datasets (Reallusion, LaFAN1, MimicKit).
- Hierarchical Backflip return is **0.75** vs **0.44** for ASE, with **4 of 5** DSD seeds producing backflips versus none for ASE; zero-shot selection wins **8 of 10** tasks, Jump **0.81** vs **0.31**.
- Ablations: dropping conditional entropy raises fixed-latent motion entropy from **0.60** to **1.08** and cuts Shield Bash return **0.39** to **0.08**; JIDS lowers root jerk **13%** and FID **12%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion denoising error on the policy's own recent states is a usable novelty signal in high dimensions, even without a valid entropy bound, outperforming nearest-neighbor estimators.
> - Coverage alone is not a skill library: without a consistency term, latents mix behaviors and downstream reuse degrades, so diversity must be organized into predictable latent-to-behavior mappings.
> - Diversity rewards readily exploit high-frequency jitter, and unnatural skills can still score high task return, so filter diversity inputs and judge repertoires by naturalness metrics, not return alone.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses the problem of learning reusable motor skills for physically simulated humanoid characters. Traditional character-control methods generally train a separate controller for each behavior, such as walking, jumping, or striking. These approaches can produce effective motions, but they require task-specific objectives, demonstrations, or manually designed control structures. Data-driven reinforcement learning and motion imitation have reduced this manual effort by allowing policies to learn from motion-capture datasets and adversarial motion priors.

A related line of work uses **latent-conditioned policies**. In these systems, a latent variable selects among multiple behaviors produced by a single low-level controller. Unsupervised skill-discovery methods typically optimize mutual information between the latent variable and the states visited by the policy. The objective is intended to produce both distinct skills and a broad range of behavior. Methods such as DIAYN, ASE, APS, DADS, and METRA use different approximations to state entropy or state distinguishability.

The central difficulty is that the marginal entropy of a high-dimensional humanoid state distribution is difficult to estimate directly. Latent-space objectives may cause different latent values to produce only small variations of a similar behavior. Particle-based estimators can be unreliable in high-dimensional spaces, while dynamics-based estimators depend on learning accurate skill-conditioned transition models. These limitations can result in a repertoire that is diverse according to an internal metric but has limited spatial, temporal, or functional variation.

DSD places this problem at the intersection of unsupervised reinforcement learning, physics-based character animation, and diffusion-based distribution modeling. Diffusion models have usually been used to imitate or generate motions. The paper instead uses a diffusion model trained on the policy’s own state distribution as a novelty estimator. The authors combine this marginal diversity objective with a conditional skill-separation objective and an adversarial motion prior. The goal is not merely to explore unusual states, but to organize naturalistic behaviors into a reusable latent skill space.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to learn a single low-level humanoid policy that contains a broad repertoire of distinct and reusable motor skills from unstructured motion data. The authors identify three requirements for such a repertoire.

First, it should include **behavioral diversity**, such as walking, running, jumping, kicking, dancing, or weapon-based actions when those motions are represented in the source data. Second, it should include **spatial and temporal variation** within those behaviors. For example, locomotion should not be restricted to a single direction or speed, and actions should be available with different timing and body configurations. Third, a particular latent value should produce a reasonably consistent behavior so that downstream controllers can select and reuse it reliably.

These requirements are formalized through mutual information between policy-induced states \(S\) and skill latents \(Z\):

\[
I(S;Z)=H(S)-H(S|Z).
\]

Maximizing the marginal entropy \(H(S)\) encourages the policy to cover a broad state distribution. Minimizing the conditional entropy \(H(S|Z)\) encourages each latent to correspond to a more coherent and distinguishable behavior. However, directly evaluating \(H(S)\) is computationally difficult for humanoid state spaces. DSD therefore seeks a practical approximation based on diffusion-model prediction errors.

The paper also addresses a common failure mode in diversity-driven reinforcement learning: the policy may exploit high-frequency jitter or stochastic action noise as a source of apparent diversity. Such motion may increase an exploration reward while producing poor-quality animation. The authors introduce a filtering mechanism, Jitter-Induced Diversity Suppression (JIDS), to make the diversity objectives focus on lower-frequency behavioral changes.

Finally, the authors evaluate whether the learned skills can be reused in two ways. In hierarchical control, a task-specific high-level policy selects latent skills for a frozen low-level controller. In zero-shot control, candidate trajectories generated offline with different latents are ranked according to a task-specific fitness function, without additional policy training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Latent-Conditioned Low-Level Policy

The low-level policy is represented as \(\pi(a|s,z)\), where \(s\) is the humanoid state, \(a\) is a proportional-derivative control target for the joints, and \(z\) is a 64-dimensional skill latent. The latent lies on the surface of a unit hypersphere. The policy is a three-layer multilayer perceptron that outputs a Gaussian action distribution.

The humanoid state includes pelvis velocities and height, local joint rotations, joint velocities, and positions of key joints. Experiments use a 34-degree-of-freedom humanoid and a 37-degree-of-freedom humanoid holding a sword and shield.

#### 3.2. Diffusion-Based Marginal Entropy Reward

A diffusion model is trained on state histories collected from the policy’s rollouts. Rather than using single states, the model receives a history of 10 consecutive states, which provides temporal information about the behavior. The diffusion model is a two-layer Transformer with four attention heads, 256 hidden units, and approximately three million parameters.

The model is trained to denoise policy-generated state sequences. At selected diffusion timesteps, the denoising prediction is converted into an equivalent noise estimate. The squared error between the actual injected noise and the predicted noise forms the marginal entropy reward:

\[
r_t^{ME}=\sum_{k\in K}\|\epsilon_\theta(s_t^k)-\epsilon\|^2,
\]

where the fixed evaluation timesteps are \(K=\{8,15,22\}\). States that are poorly modeled by the diffusion network are treated as relatively novel, producing a larger reward. The policy is therefore encouraged to visit states that expand its current state distribution.

The diffusion model is jointly updated during policy training. To reduce overfitting to only the most recent policy behavior, the model is trained from a replay buffer containing samples from multiple training iterations. New trajectories are subsampled before being added to the buffer.

This objective is a practical surrogate rather than an exact entropy maximization procedure. The paper notes that the diffusion loss is related to an upper bound on negative log likelihood, and maximizing this bound does not mathematically guarantee an increase in the true marginal entropy.

#### 3.3. Conditional Entropy and Skill Distinctness

DSD uses a skill encoder \(q\) to encourage different latent values to produce distinguishable state histories. The encoder maps a state history to a unit vector \(\mu_q(s)\). A von Mises–Fisher-inspired similarity objective rewards alignment between the encoded state and the latent that generated it:

\[
r_t^{CE}=\kappa \mu_q(s_t)^Tz_t,
\]

with \(\kappa=1\) in the experiments. The encoder is trained to predict the latent associated with each observed state history. The policy receives higher reward when its behavior makes the corresponding latent easier to identify. This encourages a fixed latent to generate a relatively concentrated behavioral distribution rather than mixing unrelated skills.

The marginal and conditional terms are combined as:

\[
r_t^{DSD}=w_{ME}r_t^{ME}+w_{CE}r_t^{CE},
\]

with weights \(w_{ME}=0.25\) and \(w_{CE}=0.75\).

#### 3.4. Motion Prior and JIDS

To preserve naturalistic motion, the method incorporates an adversarial motion prior based on AMP. A discriminator distinguishes transitions or state histories from the reference motion dataset and those generated by the policy. The policy receives the reward \(-\log(1-D)\), encouraging it to produce motions that resemble the reference distribution.

The complete low-level reward combines the DSD and AMP terms, with weights \(0.4\) and \(0.6\), respectively. The low-level policy is optimized with PPO, while the diffusion model, skill encoder, discriminator, policy, and value function are trained with Adam.

JIDS applies a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter to the observations used by the diffusion model and skill encoder. The filter uses a 10-timestep window and a 1 Hz cutoff for sequences sampled at 30 Hz. The policy and discriminator still receive the original unfiltered observations. Joint rotations are filtered using spherical interpolation rather than Euclidean interpolation.

#### 3.5. Downstream Reuse

For hierarchical control, a high-level policy receives the current state and a task goal, then outputs a latent for the frozen low-level policy. The high-level policy operates at 6 Hz.

For zero-shot control, the authors generate an offline pool of trajectories by rolling out the low-level policy with fixed, randomly sampled latents. Each trajectory is scored using a task-specific fitness function, and the latent associated with the highest-scoring trajectory is selected. This approach avoids training another policy but depends on the offline pool containing an appropriate behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments use three datasets: Reallusion, containing approximately 30 minutes of sword-and-shield motions; LaFAN1, containing approximately 160 minutes of locomotion, gestures, and dancing; and MimicKit, containing 2.5 minutes of highly dynamic motions such as backflips and jump kicks.

DSD produced broader spatial coverage than ASE and other comparison methods. Root-position heatmaps showed that baseline policies often remained near their initial location, whereas DSD generated trajectories over a wider range of directions and distances. Quantitatively, DSD achieved the highest root-motion diversity on all three datasets. Its root diversity scores were 0.130 on Reallusion, 0.178 on LaFAN1, and 0.186 on MimicKit. It also achieved the highest joint diversity on MimicKit and the second-highest values on Reallusion and LaFAN1.

DSD also obtained the lowest motion FID on every dataset: 1.64 on Reallusion, 1.88 on LaFAN1, and 0.29 on MimicKit. These values indicate closer agreement with the reference motion distributions than the tested baselines. In particular, ASE-DADS sometimes generated relatively high joint diversity but much poorer motion quality, with FID values of 16.73 on Reallusion and 64.34 on LaFAN1. This comparison shows that diversity metrics alone can reward unnatural motion.

The qualitative analysis found that DSD discovered dynamic behaviors without additional assistive forces. On MimicKit, DSD produced complete backflips, while ASE generally began the rotation but failed to complete it. On Reallusion, both methods generated jumps, but DSD produced larger jumps. Random latent samples also generated locomotion, kicks, sword attacks, jumps, and other full-body movements.

In hierarchical control, DSD achieved the highest mean task return on Backflip, Location, and Shield Bash, while remaining competitive on Steering, Dodging, and Beat Saber. Its reported returns were 0.83 for Steering, 0.75 for Backflip, 0.48 for Location, 0.41 for Dodging, 0.39 for Shield Bash, and 0.61 for Beat Saber. Across five independently trained models, four DSD policies provided successful backflip behavior, compared with three ASE-APS policies, one ASE-DADS policy, one ASE-METRA policy, and no ASE policies.

In zero-shot control, DSD achieved the highest mean fitness on eight of ten tasks. Its strongest relative results were on Jump and Duck, with scores of 0.81 and 0.94, respectively. It also performed well on locomotion, reaching, dodging, and combat tasks. DSD maintained higher fitness with smaller offline trajectory pools, indicating that its broader repertoire increased the probability of finding a task-relevant behavior through retrieval.

The ablations clarify the roles of the components. Removing conditional entropy increased fixed-latent motion entropy from 0.60 to 1.08, indicating that individual latents mixed more reference behaviors. It also reduced downstream performance. Removing AMP preserved or sometimes increased task return but produced visibly unnatural motions, showing that task success did not adequately measure motion quality. Removing JIDS increased root and joint jerk, worsened FID from 1.64 to 1.87, and slightly reduced joint diversity while leaving root diversity broadly unchanged.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main contribution is a diffusion-based method for estimating novelty in high-dimensional, physically simulated humanoid control while retaining a reusable latent representation. The results suggest that diffusion prediction errors can provide a more effective diversity signal than several existing entropy approximations when combined with temporal state histories, replay-buffer training, conditional latent prediction, and a motion prior.

Potential applications include character animation, interactive virtual agents, game systems, motion-generation tools, and reusable controllers for simulated robotics. The hierarchical and zero-shot experiments are relevant because they test not only whether a policy can generate varied motions, but whether those motions can support new goals with limited additional training.

Several limitations remain. DSD’s marginal entropy objective is not a formally exact mutual-information bound, and its effectiveness depends on diffusion-model calibration and implementation choices. The method also requires substantial simulation and training resources, including approximately 10 billion samples and thousands of parallel environments. The latent space is not explicitly organized by semantic relationships, so interpolation or direct interpretation of skills may be limited. Zero-shot control depends on a finite offline trajectory pool and cannot select behaviors that were not recorded. Finally, the evaluation is conducted in simulated humanoids and may not directly transfer to physical robots without addressing modeling errors, hardware constraints, and safety requirements.
