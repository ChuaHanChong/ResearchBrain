# Masked Visual Pre-training for Motor Control

> [!summary] Summary
> MVP pre-trains a ViT visual encoder by masked autoencoding on ~700K in-the-wild human-object-interaction images, then freezes it and trains lightweight MLP controllers on top with PPO, fusing image features with proprioception. One shared encoder solves eight simulated arm and dexterous-hand tasks from pixels, beating supervised ImageNet encoders by up to 80% absolute success rate and sometimes matching oracle state policies.

## Problem

- End-to-end RL from pixels has poor sample complexity and its task-trained encoders overfit to the specific scene, failing on new objects and configurations.
- Auxiliary self-supervised objectives like contrastive RL still learn representations only from environment-specific experience, never exploiting large unlabeled real-world image collections.
- Prior dexterous-manipulation results depend on explicit state estimation, expert policies, human demonstrations, or dynamics models, and existing pixel benchmarks lack fast simulation with realistic multi-finger robots.

## Method

- ==Masked autoencoder== pre-training with 75% patch masking on a ==ViT-Small== over the assembled HOI dataset combining Epic-Kitchens, Something-Something, and 100 Days of Hands frames.
- Frozen-encoder control architecture: image token features plus proprioceptive joint positions and velocities are linearly projected, concatenated, and fed to a four-layer SeLU MLP policy trained by ==PPO==.
- **PixMC** benchmark on IsaacGym with Franka and Kuka-Allegro robots, eight hand-designed tasks with dense rewards, wrist cameras, success metrics, and multi-GPU distributed PPO.

## Results

- **MVP** beats the supervised ImageNet encoder on **7** of **8** PixMC tasks, up to **80%** absolute success rate, and matches the oracle state model on **5** tasks at convergence.
- HOI pre-training outperforms ImageNet pre-training on **7** of **8** tasks; random frozen features give near-zero success on **6** of **8**, and unfreezing the encoder yields **0%** at all seeds.
- MVP surpasses reimplemented CURL and RAD baselines using **1** GPU versus **8**, and outperforms a larger CLIP ViT-Base encoder; distractor tests keep high success under color and shape changes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frozen generic visual representations can beat task-adapted ones for control, avoiding overfitting while removing encoder backpropagation from the RL loop entirely.
> - Pre-training data distribution matters more than scale or labels: egocentric human-object-interaction frames transfer to manipulation better than curated object-centric ImageNet.
> - Better representations show up as optimizer stability across learning rates and seeds, not only peak success; naive encoder scaling gave no gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of machine learning has witnessed significant advancements in various domains, including visual recognition, natural language processing, and audio processing, primarily driven by learning representations using large neural networks and incorporating domain knowledge. However, motor control, particularly learning complex tasks from high-dimensional pixel observations, has been identified as an area where this paradigm has not yet achieved comparable success.

Traditional approaches to motor control often involve end-to-end reinforcement learning (RL) with neural networks encoding image inputs. While conceptually appealing, these methods typically face two primary challenges: high computational expense and poor sample complexity, and a tendency for learned solutions to overfit to specific settings, limiting generalization to new scenes and objects. Prior attempts to mitigate these issues have included employing auxiliary objectives during RL training, such as contrastive learning with data augmentations, or pre-training representations on environment-specific data.

Another line of research has explored supervised pre-training, where visual encoders are trained on large labeled datasets like ImageNet, and then used or fine-tuned for downstream robotics tasks. While this has shown some benefits, it relies on human-labeled data and might not capture the specific visual features relevant for manipulation tasks. More recent developments in self-supervised learning in computer vision, particularly masked image modeling (e.g., Masked Autoencoders, MAE), have demonstrated the ability to learn general visual representations from vast amounts of unlabeled natural images, without explicit human labels or strong assumptions about data distributions.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by exploring whether the success of self-supervised visual pre-training on *real-world images* can be effectively transferred to motor control tasks. Unlike methods that train representations using environment-specific experience or human-labeled datasets, this work investigates the utility of broadly applicable visual representations learned from "in-the-wild" images for solving diverse motor control problems from pixels, without task-specific fine-tuning, state estimation, or expert demonstrations. The authors aim to bridge the gap between large-scale self-supervised representation learning in computer vision and its application in robotic motor control, an area previously considered an exception to the broader trend of representation learning success.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to demonstrate that self-supervised visual pre-training using large collections of real-world images can effectively enable neural network controllers to learn complex motor control tasks from pixel observations. This approach aims to overcome the limitations typically associated with end-to-end reinforcement learning from pixels in robotics.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Improve Sample Efficiency and Generalization in Motor Control:** Traditional end-to-end reinforcement learning for motor control from pixels is computationally expensive and suffers from poor sample complexity. Additionally, policies learned often overfit to the training environment and fail to generalize to novel scenes or objects. The authors are motivated to leverage pre-trained visual representations to provide robust and generalizable features to the controller, thereby reducing the sample complexity required for learning policies and improving their generalization capabilities.
- **Leverage Self-Supervised Learning for Visual Representations:** Inspired by the success of self-supervised learning (SSL) in other machine learning domains (e.g., computer vision and natural language processing), the authors aim to apply SSL techniques, specifically masked image modeling, to learn high-quality visual representations. This approach avoids the need for human-labeled data, which is often costly and time-consuming to acquire for diverse robotic scenarios, and does not require explicit state estimation, which can be challenging and error-prone in real-world settings.
- **Utilize Large-Scale, "In-the-Wild" Image Data:** The motivation includes exploring the potential of using readily available, unlabeled natural images and videos from sources like YouTube and egocentric datasets for pre-training. This contrasts with methods that rely on carefully curated or environment-specific data. The hypothesis is that visual representations learned from diverse real-world images, especially those involving human-object interactions, could be more relevant and generalizable for robotic manipulation tasks than those learned from datasets like ImageNet, which contain a different distribution of objects and scenes.
- **Develop a General Framework for Pixel-Based Control:** A key objective is to establish a framework where a *single, frozen* visual encoder, pre-trained without any task-specific knowledge, can serve as a robust feature extractor for various motor control tasks. This design aims to make the learning process more efficient by eliminating the need for back-propagation through a large visual encoder during RL training and preventing overfitting to individual tasks.
- **Contribute a Robust Benchmark for Future Research:** To facilitate and accelerate research in learning motor control from pixels, the authors are motivated to create and open-source a new benchmark suite, PixMC. This benchmark is designed to address limitations of existing benchmarks by providing high-resolution images, realistic robots, a fast GPU-based simulator (IsaacGym), efficient training tools, and well-defined tasks with dense rewards and metrics. This contribution aims to provide a standardized platform for evaluating visual representations and control algorithms.
- **Evaluate Against Established Baselines and Oracles:** The motivation includes rigorously comparing the proposed self-supervised approach against widely used supervised encoders (e.g., ImageNet pre-trained) and an oracle state-based model (representing an upper bound for RL performance). This comparison is critical to quantitatively demonstrate the advantages of the self-supervised representations.

In essence, the research is motivated by the desire to unlock the potential of large-scale, unlabeled real-world visual data for practical and generalizable robotic motor control, mirroring the transformative impact of representation learning in other AI subfields.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research, termed Masked Visual Pre-training for Motor Control (MVP), involves a two-stage process: first, self-supervised pre-training of a visual encoder using masked image modeling on real-world images, and second, training task-specific neural network controllers on top of the frozen, pre-trained visual representations using reinforcement learning. To support this, a new benchmark suite, PixMC, was developed.

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

The system utilizes a neural network architecture where an input image is processed by a high-capacity visual encoder, and the extracted features are combined with proprioceptive information (joint positions and velocities). A lightweight neural network controller then takes this combined embedding as input and predicts actions (delta joint angles). A key design choice is to freeze the visual encoder after pre-training, preventing task-specific fine-tuning during RL.

#### 3.2. Masked Visual Pre-training (MVP)

- **Self-Supervision Objective:** The paper adopts masked modeling, specifically the Masked Autoencoder (MAE) framework (He et al., 2021), for self-supervision. MAE works by masking out a high percentage (e.g., 75%) of random patches in an input image and then training a Vision Transformer (ViT) to reconstruct the missing pixels. During training, only the unmasked patches are fed into the MAE encoder, improving efficiency. The model learns visual representations related to color, shape, and object affordance by this reconstruction task.
- **Data Sources:** Two main types of pre-training data are considered:
 - **ImageNet (IN):** A large-scale dataset commonly used for supervised image recognition.
 - **Human-Object Interaction (HOI) dataset:** A custom dataset constructed by combining images from egocentric Epic Kitchens, YouTube 100 Days of Hands, and crowd-sourced Something-Something datasets, totaling approximately 700K images. Crucially, no human labels or temporal information from these video sources are exploited; only individual frames are sampled.
- **Visual Encoder:** A Vision Transformer (ViT-Small) with a 16x16 patch size is used as the backbone encoder. This model has 384 hidden size, 6 attention heads, and 12 blocks, with a computational cost comparable to ResNet-50. A lightweight decoder is used for reconstruction during pre-training.

#### 3.3. Learning Motor Control from Pixels

- **Frozen Encoder:** After pre-training, the visual encoder is frozen. This means its weights are not updated during the subsequent reinforcement learning phase for motor control tasks. This design choice aims to preserve general visual representations, prevent overfitting to specific tasks, and achieve computational savings by not needing to back-propagate through the large encoder.
- **Controller Design:** The controller is a small, four-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) network with SeLU activation functions. It receives a fixed-sized vector of image features (derived from the encoder's classification token, projected to 128 dimensions) concatenated with a linearly-projected proprioceptive state of the robot (joint positions and velocities). The controller outputs delta joint angles. A separate MLP critic with the same architecture is trained without shared weights.
- **Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to train the task-specific motor control policies. This algorithm is known for its performance on complex motor control tasks.
- **Training Details:** RL training involves collecting samples from multiple parallel environments for a specified number of iterations, then performing multiple gradient updates (epochs) on these samples. A cosine learning rate decay schedule is used, and extensive hyperparameter tuning (learning rate search with multiple seeds) is conducted to reduce randomness.

#### 3.4. PixMC Benchmark Suite

To facilitate the research, the authors developed a new benchmark called PixMC, designed to address limitations in existing motor control benchmarks:

- **Simulator:** NVIDIA IsaacGym is utilized for its high-performance, GPU-based physics simulation, enabling faster training times compared to CPU-based simulators.
- **Robots:** The benchmark includes two realistic robot setups:
 - **Franka:** A Franka Emika robot with a 7-DoF arm and a 2-DoF parallel jaw gripper.
 - **Kuka with Allegro Hand:** A Kuka LBR iiwa arm (7-DoF) equipped with a 4-finger Allegro hand (16-DoF), totaling 23 DoFs.
- **Observations and Actions:** High-resolution pixel observations from wrist-mounted cameras are provided, alongside proprioceptive information. Control is performed in joint angle space using position control at 60Hz.
- **Tasks, Rewards, and Metrics:** PixMC includes 8 hand-designed tasks ranging from simple reaching to object interaction (e.g., Reach, Cabinet, Pick, Move) for both robot setups. These tasks feature variations in objects, scenes, and movements. Dense, task-specific reward functions are provided, along with reward-independent success metrics.
- **Distributed Training:** PPO is implemented with distributed training capabilities to support large batch sizes across multiple GPUs, mitigating GPU memory bottlenecks when using high-resolution images and numerous parallel environments.

This comprehensive methodology allows for rigorous evaluation of self-supervised visual representations in complex, pixel-based robotic control scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across sample complexity, generalization, and ablation studies, demonstrating the efficacy of Masked Visual Pre-training (MVP) for motor control.

#### 4.1. Sample Complexity

- **Superior Performance over Supervised Baselines:** MVP consistently and significantly outperforms visual representations pre-trained with supervised learning (e.g., on ImageNet) across 7 out of 8 tasks in the PixMC benchmark. On the remaining task, it matches the supervised baseline. The absolute success rate improvement can be as high as 80%.
- **Matching Oracle Performance:** On 5 of the 8 tasks, MVP approaches or matches the performance of an oracle state-based model, which receives hand-engineered state information (e.g., 3D poses) rather than pixels. This indicates that the self-supervised visual representations can extract information nearly as effectively as direct state access for these tasks.
- **Failure of Supervised Baselines:** For more complex tasks like "pick" and "move" with both Franka and Kuka robots, the supervised baseline often exhibits a flat zero success rate, whereas MVP achieves high success rates, rivaling the oracle in the "pick" task. This highlights the limitations of supervised representations for intricate manipulation.

#### 4.2. Generalization

- **Robustness to Distractors:** In pick tasks, MVP models demonstrated robustness to distractors differing in color or shape from the trained object. Success rates remained high, indicating that the representations can effectively discern relevant object properties. However, the model was less sensitive to scale variations, with performance suggesting random picking between the original and distractor objects. The authors attribute this to scale ambiguity inherent in a single first-person camera setup.
- **Generality to Object Geometries:** The MVP framework generalized to picking tasks involving various objects from the YCB dataset (box, can, mug, banana). The Kuka robot with the Allegro hand achieved approximately 50% success rate across these diverse object geometries, demonstrating the adaptability of the learned representations and the multi-finger hand's manipulation capabilities.

#### 4.3. Ablations

- **Pre-training Data Impact:** Pre-training on the custom Human-Object Interaction (HOI) dataset consistently led to better performance than pre-training on ImageNet for 7 out of 8 tasks. This finding suggests that images rich in human-object interaction, even without labels, provide more relevant visual features for manipulation tasks than general object-centric images like those in ImageNet.
- **Necessity of Learned Representations:** A baseline using a randomly initialized and frozen visual encoder yielded near-zero success rates on 6 out of 8 tasks. This demonstrates that random features are insufficient for solving complex motor control problems, validating the importance of pre-trained visual representations.
- **Stability:** MVP showed improved stability across different learning rates and random seeds compared to the supervised baseline. While the supervised baseline could achieve non-trivial performance with optimal hyperparameter tuning, its performance degraded significantly across a wider range of settings, indicating that MVP's representations are more robust and lead to more optimizable training.
- **Impact of Unfreezing Encoders:** Attempts to train the visual encoder end-to-end with reinforcement learning (unfreezing weights) resulted in unstable training (loss going to NaN) and zero success rates, even after reducing the learning rate and using pre-trained MAE weights. This indicates that freezing the encoder, as in MVP, is critical, potentially due to the instability of RL signals or high capacity models requiring significantly more samples for end-to-end training.
- **Larger Encoders:** Preliminary experiments with a larger ViT-Base encoder did not show clear performance gains over the ViT-Small model. This suggests that simply scaling model size might require further adjustments to data or training recipes.

#### 4.4. Additional Comparisons

- **CURL and RAD:** MVP outperformed reimplementations of state-of-the-art methods that train vision encoders with environment data (CURL, RAD) for a fixed number of steps. MVP also required less computational resources (1 GPU vs. 8 GPUs for the baselines).
- **CLIP:** The self-supervised MVP visual encoder demonstrated the potential to outperform CLIP visual encoders (trained with large-scale language supervision) on certain tasks, suggesting the efficacy of pure visual self-supervision for motor control.
- **MoCo-v3:** While the MoCo-v3 self-supervised framework also achieved non-trivial performance, MAE (used in MVP) was found to be more effective, especially for "in-the-wild" images, highlighting MAE's adaptability to diverse datasets.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards enabling complex motor control from pixel observations, with several potential impacts on the fields of robotics and artificial intelligence:

- **Paradigm Shift in Robotic Control:** The work demonstrates that self-supervised visual pre-training on large, real-world image datasets is an effective strategy for learning motor control. This paradigm shifts the focus away from relying on task-specific fine-tuning, explicit state estimation, or expert demonstrations, which have traditionally been bottlenecks in robotic learning. It suggests that general visual intelligence can be leveraged for diverse robotic tasks without direct supervision on the task itself.
- **Enhanced Performance and Sample Efficiency:** By consistently outperforming supervised baselines and sometimes matching oracle state performance, the MVP approach establishes a new benchmark for pixel-based control. The substantial improvements in success rates (up to 80% absolute) make previously challenging tasks more tractable and demonstrate superior sample efficiency. This can accelerate the development and deployment of more capable robotic systems.
- **Improved Generalization Capabilities:** The robust generalization demonstrated across object colors, shapes, and geometries is crucial for real-world robotics, where environments and objects are inherently varied. The ability of MVP to adapt to novel object properties without re-training the visual encoder reduces the need for extensive data collection for every new task or object.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Freezing the pre-trained visual encoder during reinforcement learning significantly reduces computational burden and memory requirements. This makes it feasible to integrate high-capacity vision models, such as Vision Transformers, into the RL loop, which would otherwise be prohibitively expensive to train end-to-end. This efficiency is critical for scaling robotic learning to more complex scenarios.
- **New Benchmark for Research:** The contribution of the PixMC benchmark suite, built on the fast IsaacGym simulator and featuring diverse robots and tasks, provides a valuable resource for the research community. It offers a standardized, efficient, and realistic platform for developing and evaluating new pixel-based motor control algorithms, fostering accelerated progress in the field. The distributed training tools further support large-scale experimentation.
- **Guidance for Representation Learning:** The findings regarding the superiority of "in-the-wild" human-object interaction images over ImageNet for pre-training offer valuable insights into what constitutes effective visual data for robotic tasks. This guides future efforts in curating and utilizing large-scale unlabeled datasets for self-supervised learning in robotics.
- **Broader Impact on AI:** The success of applying principles from large-scale self-supervised learning (like MAE) to a traditionally challenging domain like motor control reinforces the versatility and power of this approach across AI. It encourages researchers to explore the transferability of visual representations learned from diverse sources to other embodied AI tasks, potentially leading to more generalized and human-like robotic intelligence.

In conclusion, this work significantly advances the state of the art in pixel-based motor control, demonstrating a viable path toward robust, generalizable, and computationally efficient robotic learning through self-supervised visual pre-training from real-world data.
