# Learning Variable Compliance Control From a Few Demonstrations for Bimanual Robot with Haptic Feedback Teleoperation System

> [!summary] Summary
> Comp-ACT teaches rigid position-controlled robots contact-rich manipulation from 20-30 demonstrations. A VR-controller teleoperation interface with vibration haptic feedback collects demos under Cartesian compliance control, and a CVAE Transformer policy predicts chunks of future end-effector poses, gripper commands, and time-varying stiffness conditioned on images, proprioception, and force-torque readings. It solves bimanual peg-in-hole, wiping, and drawing tasks while cutting contact forces roughly fivefold versus joint-position action chunking.

## Problem

- Rigid robots actuated only by position or velocity commands treat environmental resistance as tracking error, exerting excessive contact forces that can damage objects or hardware.
- Compliance control schemes fix this but demand per-task hand-tuning of stiffness parameters, and reinforcement approaches to learning them need unsafe exploration and reward engineering.
- Prior force-aware action-chunking imitation assumes joint-torque actuation, unavailable on collaborative and industrial arms, while existing VR teleoperation rigs lack cheap haptic feedback.

## Method

- A VR-controller ==teleoperation interface== maps relative HTC Vive pose to end-effector targets, binds trigger to gripper width and grip button to discrete stiffness modes, with wrist force mapped to controller vibration.
- **Comp-ACT** adapts ==Action Chunking with Transformers== into a ==CVAE== whose decoder predicts action chunks of Cartesian pose, gripper opening, and stiffness, conditioned on multi-view images, current pose, and force-torque.
- Predicted stiffness matrices are encoded in ==Cholesky form== and fed with target poses into a ==Forward Dynamics Compliance Control== backend, giving a 38-dimensional bimanual action space over three preset stiffness levels.

## Results

- In the simulated bimanual wiping task, joint-position ACT applied over **five times** the contact force on the table that Comp-ACT did across **20** trials per policy.
- Real-world success rates with force-torque input reached **100%** on cylinder peg-in-hole, **70%** on cuboid, and **70%** on picking-and-insertion, versus **40%**, **50%**, and **35%** without it.
- Force-torque input hurt wiping (**70%** versus **100%**) and was neutral on drawing, where SSIM similarity to demonstrations was **84.2%** with it and **84.3%** without.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning the controller's stiffness jointly with the reference trajectory, rather than the trajectory alone, converts trajectory prediction error into gentle contact instead of damaging force.
> - Force-torque conditioning is task-dependent, not universally helpful: it aids alignment-critical insertion but can prematurely terminate sustained-contact motions like wiping.
> - Acting in Cartesian task space over a compliance controller lets torque-free industrial arms inherit imitation-learning advances that otherwise presuppose joint-torque actuation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses significant challenges within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on dexterous, contact-rich tasks executed by rigid robots. Rigid robots, commonly found in industrial and collaborative settings, are typically position-controlled, which can lead to excessive contact forces and potential damage when interacting with the environment or objects. Compliance control schemes have been developed to mitigate these issues by allowing robots to manage contact forces through external sensors. However, these methods often require extensive manual fine-tuning of task-specific parameters, posing a barrier to widespread adoption.

Learning from Demonstrations (LfD) has emerged as an intuitive approach to teach robots complex manipulation skills. Instead of explicit programming or reward function engineering, LfD enables robots to learn from observed human actions. Despite its advantages, LfD faces challenges such as the need for numerous demonstrations (sample efficiency), difficulties in selecting optimal controller parameters (like stiffness), and the requirement for effective and intuitive human-robot interfaces for data collection.

This research integrates and extends existing approaches to tackle these combined challenges. It builds upon previous work in VR-based teleoperation systems, which have shown promise for intuitive human control of robots, and adapts methods like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) for robot policy learning. The paper differentiates itself from prior work by focusing on rigid robots actuated via position/velocity commands rather than torque-controlled robots. It also extends LfD by simultaneously learning both motion trajectories and variable compliance parameters in Cartesian space, an area where existing methods often separate these concerns or operate in joint space. By introducing an inexpensive haptic feedback system, the authors aim to improve the safety and intuitiveness of the data collection process, which is a crucial aspect of LfD.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the teaching of dexterous, contact-rich manipulation tasks to rigid robots by addressing key limitations in existing methodologies. This overarching goal is broken down into specific objectives, driven by several motivations:

1. **Overcome Challenges of Rigid Robots in Contact-Rich Tasks:** Rigid robots, being position-controlled, often exert excessive forces during contact, risking damage to the robot, the environment, or the manipulated objects. While compliance control can mitigate this, its reliance on fixed or manually tuned parameters limits adaptability. The motivation is to enable rigid robots to safely and effectively perform tasks requiring physical interaction by incorporating variable compliance.

2. **Improve Sample Efficiency and Parameter Learning in LfD:** Traditional LfD often demands a large number of demonstrations to learn complex behaviors, making data collection time-consuming. Furthermore, LfD typically learns motion trajectories but struggles with simultaneously learning the optimal, time-varying compliance parameters necessary for nuanced contact tasks. The objective is to develop an LfD method that can learn variable compliance control from a *few* demonstrations, reducing the burden on human operators and improving task adaptability.

3. **Develop an Intuitive and Cost-Effective Teleoperation Interface:** Effective data collection for LfD requires an interface that is easy for human operators to use, allows for natural demonstration of complex motions, and provides sufficient feedback. Existing teleoperation systems vary in intuitiveness, cost, and feedback mechanisms. The motivation is to create a teleoperation system that uses readily available and cost-effective Virtual Reality (VR) controllers, offers intuitive control, and incorporates haptic feedback to enhance operator awareness and safety during demonstrations. This addresses the need for a practical teaching interface that can capture subtle manipulation strategies.

4. **Integrate Haptic Feedback for Enhanced Safety and Control:** During teleoperation, operators need to be aware of interaction forces to prevent damage. While compliance control provides safety at the robot level, haptic feedback can provide real-time tactile information to the human operator, allowing them to adapt their demonstration actions proactively. The objective is to integrate haptic feedback via VR controller vibrations, mapping contact forces to tactile cues, thereby increasing the safety and efficacy of data collection.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire to make rigid robots more capable and versatile in real-world, contact-rich scenarios, by developing a comprehensive system that combines an intuitive data collection interface with a sample-efficient learning algorithm for adaptive, variable compliance control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system is composed of two main components: a teleoperation interface for data collection and a learning method for variable compliance control, named Comp-ACT.

**A. Teleoperation System**
The teleoperation system is designed for intuitive and safe data collection of contact-rich manipulation tasks, supporting both single-arm and bimanual robot setups.
1. **Hardware:** It utilizes commercially available Virtual Reality (VR) controllers (specifically, HTC Vive Cosmos Elite) for human input.
2. **Control Mechanism:** The system tracks the relative translation and orientation of the VR controller from a user-defined starting pose. This relative motion is mapped to the target pose of the robot's End-Effector (EE). A menu button on the controller enables/disables teleoperation and sets the starting pose. The trigger button controls the gripper's opening width (0-100%).
3. **Cartesian Compliance Control:** To ensure safety during contact-rich demonstrations with rigid robots, the teleoperation system employs a Cartesian compliance control approach. This prevents the robot from exerting excessive forces by treating environmental resistance as a position error. The specific method used is Forward Dynamics Compliance Control (FDCC), which combines impedance, admittance, and force control principles. In this work, the goal Cartesian position of the EE ($x_g$) and the stiffness of the controller ($K$) are considered controllable and time-variant parameters during teleoperation. Other parameters, such as goal wrench ($F_g$), are manually fine-tuned and kept constant. The system uses an open-source ROS implementation of FDCC.
4. **Variable Stiffness during Demonstration:** The operator can switch between three predefined stiffness modes (Low: 250, Medium: 500, High: 750 for diagonal values of the stiffness matrix) using the grip button on the VR controller. For each task and arm, one mode is fixed at medium stiffness, while the other alternates between high and low, depending on task requirements.
5. **Haptic Feedback:** Haptic feedback is provided to the operator through the vibration mechanism of the VR controllers. The intensity of the vibration is directly mapped to the contact force measured by the robot's wrist Force/Torque (F/T) sensor. This allows the operator to perceive contact forces and adjust their control actions (e.g., move away or change stiffness) in real-time, enhancing safety.

**B. Comp-ACT: Learning Variable Compliance Control**
Comp-ACT (Compliance Control via Action Chunking with Transformers) is the learning method that leverages collected demonstrations to predict future robot actions, including both motion and compliance parameters.
1. **Foundation in Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT):** Comp-ACT builds upon the ACT framework, which addresses the compounding error problem in Learning from Demonstrations (LfD) by predicting a "chunk" or sequence of future actions at each time step, rather than just the immediate next action.
2. **Modifications for Variable Compliance Control:** The key modifications from standard ACT include:
 - **Task Space Control:** Comp-ACT operates in Cartesian task space, predicting the target EE pose, instead of joint space.
 - **F/T Sensor Integration:** External F/T sensor readings are incorporated as part of the observation to condition the prediction of future actions.
 - **Simultaneous Stiffness Learning:** Crucially, Comp-ACT simultaneously learns the stiffness parameters of the compliance controller alongside the reference Cartesian trajectory.
3. **Network Architecture:** Comp-ACT utilizes a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) with a Transformer-based policy. The CVAE's decoder predicts a sequence of actions conditioned on the current observation.
 - **Observations:** The policy's inputs include RGB images from multiple viewpoints, the current Cartesian pose of the EE, and the measured F/T readings.
 - **Actions:** Each robot's action comprises three components: a 6D Cartesian pose (position and axis-angle orientation), a 1D gripper action (desired opening width), and a 12D vector representing the stiffness parameters. For a bimanual setup, this results in a 38-dimensional action space. The stiffness matrix ($K$) is encoded using its Cholesky representation ($\hat{K}$, where $K = \hat{K}^T \hat{K}$), specifically the flattened form of the upper triangular matrix, to allow for variable stiffness in both translation and rotation.
4. **Data Collection for Learning:** Demonstrations are collected using the described teleoperation system. The saved data includes the Cartesian trajectory of the robot's EE, measured F/T, the compliance control parameters (stiffness modes) selected by the operator, and camera images from multiple viewpoints.
5. **Training:** Policies are learned from scratch for 20,000 epochs, with training times varying from 2 to 5 hours depending on the number of demonstrations and cameras.

**C. Experimental Setup**
Experiments were conducted in both simulation and real-world environments.
- **Robots:** Two UR5e robot arms, equipped with built-in force-torque sensors, were used for both single-arm and bimanual tasks.
- **Cameras:** Realsense SR305 cameras were attached to the wrist of each arm, complemented by a static camera with a wider field of view positioned in front of the task space.
- **Simulation Environment:** MuJoCo physics engine within a custom robosuite environment replicated the robotic setup.
- **Computational Resources:** A machine with an Intel i7-12700F CPU and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU handled the robotic system. Simulation and policy training were performed on a separate machine with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU.
- **Tasks:** Six contact-rich manipulation tasks were used for evaluation: one bimanual task in simulation (Bimanual Wiping) and five real-world tasks (Single-arm Picking & Insertion, Single-arm Wiping, Single-arm Drawing, Bimanual Peg-in-Hole Cylinder, Bimanual Peg-in-Hole Cuboid). The number of demonstrations ranged from 20 to 30 per task, and camera configurations varied.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Comp-ACT was evaluated through both simulated and real-world experiments, including comparisons with baseline methods and ablation studies on the impact of F/T sensor data.

**A. Simulation Task (Bimanual Wiping)**
The bimanual wiping task was used to compare Comp-ACT against standard ACT (joint position-based control) and to evaluate the effect of adding F/T data as an observation.
- **Comp-ACT vs. ACT:** Both Comp-ACT and ACT policies successfully completed the task (picking/lifting hammer, wiping, returning hammer). However, a significant difference was observed in contact forces applied to the table. ACT, using joint-position control, exerted contact forces more than five times higher than Comp-ACT. This indicates that minor trajectory errors with joint-position control lead to substantial forces, whereas Comp-ACT's compliance control (especially with low stiffness during wiping) prevented excessive force even with minor trajectory deviations. This supports Comp-ACT's claim of enhanced safety for rigid robots.
- **F/T as Observation:** Adding F/T data to the ACT policy (ACT + F/T) helped suppress applied forces compared to ACT without F/T, but still resulted in excessive forces. In contrast, there was no significant difference in force profiles between Comp-ACT and Comp-ACT without F/T. The authors suggest this is because Comp-ACT's inherent compliance already leads to lower contact forces, making the F/T data less critical for force regulation in this specific wiping scenario.

**B. Real-World Tasks**
Five challenging contact-rich tasks were used to assess Comp-ACT's performance in real-world scenarios, including an ablation study on the effectiveness of F/T data as observational input. The results are summarized by success rates and qualitative observations.

- **Picking & Insertion (Single-arm):**
 - Comp-ACT with F/T: 70% success rate for the insertion phase.
 - Comp-ACT without F/T: 35% success rate for the insertion phase.
 - Both policies consistently succeeded in picking the peg. The higher success rate with F/T data indicates its contribution to precise alignment required for successful insertion.
- **Wiping (Single-arm):**
 - Comp-ACT with F/T: 70% success rate.
 - Comp-ACT without F/T: 100% success rate.
 - This was the only task where Comp-ACT without F/T performed better. Failure cases for Comp-ACT with F/T resulted from the policy ending the wiping motion prematurely. The authors hypothesize that F/T data might have erroneously signaled the policy to stop. Policies without F/T exhibited longer wiping motions.
- **Drawing (Single-arm):**
 - Comp-ACT with F/T: 100% success rate.
 - Comp-ACT without F/T: 100% success rate.
 - Success was judged by resemblance to an emoji smile. Both policies produced clear, continuous lines with appropriate force. Quantitative analysis using Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) showed negligible difference (84.2% for Comp-ACT with F/T, 84.3% for Comp-ACT without F/T). Policies without F/T again exhibited longer motions. The task demonstrated that the policy successfully switched to lower stiffness when in contact with the whiteboard, preventing excessive force (Figure 13).
- **Peg-in-Hole (Cylinder, Bimanual):**
 - Comp-ACT with F/T: 100% success rate.
 - Comp-ACT without F/T: 40% success rate.
 - This was a highly challenging task requiring accurate bimanual coordination. The inclusion of F/T data significantly improved performance.
- **Peg-in-Hole (Cuboid, Bimanual):**
 - Comp-ACT with F/T: 70% success rate.
 - Comp-ACT without F/T: 50% success rate.
 - Similar to the cylindrical peg-in-hole, F/T data improved success. Failure cases in both peg-in-hole tasks included misalignment, excessive contact force, or premature release of the peg.

In summary, Comp-ACT demonstrated high success rates for most complex contact-rich tasks, often from a limited number of demonstrations (20-30). The F/T data was generally beneficial, particularly for alignment-sensitive tasks like peg-in-hole insertions, where it enabled more robust and precise execution. However, its utility was task-dependent, occasionally leading to premature termination in tasks like wiping. Crucially, the system effectively learned to adjust stiffness parameters autonomously, maintaining safe interaction forces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning the deployment of rigid robots in dexterous, contact-rich environments. The core significance lies in enabling these widely used, but often force-unaware, robots to perform complex interaction tasks safely and adaptively with a relatively low data requirement.

1. **Enhanced Safety for Rigid Robots:** The most immediate impact is on the safe operation of rigid, position-controlled robots during physical interaction. By simultaneously learning variable stiffness parameters and motion trajectories in Cartesian space, Comp-ACT ensures that the robot can autonomously adjust its compliance. This capability mitigates the risk of excessive contact forces, which can damage the robot, manipulated objects, or the environment, a common drawback of rigid robots in contact tasks. The simulation results, showing significantly lower contact forces with Comp-ACT compared to a joint-position-controlled ACT, directly support this.

2. **Increased Applicability of Learning from Demonstrations (LfD):** The proposed Comp-ACT method makes LfD more practical for contact-rich tasks. By integrating variable compliance control into the learning framework and demonstrating high success rates from a few demonstrations (20-30), the work addresses the critical challenges of sample efficiency and optimal parameter tuning. This can reduce the time and expertise required to deploy robots for new manipulation tasks.

3. **Intuitive and Cost-Effective Data Collection:** The VR-based teleoperation system with haptic feedback provides an accessible and intuitive method for human operators to collect high-quality demonstration data. Utilizing commercial VR controllers lowers the entry barrier for research and industrial applications compared to custom-designed hardware. The haptic feedback mechanism improves operator awareness, enhancing the safety and quality of the collected demonstrations by allowing real-time adjustments to robot behavior.

4. **Advancement in Compliant Control Learning:** The method's ability to learn time-varying stiffness parameters as part of the action space, rather than relying on predefined or manually tuned values, represents a step forward in data-driven compliant control. This adaptability allows the robot to execute tasks like drawing with consistent force or performing precise insertions with appropriate rigidity or flexibility as needed.

5. **Foundation for Future Robot Autonomy:** The demonstrated success in learning complex bimanual tasks and single-arm contact tasks suggests a pathway toward more autonomous and versatile robot systems. While the current work focuses on task-specific learning, the underlying methodology lays groundwork for future research into generalization across wider task variations or multiple tasks, as highlighted in the limitations section.

In conclusion, this research provides a validated system that bridges the gap between the capabilities of rigid robots and the demands of dexterous contact-rich manipulation. By combining an intuitive teleoperation interface with a robust, sample-efficient learning algorithm for variable compliance, it contributes significantly to making robotic automation safer, more adaptable, and more widely deployable in real-world scenarios.
