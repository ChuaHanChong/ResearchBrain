# SID: Sliding into Distribution for Robust Few-Demonstration Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Sliding into Distribution (SID) framework enables robust robotic manipulation with few human demonstrations by employing an object-centric motion field to guide the robot into a demonstrated state distribution and an egocentric execution policy for task completion. This approach achieved approximately 90% success rates under out-of-distribution initializations with only two raw demonstrations per task and maintained 82-88% success against external disturbances.

## Problem

- End-to-end visuomotor policies struggle with distribution shift and out-of-distribution (OOD) execution, especially with limited training data, leading to fragility.
- Current methods often treat manipulation as a homogeneous problem, failing to differentiate between distinct approach (global geometry) and execution (local interaction) phases, making policies brittle under pose shifts.
- Achieving robust generalization across varying object poses, camera viewpoints, and dynamic disturbances typically requires extensive and costly demonstration data.

## Method

- **SID** introduces an ==object-centric motion field== that iteratively guides the robot from OOD states back into the demonstrated approach manifold, acting as an online recovery mechanism.
- An ==egocentric execution policy== performs precise, task-specific manipulation once the robot is within the reliable operating region, minimizing reliance on absolute workspace coordinates.
- Kinematically consistent ==egocentric data augmentation==, which reprojects only the target object's point cloud, is used to expand perceptual diversity while preserving action-observation consistency for policy training.

## Results

- SID achieved **around 90% success rates** on OOD initializations across six real-world tasks using only two raw demonstrations, outperforming baselines by a large margin.
- The closed-loop inference pipeline (SID-closed) demonstrated **82-88% robustness** to external disturbances and strong performance (**88-90%**) in unstructured, cluttered scenes.
- SID effectively enabled sub-skill reuse and composition for novel long-horizon manipulation tasks by invoking pre-trained sub-skill models stage-by-stage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly managing distribution shift through an online, learned motion field allows robust few-demonstration manipulation across wide spatial variations and disturbances.
> - Decomposing manipulation into distinct approach and execution phases, each with specialized modules, significantly enhances robustness and data efficiency compared to single-policy approaches.
> - Combining egocentric observation representations with targeted data augmentation and a confidence-based closed-loop inference pipeline enables continuous mitigation of distribution drift during complex, multi-stage tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation faces challenges in achieving robust generalization across varying object poses, camera viewpoints, and dynamic disturbances, particularly when trained with limited demonstration data. Current approaches to robot learning from demonstrations often struggle with "distribution shift," a common failure mode where policies encounter states or observations not adequately represented in the training demonstrations. This issue becomes more pronounced in low-coverage datasets and can undermine the performance of even expressive end-to-end visuomotor policies.

Existing learning-based methods have made progress in areas such as long-horizon imitation learning, multi-task policy architectures (e.g., transformer-based policies), and diffusion-based visuomotor policies. While these methods can represent complex behaviors, they typically require extensive data to cover variations in approach and interaction phases. Equivariant policy designs aim to provide robustness to shifts aligning with underlying symmetries (e.g., rigid SE(3) transforms), but often involve architectural complexities that can limit expressive power or ease of deployment. Many of these approaches treat the entire manipulation episode as a homogeneous control problem, which can be problematic because a manipulation task typically involves distinct phases, such as an initial approach phase requiring global geometric recovery and a subsequent execution phase demanding precise, localized interaction. A single policy attempting to cover both regimes with limited data can become brittle under pose shifts and perturbations.

Coarse-to-fine decomposition strategies have shown effectiveness in one-shot settings by structuring the control problem into stages, suggesting that explicit structural decomposition can mitigate out-of-distribution (OOD) failures when data is scarce. However, existing coarse-to-fine pipelines are often open-loop during execution and may be sensitive to the initial state's proximity to the first demonstrated waypoint. Object-centric policy learning attempts to improve robustness by representing goals and behaviors relative to objects, often leveraging 6D pose estimation or segmentation. However, these methods typically focus on improving representations rather than explicitly managing distribution shifts for robust generalization with minimal demonstrations.

The presented work, Sliding into Distribution (SID, builds upon these foundations by proposing a structured framework that explicitly manages distribution shift through an online recovery mechanism. It aims to combine the benefits of object-centric representations and phased control without the extensive data requirements or sensitivity limitations of prior methods, particularly focusing on few-demonstration scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables robust robotic manipulation with only a few human demonstrations, specifically addressing the challenges of generalization across varying object poses, camera viewpoints, and dynamic disturbances. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Addressing Distribution Shift with Sparse Data:** End-to-end visuomotor policies often struggle when test-time observations deviate significantly from the limited demonstration data, a phenomenon known as out-of-distribution (OOD) execution. This is a critical barrier to deploying robots in unstructured or novel environments where exact replication of training conditions is not feasible. The authors aim to explicitly manage this distribution shift.

2. **Differentiating Manipulation Regimes:** A typical manipulation episode involves qualitatively different phases: an "approach phase" (recovering global geometry, often underdetermined) and an "execution phase" (local, interaction-rich, sensitive to small deviations). Training a single end-to-end policy for both with limited data leads to fragility. The motivation is to decompose the problem into specialized modules for each phase to enhance robustness and data efficiency.

3. **Enhancing Data Efficiency:** Acquiring large, diverse datasets for robotic manipulation is costly and time-consuming. The goal is to achieve strong generalization with an extremely small number of demonstrations (e.g., one or two), making robot learning more practical for real-world deployments.

4. **Robustness to Real-World Challenges:** The framework is motivated to maintain reliability not only under initial pose and viewpoint shifts but also against external disturbances, cluttered scenes, and in long-horizon, multi-step tasks.

5. **Enabling Skill Reuse and Composition:** Current methods often require retraining for novel task compositions. A further objective is to facilitate the modular combination of learned sub-skills to solve new, more complex tasks without extensive new demonstrations or retraining.

To achieve these objectives, SID proposes to learn an object-centric motion field to iteratively guide the robot from OOD states back into the demonstrated manifold, and then leverage a lightweight egocentric execution policy for task-specific manipulation within this reliable operating region. This approach seeks to mitigate OOD failures by explicitly managing distribution recovery online.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Sliding into Distribution (SID) is a structured manipulation framework comprising four main components: an object-centric motion field, an egocentric data augmentation module, an egocentric execution policy, and two inference pipelines.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The framework addresses few-demonstration robot manipulation under workspace-wide variation and disturbances. A task is defined by a small set of demonstrations (M ≤ 2), where each demonstration is a sequence of egocentric observations (wrist-centric colored point cloud P_t and gripper width g_t) and relative end-effector actions (a_t in SE(3)). The goal is to learn a control system that succeeds under large spatial variations and disturbances using only these limited demonstrations.

**B. Object-Centric Motion Field**
The motion field, denoted f_θ, is designed to address distribution shift by funneling the system toward the demonstrated approach manifold.

1. **Canonicalization:** A canonicalization operator C extracts an object-centric representation x_t and an anchor pose ¯x_t from an observation. The anchor pose ¯x_t (T_c_obj(t) ∈ SE(3) in the wrist-camera frame) specifies the target object's pose and anchors an object-centered frame. In this work, x_t is set to be the target object's pose, T_c_obj(t). This pose is used to construct and execute the motion field and ensure geometric consistency for augmentation, but is not directly input to the execution policy.

2. **Demonstrated Approach Manifold:** The motion field is restricted to monotonic approach segments of demonstrations, where corrective behavior toward an approach goal is well-defined. X_demo represents the set of object-centric approach states from the demonstrations.

3. **Distance-Induced Sliding Field:** A pose-aligned potential E(x) is defined using an SE(3) distance between anchor poses d(x, x_j). The potential E(x) is the squared minimum distance to the demonstrated set X_demo. A gradient-descent-like update is performed without explicit gradient computation. For any query state x, the nearest demonstrated state x∗(x) is identified. The SE(3) displacement between anchor poses is computed as a 6D twist. A unit descent direction is formed from this twist, and the target sliding step v∗(x) is defined as a distance-scheduled step size (η(·)) multiplied by this unit direction.

4. **Learning and Execution:** The motion field f_θ is parameterized as a neural network that maps an object-centric state (x) and an object key (k_obj) to a 6D twist. It is trained to regress the target sliding steps v∗(x) using a mean squared error loss. At inference, the system iteratively queries the field and executes the predicted sliding step until the magnitude of the predicted step falls below a small threshold, indicating convergence to the demonstrated manifold.

**C. Egocentric Data Augmentation**
This module expands perceptual diversity while preserving action-observation consistency.

1. **Egocentric Observation Representation:** The policy uses point clouds from a wrist-mounted camera, providing an egocentric representation. This allows for consistent rigid transformations via hand-eye calibration.

2. **Data Augmentation via Point Cloud Reprojection:** To synthesize observations under perturbed viewpoints, a random end-effector pose perturbation (T_ee_ee_aug) is sampled and converted to a camera-frame perturbation using fixed hand-eye calibration. Crucially, *only the target-object point cloud* is transformed using this reprojection, while the gripper point cloud remains unchanged. This ensures physical consistency. Actions are also updated consistently for perturbed consecutive frames.

3. **ID/OOD Augmentations:** The sampling space of perturbations is controlled to generate both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) augmentations. ID samples, derived from small perturbations, are used for policy training. OOD samples, generated from larger, disjoint perturbations, serve as negative examples for an auxiliary ID-confidence head. Each augmented sample is labeled (p_ID ∈ {0, 1}).

**D. Egocentric Execution Policy**
The policy performs task-specific manipulation once the system is within a reliable operating region.

1. **Egocentric, Base-Free Interface:** The policy conditions only on egocentric observations (segmented point cloud and gripper width) and predicts relative end-effector motions and a gripper command. It also includes a termination signal. This design promotes generalization by decoupling execution from absolute workspace placement. A task key (k_task) allows for conditioning on specific subtasks.

2. **Conditional Flow Matching:** The policy is trained using conditional flow matching. It samples a data action chunk and a base sample (Gaussian noise), defines a linear probability path between them, and trains a conditional velocity network to predict the constant target velocity field along this path. Inference involves integrating the learned ODE.

3. **Network Architecture:** A PointNet-style visuomotor encoder embeds the segmented point cloud and gripper width. An action head predicts the action-space velocity field. An auxiliary ID-confidence head (c_ϕ) is attached to the shared observation encoder, trained to predict whether an observation is in-distribution or out-of-distribution (using ID/OOD labels from augmentation). Stop-gradient is applied from the auxiliary head to prevent it from altering the action prediction representation.

**E. Inference Procedures of SID**
SID offers two inference pipelines:

1. **Open-Loop Inference Pipeline (Alg. 1):** The system first iteratively executes sliding steps from the motion field until the field's predicted magnitude falls below a threshold (ϵ_field). Once aligned, control is handed off to the execution policy, which takes action chunks until a termination signal is met.

2. **Closed-Loop Inference Pipeline (Alg. 2):** This pipeline incorporates the ID confidence from the auxiliary head. If the policy's ID confidence (p_ID) is above a threshold (ϵ_ID), the policy executes. If not, the system checks the pose confidence (p_pose) from the foundation pose estimator. If pose confidence is high, the motion field is invoked to re-align the system. If pose confidence is low, a RECOVERY action (moving the end-effector to a safe, visible pose) is executed before re-evaluating. This provides a continuous safeguard against distribution drift during execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated SID across six real-world manipulation tasks: OPEN DRAWER, POUR WATER, HANG CUP, HANG TAPE, PNP-BOX, and MULTI-PNP-BOX, using only two raw demonstrations per task (augmented to 100 training samples). SID was compared against five baselines: ACT, DP3, π_0.5, Ret-BC, and MT3, which were provided with 10 to 100 demonstrations.

**Q1: Workspace-wide Generalization under OOD Initializations**
- SID-open and SID-closed achieved approximately 90% success rates under OOD initializations across tasks (Table I), demonstrating strong spatial generalization.
- All baselines (except MT3 and Ret-BC) suffered significant performance degradation in OOD settings, with success rates often dropping to 0%.
- SID consistently outperformed MT3 and Ret-BC, which also aim for generalization, attributed to SID's online motion field updates that enable more accurate re-entry into the training distribution compared to retrieval-based alignment from a single initial observation.
- SID-closed consistently outperformed SID-open, indicating the benefit of continuous distribution drift mitigation in post-contact phases.

**Q2: Robustness to External Disturbances**
- SID-closed demonstrated high robustness to external disturbances, with success rates of 82-88% across four tasks (Table II).
- SID-open was more robust than most baselines due to its continuously updated motion field but could degrade under strong disturbances in execution phases.
- Baselines like MT3 and Ret-BC showed substantial performance drops in dynamic scenarios, as their alignment methods do not update online. π_0.5 showed strong dynamic robustness among baselines, attributed to its hierarchical policy and replanning.

**Q3: Reliability in Unstructured, Cluttered Scenes**
- SID (both variants), MT3, and Ret-BC maintained strong performance in cluttered, unstructured layouts (Table III), with SID-closed achieving 88-90% success.
- This robustness is attributed to the use of vision foundation models (SAM2/SAM3) for explicit target-object segmentation, which filters out distractors and provides stable, target-centric visual inputs.
- Other baselines degraded markedly in clutter without explicit target segmentation.

**Q4: Handling Long-Horizon Manipulation Tasks**
- SID achieved consistently strong performance on multi-stage long-horizon tasks (PNP-BOX and MULTI-PNP-BOX) (Table I), outperforming baselines.
- This advantage is attributed to SID's decomposition design: the motion field handles transitions between subtask distributions, reducing the learning burden on the execution policy.
- An extended MAKE COFFEE task (6 stages) achieved a 14/25 overall success rate (Table IX), demonstrating potential for complex long-horizon tasks, though with room for improvement in very precise, contact-rich subtasks.

**Q5: Sub-skill Reuse and Composition**
- SID demonstrated effective skill reuse and modular re-composition on two novel long-sequence tasks (BOX RETRIEVAL & HANGING, PICK NEW OBJECTS INTO BOX) by invoking pre-trained sub-skill models stage-by-stage (Fig. 6).
- While achieving competitive performance, a performance gap was observed compared to original single-skill success rates, primarily due to occlusion-induced grasping difficulty and distribution deviations from novel objects/interactions in the recomposed settings.

**Ablation Studies (Appendix D and G):**
- **Egocentric Data Augmentation:** Enabling egocentric data augmentation substantially improved performance across all tasks, especially long-horizon ones (Table IV). This broadens the training distribution, improving tolerance to pose estimation errors and increasing policy robustness.
- **Egocentric vs. Fixed Camera Viewpoint:** The egocentric camera viewpoint consistently and significantly outperformed a fixed-camera viewpoint (Table V), with an average improvement of over 50 percentage points in success rate. This is because the motion field can actively reframe egocentric observations to bring the target back into an in-distribution configuration, a capability unavailable with a fixed global camera.
- **Effect of Number of Demonstrations:** SID achieved strong performance with only two raw demonstrations (augmented to 100 samples). Increasing raw demonstrations to 10 or 50 yielded modest additional gains (Table VII), suggesting diminishing returns beyond a minimal number of raw demonstrations after augmentation.
- **Alternative Motion-Field Formulations:** The learned motion field performed comparably to or slightly better than explicit closed-loop pose-based alignment variants (Align-C) (Table VIII). While Align-C can be effective with reliable pose estimates and manual correspondences, the learned motion field offers a more general formulation without manual specification.
- **Cross-Object Generalization:** Performance decreased as test objects became less visually and geometrically similar to the training object (Table VI), indicating that while SID has some transfer capability, significant visual/geometric shifts still pose a challenge, particularly for segmented point-cloud observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Sliding into Distribution (SID) framework represents an advancement in robust robotic manipulation from few demonstrations, particularly by explicitly addressing the problem of distribution shift. Its significance and potential impact lie in several key areas:

1. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** By achieving approximately 90% success rates under OOD conditions with as few as two raw demonstrations per task, SID substantially lowers the data requirements for deploying complex manipulation skills. This makes robot learning more accessible and practical for real-world applications where collecting large, diverse datasets is often prohibitive.

2. **Robustness to OOD States and Disturbances:** The core contribution of managing distribution shift online—by using an object-centric motion field to "slide" OOD states back into the demonstrated manifold—provides a fundamental mechanism for handling variations in object pose, camera viewpoint, and external dynamic disturbances. This addresses a critical limitation of many end-to-end visuomotor policies that are brittle when encountering unseen states. The closed-loop variant further strengthens this by continuously monitoring policy-side distribution drift and triggering re-alignment or recovery.

3. **Structured and Decomposed Control:** SID's decomposition of manipulation into distinct approach and execution phases, each handled by specialized modules, improves robustness and reduces the learning burden on a single policy. This structure allows the system to effectively recover global geometry during approach and then perform precise, local interactions, leading to more reliable multi-stage execution.

4. **Novel Data Augmentation Strategy:** The kinematically consistent point-cloud reprojection augmentation, which selectively perturbs only the object point cloud while preserving gripper geometry and action-observation validity, is a novel approach to expanding perceptual diversity. This method effectively enlarges the "capture range" of the motion field and the robustness of the execution policy without corrupting supervision.

5. **Facilitating Skill Reuse and Composition:** The modular nature of SID, with lightweight components that can be invoked stage-by-stage, demonstrates a pathway for reusing and composing pre-trained sub-skills to tackle novel, long-horizon tasks. This capability can significantly reduce the effort required to develop new robotic applications by building upon existing skill libraries.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Pose Estimation Dependency:** SID relies on an off-the-shelf 6D object pose estimator. Its performance can degrade for challenging objects (e.g., transparent objects) or under rapid motion, where pose estimates become unreliable.
- **Limited Scene Awareness and Approach Feasibility:** The method does not explicitly model obstacles or collision constraints. The motion field assumes the demonstrated approach manifold remains observable and reachable, which may not hold in heavily cluttered or constrained environments where feasible approach regions are occluded or blocked.
- **Limited Cross-Object Generalization:** While showing some transfer to visually similar objects, the current framework does not demonstrate strong generalization to entirely unseen objects or categories, indicating a need for more object-agnostic representations in future work.

Despite these limitations, SID offers a new paradigm for few-shot manipulation by explicitly managing distribution shift through online distribution recovery, showcasing significant potential for making robotic systems more adaptive and deployable in real-world, dynamic settings with minimal human effort.
