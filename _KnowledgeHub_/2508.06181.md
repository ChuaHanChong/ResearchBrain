# Beyond Constant Parameters: Hyper Prediction Models and HyperMPC

> [!summary] Summary
> The Hyper Prediction Model (HyperPM) and HyperMPC, developed by researchers at Poznan University of Technology and IDEAS NCBR, enhance Model Predictive Control by learning and predicting time-varying parameter trajectories over the prediction horizon. This approach yields improved prediction accuracy by up to 73.12% and control performance gains up to 300% across diverse robotic tasks, including autonomous racing, while maintaining low computational overhead.

## Problem

- Existing Model Predictive Control (MPC) dynamics models often assume constant parameters over the prediction horizon, limiting their ability to anticipate and compensate for time-varying unmodeled dynamics.
- Optimization-based MPC faces computational constraints, making it challenging to integrate complex data-driven models for accurate long-horizon prediction without significantly increasing solver complexity.
- Many real-world systems exhibit partial observability, where crucial states (e.g., payload swing, tire deformation) influencing dynamics are unobserved, leading to prediction errors in models that do not account for hidden dynamics.

## Method

- A **Hyper Prediction Model (HyperPM)** is introduced, a neural network framework that encodes unmodeled dynamics as time-varying parameters within an existing analytical dynamics model.
- HyperPM predicts entire trajectories of these time-varying parameters over the MPC prediction horizon, conditioning on recent state/control history and the planned future control sequence using a ==causal prediction architecture== and ==B-spline interpolation==.
- HyperPM is integrated into an optimization-based MPC framework (**HyperMPC**) by performing a single, lightweight forward pass to generate parameter trajectories before the main optimization loop, thus maintaining real-time computational efficiency.

## Results

- **HyperPM** improved long-horizon prediction accuracy by **33.19%** for a pendulum with backlash and **48.75%** for real-world F1TENTH racing compared to models with constant parameters.
- **HyperMPC** achieved a **300%** improvement in control performance (lower accumulated cost) for pendulum swing-up and an **8%** reduction in total cost with fewer safety penalties for real-world F1TENTH racing.
- The method maintained similar MPC solver runtimes (**~10.61 ms**) to baselines, with HyperPM adding only **~1.85 ms** (on a mobile CPU) for its forward pass, confirming its low overhead for real-time applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predicting time-varying trajectories of model parameters, rather than static values, allows MPC to proactively anticipate and compensate for future changes in unmodeled dynamics, significantly improving long-horizon performance.
> - Conditioning the parameter prediction on the planned future control sequence is crucial for accurately anticipating the evolution of dynamics, especially in complex systems where actions directly influence unmodeled effects.
> - The method achieves enhanced prediction and control performance while maintaining computational efficiency for real-time optimization-based MPC by offloading complex dynamic parameter prediction to a lightweight network outside the iterative optimization process.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a prominent and widely adopted method for controlling robotic systems, particularly in scenarios demanding high performance, such as agile drone flight, autonomous racing, and legged locomotion. Its efficacy is fundamentally dependent on the quality of the underlying dynamical system model, which must be accurate, robust, and computationally efficient.

Existing approaches to dynamics modeling within MPC face a trade-off between accuracy and computational complexity. Traditional MPC often relies on reduced-order analytical models derived from first principles, which offer computational efficiency but may lack the accuracy to capture complex, unmodeled phenomena. To enhance accuracy, various data-driven components have been integrated, including Gaussian Processes (GP), polynomial expansions, or small neural networks. However, these augmentation methods typically predict system evolution based solely on the current state and control, which can be insufficient to capture the influence of unobserved or unmodeled system states over an extended prediction horizon. Phenomena such as mass transfer, tire deformation, suspension movement, or payload dynamics in a drone introduce time-varying effects that are difficult to model accurately without increasing computational burden or requiring a fully observable system state.

For optimization-based MPC, which is a focus of this work, the computational demands necessitate lightweight models that do not significantly increase the complexity of the optimization problem. While sampling-based MPC offers greater flexibility in model choice, it often requires substantial computational resources, such as powerful GPUs for parallel simulations.

Prior work has explored addressing model inaccuracies through adaptation laws, online parameter prediction, or incremental model learning. Notably, hyper-neural networks have been used to predict parameters for dynamics models, as seen in HyperDynamics and Deep Dynamics. However, a common limitation in these existing methods is the assumption that the identified or predicted model parameters remain *constant* over the entire MPC prediction horizon. This assumption restricts the model's ability to anticipate future changes in unmodeled dynamics.

This research addresses this gap by proposing a method that not only learns model parameters but also predicts their *time-varying trajectories* over the prediction horizon. This allows MPC to proactively account for anticipated changes in system dynamics that arise from unmodeled or partially observable states, within the computational constraints of optimization-based MPC.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the long-horizon prediction accuracy of dynamics models used in Model Predictive Control, thereby enhancing overall control performance, particularly for systems with complex and partially observable dynamics.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current MPC dynamics modeling:
1. **Inadequate Capture of Unmodeled Dynamics:** Many real-world robotic systems exhibit complex behaviors (e.g., backlash in joints, payload swing, tire deformation, load transfer in vehicles) that are often unmodeled or only partially observable through the system's state. Existing models struggle to accurately represent these effects because they typically rely on instantaneous state-control inputs and fixed parameters.
2. **Computational Constraints in Optimization-Based MPC:** While data-driven models can improve accuracy, their integration into gradient-based MPC is often limited by computational complexity. Models that require conditioning on extensive historical data or complex internal states become computationally prohibitive within the MPC's real-time optimization loop.
3. **Static Parameter Assumption:** Existing parameter adaptation or prediction schemes for dynamics models largely assume that the identified parameters remain constant throughout the entire prediction horizon. This assumption prevents the controller from anticipating how unmodeled dynamics will evolve over time, leading to suboptimal control decisions, especially over longer horizons.
4. **Partial Observability Issues:** In scenarios where key states influencing system dynamics are unobserved (e.g., the swing of a rope-suspended payload when only the drone's state is observed), a truly Markovian representation of the system dynamics is infeasible. This results in inevitable prediction errors for models that do not account for these hidden dynamics.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the research are:
- To develop the **Hyper Prediction Model (HyperPM)**, a novel framework that encodes unmodeled dynamics as time-varying parameters within an existing dynamics model.
- To enable HyperPM to predict the *trajectories* of these time-varying parameters over the MPC prediction horizon by conditioning on historical observations and, critically, on the *planned future control sequence*.
- To integrate HyperPM into an MPC framework, termed **HyperMPC**, which leverages these time-varying parameter trajectories to anticipate and compensate for unmodeled effects proactively.
- To demonstrate that HyperPM maintains the computational efficiency and robustness of the base model while providing enhanced prediction accuracy.
- To validate the improved prediction accuracy and control performance of HyperPM and HyperMPC on challenging simulated and real-world robotic tasks, specifically a pendulum with backlash, a drone with a rope-suspended payload, and F1TENTH autonomous racing.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the Hyper Prediction Model (HyperPM) to address the limitations of conventional dynamics models in MPC, particularly regarding time-varying unmodeled dynamics. HyperPM projects unmodeled dynamics onto time-dependent parameters of an existing analytical model, whose evolution over the prediction horizon is learned and anticipated by a neural network. This model is then integrated into an optimization-based MPC framework, termed HyperMPC.

#### 3.1. Hyper Prediction Model (HyperPM)

The core idea of HyperPM is to capture unmodeled dynamics by allowing the parameters ($\theta$) of an existing analytical dynamics model ($\dot{\hat{x}} = f_\theta(\hat{x}, u)$) to vary over time. Instead of predicting static parameters, HyperPM predicts an entire trajectory of parameters $\theta[t_c, t_c + t_p]$ over the prediction horizon. This prediction is conditioned on three inputs:
1. **Recent state history:** $x[t_c - t_h, t_c]$
2. **Corresponding control history:** $u[t_c - t_h, t_c]$
3. **Planned control sequence:** $\hat{u}[t_c, t_c + t_p]$

The architecture of HyperPM, typically implemented with neural networks, consists of four main components:
- **History Encoder:** A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) based recurrent neural network processes the history of states and actions ($x[t_c - t_h, t_c]$, $u[t_c - t_h, t_c]$) to extract a latent representation of the unobserved, unmodeled state. The final hidden state of the GRU serves as this representation.
- **Expected Controls Preprocessor:** The planned future control sequence $\hat{u}[t_c, t_c + t_p]$ is represented using control points of a B-spline curve. This compression allows for flexibility in input sampling time, reduces the network size, and emphasizes general trends over individual actions. To enhance robustness, especially during deployment when MPC-generated controls are imperfect, Gaussian noise (controlled by $\sigma_{robust}$) is injected into these B-spline control points during training.
- **Causal Parameters Predictor:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) takes the latent representation from the history encoder and the processed future control sequence to generate changes in the dynamics model parameters over time. Causality is enforced by masking weights, preventing future actions from influencing past parameter predictions and avoiding spurious correlations.
- **Parameters Interpolation:** The generated parameter changes are interpreted using a scale factor ($\Delta_{max}$) and a bias term ($\theta_{const}$, which minimizes the average prediction error over the dataset). These values are then interpolated using B-splines to produce the final time-varying parameter trajectory $\theta[t_c, t_c + t_p]$. This design also allows for extending the prediction horizon beyond training duration by smoothly transitioning to nominal parameter values. An L1 norm regularization term is applied to parameter changes during training to encourage minimal deviations from nominal values unless necessary.

#### 3.2. Training Procedure

The training of HyperPM is designed to optimize for long-horizon prediction accuracy, crucial for MPC:
1. **Parameter Trajectory Prediction:** HyperPM predicts the full parameter trajectory $\theta[t_c, t_c + t_p]$ based on historical and planned future inputs.
2. **Dynamics Rollout:** The predicted parameter trajectory is discretized, and the time-varying dynamics model $f_{\theta_t}$ is rolled out using a 4th-order Runge Kutta (RK4) integration scheme to generate a predicted state trajectory $\hat{x}[t_c+dt, t_c+t_p]$.
3. **Loss Calculation:** A mean square prediction error is computed between the predicted state trajectory and the ground-truth state trajectory from the dataset. This error is combined with the L1 regularization term on parameter changes to form the total loss.
4. **Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT):** Gradients of the loss function are backpropagated through time to update the weights and biases of HyperPM. This ensures direct optimization for long-horizon performance.

#### 3.3. HyperMPC Integration

HyperPM is integrated into an optimization-based MPC framework:
1. At each control interval, the HyperPM is called with the current observation history ($x[t_c - t_h, t_c]$, $u[t_c - t_h, t_c]$) and the planned control sequence from the *previous* MPC iteration ($\hat{u}[t_c, t_c + t_p]$).
2. HyperPM outputs a trajectory of time-varying model parameters $\theta[t_c, t_c + t_p]$.
3. These predicted parameters are then injected into the nominal dynamics model used by the MPC solver.
4. The MPC solver computes an optimal control sequence by minimizing an objective function, using the enhanced, time-varying dynamics model.
5. Only the first control action from the optimized sequence is applied to the physical plant.
6. The observation history and planned action buffers are updated, and the process repeats.

#### 3.4. Computational Efficiency

A key aspect of HyperMPC is that it maintains the computational complexity of the primary dynamics model *within the MPC optimization loop*. The addition is a single forward pass through the lightweight HyperPM neural network, performed *before* the optimization problem is solved. This allows the method to be suitable for real-time applications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated HyperPM and HyperMPC across three challenging tasks: simulated pendulum swing-up with backlash, simulated drone trajectory tracking with a rope-suspended payload, and real-world F1TENTH autonomous racing. The results were compared against several baselines, including analytical models with constant parameters (`const_s`, `const_l`), HyperDynamics-inspired models predicting static parameters (`HD_s`, `HD_l`), and a residual neural network model (`res`).

#### 4.1. Long-Horizon Prediction Accuracy

- **Pendulum with backlash:** HyperPM achieved a 33.19% improvement in prediction accuracy (lower error) compared to an analytical model with constant parameters (`const_s`). Baselines like `HD_s` struggled, exhibiting unstable predictions due to overfitting.
- **Drone with rope-suspended payload:**
 - Using a nominal dynamics model, HyperPM showed a 73.12% improvement in prediction accuracy over `const_l`.
 - When augmented with a residual model, HyperPM still delivered an 11.82% improvement over `const_l`, indicating its ability to anticipate complex, time-varying unmodeled effects that residual models alone might miss. This improvement was linked to increasing rope length, confirming HyperPM's role in compensating for unmodeled payload dynamics.
- **F1TENTH autonomous racing (real-world):** Among models of comparable size, HyperPM achieved a 48.75% improvement in prediction accuracy over `const_s`. While a residual model (`res`) showed the lowest error overall, HyperPM demonstrated strong performance without a significantly increased parameter count.

#### 4.2. MPC Control Performance

- **Pendulum swing-up with backlash:** HyperMPC (using HyperPM) demonstrated a substantial 300% improvement in control performance (lower accumulated cost) compared to MPC with an analytical model. Notably, HyperMPC achieved a 100% success rate in stabilizing the pendulum upright and a 0% solver failure rate, whereas baselines, including an "oracle adaptation" that had perfect knowledge of backlash effects but assumed constant parameters, failed to swing up the pendulum or suffered high solver failure rates (e.g., `HD_l` with 95% solver failure).
- **Drone trajectory tracking with payload:** HyperMPC (using HyperPM) outperformed both the `oracle adaptation` baseline and `HD_l` in both nominal and residual dynamics model settings. This translates to an approximate 9% improvement in cost over MPC with the nominal `const_l` model.
- **F1TENTH autonomous racing (real-world):** HyperMPC with HyperPM outperformed all other methods, including the residual network that had marginally lower prediction errors. It achieved an 8% improvement in total cost and reduced safety penalties, which includes track boundary violations. This suggests HyperPM offers better generalization to online control distributions compared to residual networks, which might overfit to the training data distribution.

#### 4.3. Computational Performance

- For F1TENTH racing, HyperMPC maintained similar MPC solver runtimes (~10.61 ms) to baseline methods (`const_l`, `HD_l`). The additional computational cost for HyperPM was a single forward pass, typically around 1.85 ms on a mobile CPU, confirming its low overhead within the real-time control loop.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Impact of Expected Future Trajectories:** Removing the conditioning on expected future actions from HyperPM (HyperPM - EAC) resulted in worse prediction performance for both pendulum and F1TENTH tasks. For F1TENTH, HyperPM - EAC was less accurate than `HD_l`, highlighting the importance of anticipated actions for effective time-varying parameter prediction, especially in complex systems.
- **Impact of $\sigma_{robust}$ Hyperparameter:** Training with a moderate noise level ($\sigma_{robust} = 0.2$) in the B-spline control points of future actions yielded the best validation performance for F1TENTH, demonstrating that this regularization technique enhances robustness and reduces overfitting to the training data.

#### 4.5. Interpretability

- For the F1TENTH car, HyperPM's predicted time-varying `l_r` parameter (related to the distance from the center of gravity to the rear axle) showed a strong correlation with physically derived load transfer calculations. This indicated that HyperPM captures unmodeled effects, such as load transfer during acceleration and braking, in an interpretable manner by virtually adjusting physical parameters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the Hyper Prediction Model (HyperPM) and its integration into the HyperMPC framework, offering a method to enhance the predictive accuracy and control performance of robotic systems operating under complex and partially observable dynamics.

The significance of this work lies in several aspects:
- **Addressing Fundamental Limitations:** HyperPM directly addresses the critical limitation of existing MPC dynamics models that assume constant parameters over the prediction horizon. By learning and predicting *trajectories* of time-varying parameters, it enables the controller to proactively anticipate and compensate for dynamic changes arising from unmodeled phenomena (e.g., backlash, payload swing, vehicle load transfer), which are often missed by instantaneous state-based models.
- **Improved Performance in Challenging Tasks:** The experimental results consistently demonstrate superior long-horizon prediction accuracy and significant improvements in downstream control performance across diverse and demanding tasks, including real-world autonomous racing. This suggests that HyperMPC can facilitate more agile, precise, and robust control for systems operating at their physical limits.
- **Computational Efficiency for Real-time Applications:** Crucially, HyperMPC achieves these performance gains without significantly increasing the computational complexity within the MPC optimization loop. The HyperPM model's lightweight neural network only requires a single forward pass before the optimization, making the approach suitable for real-time embedded systems.
- **Robustness to Uncertainty:** The methodology incorporates mechanisms like noise injection during training ($\sigma_{robust}$) to make HyperPM robust to the inherent imperfections of MPC-generated future control trajectories, which are typically less certain than historical observations.
- **Enhanced Model Interpretability:** By encoding unmodeled dynamics into physically meaningful parameters (e.g., changes in center of gravity for a race car), HyperPM offers a degree of interpretability. This can provide insights into system behavior and guide future analytical model refinements, moving beyond purely black-box data-driven approaches.

The potential impact of this research is broad, enabling advances in:
- **High-performance Robotics:** Facilitating more aggressive and stable control for drones, autonomous vehicles, and legged robots where accurate anticipation of dynamic changes is critical.
- **Systems with Partial Observability:** Providing a robust framework for controlling systems where a complete, Markovian state representation is either infeasible or computationally prohibitive.
- **Adaptive Control Systems:** Laying groundwork for more advanced adaptive control strategies that can learn and predict dynamic changes over time, extending beyond mere instantaneous adaptation.

However, the authors acknowledge certain limitations. The method's reliance on relatively accurate predictions of future control inputs means its performance can be affected by the quality of these predictions, especially over long horizons. Additionally, the inherent policy mismatch between the training data (generated by a potentially different policy) and the deployed controller might lead to distributional shifts during operation. The authors suggest online learning as a potential mitigation for this issue, indicating a direction for future research.
