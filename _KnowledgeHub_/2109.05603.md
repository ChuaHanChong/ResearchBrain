# Learning to Navigate Sidewalks in Outdoor Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A learning framework empowers quadrupedal robots to autonomously navigate complex urban sidewalks, following waypoints over kilometers in real outdoor environments. This is achieved through a two-staged simulation-based training process, employing intermediate semantic features and specialized sim-to-real transfer techniques for robust performance.

## Problem

- Enabling quadrupedal robots to autonomously navigate urban sidewalks for applications like last-mile delivery and patrol.
- Existing navigation systems often rely on extensive manual engineering or struggle with unstructured, dynamic outdoor environments and complex behaviors such as pedestrian avoidance.
- Learning-based methods for outdoor navigation face a significant sim-to-real gap, hindering the transfer of policies from simulation to complex real-world scenarios.

## Method

- A two-staged 'learning by cheating' framework trains an expert teacher with privileged information in an abstract PyBullet environment, then transfers this policy to a student in a high-fidelity CARLA simulator.
- The student policy leverages realistic sensors, specifically using intermediate feature layers from a re-trained semantic segmentation network (BiSeNet) as visual input (RFEAT).
- Targeted data augmentation and retraining of the semantic network for sidewalk perspectives, combined with LiDAR and GPS for localization, mitigate the sim-to-real gap.

## Results

- The quadruped robot successfully navigated **3.2** kilometers across six diverse urban routes in Atlanta, demonstrating robust long-distance sidewalk traversal with minimal human intervention.
- The 'INF-SEM-FEAT' agent achieved an **83.3%** success rate in real-world obstacle avoidance tests, effectively navigating past 17 out of 19 naturally occurring static and dynamic obstacles.
- The two-staged learning framework converged in approximately 30 hours, significantly reducing training time compared to an estimated 300+ hours for direct end-to-end learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Intermediate feature representations from semantic networks are more robust for sim-to-real transfer in outdoor navigation than raw RGB images or final semantic classifications.
> - Adapting semantic network training objectives to account for sidewalk perspectives and the robot's current occupancy significantly improves real-world performance and generalizability.
> - A two-staged teacher-student learning framework enables efficient policy training, reducing the required simulation time tenfold compared to single-stage end-to-end approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the challenge of outdoor navigation for robotic systems, specifically focusing on sidewalk traversal in urban environments. This domain is critical for developing autonomous robotic workers for applications such as last-mile delivery and neighborhood patrol.

The field of robot navigation has seen significant advancements, broadly categorized into hand-engineered and learning-based approaches. Early work, such as that by Morales et al. (2007) and Kümmerle et al. (2015), demonstrated impressive autonomous navigation over mapped trajectories or using pre-built maps and extensive sensor arrays. However, these systems often required substantial manual engineering for sensor configuration, calibration, processing, and fusion, and struggled with complex behaviors like pedestrian avoidance.

More recently, deep learning has facilitated the development of effective navigation policies, particularly in indoor environments. High-fidelity simulators like Habitat and iGibson have enabled large-scale training of visual navigation algorithms, leading to agents capable of navigating unseen indoor environments without maps, as shown by Wijmans et al. (2019) and Chaplot et al. (2020).

In contrast, learning-based algorithms for outdoor navigation, especially on sidewalks, are less developed. Previous outdoor work includes Müller et al. (2018), who trained a waypoint navigation policy for a toy-sized truck on empty roads, and Kahn et al. (2021), who learned a sidewalk-following policy in the real world by leveraging human operator disengagements. This paper distinguishes itself by aiming to learn a policy for following designated waypoints on sidewalks without relying on real-world data for policy training, addressing the complexities of unstructured, dynamic urban sidewalks lacking explicit guiding lanes.

The research builds upon the "learning by cheating" or teacher-student framework, introduced by Chen et al. (2020), which involves training an expert teacher with privileged information and then cloning its behavior to a student with realistic sensor modalities. This framework has been applied to robust locomotion policies on challenging terrains by Lee et al. (2020). The current work extends this framework to real quadrupeds operating in urban areas and incorporates techniques to accelerate learning through fast simulation environments.

A significant challenge in robotic learning is the sim-to-real gap, where policies trained in simulation perform poorly when deployed in the real world. Prior research has explored various mitigation strategies, including domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017), generative adversarial networks to reduce visual discrepancies (James et al., 2019), and image-based data augmentation (Laskin et al., 2020). Müller et al. (2018) also employed pretrained semantic networks for visual inference in sim-to-real transfer. This paper specifically addresses the sim-to-real gap for sidewalk navigation by analyzing design choices for sensing modalities, network architectures, and training procedures, including adapting semantic networks for sidewalk perspectives and utilizing intermediate semantic features.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning framework that enables a quadrupedal robot to autonomously navigate sidewalks in urban outdoor environments. This involves several specific sub-objectives:
1. **Route Following:** The robot must be able to follow a route plan, similar to those generated by public map services like Google or Apple Maps.
2. **Sidewalk Adherence:** The robot needs to consistently remain on the sidewalk, distinguishing it from adjacent areas like roads or private property.
3. **Collision Avoidance:** The robot must avoid collisions with both static obstacles (e.g., trees, poles, trash bins) and dynamic agents (e.g., pedestrians, dogs, cyclists) present in urban settings.

The motivation for this work stems from the increasing demand for autonomous robotic workers in urban environments for tasks such as last-mile delivery and neighborhood patrol. For these applications, robots require advanced navigation capabilities that mirror the human skill of traversing sidewalks.

Despite similarities to autonomous driving and indoor navigation (e.g., requiring route following with noisy localization and egocentric sensors), outdoor sidewalk navigation presents unique challenges. Urban sidewalks are inherently more unstructured, feature a wide variety of static and dynamic objects, and lack explicit guiding lanes that are common in road networks. This unstructured nature makes traditional hand-engineered navigation systems difficult to develop and deploy effectively, particularly for complex behaviors like pedestrian avoidance.

Therefore, the researchers are motivated to leverage learning-based approaches. While "learning by cheating" frameworks (teacher-student) offer sample-efficient learning, directly applying them to outdoor navigation often results in poor real-world performance due to the substantial sim-to-real gap. A core motivation for this paper is to systematically analyze and mitigate the primary sources of this sim-to-real gap specifically for quadrupedal robots navigating dynamic outdoor sidewalks. This involves proposing and evaluating novel methodologies for designing sensing modalities, network architectures, and training procedures to facilitate zero-shot policy transfer from simulation to complex real-world environments without prior environmental curation or arrangement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a two-staged learning framework, inspired by the "learning by cheating" approach, to train a visual navigation policy for a quadrupedal robot.

**A. Teacher Training in Abstract World**
The initial stage focuses on efficiently learning an ideal control policy using privileged information in a simplified "abstract world."
- **Environment:** A custom abstract world is built using PyBullet, generated from actual OpenStreetMap data of the Helsinki area (approximately 1 km²). This environment includes essential navigation information such as walkable/non-walkable areas and static/dynamic objects (represented as cylinders and cuboids). Sidewalk widths are varied (2 to 5 meters) to enrich training. The abstract world is computationally efficient, generating samples over 10 times faster than a high-fidelity simulator.
- **Task Definition:** The teacher agent's objective is to follow a list of waypoints (10-15 meters apart) along sidewalks, avoiding collisions, and staying within sidewalk boundaries.
- **Reward Function:** A composite reward function is used: `r_success` (sparse, +10 for reaching goal within 0.5m), `r_termination` (sparse, -10 for collision, leaving sidewalk, or timeout), `r_approach` (continuous, for progress towards goal), and `r_life` (small penalty, -0.01 to prevent idling).
- **Observation Space (Privileged):**
 - **Bird-Eye-View (BEV) Image:** A 128x128 top-down binary image covering an 18x18m region, indicating walkable areas versus objects/obstacles. Three previous BEVs are stacked to provide historical context.
 - **Bird-Eye-View LiDAR (BLID):** Simulated LiDAR readings from 64 rays, measuring distances to nearest objects.
 - **Goal Direction & Distance (GDD):** Relative goal location in local polar coordinates.
- **Action Space:** Continuous values for forward/backward speed (20 cm to -10 cm per step) and yaw rotation (-54° to 54° per step).
- **Learning Algorithm:** A modified Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm is used, with Actor and Critic networks adapted to integrate BLID and GDD observations with features from a visual encoder. The teacher policy is trained until it achieves an 83% success rate in a high-fidelity validation environment.

**B. Student Training in High-Fidelity World**
The second stage involves transferring the learned teacher's behavior to a student agent equipped with realistic sensors in a high-fidelity simulator.
- **Environment:** The CARLA simulator (built on Unreal Engine 4) is used. It features multiple town blocks (0.6 km²), various weather conditions, and sidewalks densely populated with random objects to increase complexity. Training is conducted across 7 towns and 14 weather conditions.
- **Learning Algorithm:** The Dataset Aggregation Method (DAGGER) is employed for behavior cloning. The student policy generates rollouts, and the teacher (in the high-fidelity simulator, which can also generate abstract observations) provides expert actions. The student's parameters are updated by minimizing the L1 loss between its actions and the teacher's. To bootstrap learning, the replay buffer is prefilled with 120,000 successful teacher experience pairs.
- **Observation Space (Realistic):**
 - **Real Feature (RFEAT):** A 128x60x80 feature tensor extracted from RGB images using a pre-trained semantic network (BiSeNet without its final layer).
 - **Real LiDAR (RLID):** 272 LiDAR readings, representing distances to the nearest objects (capped at 6 meters).
 - **Real Goal Direction & Distance (RGDD):** Relative goal location derived from GPS signals.
- **Student Architecture:** Comprises a pre-trained BiSeNet (for feature extraction), a Network-In-Network component, and a 3-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) for feature processing.
- **Efficiency:** The two-staged framework converges in approximately 30 hours, significantly faster than the estimated 300+ hours required for learning from scratch.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer Techniques**
A major focus is on bridging the sim-to-real gap, which is achieved through careful design of sensing modalities and data augmentation.
- **Sensing Modalities Selection:** An ablation study is conducted based on three criteria: usefulness, learning difficulty, and sensitivity to the sim-to-real gap.
 - **Sanity Check:** An "ULTIMATE" agent (with all ideal simulated sensors) achieved 76.86% success in simulation, indicating the transfer from teacher to student with ideal sensors is feasible.
 - **Visual Sensors:** Raw RGB, depth, and ground-truth semantic images were evaluated. Depth cameras (infrared-based) were deemed unsuitable for outdoor sunlight. Raw RGB and inferred semantic images (INF-SEM) showed poor real-world transfer (7.1% and 25% success, respectively), despite strong simulation performance. The key insight was that the final classification layer of semantic networks discards valuable features. Thus, the authors proposed using the **intermediate feature layer** of the semantic network (BiSeNet) as input (RFEAT) for the "INF-SEM-FEAT" agent. This agent achieved 77.90% in simulation and 83.3% in real-world tests, demonstrating improved robustness.
 - **LiDAR:** Selected for its utility in collision avoidance and relative robustness to real-world noise. All agents were trained with LiDAR.
 - **Localization:** GPS (from a Pixel 5 phone) was chosen over visual odometry (Intel Realsense T265) due to the latter's error accumulation over long distances. Acknowledging GPS latency and noise issues, particularly due to robot vibrations, the phone was carried by a human operator.
- **Data Augmentation for Semantic Network:**
 - Publicly available semantic networks (e.g., BiSeNet, EfficientPS) were found to perform poorly for sidewalk navigation due to "perspective shifts" (trained primarily on road-level autonomous driving datasets).
 - A BiSeNetV2 model was retrained using an augmented dataset comprising: 17,535 sidewalk and 9,704 road images from GTA; 6,000 road and 2,400 sidewalk images from CARLA; and 2,975 real images from Cityscapes.
 - To address the issue of networks leveraging surface location as a strong prior (tending to predict the surface under the camera as a vehicle road), the training objective was modified. The network was trained to segment the region the robot *currently occupies* (e.g., segment the road if on the road, segment the sidewalk if on the sidewalk). This approach improved performance on 35 manually annotated real robot images.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the efficacy of the proposed learning framework and sim-to-real transfer techniques.

- **Learning Efficiency:** The two-staged learning framework significantly reduced training time, converging in approximately 30 hours compared to over 300 hours estimated for a single-stage, end-to-end approach. This highlights the sample efficiency gained from the teacher-student paradigm.

- **Effectiveness of Sim-to-Real Transfer Methodologies:**
 - **Sensor Modality Impact:** The ablation study demonstrated that the choice of sensing modalities is critical for real-world deployment. The "INF-SEM-FEAT" agent, which utilizes intermediate feature layers from a semantic segmentation network, combined with LiDAR and GPS-derived goal information, achieved the highest success rate in real-world tests.
 - **Robustness of Feature-based Visual Input:** In obstacle avoidance tests on a 15-meter sidewalk course, the INF-SEM-FEAT agent achieved an 83.3% success rate. This contrasted with the significantly lower success rates of the "INF-SEM" (7.1%) and "RGB" (25.0%) agents. The INF-SEM-FEAT agent exhibited greater robustness to varying lighting conditions and textures, which the authors attributed to the richer information contained in the feature layer compared to thresholded semantic predictions or raw RGB pixels. Minor collisions were sometimes observed and attributed to sensor limitations, such as objects being outside the camera's field of view or too small for LiDAR detection.
 - **Improved Semantic Segmentation:** The targeted data augmentation and retraining strategy for the semantic network, which focused on sidewalk perspectives and segmenting the robot's current occupancy region, led to improved semantic classification performance in real-world sidewalk scenarios.

- **Long-Distance Sidewalk Navigation in Real-World Environments:**
 - **Overall Performance:** The quadruped robot successfully navigated a total of 3.2 kilometers across six diverse routes in Atlanta, USA, using the learned navigation policy and its onboard sensors, with a limited number of human interventions. The longest non-interrupted segment of navigation was 320 meters, with mean non-interrupted distances ranging from 31m to 198m across different courses.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance:** The robot successfully avoided 17 out of 19 naturally occurring obstacles, including static objects (poles, trash bins) and dynamic "pedestrians" (human supervisors). The two minor collisions that occurred (with a trash bin and a narrow pole) did not require human intervention, as the robot attempted avoidance but brushed the obstacle.
 - **Sidewalk Following:** The agent generally demonstrated a good ability to stay on sidewalks. This indicates that the policy not only targets goal locations but also prioritizes adherence to sidewalk boundaries. However, failures in sidewalk adherence occurred in visually challenging situations, such as transitions between bright regions and shadows, and when encountering "grassy sidewalks" that were sometimes misclassified as non-walkable areas.
 - **Localization Challenges:** Occasional significant delays or noise in GPS signals led to localization errors, impacting waypoint switching logic and sometimes resulting in sidewalk violations.
 - **Driveway Confusion:** Driveways, which were not included in the training data, posed a frequent challenge, causing the agent to misinterpret their semantics and sometimes direct the robot off the sidewalk. These instances often required manual intervention.
 - **Q-function Interpretability:** Analysis of the learned Q-function values provided interpretable insights, showing a decrease in Q-values when anomalies such as sidewalk violations, human obstacles, or sun glare were detected, and a recovery as these situations were resolved.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic navigation by demonstrating a practical and robust approach for quadrupedal robots to navigate complex outdoor sidewalk environments. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Advancement in Autonomous Outdoor Navigation:** The paper presents a verified method for long-distance outdoor navigation on sidewalks, a domain that is more unstructured and dynamic than typical indoor or road-based navigation scenarios. This is a crucial step towards equipping robots with the capability to operate autonomously in complex urban landscapes.
- **Validation of Sim-to-Real Transfer Techniques:** The work provides evidence for the effectiveness of a two-staged learning framework in conjunction with specific sim-to-real methodologies. It underscores that careful design of sensing modalities, particularly the use of intermediate feature representations from semantic networks, is pivotal for successful transfer from simulation to the real world without extensive real-world policy training data.
- **Methodological Contributions for Visual Policies:** The findings highlight the importance of adapting visual perception models for specific operational contexts (e.g., sidewalk perspective semantic segmentation) and suggest that raw semantic predictions might be less informative than the richer feature layers preceding them. This insight can inform the design of more robust visual policies for robotic agents.
- **Enabling Human-Assistive Robotic Applications:** The ability of quadruped robots to autonomously navigate sidewalks is a foundational technology for a range of human-assistive applications, including last-mile delivery, neighborhood security patrols, and assistive mobility for individuals. This work represents a significant step towards the deployment of such robotic workers in urban settings.
- **Reduction in Real-World Data Dependence:** By demonstrating successful zero-shot policy transfer, the research contributes to reducing the reliance on costly, time-consuming, and potentially hazardous real-world data collection for policy training. This makes the development and deployment of autonomous systems more feasible.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as challenges with localization errors, minor collisions due to limited sensing, and the inability to cross roads autonomously, provide clear directions for future research. This includes investigating stateful models (e.g., recurrent neural networks), leveraging perceived semantics for waypoint transitions, and incorporating complex behaviors like road crossing into the training framework.
