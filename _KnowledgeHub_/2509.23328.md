# Space Robotics Bench: Robot Learning Beyond Earth

> [!summary] Summary
> Space Robotics Bench (SRB) is an open-source Isaac Sim/Isaac Lab framework for robot learning in space. It couples on-demand procedural content generation of terrains, assets and robot morphologies with domain randomization and massively parallel GPU simulation, plus 15+ mission-relevant benchmark tasks and an automated sim-to-real bridge. A DreamerV3 rover policy transferred zero-shot with 3.3 cm tracking error.

## Problem

- Mission-specific space simulators (VIPER RSIM, HeliCAT-DARTS) chase single-digital-twin fidelity, have restricted access, and are architected for verification rather than the data volume learning needs.
- Learning-focused space platforms stay coupled to one robot and one operational domain, so generalization across morphologies or across orbital, lunar and Martian settings cannot be studied.
- Terrestrial robot-learning benchmarks are Earth-biased and converging on tabletop manipulation; procedural generation there draws on fixed asset datasets, never the robot's own embodiment.

## Method

- A two-layer diversity engine: runtime ==procedural content generation== via a Blender backend (Perlin/Voronoi-layered terrain, procedural satellites and excavation tools) baked to USD, plus per-episode ==domain randomization== of gravity, friction, cohesion, lighting.
- A modular ==Isaac Lab== architecture with a registry of composable robots, actuation models (==OSC==, ==IK==, thruster) and five domain presets, exposed through ==Gymnasium== and ==ROS 2==, accelerated by Rust and TorchScript.
- A benchmark suite of mobile, manipulation and mobile-manipulation tasks with ==POMDP== dictionary observations separating privileged state from sensors, plus a ==reflection-based sim-to-real bridge== that auto-generates hardware-interface environments from simulated ones.

## Results

- Zero-shot on a Leo Rover over basalt gravel, DreamerV3 tracked waypoints at **2.3-3.6 cm** / **1.7-2.3°** error versus **11.2-14.8 cm** for PPO, PPO-LSTM and TD3.
- Procedural training beat single-terrain training by **21%** lower position and **72%** lower orientation ATE on hardware; out-of-distribution returns rose **1.91×** for novel objects and **9.06×** for unseen excavation tools.
- Learned-compliance OSC cut jerk **58%** on debris capture and **56%** on excavation; depth-camera input raised scooped regolith volume **93%**; throughput reached **100k** steps/s on landing with **1024** environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treat the sim-to-real gap as a distribution to cover, not a fidelity target to hit; mastering diversity beat replicating one environment on real hardware.
> - Randomizing the robot's own embodiment pays off far more than randomizing scenery: morphology diversity gave a ninefold OOD gain, environment diversity barely 3%.
> - Model-based latent-imagination agents can dominate on transfer while costing roughly 5× the wall-clock of on-policy training; long-horizon assembly tasks stayed unsolved by all baselines.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust autonomous systems is identified as crucial for future space exploration and utilization, which envisions missions involving autonomous planetary rovers and robotic manipulators for orbital construction. Robot learning, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), offers a methodology for acquiring adaptive behaviors necessary for operating in unstructured, extreme extraterrestrial conditions with minimal human supervision. However, the adoption of robot learning in space robotics faces significant challenges, including the prohibitive cost of real-world technology demonstrations, extreme data scarcity, and the limited accessibility or narrow scope of existing space-grade simulators.

Current space simulation frameworks typically fall into two categories. Traditional simulators like GMAT and Basilisk focus on high-fidelity astrodynamics verification. More specialized, mission-specific simulators, such as VIPER RSIM, HeliCAT-DARTS, EELS-DARTS, Astrobee Sim, and Int-Ball2 Sim, are developed by space agencies for verification and validation (V&V) of single robotic systems in predefined environments. These mission-specific tools are designed to model a static, singular reality, which is ill-suited for the massive data generation and environmental diversity required by modern robot learning algorithms. Furthermore, many remain restricted in access, hindering broader research. The research community has also developed learning-focused simulators like OmniLRS (for photorealistic rendering), RLRoverLAB, RANS, SpaceRobotEnv, and GraspPlanetary. While these platforms advance the field, they are often tightly coupled to specific research objectives or a single robotic platform, limiting their utility for studying generalization across diverse robots or applications.

Terrestrial robot learning benchmarks (e.g., RLBench, RoboHive, Robosuite, FurnitureBench, RoboPianist, HumanoidBench) have significantly advanced the field by providing standardized platforms for algorithm comparison. However, these benchmarks largely exhibit a terrestrial bias, focusing on structured, Earth-centric scenarios that do not encompass the unique physics and challenges of space environments, such as microgravity, deformable terrains, or orbital dynamics. Many are also maturing around specific domains like tabletop manipulation.

A fundamental challenge for any simulation-based approach is the sim-to-real gap, where policies trained in simulation may fail in physical environments due to unmodeled complexities. Domain randomization (DR) is a recognized strategy for creating transferable policies by varying physical and visual parameters in simulation. Procedural content generation (PCG) further enhances diversity by algorithmically creating varied environment entities, promoting generalizable understanding of physics. While PCG has been effective in simpler 2D environments or for diversifying static asset collections, its application in complex 3D robotics, especially for generating entire worlds and robot morphologies on demand, represents an active area of research.

This work, by introducing the Space Robotics Bench (SRB), positions itself to bridge these identified gaps. SRB uniquely synthesizes space-relevant physics and tasks with an open-source, accessible, and massively parallel simulation framework, leveraging advanced PCG and DR. This approach aims to address data scarcity, overcome the limitations of existing simulators, and provide a diverse testbed that extends beyond terrestrial tasks, thereby accelerating the development of robust, learning-based autonomy for space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce the Space Robotics Bench (SRB), an open-source simulation framework designed to accelerate robot learning in space. The framework aims to facilitate the development, benchmarking, and deployment of robust autonomous systems required for future space missions.

The motivation for developing SRB stems from several critical challenges currently hindering the adoption of robot learning in space robotics:

- **High Cost and Data Scarcity:** Real-world technology demonstrations in space are prohibitively expensive, and extraterrestrial environments offer limited data, severely restricting the ability to train data-intensive robot learning algorithms.
- **Limitations of Existing Simulators:**
 - **Overfitting to Singular Realities:** Existing space-grade simulators, often mission-specific and high-fidelity digital twins, are designed for verification in well-defined scenarios. However, policies trained in such singular environments risk overfitting to specific assumptions, leading to brittle systems that fail when confronted with the unknown and unpredictable variations of real extraterrestrial conditions.
 - **Lack of Diversity and Scalability:** These simulators are not architected to support the massive data generation and vast environmental diversity required by modern robot learning methods. They frequently lack broad applicability across different robots or tasks and are often inaccessible to the wider research community, thereby acting as a barrier to innovation.
- **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The inherent discrepancy between simulated and real-world environments poses a significant challenge for transferring learned policies. Instead of pursuing perfect simulation fidelity, which is difficult to achieve for unknown environments, SRB proposes an alternative paradigm: mastering a massive diversity of scenarios. By exposing an agent to a vast range of environments through procedural content generation (PCG) and domain randomization (DR), the aim is to compel the agent to learn a generalizable understanding of underlying physics, treating the sim-to-real gap as a distribution of potential realities to be overcome.
- **Need for Robust Autonomous Systems:** The ambitious goals for future space exploration, involving autonomous rovers and robotic manipulators operating over long durations in unstructured, extreme environments, necessitate robotic systems that are reliable, adaptive, and capable of functioning with minimal human supervision. Robot learning is considered a powerful paradigm for achieving these adaptive behaviors, but it requires suitable development and testing environments.

In summary, SRB seeks to bridge the gap between the demand for robust space autonomy and the current limitations in data availability, simulation capabilities, and generalization techniques, by providing an accessible, diverse, and scalable simulation platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Space Robotics Bench (SRB) is an open-source simulation framework built upon NVIDIA Isaac Sim and the Isaac Lab robot learning framework, leveraging their capabilities for parallelized physics and rendering. Its architecture is modular and designed to realize a procedural paradigm tailored for space robotics.

**A. Framework Architecture**
SRB's design is guided by four principles:
- **Modularity and Extensibility:** Assets, robots, actuation models, and tasks are self-contained components registered within an internal registry. This allows for easy introduction of new elements via standard model formats and data-validated configurations. A streamlined sim-to-real mechanism automates the translation of simulated task definitions into compact wrappers with modular hardware interfaces.
- **Accessibility and Integration:** SRB provides a native interface for ROS 2 middleware and ensures all tasks are compatible with the Gymnasium API, enabling seamless integration with existing RL libraries. A unified command-line interface offers code-free control over experimental parameters.
- **Scale and Performance:** Engineered for high-throughput data acquisition, SRB enhances the GPU-accelerated capabilities of Isaac Lab with Rust extension modules for CPU-bound logic via PyO3, and offloads computationally expensive task logic to the GPU using TorchScript.
- **Fidelity and Diversity:** The framework combines explicit physics modeling with an enforced robustness strategy. It incorporates space-relevant physics (rigid body dynamics, particle interactions for granular materials, variable gravity) and approximates unmodeled phenomena (atmospheric drag) through random disturbances. This, combined with on-demand procedural asset generation and extensive domain randomization, creates rich training distributions.

**B. Procedural Paradigm for Generalization**
SRB's strategy for generalization relies on two complementary layers of diversity:
- **Procedural Content Generation (PCG):** An on-demand asset factory using Blender as its backend programmatically synthesizes a virtually unlimited variety of 3D assets. Node-based parametric blueprints define families of 3D models, enabling the creation of customizable environments (e.g., lunar surfaces with layered Perlin and Voronoi noise for topography and craters). This versatility extends to robot morphology, including procedural pipelines for satellites and excavation tools with randomized dimensions and geometries. An automated runtime pipeline links SRB to the procedural engine, generating unique asset variants in a headless background process and exporting them in USD format.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Complementary to PCG, SRB applies a comprehensive layer of DR at the start of each training episode. This technique systematically varies physical parameters (gravity, object inertia, surface friction, granular media cohesion) and visual parameters (lighting conditions, sensor noise profiles) to enforce robustness against unmodeled effects and sensor variations.

**C. Domains**
SRB introduces several template domains with unique physical characteristics (e.g., gravity magnitude, solar illumination properties):
- **Orbit:** Microgravity space environments (g=0.00 m/s²).
- **Asteroid:** Small irregular bodies (g=0.14±0.14 m/s²).
- **Moon:** Lunar surface with regolith (g=1.62±0.01 m/s²).
- **Mars:** Martian surface with rocks (g=3.72±0.01 m/s²).
- **Earth:** Base terrestrial conditions (g=9.81±0.03 m/s²).
Most tasks are domain-agnostic to support cross-domain research.

**D. Robots**
The framework features a diverse and extensible collection of pre-configured robots, addable via USD models and Python classes. This includes:
- **Mobile Robots:** Wheeled rovers (Perseverance), legged robots (Spot), aerial rotorcraft (Ingenuity), and spacecraft (ISS, PCG cubesat).
- **Manipulators:** Serial robot arms (Canadarm, Franka).
- **End-Effectors:** Active (gripper) and passive (scoop) tools.
- **Mobile Manipulators:** Humanoids (Unitree H1/G1) and compositional combinations of any mobile base, manipulator arm, and end-effector, allowing programmatic creation of over 5000 unique configurations.

**E. Actuation Models**
SRB provides a library of modular actuation models that map normalized agent actions to low-level robot commands, with automatic composition for multi-component robots. High-level, robot-agnostic abstractions are emphasized for generalization, including:
- **Wheeled:** Target linear and angular velocity, target joint velocities.
- **Legged & Humanoid:** Target joint positions.
- **Aerial:** Target linear and angular accelerations.
- **Spacecraft:** Thruster activation (static/gimbaled), target linear and angular acceleration.
- **Manipulator:** Operational Space Control (OSC), Inverse Kinematics (IK), target joint positions.
- **End-Effector:** Target joint positions, target joint velocities.

**F. Support for Diverse Workflows**
While optimized for RL, SRB supports various autonomy research workflows:
- **Teleoperation:** For collecting human demonstration data (imitation learning, offline RL).
- **ROS 2 Interface:** Exposes sensor data and control interfaces as standard topics and services, bridging to external software stacks for validating classical planning and control algorithms.
- **Multi-modal Sensors:** Generates and streams rich visual data (RGB, depth, segmentation) with ground truth annotations for perception-based systems.
- **Configurable Tasks:** Reward functions and termination conditions are configurable, allowing investigation into reward shaping or curriculum learning strategies.

**G. Sim-to-Real Workflow**
SRB provides a streamlined workflow for transferring policies to physical hardware:
- An automated mechanism inspects a simulated Gymnasium environment at runtime and generates a lightweight, hardware-specific, real-world counterpart.
- This real-world environment routes agent actions and observations through modular hardware interfaces (e.g., ROS 2 publishers/subscribers) that abstract low-level communication protocols.
- The process handles details like action space scaling and action rate. Trained policies can be deployed or fine-tuned on physical robots using the unified command-line interface.

**H. Benchmark Tasks**
SRB includes a suite of benchmark tasks designed to evaluate robotic capabilities across mission-relevant challenges in space, organized into three categories:
- **Design Philosophy:** Tasks focus on complete, mission-relevant skills (e.g., combined pick-and-place for peg-in-hole) rather than isolated sub-problems. Core task logic is shareable and adaptable across domains and robot morphologies. Tasks are configurable to enable research into generalization by controlling environmental diversity.
- **Categories:**
 - **Mobile Robotics:** Landing, rendezvous, orbital evasion, velocity tracking (with legged variants), waypoint navigation (with legged and orbital variants).
 - **Manipulation (Fixed Base):** Debris capture, sample collection (with multi-sample variant), excavation, peg-in-hole (with multi-assembly variant), screwdriving, solar panel assembly.
 - **Mobile Manipulation:** Mobile debris capture, mobile excavation.
- **Observations:** Tasks are formulated as partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) with multi-modal observation spaces (dictionary of tensors). Observations are categorized as State (privileged sim info), Proprioception (internal measurements), Visual (rendered images with ground truth), and Commands (high-level control signals). Visual tasks are registered separately (_visual suffix). Observations are distinguished by fixed or variable dimensionality to support cross-morphology policy learning.
- **Rewards:** Each task implements a composite reward function, a weighted sum of terms that encourage incremental progress toward objectives while penalizing undesirable behaviors (e.g., high-frequency actions, jerk, contact forces, energy consumption). Reward functions are designed to produce competent baselines and are fully modifiable to enable research into reward shaping.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides baseline performance metrics for the SRB framework and evaluates standard reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms across its benchmark tasks, complemented by experimental case studies validating its procedural paradigm.

**A. Simulation Throughput**
The framework's throughput was evaluated on a workstation with an AMD Ryzen 9 7950X CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Results indicate high-throughput capabilities across tasks:
- Computationally lighter tasks, such as `landing`, achieved over 100,000 steps per second with 1024 parallel environments.
- Most locomotion and manipulation tasks, involving more complex contact dynamics, reached approximately 15,000 steps per second with 512 parallel environments.
- The `excavation` task, utilizing high-fidelity particle simulation for granular media, was more intensive, maintaining around 40 steps per second regardless of the environment count.
These results demonstrate that SRB facilitates efficient data acquisition for modern learning algorithms.

**B. Reinforcement Learning Baselines**
Three RL algorithms—Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3), and DreamerV3—were trained on default task configurations using state-based observations.
- **DreamerV3** consistently exhibited superior sample efficiency, converging to higher episodic returns and success rates across nearly all tasks. However, this performance came with a higher computational cost, requiring on average 5.3 times longer wall-clock training time than PPO for the same number of environment steps.
- Tasks requiring long-horizon planning and high-dimensional coordination, specifically `solar_panel_assembly` and `mobile_debris_capture`, remained unsolved by all three algorithms, highlighting them as significant open challenges.

**C. Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**
A critical validation involved a zero-shot transfer experiment for dynamic waypoint tracking with a Leo Rover on basalt gravel in a lunar-analogue testbed.
- **Algorithmic Comparison:** PPO, PPO with LSTM, TD3, and DreamerV3 agents were trained purely in SRB and then deployed to the physical rover. DreamerV3 demonstrated superior real-world performance, achieving substantially lower Average Tracking Error (ATE) across various velocity profiles. For instance, at 5 cm/s, DreamerV3 had an ATE of 2.3 cm / 1.7°, significantly better than PPO's 13.2 cm / 7.8°. DreamerV3 was subsequently selected for further experiments.
- **Role of Procedural Diversity:** DreamerV3 agents trained under two conditions—`Static` (all parallel instances shared a single procedurally generated terrain) and `Procedural` (each of 512 instances used a unique terrain)—were compared. While both performed well in simulation, the `Procedural` agent demonstrated superior real-world capabilities. At 15 cm/s, the `Procedural` agent maintained an ATE of 3.3 cm and 1.9°, whereas the `Static` agent's error was 4.2 cm and 6.8°. The `Procedural` agent achieved a 21% lower location ATE and a 72% reduction in orientation ATE. This result supports the effectiveness of the procedural paradigm in creating policies robust to physical world variations.

**D. General Principle of Diversity**
Further simulation-based experiments investigated the impact of procedural diversity across three distinct axes:
- **Environmental Diversity:** In `locomotion_waypoint_navigation`, procedural training yielded a slight advantage (1.03 normalized return) in zero-shot transfer to out-of-distribution environments.
- **Object Diversity:** For `sample_collection`, the performance of the statically trained agent decreased by nearly 50% when encountering unseen procedural rocks. In contrast, the procedural agent maintained high performance (1.91 normalized return), generalizing to novel object geometries.
- **Robot Morphology Diversity:** In the `excavation` task, an agent trained on a single tool geometry failed to adapt to novel tool geometries, while a generalist agent trained on a procedural distribution of 16 unique tools successfully generalized, achieving a nearly tenfold increase in performance (9.06 normalized return).
These results confirm that systematic exposure to procedural variation, in the environment, interactive objects, or robot embodiment, produces robust policies.

**E. Learning Compliant Manipulation**
SRB was utilized to compare a conventional rigid Inverse Kinematics (IK) controller against an adaptive Operational Space Control (OSC) policy that learned to dynamically modulate its stiffness and damping gains.
- In `debris_capture`, the adaptive OSC agent achieved a 58% reduction in motion jerkiness.
- For high-precision assembly in `peg_in_hole`, the compliant OSC policy led to a higher final success rate.
- In `excavation`, learned compliance was essential for managing unpredictable forces, allowing the agent to excavate more material with 56% less jerk.
These findings suggest SRB is an effective platform for developing advanced, safer, and more effective compliant control methods for complex physical interaction in space.

**F. End-to-End Visuomotor Control**
SRB supports diverse camera configurations for investigating end-to-end visuomotor control.
- In the `excavation` task, augmenting an agent with visual feedback from two simulated depth cameras resulted in a 93% increase in the volume of scooped regolith compared to a proprioceptive-only agent.
This demonstrates SRB's utility as a platform for developing and benchmarking perception-driven policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Space Robotics Bench (SRB) makes several contributions to the fields of space robotics and robot learning. It addresses identified gaps in existing research tools by providing an open-source, accessible, and scalable simulation framework.

The work's central finding is the empirical validation of its procedural paradigm: that robust, generalizable autonomy for space robotics can be achieved through the mastery of immense procedural diversity. The successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a rover navigation policy, trained entirely within diverse procedural environments, confirms that this approach can produce policies deployable on physical hardware. This outcome reframes the challenge of developing autonomous systems for unknown environments by shifting focus from perfect simulation fidelity to building statistical assurance against a vast distribution of possible realities.

SRB's contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap:** By emphasizing extensive procedural generation and domain randomization, SRB provides a validated pathway for creating policies that are inherently robust to the variations found in physical environments. This capability is particularly relevant for space, where real-world conditions are often unknown and difficult to replicate precisely in a single, high-fidelity simulation.
- **Accelerating Space Robotics Research:** SRB lowers the barrier to entry for robot learning research in space by offering a standardized, open-source testbed. Its modular architecture, compatibility with standard APIs (ROS 2, Gymnasium), and high-throughput parallel simulation capabilities enable efficient data generation and rapid iteration in policy development.
- **Enabling Generalization Studies:** The framework's ability to procedurally generate diverse environments, interactive objects, and robot morphologies (including compositional mobile manipulators) facilitates systematic investigations into how different levels and sources of diversity impact policy robustness and generalization. This is crucial for developing versatile robots capable of operating in varied space missions.
- **Advancing Control and Perception:** SRB serves as a versatile platform for exploring advanced control strategies, such as adaptive compliant manipulation, and for developing end-to-end visuomotor control policies that directly utilize rich sensory inputs. The experimental results demonstrate the benefits of these approaches in terms of safety, precision, and task effectiveness.
- **Fostering Collaboration and Innovation:** The open-source nature of SRB encourages broader community engagement, allowing researchers to leverage and contribute to a shared resource. By providing challenging benchmark tasks, including those that remain unsolved by current state-of-the-art RL algorithms, SRB establishes new frontiers for research in areas like hierarchical learning and complex motor control.
- **Complementing Existing Tools:** SRB is presented as a complement to existing mission-specific, high-fidelity simulators, serving as an early-stage development platform for creating generalized, robust policies rather than for final mission-critical verification.

The paper acknowledges limitations, including the persistent sim-to-real gap for complex sensory modalities (e.g., noisy depth maps) and the fact that procedural assets are plausible approximations rather than scientifically exact representations of extraterrestrial environments. Similarly, the physics simulation does not model all peculiarities of space (e.g., vacuum, thermal cycles, radiation). However, these limitations are precisely what motivate SRB's diversity-driven approach, aiming to create systems that can better handle inevitable discrepancies between simulation and the complexities of space.

Overall, SRB provides a foundational resource for developing the trusted autonomous systems necessary for future space exploration and utilization, contributing to the realization of multiplanetary endeavors.
