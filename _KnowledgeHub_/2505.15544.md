# A Temporal Difference Method for Stochastic Continuous Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Tokyo introduced "differential TD" (dTD), a model-free temporal difference method for policy evaluation in continuous dynamic systems governed by stochastic differential equations. This approach reformulates the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation to implicitly leverage system continuity from observed state transitions, achieving faster learning rates and robust performance in continuous control tasks compared to standard TD.

## Problem

- Traditional model-free reinforcement learning methods, when applied to continuous systems, often implicitly discard explicit continuity information, potentially leading to less efficient learning.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches that leverage the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation are typically model-based, requiring explicit knowledge of the system's drift and diffusion coefficients.
- It is challenging to design model-free RL algorithms that effectively exploit the structural information of continuous dynamics embedded in the HJB equation, particularly while ensuring compatibility with modern deep reinforcement learning frameworks.

## Method

- Developed "==differential Temporal Difference==" (dTD), a model-free TD method that transforms the Bellman expectation equation into an HJB-like form, expressing unknown dynamics coefficients as limits of sample-based finite differences.
- Proposed a practical implementation of dTD that computes the required derivatives without explicit knowledge of the system's drift or diffusion, relying solely on observed state transitions.
- Introduced β-dTD, a hybrid approach that linearly combines the dTD error with the classical TD error, enhancing stability and performance when dTD is applied with neural network function approximators in deep RL.

## Results

- The β-dTD method consistently outperformed the β-naive-dTD variant and generally demonstrated faster learning rates than standard TD across various continuous control benchmarks (Hopper, HalfCheetah, Ant, Humanoid) with varying noise levels.
- Optimized β values for β-dTD were substantial (e.g., **0.57**-**0.74** in Hopper and HalfCheetah), indicating that the dTD component significantly contributes to the learning process when properly formulated.
- β-dTD exhibited similar robustness to process noise as standard TD, with both methods showing comparable performance degradation at higher noise levels (coefficient = **0.05**), suggesting its practical applicability in noisy environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inherent continuity information within a system's dynamics, as encoded by the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation, can be leveraged in a model-free setting by expressing the expectations of drift and diffusion terms through sample-based finite differences of state transitions.
> - By incorporating derivatives of the value function into the temporal difference target, dTD enables agents to implicitly perceive and utilize the continuous nature of the environment, leading to more informed updates.
> - A hybrid approach (β-dTD) that balances the theoretically grounded dTD with the empirical stability of classical TD is crucial for robust implementation and superior performance in deep reinforcement learning contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated success across diverse domains, including discrete systems like board games and continuous state-time systems such as robotic control. Much of the field's advancement has focused on algorithmic and optimization refinements, such as objective function design, exploration-exploitation strategies, and architectural innovations. This paper positions itself in a distinct but less explored area: "continuous RL," where system dynamics are precisely described by ordinary differential equations (ODEs) or stochastic differential equations (SDEs).

Traditional RL approaches, even when applied to continuous systems, often discretize the problem or implicitly handle continuity, thereby not fully exploiting available prior knowledge about the continuous nature of the dynamics. The Bellman equation, a cornerstone of RL, encodes continuity within its transition kernel. However, in model-free settings where transitions are approximated using samples, this explicit continuity information is often lost.

In contrast, the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation, the continuous counterpart of the Bellman optimality equation, intrinsically retains continuity information within the arguments of its expectation term, rather than solely in a transition kernel. This property makes the HJB equation a theoretically appealing target for continuous RL. However, prior work leveraging the HJB equation has predominantly been model-based, requiring explicit knowledge of the system's drift ($\mu$) and diffusion ($\sigma$) coefficient functions. This inherent limitation prevents its direct application in model-free RL, where such dynamics are unknown.

The presented work aims to bridge this gap between stochastic control theory (which uses the HJB equation) and model-free reinforcement learning. It seeks to develop an approach that can leverage the structural information of continuous dynamics, as captured by the HJB equation, without needing explicit access to the underlying system model. This is particularly relevant for real-world applications like robot control and autonomous driving, which inherently involve continuous dynamics.

Related research in continuous RL can be broadly categorized:
- **Deterministic Dynamics (ODEs):** Early work explored model-free approaches and TD methods for ODE systems. Recent contributions include model-free Q-learning (Kim et al., 2021) and model-based continuous-time optimization using Neural ODEs (Yıldız et al., 2021). Key challenges included the collapse of Q-functions in continuous RL (Tallec et al., 2019).
- **Stochastic Dynamics (SDEs):** Initial explorations were often model-based (Munos and Bourgine, 1997). More recently, theoretical foundations have been established for SDE systems, including entropy-regularized control (Wang et al., 2020), martingale-based policy evaluation and learning (Jia and Zhou, 2022a,b, 2023), and policy optimization extensions (Zhao et al., 2023). Many of these methods either rely on full trajectory information, are restricted to finite horizons, or remain incompatible with current deep RL frameworks, making direct comparison with general model-free deep RL challenging.

The current paper distinguishes itself by proposing a model-free TD method explicitly targeting the HJB equation for general SDEs, compatible with common deep RL algorithms, and demonstrating its application in continuous control benchmarks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a model-free temporal difference (TD) method that can effectively learn value functions in continuous dynamic systems governed by stochastic differential equations (SDEs). This method aims to integrate the continuity information inherent in the system dynamics directly into the learning process, specifically by targeting a variant of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation, without requiring prior knowledge or estimation of the system's drift ($\mu$) and diffusion ($\sigma$) coefficient functions.

The motivation for this work arises from several limitations and opportunities within current reinforcement learning paradigms:

1. **Limitations of Existing HJB-based RL:** While the HJB equation offers a theoretically sound framework for continuous optimal control, its application in RL has traditionally been restricted to model-based settings. These methods necessitate explicit knowledge of the system's dynamics, meaning the coefficient functions of the SDEs must be known beforehand. This requirement significantly limits their applicability in many real-world scenarios where precise system models are unavailable or difficult to obtain. The authors seek to overcome this constraint, enabling the benefits of HJB-based formulations in a model-free context.

2. **Inefficiency of Standard TD for Continuous Systems:** Standard discrete-time TD methods, when applied to continuous systems, typically discretize time. During sample-based approximation of expectations, the crucial information about the system's underlying continuous dynamics (encoded in the transition kernel) is implicitly discarded. This means the learning agent operates without explicitly leveraging the inherent continuity of the environment, potentially leading to less efficient learning or requiring more samples. The paper is motivated by the desire to design algorithms that allow agents to "perceive and leverage the system’s continuity information" more directly.

3. **Exploiting the HJB Equation's Structure:** The HJB equation is unique in that it encodes continuity not just in the transition kernel (as in the Bellman equation), but also in the arguments of its expectation (involving derivatives of the value function). This structural property, if harnessed correctly, could allow for sample-based approximations that retain the continuity information, even when the underlying dynamics are unknown. The challenge lies in re-framing the HJB equation in a way that its dependency on the unknown model coefficients ($\mu$ and $\sigma$) can be circumvented through sample-based estimation.

4. **Focus on Policy Evaluation:** The authors clarify their focus on policy evaluation (learning the value function for a fixed policy) using the V-Bellman expectation equation. This choice is motivated by practical considerations: the V-Bellman optimality equation is incompatible with model-free frameworks, and Q-functions often degenerate or introduce unnecessary complexity in continuous-time systems (Tallec et al., 2019). By focusing on policy evaluation, the research aims to establish a clear foundation for leveraging continuity.

In essence, the core motivation is to enhance the efficiency and theoretical grounding of model-free reinforcement learning in continuous environments by developing a TD method that respects and utilizes the system's continuity through a reformulated HJB equation, thereby bridging a gap between stochastic control theory and practical deep RL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a novel temporal difference (TD) method, termed "differential TD" (dTD), for policy evaluation in stochastic continuous dynamic systems (SDEs). The approach is model-free, meaning it does not require prior knowledge of the system's drift ($\mu$) or diffusion ($\sigma$) coefficients.

#### 3.1. Problem Setting and Background

The system dynamics are modeled by a controlled SDE:
$dS_t = \mu(S_t, A_t)dt + \sigma(S_t, A_t)dB_t$,
where $S_t$ is the state, $A_t$ is the action drawn from a stochastic policy $\pi( \cdot | S_t)$, and $B_t$ is Brownian motion. The focus is on the model-free setting. The Bellman expectation equation for a value function $V^\pi(s_t)$ in continuous time, when discretized, is given by:
$V^\pi(s_t) = E_{p^\pi} [\rho(s_t, A_t)\Delta t + e^{-\gamma \Delta t} V^\pi(S_{t+\Delta t})]$.
The key limitation of this standard formulation for model-free learning is that the system dynamics information is primarily embedded in the transition kernel $p^\pi$, which is discarded when using sample-based approximations, thus losing continuity information.

#### 3.2. Deriving dTD from the HJB Equation

The core of the methodology involves transforming the Bellman expectation equation into a variant of the HJB equation that embeds dynamic information within the expectation argument, making it robust to sample-based approximation. This is achieved by applying Itô’s formula to expand $V^\pi(S_{t+\Delta t})$, resulting in the HJB equation for a fixed policy:
$V^\pi(s_t) = \frac{1}{\gamma} E_\pi \left[ \rho(s_t, A_t) + \sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i(s_t, A_t) \frac{\partial V^\pi(s)}{\partial s_i}|_{s_t} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n [\sigma(s_t, A_t)\sigma^\top(s_t, A_t)]_{ij} \frac{\partial^2 V^\pi(s)}{\partial s_i \partial s_j}|_{s_t} \right]$.
A direct implementation of this equation for model-free learning is still problematic because it explicitly depends on the unknown coefficients $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

The crucial innovation lies in **Proposition 1**, which demonstrates that the expectations of the drift ($\mu_i$) and diffusion ($[\sigma\sigma^\top]_{ij}$) terms can be expressed as limits of sample-based finite differences of state transitions:
$E_\pi [\mu_i(s_t, A_t)] = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} E_{p^\pi}\left[ \frac{S^i_{t+\Delta t} - s^i_t}{\Delta t} \right]$
$E_\pi [[\sigma(s_t, A_t)\sigma^\top(s_t, A_t)]_{ij}] = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} E_{p^\pi}\left[ \frac{(S^i_{t+\Delta t} - s^i_t)(S^j_{t+\Delta t} - s^j_t)}{\Delta t} \right]$
By substituting these expressions (approximated for small $\Delta t$) into the HJB equation, the authors reformulate the HJB equation into a model-free form. This leads to the definition of **differential temporal difference (dTD)**:
$dTD := \frac{1}{\gamma} \left( \rho(s_t, a_t) + \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{s^i_{t+\Delta t} - s^i_t}{\Delta t} \frac{\partial \hat{V}(s)}{\partial s_i}|_{s_t} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{(s^i_{t+\Delta t} - s^i_t)(s^j_{t+\Delta t} - s^j_t)}{\Delta t} \frac{\partial^2 \hat{V}(s)}{\partial s_i \partial s_j}|_{s_t} \right) - \hat{V}(s_t)$,
where $\hat{V}$ is the estimated value function. This formulation allows the agent to implicitly leverage continuity information (through the derivatives) from observed state transitions without explicit knowledge of $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

#### 3.3. Convergence Analysis

The theoretical convergence of dTD is analyzed under an idealized continuous-time setting where the value function itself can be directly manipulated. The fixed-point equation $V = T V$ (where $T$ is the HJB operator for a fixed policy) is shown to be equivalent to a linear elliptic PDE: $(\gamma I - L_\pi)V(s) = \bar{\rho}(s)$, where $L_\pi$ is the infinitesimal generator and $\bar{\rho}$ is the policy-averaged reward.
**Lemma 1** establishes the existence and uniqueness of this fixed point $V^\pi \in H^1(S)$ under specific conditions (Assumption 1):
1. **Bounded Domain:** State space $S$ is a bounded, connected open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
2. **Reflecting Boundary:** Neumann condition $n(s) \cdot (D(s) \nabla V(s)) = 0$ on the boundary $\partial S$.
3. **Coefficient Regularity:** Policy-averaged reward $\bar{\rho}(s)$, drift $\bar{\mu}(s)$, and diffusion matrix $D(s)$ are bounded and Lipschitz continuous.
4. **Uniform Ellipticity:** The policy-averaged diffusion matrix $D(s)$ is symmetric and uniformly elliptic, implying non-degenerate noise in all directions.
5. **Discount Factor:** $\gamma > 0$ is sufficiently large to ensure coercivity of the associated bilinear form.
**Proposition 2** then proves that the continuous-time dynamics $\frac{\partial V(t)}{\partial t} = T V(t) - V(t)$ converges exponentially fast to the unique fixed point $V^\pi$ in the $L^2(S)$ norm. This proof utilizes the Lax-Milgram theorem to guarantee coercivity and boundedness of the bilinear form.

#### 3.4. Implementation in Deep Reinforcement Learning

- **Loss Function:** For deep RL, the value function $\hat{V}$ is represented by a neural network. The dTD error is used to define a loss function. Two variants are explored:
 - **Naive-dTD:** Treats $\hat{V}(s_t)$ as the prediction and the rest of the dTD expression as the target.
 - **dTD (motivated):** Inspired by Taylor expansion, treats terms involving derivatives of $\hat{V}(s_t)$ as the prediction and the remaining terms as the target. This formulation was empirically found to perform better.
- **Stabilization (β-dTD):** To address practical instabilities arising from function approximation in deep RL, a hybrid scheme called **β-dTD** is introduced. This method linearly combines the classical TD error (with weight $1-\beta$) with the dTD error (with weight $\beta$). The classical TD error provides empirical stability, while the dTD error injects gradient information from continuous dynamics.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Although dTD involves second derivatives (Hessian), its computation can be optimized to $O(n)$ (where $n$ is state dimension) instead of $O(n^2)$ by rearranging calculations to avoid direct Hessian computation, e.g., using properties of vector-Jacobian products.
- **Discrete Environment Compatibility:** The continuous reward rate $\rho(s_t, a_t)$ and continuous discount factor $e^{-\gamma \Delta t}$ are mapped to their discrete counterparts $r(s_t, a_t)$ and $\gamma_{\text{discrete}}$ to enable compatibility with discrete-time simulators commonly used in RL benchmarks. Specifically, $\rho(s_t, a_t) = r(s_t, a_t)/\Delta t$ and $\gamma = - \frac{1}{\Delta t} \log(\gamma_{\text{discrete}})$.

#### 3.5. Experimental Design

Experiments were conducted using the Brax library in Mujoco environments (Hopper, HalfCheetah, Ant, Humanoid), which feature mid-to-high dimensional state spaces. Process noise was introduced to simulate SDE systems ($s_i \leftarrow s_i + \text{coef} \times |s_i| \times \text{noise}$), with $\text{coef} = 0.00, 0.01, 0.05$. Baselines included standard TD, β-naive-dTD, and β-dTD, all integrated with the PPO algorithm. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using DEHB.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation aimed to assess the performance of the proposed dTD method and its variants against standard Temporal Difference (TD) in continuous control tasks. The key findings are as follows:

1. **Comparison of β-naive-dTD vs. β-dTD:**
 - **Performance:** β-dTD consistently outperformed β-naive-dTD across all tested environments (Hopper, HalfCheetah, Ant, Humanoid) and noise levels (coef = 0.00, 0.01, 0.05).
 - **Optimized Beta Values:** For β-naive-dTD, the optimized $\beta$ values were generally small (e.g., 0.08, 0.07, 0.23, 0.02 across environments). This suggests that the naive-dTD component had a minimal contribution to the update rule, making the effective learning rule close to standard TD.
 - **Performance vs. Standard TD:** Despite the small $\beta$ values, β-naive-dTD performed significantly worse than standard TD. The authors propose two possible explanations: (1) the chosen $\beta$ might still not be sufficiently small to completely mitigate potential adverse effects of the naive-dTD term, or (2) hyperparameter tuning resources were predominantly allocated to optimizing $\beta$, potentially leading to suboptimal tuning of other TD-related parameters.

2. **Comparison of Standard TD vs. β-dTD:**
 - **Overall Performance:** β-dTD generally outperformed standard TD or achieved comparable performance in all tested cases. While the magnitude of improvement varied, β-dTD consistently showed a tendency for a faster rate of improvement during training.
 - **Convergence:** The final episodic returns of both TD and β-dTD tended to converge to similar levels. This outcome is attributed to both methods being derived from the same underlying Bellman equation, implying that they should eventually learn similar value functions.
 - **Efficiency:** The advantage of β-dTD appears to be in its learning efficiency, as it leverages continuity information, leading to more informative updates and quicker performance gains.

3. **Significance of dTD in β-dTD:**
 - Unlike β-naive-dTD, the optimized $\beta$ values for β-dTD were not negligible (e.g., 0.57 in Hopper, 0.74 in HalfCheetah, 0.24 in Ant, 0.33 in Humanoid). This indicates that the dTD component plays a substantial and meaningful role in the learning process when implemented with the "motivated" prediction-target split. A larger $\beta$ implies that the model-free HJB-based updates contribute significantly to the overall learning update.

4. **Impact of Process Noise:**
 - **Robustness:** Both β-dTD and standard TD exhibited similar robustness to process noise.
 - **Performance Degradation:** At moderate noise levels (coef = 0.01), neither method experienced significant degradation in performance. However, at higher noise levels (coef = 0.05), both β-dTD and TD showed a comparable reduction in performance, particularly in environments such as Ant and HalfCheetah. This suggests that the inherent noise robustness characteristics of dTD are similar to those of standard TD within the tested range.

In summary, the results suggest that the proposed β-dTD method is a viable and often more efficient alternative to standard TD for continuous control problems. It effectively incorporates continuity information without requiring explicit model knowledge, leading to faster learning rates, especially when the dTD component is formulated appropriately and combined with classical TD for stability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces differential TD (dTD), a novel temporal difference method, marking a notable contribution to the field of reinforcement learning in continuous-time and continuous-state systems.

**Theoretical Significance:**
- **Bridging Stochastic Control and Model-Free RL:** The work represents a significant step towards bridging the theoretical foundations of stochastic control (rooted in the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation) with the practical capabilities of model-free reinforcement learning. By reformulating the HJB equation using sample-based finite differences of state transitions, dTD enables its application without explicit knowledge of the system dynamics (drift and diffusion coefficients), a long-standing challenge.
- **Leveraging Continuity Information:** dTD provides a mechanism for RL agents to inherently perceive and leverage the continuity of system dynamics, even under sample-based approximations. Unlike traditional TD methods where continuity information in the transition kernel is lost, dTD embeds this information into the arguments of its expectation, allowing for more informed updates.
- **Theoretical Convergence Guarantee:** The establishment of exponential stability for the idealized continuous-time dynamics of the HJB equation, using techniques from linear elliptic PDE theory, provides a crucial theoretical underpinning for the method. This contributes to the understanding of convergence properties for continuous RL algorithms.

**Practical Significance and Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Learning Efficiency:** Empirical results demonstrate that β-dTD, a hybrid approach combining dTD with classical TD, generally outperforms or matches standard TD, often exhibiting a faster rate of improvement. This suggests that incorporating continuity information can lead to more efficient learning, which is critical for complex, real-world applications where data collection might be expensive or time-consuming.
- **Applicability to Real-world Continuous Control:** The method is directly applicable to domains where dynamics are naturally described by SDEs, such as robotics, autonomous driving, and process control. The model-free nature of dTD removes the burden of developing accurate system models, broadening the scope of problems that can be tackled with HJB-based RL.
- **Robustness and Stability in Deep RL:** The introduction of β-dTD addresses practical stability issues encountered when applying dTD with neural network function approximators. This hybrid scheme balances the theoretical advantages of dTD with the empirical stability of classical TD, making the approach more robust for deep reinforcement learning.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays a foundation for further exploration into continuous RL. It opens avenues for:
 - **Bridging Theory-Practice Gap:** Investigating how the theoretical convergence conditions (e.g., bounded domain, uniform ellipticity) can be practically managed or relaxed in deep RL settings.
 - **Variance Reduction:** Developing improved estimators or regularization techniques to reduce the variance of dTD updates.
 - **Methodological Extensions:** Adapting dTD to a wider array of existing TD-based techniques, including off-policy learning, Q-learning, and various actor-critic architectures.

While the theoretical analysis relies on strong assumptions that can be challenging to satisfy or verify in practice, the empirical demonstrations highlight the potential of dTD. The work contributes a novel perspective on how to integrate the mathematical rigor of continuous control with the adaptable power of model-free deep learning, paving the way for more sophisticated and efficient RL solutions for continuous dynamic systems.
