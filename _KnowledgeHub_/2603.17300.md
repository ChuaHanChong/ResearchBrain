# ReSteer: Quantifying and Refining the Steerability of Multitask Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> ReSteer introduces a framework to quantify and enhance the ability of multitask robot policies to adapt to new instructions mid-execution, showing a 2.2x improvement in real-world steering success while maintaining high single-task proficiency.

## Problem

- Multitask robot policies often disregard mid-execution instruction changes, hindering dynamic interaction and limiting their utility in real-world human-robot collaboration scenarios.
- This 'steerability' deficit is hypothesized to stem from training datasets that typically feature constant language instructions throughout trajectories, causing policies to over-index on visual state information rather than language cues.
- Existing language conditioning and task switching methods for robotics often assume robust primitive instruction following or restrict task updates to specific pre-defined phase boundaries, lacking continuous, user-driven redirection from arbitrary states.

## Method

- The **ReSteer** framework formally defines steerability using metrics like Directional Steerability and ==Steerability Coverage Ratio (SCR)==, and utilizes ==Conditional Mutual Information (CMI)== as a rollout-free proxy to efficiently identify 'instruction-blind' states.
- ==SteerGen==, a stage-aware data generator, synthesizes new training trajectories where task instructions change mid-execution, strategically creating instruction-contrasting supervision at shared states.
- ==Self-Refining Behavioral Cloning (SRBC)== iteratively fine-tunes the policy on successful steering trajectories collected during deployment, reinforcing reliable steerable behaviors to maximize SCR.

## Results

- ReSteer achieved an **8.8%** absolute improvement in steerability in simulation on the LIBERO-Goal benchmark, indicating enhanced responsiveness to language instructions across intermediate execution states.
- In real-world experiments on the DROID platform, ReSteer yielded a **73%** steering success rate, representing a **2.2x** improvement over the finetuned baseline (**33%**), while maintaining **85%** single-task success.
- ReSteer-enhanced policies demonstrated the capability to execute chained long-horizon task sequences with multiple mid-execution steering commands, showcasing improved interactive and reactive robotic assistance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditional Mutual Information (CMI) serves as an effective and efficient proxy for steerability, showing a strong correlation (r=0.74) with ground-truth task-switching success and enabling diagnosis of instruction-blind regions.
> - The limited steerability of current multitask robot policies is largely attributable to a data distribution pathology where static language in training data causes policies to prioritize state over dynamic language conditioning.
> - Targeted data augmentation that includes explicit mid-execution instruction switches, especially when guided by CMI to prioritize low-steerability states, significantly improves sample efficiency and policy responsiveness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in the field of language-conditioned multitask robot policies, specifically Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, exemplified by approaches such as π0.5, OpenVLA, RT-1/RT-X, and MolmoACT, have demonstrated capabilities in unifying language-specified goals with visual understanding. They leverage pretrained multimodal backbones to enable robots to perform dozens of tasks, generalize across different robot embodiments, and engage in various forms of embodied reasoning.

However, a recurring limitation in existing VLA policies is "steerability," which refers to a policy's ability to adapt its behavior in response to new task instructions issued during ongoing execution. Current policies frequently exhibit a tendency to disregard mid-execution instruction changes, instead continuing to execute the original task based primarily on state information. This behavior restricts the interactive utility of robots, preventing users from dynamically redirecting a robot's actions.

Previous research in language conditioning for robotics has explored several related areas:
- **Language Conditioning and Interactive Prompting:** Works like Interleave-VLA, ChatVLA, and Robix have focused on improving the grounding of language instructions, retaining open-world reasoning, or decomposing high-level requests into sub-prompts. Some interactive systems allow for user feedback. SwitchVLA investigated task switching but relied on pre-segmented phases or engineered labels. CAST synthesized counterfactual language to improve instruction following. These methods generally assume robust primitive instruction following or constrain task switching to predefined modes.
- **Steerability and Reasoning Beyond Language:** Other approaches include latent-space manipulation, where language-driven embeddings interpolate between behaviors, and reasoning-based planning, which augments policies with textual plans. While these enhance representation transfer or execution planning, they typically assume a single, fixed intent per rollout or restrict task updates to specific phase boundaries, thus lacking mechanisms for high-frequency, user-driven redirection from arbitrary intermediate states.

ReSteer distinguishes itself by explicitly defining, quantifying, and improving steerability as a continuous, state-dependent property of a closed-loop policy. It focuses on enabling prompt changes at any execution state without relying on fixed task boundaries or external planners. The work posits that the issue of limited steerability stems from the nature of current training datasets, which typically consist of independently collected demonstrations for each task, leading policies to over-index on state information rather than language conditioning during execution. By proposing a framework to address this data-centric pathology, ReSteer aims to enhance the interactive and reactive capabilities of multitask robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:
1. **Quantify Task Steerability:** Develop a formal definition and a metric for evaluating a robot policy's ability to properly condition on new task instructions from arbitrary states during execution.
2. **Diagnose the Root Cause of Limited Steerability:** Investigate why existing multitask policies, despite strong single-task performance, exhibit poor steerability, hypothesizing a link to the distribution of training data.
3. **Propose a Framework for Improving Steerability:** Develop a data-centric methodology, ReSteer, that generates targeted training data and refines policies to enhance their responsiveness to mid-execution instruction changes.
4. **Validate the Proposed Framework:** Empirically evaluate ReSteer's effectiveness in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from a recognized failure mode in current language-conditioned multitask robot policies: their limited "steerability." While these policies can execute individual tasks, they often fail to respond when a user provides a new instruction mid-execution. For instance, a robot commanded to "close the oven" may ignore a subsequent instruction to "put the bowl in the sink" if it is already in the process of moving towards the oven, even if it can perform both tasks independently. This behavior, illustrated by examples where policies continue with original tasks despite new prompts, severely restricts the interactive utility and flexibility of robotic systems in dynamic, human-centric environments.

The authors hypothesize that this lack of steerability is rooted in the structure of typical training datasets. These datasets often consist of demonstrations collected for each task independently, where language instructions remain constant throughout a trajectory. This practice may lead policies to learn to prioritize visual state information over language prompts as a determinant of action, a form of shortcut learning. The language input becomes informative for only a small fraction of the total task execution.

To formalize this intuition, the research proposes using conditional mutual information (CMI) I(A; L|S) as an approximation of steerability. This metric quantifies how much language prompts reduce the variance of predicted action distributions, thereby indicating how meaningfully instructions constrain action selection at a given state. The motivation is to enhance scenarios where instruction changes elicit corresponding behavioral switches, thereby improving the robot's ability to be meaningfully controlled at any point in time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ReSteer framework is designed to quantify and improve task steerability in multitask robot policies through a two-stage data generation and refinement process.

**A. Quantifying Steerability**
The paper first establishes a formal definition of steerability based on a policy $\pi(a | s, \ell)$ operating in an MDP with state space $S$, action space $A$, and language instructions $\ell$.

- **Success Probability $P^\pi_k(s)$:** The probability that policy $\pi$ successfully completes task $k$ when initialized from state $s$ under instruction $l_k$.
- **Policy-Induced State Visitation Set $S_k(\pi)$:** The set of states from which policy $\pi$ can successfully complete task $k$ with a high probability (above a threshold $\alpha$).
- **Union of Task Visitation Sets $S^\text{union}_{i,j}(\pi)$:** The set of states where policy $\pi$ can successfully complete at least one of tasks $i$ or $j$. This defines the overall task feasibility.
- **Directional Steerability $S^\text{steer}_{i \rightarrow j}(\pi)$:** A state $s$ is steerable from task $i$ to task $j$ if, after switching the instruction from $l_i$ to $l_j$ at state $s$, the policy can still complete task $j$ with high probability.
- **Bidirectional Steerability $S^\text{steer}_{i \leftrightarrow j}(\pi)$:** The intersection of $S^\text{steer}_{i \rightarrow j}(\pi)$ and $S^\text{steer}_{j \rightarrow i}(\pi)$, indicating states where switching is successful in both directions.
- **Steerability Coverage Ratio (SCR) $SCR_{i \leftrightarrow j}(\pi)$:** Defined as the ratio $\frac{|S^\text{steer}_{i \leftrightarrow j}(\pi)|}{|S^\text{union}_{i,j}(\pi)|}$. A higher SCR implies broader support for task switching across the policy’s feasible state space.

**Evaluation Protocol:** To evaluate steerability, the policy executes an initial task $\tau_i$. At sampled timesteps during this execution, the language instruction is switched to a target task $\tau_j$. Success is measured if the policy, conditioned on the new prompt $l_j$, completes task $j$. This process is repeated for various (state, prompt) pairs, with multiple rollouts (e.g., 10) to account for stochasticity. This exhaustive evaluation quantifies steerability by generating a steerability matrix and an average steerability score.

**B. ReSteer Framework Components**
The ReSteer framework comprises three main components: (i) a steerability estimator based on CMI, (ii) a steerable data generator (SteerGen), and (iii) a self-refinement pipeline (SRBC).

1. **Conditional Mutual Information (CMI) as a Steerability Proxy:**
 - **Concept:** CMI, defined as $I(A; L | S = s) = H(A | S = s) - H(A | S = s, L)$, quantifies the reduction in action uncertainty when the language instruction is known at a given state $s$. A high CMI suggests that language strongly modulates the action distribution, indicating higher steerability. Low CMI implies weak language-action coupling, suggesting states where instruction switches are unlikely to induce meaningful behavioral change.
 - **Theoretical Link to SCR:** The paper establishes a theoretical link, showing that CMI at a state $s$ is equivalent to the Jensen–Shannon divergence between instruction-conditioned action distributions. This implies that if a state is steerable, its CMI must exceed a certain threshold $\tau$. This means states with $I(A; L | S = s) < \tau$ are "instruction-blind" and cannot support task switching. This relationship allows CMI to serve as a rollout-free proxy for identifying low-steerability regions.
 - **CMI-Guided State Prioritization:** CMI is used to prioritize data collection. For each state $s_t$ in a buffer, a weight $w(s_t) \propto g(I(A; L | s_t))$ is assigned, where $g(\cdot)$ is a shaping function that prioritizes low-CMI (low-steerability) states. This allows for sampling start states for new rollouts in regions where steering data is most needed, improving sample efficiency.

2. **SteerGen: Stage-Aware Steering Data Generation:**
 - **Motivation:** Standard multitask datasets lack instruction-contrasting supervision at shared states, limiting steerability. SteerGen synthesizes new trajectories where task instructions change mid-execution.
 - **Stage-Aware Segmentation:** Demonstrations are segmented into semantic stages (e.g., pre-grasp, transport, place) using kinematic and contact cues. This restricts steering to stage-consistent regions, simplifying trajectory synthesis.
 - **Shortest-Path End-State Selection:** To create a steering trajectory from a source task $\tau_i$ at state $s$ to a target task $\tau_j$, a stage-matched end state $s'$ is selected from a $\tau_j$ demonstration. This selection minimizes a shortest-path cost for smooth transitions.
 - **Steering Trajectory Generation:** A transition from $s$ to $s'$ is instantiated using linear interpolation, followed by execution of $\tau_j$ from $s'$. Successful executions are recorded as steering transitions and added to the training set.

3. **Self-Refining Behavioral Cloning (SRBC):**
 - **Motivation:** While SteerGen introduces new task-switching trajectories, the policy may not reliably execute them, leaving the steerable set small. SRBC aims to "sharpen" the policy's response.
 - **Mechanism:** SRBC iteratively deploys the policy to collect rollouts involving task switching. Only successful steering trajectories ($D_{succ}$) are retained. The policy is then finetuned on an augmented dataset $D \leftarrow D \cup D_{succ}$, reinforcing successful steering behaviors through behavioral cloning. This process ensures that the theoretically induced steerability from generated data is reliably realized in practice, maximizing SCR.

**C. Implementation Details:**
The base model used for experiments is $\pi_{0.5}$. CMI calculation involves estimating action entropy terms using Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE) and sampling action chunks. For real-world experiments, the DROID platform is utilized.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, supporting the hypotheses regarding steerability quantification and improvement.

**A. Initial Steerability Evaluation of Multitask Policies (H1 Context)**
An exhaustive evaluation of state-of-the-art VLA policies (π0.5, OpenVLA-OFT, MolmoACT) on the LIBERO-Goal benchmark revealed a common lack of steerability. Despite achieving over 90% single-task success, none of these models exhibited reliable cross-task steering under prompt switches. For example, π0.5 had a steerability score of 0.403, MolmoACT 0.295, and OpenVLA-OFT 0.252, indicating a substantial deficit in in-domain steerability and motivating the need for targeted data generation.

**B. Simulation Experiments (LIBERO-Goal Benchmark)**

- **H1: Stage-aware data augmentation increases overlap between task state distributions, leading to improved in-domain steerability across tasks.**
 - Fine-tuning with ReSteer's augmented data (SteerGen component) resulted in an 8.8% absolute improvement in steerability compared to baseline policies on LIBERO-Goal. This indicates that the generated data successfully expands the policy's ability to respond to language instructions across intermediate execution states.
 - A CAST-style variant also improved steerability, but underperformed ReSteer, suggesting that providing complete steering trajectories, as done in ReSteer, is more effective than augmenting with only initial action snippets.
 - Analysis of task-level steerability improvements showed that ReSteer yielded consistent positive gains across all source tasks, with particularly large gains (up to 0.143 average improvement over baseline) at later timesteps and for contact-rich manipulation tasks, where baseline policies tended to over-commit to the original goal.

- **H2: CMI serves as an effective proxy for steerability, reducing the need for exhaustive rollout-based evaluation.**
 - An empirical correlation of $r = 0.74$ was observed between CMI and the ground-truth steerability score across diverse model architectures and data augmentation strategies. This correlation was even stronger ($>0.9$) within specific model families (e.g., π0.5 variants).
 - This finding validates CMI as a reliable, rollout-free proxy for identifying instruction-blind states where the policy fails to differentiate behavior based on language.

- **H3: CMI-guided state sampling improves the sample efficiency of steering data generation.**
 - When comparing CMI-guided sampling against uniform random sampling for collecting steering trajectories, CMI-guided sampling consistently yielded higher and more consistent improvements in steerability as the dataset scaled.
 - CMI-guided sampling focused data generation on low-CMI states, demonstrating better sample efficiency by concentrating supervision on regions where the policy was least responsive to language.

- **H4: SRBC improves the success rate of steerable behaviors, bridging the gap between induced steerability from dataset overlap and realized steerability at execution time.**
 - The Self-Refining Behavioral Cloning (SRBC) component consistently improved steerability in both baseline (π0.5) and ReSteer-augmented settings. It achieved the highest overall success rate when integrated into the full ReSteer pipeline.
 - This indicates that reinforcing successful steering executions "sharpens" the policy's response to instruction changes, making the theoretically induced steerability more robust and reliable in practice.

**C. Real-World Experiments (DROID Platform)**
Experiments were conducted in a kitchen-style environment using the DROID platform and a π0.5 DROID checkpoint, focusing on four manipulation tasks and three steering scenarios.

- **Baseline Performance:** Standard teleoperation finetuning (π0.5-FT) achieved a high single-task success rate (90%) but showed limited steerability (33%) compared to the zero-shot baseline (7%). This suggests a "specialization pathology" where the policy over-indexes on state information, ignoring mid-execution instruction changes.
- **ReSteer Performance:** ReSteer achieved a 73% steering success rate—a 2.2× improvement over the finetuned baseline—while maintaining high single-task proficiency (85%).
- **Specific Improvements:** The most significant gains were observed in pre-grasp stage transitions, such as switching from "white bowl on shelf" to "close oven" or from "tea bag in drawer" to "white bowl on shelf," where ReSteer achieved 100% success compared to the baseline's 40% and 20%, respectively. This indicates ReSteer's ability to address high-conflict decision points requiring changes in object commitment.
- **Long-Horizon Task Sequences:** ReSteer-enhanced policies demonstrated the capability to execute chained long-horizon task sequences with multiple mid-execution steering commands (e.g., closing oven, then putting a tea bag in a drawer, then putting a bowl in the sink, then on a shelf). This highlights the framework's contribution to interactive and reactive robotic assistance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to the field of robotic control by addressing the issue of task steerability in multitask robot policies. The findings suggest that existing language-conditioned policies often exhibit limited responsiveness to mid-execution instruction changes, which restricts their utility in interactive scenarios.

The primary significance of ReSteer lies in its systematic approach to:
1. **Formalizing and Quantifying Steerability:** By introducing definitions such as directional and bidirectional steerability and the Steerability Coverage Ratio (SCR), the work provides a structured framework for evaluating how well a policy can switch tasks dynamically. The establishment of Conditional Mutual Information (CMI) as a practical, rollout-free proxy for steerability offers an efficient diagnostic tool for identifying instruction-blind regions in a policy's behavior.
2. **Addressing Data Distribution Deficiencies:** The research identifies that traditional single-instruction training datasets contribute to poor steerability. ReSteer directly addresses this by proposing a data-centric framework that synthesizes "steering data" designed to teach policies how to respond to instruction changes at arbitrary states.
3. **Improving Interactive Robot Capabilities:** The ReSteer framework, through its Stage-Aware Steering Data Generation (SteerGen) and Self-Refining Behavioral Cloning (SRBC) components, demonstrates improvements in task steerability in both simulated and real-world environments. The observed 2.2× improvement in steerability in real-world experiments suggests that robots can become more reactive and amenable to human instruction during ongoing tasks. This enhances the potential for more natural and flexible human-robot collaboration, as users can dynamically redirect robot actions without restarting tasks.
4. **Promoting Robust Policy Development:** By providing a method to diagnose and rectify steerability limitations, ReSteer contributes to developing more robust and reliable robot policies. The ability to chain multiple steered tasks signifies progress toward long-horizon, complex manipulation sequences under continuous human supervision.

The potential impact of this work extends to the broader development of large robot policies. It highlights the importance of quantifying and understanding steerability as a critical factor for reliable policy deployment, beyond single-task success metrics. The proposed methods for data collection and policy refinement offer practical strategies for enhancing the interactive capabilities of future robotic systems, motivating further research into data distribution pathologies and data collection strategies for improving robot responsiveness in dynamic environments. The authors' commitment to releasing code, data, and models further supports community engagement and reproducibility.
