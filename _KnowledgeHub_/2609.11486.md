# FreeFlow: A Bias-free Hierarchical Transformer for Optical Flow Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> FreeFlow is a hierarchical transformer for optical flow that drops all flow-specific machinery: no correlation volumes, warping, iterative refinement, or convex upsampling. A Siamese encoder-decoder stacks window, shifted-window, and downsampled global attention over 8x8 patches, with cross-view completion pretraining and a three-layer convolutional head. It reaches state of the art on Sintel, KITTI-2015, and Spring at native 1080p with low memory.

## Problem

- Modern flow pipelines hard-wire correlation volumes, warping, and iterative refinement, constraining expressivity and producing complex architectures that resist scaling and repurposing.
- Generic transformer alternatives still retain flow-specific structure: diffusion methods stay iterative, and recent warping-alone approaches keep iterative feature or image warping.
- Existing high-resolution strategies fail: per-tile inference exchanges nothing across tile borders, while late multi-scale fusion delays global context and leaves seams and inconsistent motion.

## Method

- ==Bias-free hierarchical encoder-decoder==: shared-weight ==Siamese encoders== over 8x8 patches, decoder alternating ==self- and cross-attention== between views, flow read out by a simple three-layer convolutional head.
- ==Dense Feature Fusion== via three attention blocks in fixed order: ==window attention== on a 4x4 grid, half-window ==shifted attention== for cross-tile exchange, and ==global attention== at 2x reduced resolution.
- Training recipe: ==cross-view completion pretraining== at 0.95 masking with mask tokens injected at the encoder, variable-resolution fixed-token-budget finetuning, ==RoPE==, log-token-count attention scaling, ==Mixture-of-Laplace loss==.

## Results

- FreeFlow-L leads Spring with **0.278** EPE and **1.048** Fl at **2.58 GB** for 1080p inference, improving EPE **9%** and Fl **14%** over the strongest warping baseline.
- Ranks first on Sintel Clean **0.68** and Final **1.48**, cutting Clean **14%** versus GeoViT, and reaches **3.23** Fl-all on KITTI-2015, best among two-frame non-stereo methods.
- Ablations: all three attention types plus 0.95 masking beat the 0.9 default by **9.3%** in 1px; substituting vanilla ViT blocks is nearly **6x** slower at comparable accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task-specific inductive biases are an accuracy-per-parameter shortcut, not a requirement; generic attention plus matched pretraining reaches the same ceiling and scales more predictably.
> - Where features fuse matters more than how many scales exist: repeated cross-tile exchange throughout the network beats late decoder-only fusion for global motion coherence.
> - Pretraining hyperparameters and architecture interact, so masking ratio must be tuned jointly with patch size; zero-shot transfer stays data-limited and does not improve monotonically with capacity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Optical flow estimation seeks to predict the apparent two-dimensional motion of every pixel between two images. It is used in video analysis, object tracking, frame interpolation, video restoration, motion compensation, and autonomous driving. The task has traditionally been addressed through a combination of physical assumptions, handcrafted descriptors, and architectural mechanisms designed specifically for correspondence estimation.

Classical methods formulated optical flow as an optimization problem based on assumptions such as brightness constancy and spatial smoothness. Deep learning initially demonstrated that a feed-forward network could directly regress optical flow, as in FlowNet. Subsequent systems, however, reintroduced task-specific structure to address large displacements, occlusions, fine motion boundaries, and the limited receptive fields of convolutional networks. PWC-Net introduced pyramids, feature warping, and local cost volumes. RAFT used an all-pairs correlation volume and recurrent iterative refinement. Later methods added specialized aggregation, uncertainty modeling, temporal memory, and improved upsampling.

Transformer-based methods have increasingly challenged this design pattern. In image classification, depth estimation, segmentation, and three-dimensional vision, relatively general-purpose transformer architectures have learned spatial relationships directly from data. Several recent optical-flow methods have moved in the same direction, but most retain at least one flow-specific component. For example, some use correlation or cost volumes, while others use feature or image warping, iterative updates, or computationally expensive high-resolution tiling.

FreeFlow is positioned as a more complete removal of these specialized mechanisms. It uses no explicit correlation volume, feature warping, iterative refinement, convex upsampling, or dedicated flow decoder. Instead, it treats optical flow as dense two-view prediction using a hierarchical transformer encoder–decoder. The work also addresses a practical issue in this area: high-resolution inference. Processing full-HD images is important for preserving small objects and motion boundaries, but global self-attention is computationally expensive. FreeFlow therefore combines local, cross-window, and reduced-resolution global attention.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The first objective is to determine whether state-of-the-art optical-flow accuracy can be achieved without the architectural assumptions that dominate modern flow systems. The authors specifically target correlation volumes, warping-based alignment, recurrent or iterative refinement, and specialized upsampling. These mechanisms are effective, but they also encode predefined solutions to correspondence estimation. According to the paper, they can limit model flexibility, make scaling more difficult, and require separate engineering for efficient inference.

The second objective is to develop a model suitable for high-resolution images without relying on inference-time tiling that prevents information exchange between tiles. Tiling reduces the memory requirement of transformer processing, but independently processed tiles may not share information until late prediction averaging. This can cause inconsistent estimates when motion spans tile boundaries. A late-fusion design can combine features from multiple scales, but the authors argue that it also delays the interaction between coarse and fine representations.

FreeFlow is motivated by the hypothesis that high-resolution optical flow requires two properties simultaneously. It must preserve local structure, including thin objects and sharp motion boundaries, while also propagating long-range information to resolve large displacements and ambiguous regions. The proposed solution is repeated dense feature interaction throughout the encoder and decoder rather than feature fusion only at the final prediction stage.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

FreeFlow receives two RGB images, \(I_1\) and \(I_2\), of height \(H\) and width \(W\). Each image is divided into non-overlapping \(8 \times 8\) patches. The patches are projected into tokens, producing \(N=(H/8)(W/8)\) tokens with embedding dimension \(D\).

The two token sequences are processed by Siamese encoders with shared weights. This produces feature representations \(F_1\) and \(F_2\). A transformer decoder then processes the two feature maps using alternating self-attention and cross-attention. In the cross-attention operation, tokens from the first image act as queries and tokens from the second image provide keys and values. Repeated cross-view interaction allows the model to infer motion relationships without constructing an explicit correlation volume.

The decoder output is reshaped into a spatial feature map and passed to a lightweight prediction head. The head consists of a \(3 \times 3\) convolution, a \(1 \times 1\) convolution producing 4,096 channels, and a transposed convolution with kernel and stride 8. It directly predicts a full-resolution five-channel output. Two channels represent horizontal and vertical flow, while three channels parameterize uncertainty terms for the mixture-of-Laplace training loss. The predicted flow is multiplied by eight to convert the patch-space output into pixel units.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Attention

Each encoder and decoder layer applies three attention block types in a fixed sequence.

1. **Window attention** partitions the token map into a \(4 \times 4\) grid of 16 non-overlapping windows. Attention is computed independently inside each window, reducing the quadratic cost of full-resolution global attention and allowing detailed local processing.

2. **Shifted-window attention** shifts the window partition by approximately half a window before computing attention. The shifted arrangement allows tokens near the boundaries of the original windows to communicate. This also enables information to pass across the effective processing tiles.

3. **Global attention** first downsamples the token map by a factor of two using a stride-2 convolution. Full attention is then applied at the reduced resolution, followed by transposed-convolution upsampling. This provides long-range interaction at a lower computational cost.

Rotary positional embeddings encode token positions. The authors also scale attention logits by the logarithm of the token count. This resolution-adaptive scaling is intended to improve performance when inference resolution exceeds the resolutions used during training.

#### 3.3. Training Procedure

Training begins with cross-view completion pretraining, following the CroCo framework. One image in a pair is heavily masked, with masked patches replaced by a learned token. The model reconstructs the missing content using the second view. The pretraining data consist of image pairs from ARKitScenes, MegaDepth, and 3DStreetView, totaling approximately 3.7 million samples. Training uses \(224 \times 224\) crops for 346,000 steps.

The authors modify the standard CroCo masking procedure by inserting the learned mask representation at the encoder input. This is necessary for the hierarchical architecture, while the reconstruction objective remains unchanged.

Optical-flow fine-tuning uses TartanAir, FlyingThings3D, Sintel, KITTI-2015, and HD1K. The mixture is weighted toward TartanAir, Sintel, and Things, with smaller contributions from KITTI and HD1K. Training frames are upsampled by a factor of two to better represent high-resolution motion. Rather than using a single fixed crop size, the authors sample variable rectangular crops subject to a fixed token budget. This varies aspect ratios while controlling memory use.

The training process includes a lower-token-count stage, a higher-resolution stage, and benchmark-specific fine-tuning for Sintel, KITTI, and Spring. The model is optimized with AdamW and a mixture-of-Laplace loss, which predicts both flow and uncertainty parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Benchmark Performance

On the Spring benchmark, FreeFlow-L reports a 1-pixel outlier rate of 3.192, EPE of 0.278, Fl of 1.048, and WAUC of 95.235. It obtains the lowest EPE and Fl among the compared methods in the supplied table. Its 1080p inference uses approximately 2.58 GB of memory, with a reported runtime of about 231 milliseconds on an NVIDIA RTX 3090. FreeFlow-M uses 1.66 GB and FreeFlow-S uses 1.02 GB, showing a tradeoff between model size, speed, memory, and accuracy.

FreeFlow-L reports 0.68 EPE on Sintel Clean and 1.48 EPE on Sintel Final. These results are lower than the cited GeoViT and VideoFlow comparisons, although some competing systems use multiple frames while FreeFlow uses two. On KITTI-2015, FreeFlow-L reports an Fl-all score of 3.23, outperforming the listed non-stereo and two-frame baselines.

The qualitative examples indicate that FreeFlow can preserve thin structures and detailed object boundaries while maintaining coherent motion in blurred or ambiguous regions. These observations are consistent with the intended role of dense local–global feature exchange, although qualitative comparisons are based on selected benchmark examples and do not replace quantitative evaluation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Results

The ablation studies support the contribution of the hierarchical attention design. Shifted-window attention consistently improves accuracy, indicating that communication across window boundaries is important. Removing global attention can produce visible motion inconsistencies, even when aggregate validation metrics change only modestly.

The pretraining masking ratio interacts with the architecture. With all three attention types enabled, a 0.95 masking ratio performs better than the CroCo default of 0.90. On the Spring sub-validation split, the reported 1-pixel error improves from 0.688 to 0.624 and EPE from 0.170 to 0.157. The authors attribute this partly to the use of smaller \(8 \times 8\) patches, which reduces the distance between masked and visible regions and therefore requires a higher masking ratio to maintain a sufficiently challenging task.

An \(8 \times 8\) patch model is also compared with a larger \(16 \times 16\) patch variant at approximately matched inference time. The smaller-patch model uses 35 million parameters and 1.02 GB of memory, compared with 279 million parameters and 2.91 GB for the larger-patch variant. Its 1-pixel error is lower, although its EPE is slightly higher, suggesting that small patches offer a favorable detail–efficiency tradeoff.

Scaling from FreeFlow-S to M to L produces consistent benchmark improvements. This regular scaling behavior is a stated advantage of the uniform encoder–decoder structure. Additional experiments show that adding GeoViT-like warping and iterative refinement can further reduce error in some configurations, but increases inference time and reintroduces task-specific structure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s principal significance is architectural. It provides evidence that optical-flow estimation does not necessarily require explicit matching volumes, warping, recurrent updates, or specialized upsampling when sufficient data, pretraining, and transformer capacity are available. This may simplify future systems and make them easier to adapt to related dense correspondence tasks, such as stereo, depth, scene flow, and geometric reconstruction.

The high-resolution design is also relevant for practical deployment. Native 1080p processing with relatively low memory use may be useful in video restoration, robotics, autonomous driving, and frame interpolation, where small structures and motion boundaries affect downstream performance.

However, the “bias-free” characterization should be interpreted narrowly. FreeFlow removes flow-specific inductive biases, but it still uses several architectural assumptions, including patchification, hierarchical windows, shared two-view encoders, positional embeddings, and cross-view completion pretraining. Its performance also depends on extensive pretraining and multi-dataset fine-tuning. Zero-shot results are not uniformly improved by increasing model size, indicating that data coverage remains important. Finally, comparisons across methods involve differences in training data, input resolution, multi-frame access, and post-processing. The work therefore supports the feasibility of a more generic optical-flow architecture, while leaving open questions about data efficiency, transfer to unseen motion domains, and the relative contribution of architecture versus training scale.
