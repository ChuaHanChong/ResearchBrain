# Active Domain Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> Active Domain Randomization (ADR) learns which simulator parameter settings to train on instead of sampling them uniformly. It casts the search over randomization space as reinforcement learning: Stein Variational Policy Gradient particles propose environments, and a discriminator rewards settings whose rollouts differ from a reference environment. Training preferentially on these hard instances yields policies that generalize better, extrapolate outside training ranges, and transfer to real robots with lower variance.

## Problem

- Uniform domain randomization samples every parameter setting with equal probability, so training wastes time on easy environment instances and yields suboptimal, high-variance policies.
- Sample complexity of randomization grows exponentially with the number of randomized parameters, making informative environments increasingly sparse in high-dimensional spaces even for sim-to-sim transfer.
- When transfer fails, practitioners retune randomization ranges by trial-and-error with no diagnostic insight; alternative learned simulators need target-domain rollouts or unstable zero-sum adversarial training.

## Method

- **ADR** reformulates parameter selection as an RL problem: states are randomization configurations, actions are continuous edits to them, and a learned sampling policy replaces uniform sampling.
- The sampling policy is parameterized by ==Stein Variational Policy Gradient==, an ensemble of 10-15 particles whose attractive-repulsive kernel update proposes diverse environment settings in parallel.
- A ==discriminator reward== scores each proposed environment by how well it distinguishes randomized rollouts from reference-environment rollouts, giving a difficulty proxy that adapts as the **DDPG** agent improves.

## Results

- On LunarLander with engine strength randomized over [8, 20], ADR nears the oracle range [8, 11] in generalization, while baseline and uniform randomization fail on low-strength instances.
- In Pusher-3DOF trained only on the easier **0.67-1.0x** parameter box, ADR beat uniform randomization on all but one held-out test region with lower variance; uniform randomization visibly unlearned early gains.
- Zero-shot transfer of **five** seeded policies to real Poppy Ergo Jr robots, **125** evaluations per setting, gave ADR equal or better accuracy with consistently smaller spread across altered torque and friction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Uniform sampling is not neutral: mixing easy and hard variations lets agents learn and unlearn behaviors, so training-distribution shape matters as much as its range.
> - A discriminator against a reference rollout supplies a difficulty signal without any target-domain data, a cheap curriculum proxy wherever an oracle or supervised loss is unavailable.
> - Caution: learned sampling exposes bad randomization ranges by over-proposing unsolvable settings, so inspect the sampling distribution before blaming the policy for failed transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated capabilities in solving complex tasks within simulated environments. However, a significant challenge remains in transferring policies learned in simulation to real-world scenarios, a problem often referred to as the "sim2real gap." Zero-shot domain transfer, where a policy learned in a source domain (simulation) is applied to a target domain (real world) without finetuning, is a particularly active area of research. This approach is valuable when real-world data collection is expensive, difficult, or hazardous.

Domain Randomization (DR) has emerged as a prominent technique for addressing the sim2real gap. In DR, various environment parameters (e.g., friction, mass, visual textures) are randomly varied within predefined ranges during simulation training. The underlying principle is that by exposing the agent to a wide spectrum of environmental variations, the real-world environment will appear as just another instance within this randomized distribution, thereby fostering generalization.

However, existing domain randomization methods, primarily Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR), typically sample these parameters uniformly. Recent studies have indicated that this uniform sampling strategy can lead to several limitations. The sample complexity may increase exponentially with the number of randomization parameters, and policies trained with UDR can exhibit suboptimal performance and high variance upon transfer, even between different simulated environments. Furthermore, when UDR fails, identifying the root cause and adjusting randomization ranges often involves heuristic, trial-and-error processes.

This work contributes to the landscape by proposing an alternative to uniform sampling within DR. It positions itself as an advancement that aims to make domain randomization more efficient and effective by actively identifying and prioritizing the most informative environmental variations for training. It contrasts with approaches that learn parameters of a simulator to match a target domain (e.g., Chebotar et al., Ruiz et al.) by not requiring access to the target domain for reward calculation, thereby aiming to avoid overfitting to a specific target. It also differentiates from adversarial methods like Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (RARL) by removing zero-sum game dynamics, which can impact training stability, and by offering reusable components (sampler and discriminator) for subsequent agent training. The paper builds on concepts from active learning and generalization in DRL, acknowledging that not all training examples (or in this case, environment instances) contribute equally to robust learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of domain randomization by introducing an adaptive sampling strategy, thereby leading to more robust and generalized policies for zero-shot domain transfer, particularly in sim2real contexts.

The detailed motivations driving this objective are:

1. **Address Suboptimality and High Variance in Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR):** The authors hypothesize and empirically demonstrate that uniformly sampling environment parameters, as done in UDR, can lead to policies that are suboptimal in performance and exhibit high variance upon transfer. This suboptimality is attributed to treating all generated environment instances as equally useful, even though some might be less informative or even detrimental to generalization. The motivation is to overcome this limitation.
2. **Improve Training Efficiency by Focusing on Informative Environments:** The research aims to explore whether certain environmental variations within the defined randomization space are more "informative" or "challenging" for an agent's learning process. The hypothesis is that dedicating more training resources to these difficult instances can lead to better overall generalization. This is motivated by the understanding that in high-dimensional randomization spaces, informative environments might be sparse, making uniform sampling inefficient.
3. **Eliminate Heuristic Parameter Range Tuning:** Current UDR practices often involve manual, trial-and-error adjustments of randomization ranges after failed transfers. This process is time-consuming and lacks insight into why specific ranges are effective. The work is motivated to replace this heuristic approach with an algorithmic method that learns an optimal sampling strategy automatically.
4. **Enhance Robustness and Consistency of Transferred Policies:** Particularly for sim2real applications, policies must be robust and consistent. The authors observed that UDR can lead to inconsistent behaviors, potentially due to "catastrophic forgetting" of beneficial behaviors when training on a mix of easy and hard MDP instances. A core motivation is to develop a method that yields more consistent and predictable policy behavior upon transfer to unseen domains.
5. **Provide Interpretability into Agent Deficiencies and Environment Parameters:** The proposed method is motivated to offer insights into which specific regions of the randomization space an agent is struggling with. This interpretability can help diagnose issues before costly real-world deployments and guide the selection of more sensible initial randomization ranges.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to transform domain randomization from a static, uniform sampling scheme into a dynamic, active learning process that continuously adapts to the agent's current capabilities, thereby fostering stronger and more reliable generalization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **Active Domain Randomization (ADR)**, an algorithm designed to learn a parameter sampling strategy by identifying and prioritizing informative environment variations. ADR reformulates domain randomization as an active search problem for challenging environments that maximize utility for the agent's policy.

The core components and approach of ADR are detailed below:

1. **Problem Formulation:** The work frames the search for informative environments as a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem. The sampling strategy for environment parameters is itself learned by an RL agent. This differs from traditional Bayesian Optimization (BO), as the utility function (agent's performance) is non-stationary due to the ongoing training of the agent policy.

2. **SVPG Sampler (μ_φ):**
 - To explore the high-dimensional randomization space efficiently, ADR parameterizes its sampling policy, μ_φ, using **Stein Variational Policy Gradient (SVPG)**.
 - SVPG learns an ensemble of policies, or "particles," each proposing a set of environment parameters (ξ_i). This ensemble approach allows for both exploitation (moving particles towards high-reward regions) and exploration (repulsing similar policies to maintain diversity) within the randomization space.
 - Each particle (representing a sampling strategy) is updated based on a learned reward signal, guiding the ensemble to propose more informative environment configurations.

3. **Discriminator-Based Reward (D_ψ):**
 - ADR learns a discriminator, D_ψ, to generate a reward signal for the SVPG sampler.
 - The discriminator's role is to distinguish between policy rollouts generated in a *randomized environment* (E_i = S(ξ_i)) and rollouts from a *reference environment* (E_ref = S(ξ_ref), typically the default simulation).
 - The reward for the SVPG particle that proposed ξ_i is defined as `r_D = log D_ψ(y|τ_i ~ π(.; E_i))`, where `y` indicates the discriminator's prediction that trajectory `τ_i` came from a randomized environment.
 - Intuitively, the SVPG sampler is rewarded for finding environment configurations where the agent policy behaves *differently* or performs worse compared to the reference environment. This difference signifies that the agent is struggling in those randomized instances, making them "informative" or "challenging." As the agent improves, it becomes harder for the discriminator to differentiate the trajectories, thus shifting the sampler's focus to new challenging regions.

4. **Agent Policy Training (π_θ):**
 - The primary agent policy, π_θ, is trained using its task-specific reward function within the randomized environments (E_i) proposed by the SVPG sampler. This mirrors the training process in traditional DR.
 - The agent policy's updates are independent of the discriminator-based reward; it only uses the environment's inherent reward to learn the task.
 - The paper utilizes Deep Deterministic Policy Gradients (DDPG) for training the agent policy, though the framework is presented as compatible with other on- or off-policy algorithms.

5. **Training Loop Integration (Algorithm 1):**
 - **Initialization:** Agent policy (π_θ), SVPG particles (μ_φ), discriminator (D_ψ), and the reference environment (E_ref) are initialized.
 - **Environment Proposal:** SVPG particles propose multiple sets of environment parameters (ξ_i).
 - **Rollouts:** For each ξ_i, a randomized environment E_i is generated. The agent policy π_θ executes rollouts in both E_i (yielding τ_i) and E_ref (yielding τ_ref).
 - **Discriminator Update:** The discriminator D_ψ is trained to distinguish between the collected randomized (τ_i) and reference (τ_ref) trajectories using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and binary cross-entropy loss.
 - **SVPG Update:** A reward r_D is calculated for each ξ_i based on D_ψ's current prediction. The SVPG particles μ_φ are then updated using this reward and Equation 2 (balancing exploitation and exploration).
 - **Agent Update:** The agent policy π_θ is updated using task-specific rewards from the trajectories τ_i collected in the proposed randomized environments.
 - This cycle continues, allowing the SVPG sampler to adapt its distribution of proposed environments based on the agent's evolving capabilities.

6. **Experimental Setup:**
 - **Environments:** Experiments were conducted on OpenAI Gym environments, including LunarLander-v2 (1 randomization parameter: main engine strength), Pusher-3DOF-v0 (2 parameters: puck damping, puck friction loss), and ErgoReacher-v0 (8 parameters: joint gains and maximum torques).
 - **Sim2Real:** Zero-shot transfer experiments were performed on real Poppy Ergo Jr. robots for ErgoReacher-v0 and ErgoPusher-v0, evaluating performance after training solely in simulation.
 - **Baselines:** Performance was compared against a baseline (default environment dynamics), Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR), and an "Oracle" (hand-picked optimal randomization range for LunarLander-v2).
 - **Evaluation:** Generalization was assessed across varied environment settings (some held-out from training), measuring metrics like performance scores, distance to target, and policy variance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents empirical evidence supporting the efficacy of Active Domain Randomization (ADR) in improving agent generalization and policy robustness compared to Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR).

1. **Suboptimality of Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR):**
 - In the LunarLander-v2 environment, UDR policies demonstrated suboptimal generalization, particularly failing to solve tasks with lower main engine strengths (MES) compared to a baseline trained on a single simulator instance or the Oracle (hand-picked optimal range).
 - UDR also exhibited high variance in learning curves for more complex environments like Pusher3Dof-v0 and ErgoReacher-v0, indicating inconsistent performance throughout training and a tendency for "catastrophic forgetting" of previously learned beneficial behaviors.

2. **Superior Generalization with ADR:**
 - **LunarLander-v2:** ADR achieved generalization levels comparable to, or even exceeding, the "Oracle" setting on challenging MES ranges, significantly outperforming both the baseline and UDR policies.
 - **Adaptive Sampling:** ADR demonstrated an adaptive sampling strategy. Initially, it explored the randomization space broadly, but as the agent improved, it learned to focus on specific, more challenging regions (e.g., higher MES values initially, then lower MES values as those were mastered). This dynamic adaptation contributed to continuous generalization improvement.

3. **Effectiveness in High-Dimensional and Uninterpretable Randomization Spaces:**
 - **Pusher-3DOF-v0 (N_rand = 2):** ADR showed improved robustness to extrapolation, outperforming UDR in most unseen test regions beyond the training distribution. The sampling histogram visually confirmed that ADR prioritized regions corresponding to harder, "sliding puck" dynamics, allowing for better generalization. Policies trained with ADR also displayed lower variance.
 - **ErgoReacher-v0 (N_rand = 8, uninterpretable):** In this environment with complex, interacting parameters, ADR consistently outperformed UDR. It achieved better generalization on held-out, extremely difficult environments (e.g., very low torque/gain values that could lead to catastrophic failure states). ADR policies maintained more consistent performance and lower variance, contrasting with UDR's high-variance and unpredictable behavior.

4. **Enhanced Sim2Real Transfer Robustness and Consistency:**
 - For zero-shot transfer onto real Poppy Ergo Jr. robots (ErgoReacher and ErgoPusher tasks), ADR policies exhibited overall better or similar average performance compared to UDR policies trained under identical conditions.
 - Critically, ADR policies demonstrated significantly lower spread and greater consistency across multiple independent simulation-trained policies and varying real-world environmental conditions (e.g., different torque strengths, puck friction). This consistency is presented as a crucial advantage for safe and reliable deployment on physical hardware.

5. **Interpretability Benefits:**
 - ADR provided insights into problematic randomization ranges. For example, in LunarLander-v2, ADR could identify parameter values that made the task unsolvable, leading to a "lopsided" sampling distribution. This capability allows for pre-transfer analysis and adjustment of parameter ranges, which is not readily available with UDR.

6. **Bootstrapping New Agent Training:**
 - The learned SVPG sampling strategy and discriminator from a trained ADR instance could be successfully reused to train new agents from scratch. This bootstrapped training led to policies with even better generalization than the original ADR-trained agent, albeit with slightly slower initial learning on the default environment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Active Domain Randomization (ADR) algorithm represents a step in addressing the limitations of traditional uniform sampling in domain randomization. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

1. **Advancement in Sim2Real Transfer:** ADR offers a more robust and consistent methodology for transferring policies from simulation to reality. By explicitly seeking out and dedicating more training to challenging environment variations, it mitigates the risk of policies overfitting to specific simulator details or failing catastrophically in unseen real-world conditions. The demonstrated reduction in policy variance in real-robot experiments is particularly impactful, as consistency is critical for safe and predictable robot operation.

2. **Improved Generalization Capabilities:** The work shows that actively learning a sampling strategy leads to policies with superior generalization across a wider range of environmental parameters, including those outside the original training distribution. This is important for deploying autonomous agents in dynamic and unpredictable real-world settings where all possible variations cannot be foreseen or explicitly modeled.

3. **Enhanced Training Efficiency and Resource Allocation:** In high-dimensional randomization spaces, uniformly sampling can be inefficient due to the sparsity of truly informative environments. ADR's ability to focus computational resources on the "most informative" environments could lead to more efficient training processes and potentially reduce the overall data requirements for achieving robust generalization. This efficiency becomes increasingly relevant as DRL tasks grow in complexity.

4. **Increased Interpretability and Diagnostic Tools:** ADR provides a mechanism to understand which aspects of an environment currently pose the greatest difficulty for an agent. The learned sampling distribution and discriminator offer insights into the agent's weaknesses and problematic parameter regions, enabling developers to diagnose issues and refine simulation parameters *before* deploying policies on expensive or sensitive real-world hardware. This pre-transfer analysis capability can streamline the development and deployment pipeline.

5. **Foundation for Future Research in Adaptive Environment Generation:** ADR's formulation of environment sampling as an RL problem, coupled with SVPG and a discriminator-based reward, opens avenues for further research. Future work could explore more sophisticated reward functions for the sampler, integrate with meta-learning techniques for faster adaptation, or investigate methods for automatically determining appropriate randomization ranges. The reusability of the learned sampler and discriminator also suggests possibilities for transfer learning between different agent architectures or tasks.

6. **Broader Applicability beyond Robotics:** While demonstrated in robotics tasks, the principles of active and adaptive environment randomization could extend to other domains in DRL where generalization across varied inputs or conditions is crucial, such as in game AI, autonomous driving, or complex control systems.

In summary, ADR moves beyond heuristic and static approaches to domain randomization, introducing a dynamic, intelligent system that learns to generate more effective training experiences. This contributes to making DRL agents more reliable and capable of deploying successfully in the real world.
