# QuietPaw: Learning Quadrupedal Locomotion with Versatile Noise Preference Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and Google DeepMind developed QuietPaw, a Conditional Noise-Constrained Policy (CNCP) framework that enables quadrupedal robots to continuously adjust locomotion noise levels without requiring retraining. This method, leveraging decomposed representation learning, maintains locomotion performance while achieving adaptive noise reduction and superior cost-performance trade-offs in both simulation and real-world experiments.

## Problem

- Quadrupedal robots, despite high agility, generate significant footstep noise, limiting their deployment in human-centered environments like homes and offices.
- There is an inherent trade-off between maximizing locomotion performance (e.g., speed, agility) and minimizing noise, and fixed noise objectives restrict adaptability.
- Current methods struggle to create a single policy that can generalize across diverse noise preferences and environmental conditions without computationally expensive retraining.

## Method

- The QuietPaw framework introduces a Conditional Noise-Constrained Policy (CNCP) using Safe Reinforcement Learning (RL) that conditions robot behavior dynamically on a desired noise constraint threshold (ϵ).
- It incorporates adjustable noise-reduction embedding decomposition by employing Successor Features (SFs) within critic networks, factorizing state-dependent representations from condition-dependent weight functions.
- The policy is trained using a PPO-Lagrangian algorithm across a range of noise constraints, maintaining separate Lagrange multipliers for each constraint level to balance reward and cost objectives.

## Results

- In simulation, CNCP achieved the lowest average cost violation (0.107 ± 0.018) and tracking error (0.298 ± 0.007 m/s) among conditioned policies on even terrain, outperforming direct conditioning methods.
- The method generated a superior Pareto front, demonstrating higher hypervolume (10.416 × 10^-2) and lower sparsity (1.138 × 10^-2) across target velocities, indicating better cost-performance trade-offs.
- Real-world experiments on a Unitree Go2 robot showed successful, continuous noise reduction as the commanded noise level (ϵ) increased, consistently maintaining lower decibel levels across various speeds and surface types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposed representation learning with Successor Features effectively enables a single policy to learn complex trade-offs and adapt to continuous, dynamic noise constraints without retraining.
> - Achieving a superior Pareto front (optimal trade-off between performance and noise) is possible through a conditional policy, allowing for flexible adjustment of noise levels while preserving locomotion capabilities.
> - Explicitly modeling and controlling noise during locomotion is crucial for broadening the practical, real-world deployment of quadrupedal robots in socially sensitive environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) have significantly enhanced the agility and adaptability of quadrupedal robots, enabling them to navigate complex real-world environments. Existing research has predominantly focused on optimizing various performance metrics such as agility, stability, energy efficiency, and robustness across challenging terrains, as evidenced by works exploring robust walking, jumping, dribbling, pushing, climbing, parkour, and high-speed running,–.

However, the noise generated by foot contacts during locomotion, particularly when robots operate at full capacity, has been largely unaddressed in the context of policy optimization. This oversight presents a barrier to the widespread deployment of quadrupedal robots in human-centered environments such as homes, offices, and hospitals, where excessive noise can be disruptive. The need for quiet operation introduces a trade-off with optimal locomotion performance, as reducing impact forces typically leads to less aggressive and potentially less agile movements.

Prior work has explored acoustic awareness in robotics, including nonverbal sound in human-robot interaction, noise-aware path planning for drones, and methods for achieving quieter operation by minimizing foot-ground collisions. In the domain of learning-based approaches for noise reduction in quadrupedal locomotion, some methods, such as that by Watanabe et al., have employed RL to minimize foot contact velocity for quieter walking. However, these methods typically optimize for a fixed noise objective, which limits their adaptability to varying noise preferences or deployment conditions.

Constrained policy learning, or safe reinforcement learning (Safe RL), provides a framework to maximize reward while satisfying predefined constraints. Existing Safe RL approaches, often employing Lagrangian-based methods or multi-objective optimization, primarily assume a fixed, single constraint level during both training and deployment. While some recent studies have investigated dynamic safety constraints, these efforts have largely been confined to simpler problems in simulation, leaving real-world robotic applications underexplored.

This work addresses these identified gaps by proposing a framework that enables adaptive and versatile noise control for quadrupedal robots. It departs from fixed-objective noise minimization or single-constraint Safe RL by introducing a conditional policy that can dynamically adjust its behavior based on varying noise reduction requirements without requiring retraining. Furthermore, it tackles the challenge of effective policy steering under such conditions by employing decomposed representation learning, which has been shown to improve generalization and Pareto-optimal trade-offs in multi-objective settings. By doing so, this research contributes to the development of more socially aware and practical quadrupedal robots for diverse real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while quadrupedal robots have achieved high levels of agility, their operation often generates significant footstep noise. This noise is undesirable and disruptive in environments shared with humans, such as residential areas, workplaces, and medical facilities. Therefore, a critical challenge for the practical, real-world deployment of these robots is the ability to balance locomotion performance with adaptable noise constraints.

The authors identified three specific challenges in achieving adaptive noise control:

1. **The inherent trade-off between agility and noise minimization:** Policies optimized for maximal task performance (e.g., speed, stability) tend to produce louder movements, whereas policies focused purely on noise reduction may sacrifice agility and efficiency. The objective is to find a solution that maximizes locomotion performance within specified noise limits.
2. **The need for generalization across diverse deployment conditions and noise preferences:** A single, fixed noise constraint policy is insufficient because the same actions can result in different noise levels depending on the terrain (e.g., carpet versus pavement), and different users or applications may have varying noise tolerance thresholds. Retraining a policy for every new noise level or scenario is computationally inefficient and impractical. Thus, a versatile policy that can be flexibly adjusted without retraining is required.
3. **The difficulty of effectively steering policies based on noise requirements:** Simply concatenating the desired noise constraint as an input to the policy often fails to induce meaningful and nuanced behavioral changes, as the policy may struggle to learn the complex trade-offs involved in simultaneously optimizing agility and minimizing noise. An improved method for incorporating and utilizing constraint information is necessary for effective policy adaptation.

To address these motivations and challenges, the key objectives of this work are:

- **To develop a conditional reinforcement learning (RL) framework for noise-constrained locomotion.** This framework is designed to enable continuous adjustment of noise reduction levels without the need for retraining, thereby enhancing adaptability for real-world deployment.
- **To propose a decomposed value estimation approach.** This approach, leveraging Successor Features (SFs) within the critic network, aims to improve Pareto-optimality and ensure that the policy's behavior is effectively steered by varying noise constraints. By disentangling state representations from condition-dependent representations, the method seeks to facilitate generalization across different noise levels.
- **To conduct extensive validation through simulation and real-world experiments.** The objective is to demonstrate that the proposed Conditional Noise-Constrained Policy (CNCP) can flexibly minimize noise while maintaining locomotion performance, effectively advancing the Pareto front (representing optimal trade-offs) across diverse environments and constraint levels.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, termed QuietPaw, introduces a Conditional Noise-Constrained Policy (CNCP) framework that utilizes a constrained learning-based algorithm to enable flexible, noise-aware locomotion.

**A. Noise-Constrained Locomotion with Safe RL**
The research defines locomotion noise primarily as arising from foot-ground impacts, correlating with impact energy, which is proportional to the squared impact velocity and contact force. Since direct noise measurements are not feasible in simulation, a **noise cost function** `c(s, a)` is designed:
`c(s, a) = λ1 * Σ_i exp((F_max - ||F_i||) / σ_F) + λ2 * Σ_i exp(-v^2_impact,i / σ_v)`
Here, `F_i` is the contact force for foot `i`, `F_max` is a maximum force threshold, and `v_impact,i` is the impact velocity. Exponential penalty functions are used to model the non-linear perception of noise, ensuring significant penalties for large forces and velocities. Scaling factors `σ_v = 0.5`, `σ_F = 50`, and `λ1 = λ2 = 0.5` are applied.
The problem is formulated as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP), where the objective is to find an optimal policy `π*` that maximizes the expected reward `J_π_r(s0)` while keeping the expected cumulative cost `J_π_c(s0)` below a predefined noise constraint threshold `ϵ`.

**B. Conditional Policy for Adjustable Noise Reduction**
To overcome the limitation of standard Safe RL, which typically learns policies for a single fixed constraint, CNCP introduces a versatile **conditional policy** `π(· | s, ϵ)`. This policy adapts its behavior dynamically to different noise constraints `ϵ` without requiring retraining for each new constraint level. The policy is conditioned on a distribution of noise thresholds `ϵ ∈ E`, allowing a single policy to generalize across the entire range of desired noise reductions.

**C. Adjustable Noise-reduction Embedding Decomposition**
To address the challenge that simply concatenating `ϵ` to the policy input often fails to induce effective behavioral differences, CNCP incorporates **decomposed representation learning** using Successor Features (SFs), within the critic networks.
In conditional Safe RL, both the reward critic `V_ϕ_r(s, ϵ)` and the cost critic `V_ψ_c(s, ϵ)` are condition-dependent. Instead of directly learning these functions, they are factorized into a state-dependent representation `ξ_f(s)` and a constraint-dependent weight function `w_f(ϵ)`:
`V_θ_f_f(s, ϵ) = ξ_f(s)^⊤ w_f(ϵ)`
Here, `ξ_f(s)` encodes shared state dynamics independent of `ϵ`, while `w_f(ϵ)` modulates this representation to adapt to different noise thresholds. This factorization is intended to improve generalization and efficiency by allowing a single state representation to be adjusted for various constraint levels. The critic networks are optimized to approximate the reward and cost returns.

**D. Policy Training**
The policy is trained across a range of constraint levels `ϵ ∈ E`, uniformly interpolated over ``, with each level assigned to a distinct environment. The training utilizes **PPO-Lagrangian**, an algorithm that employs a Lagrange multiplier `λ` to dynamically regulate cost constraints. Unlike standard Safe RL, CNCP maintains `N` distinct Lagrange multipliers `Λ = {λ_i | i = 1..., N}`, one for each environment `i` corresponding to its `ϵ_i`. Each rollout policy conditioned on `ϵ_i` maintains separate buffers for `λ_i` updates, advantage estimation, and surrogate loss calculations.
The advantage function `A_hat_i_t` is computed incorporating `λ_i` to balance reward and cost advantages. The actor network's surrogate loss is averaged across all `ϵ_i ∈ E`. The Lagrange multipliers `λ_i` are updated using a PID controller.
**Simulation Setup:** The training is conducted in Isaac Gym, using a modified reward function from. The cost function is normalized to ``. The actor network has hidden dimensions ``. The critic network shares the same hidden dimensions but outputs a 64-dimensional feature vector `ξ_f(s)`, while the threshold-conditioned function `w(ϵ)` outputs a 64-dimensional vector. Training involves 4000 parallel environments for 10,000 iterations over approximately 5 hours on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. A two-stage curriculum is employed: first, learning a base locomotion policy for velocity and gait tracking, then gradually shifting focus to noise minimization.
**Baselines:** The method is compared against:
- **Conditioned policies:** CONC (direct concatenation of `ϵ`) and R&C (feature expansion by repeating `ϵ` 10 times).
- **Oracle policies (unconditioned, trained separately for each evaluation condition):** Oracle SafeRL (8 PPO-Lagrangian policies) and Oracle Multi-Objective RL (MORL) (8 PPO policies with varying reward scales for noise).
**Hardware Setup:** Real-world experiments are performed on a Unitree Go2 robot. The policy runs at 50Hz, sending joint position commands tracked by a PD controller. Locomotion noise is recorded using a smartphone microphone, and Sound Pressure Level (SPL) in decibels (dB SPL) is calculated by subtracting environmental noise. Velocity tracking performance is measured using an OptiTrack motion capture system with PrimeX 22 cameras.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CNCP was conducted in both simulation and real-world scenarios, focusing on cost violation, tracking error, Pareto optimality, and adjustable noise reduction.

**A. Reward and Cost Violation in Simulation**
Table I presents a comparison of average normalized cost violation and tracking error across even and rough terrains.
- **Cost Violation:** CNCP achieved the lowest average cost violation among the conditioned policies, with `0.107 ± 0.018` on even terrain and `0.158 ± 0.021` on rough terrain. This was compared to CONC (`0.127 ± 0.027` and `0.169 ± 0.038`) and R&C (`0.135 ± 0.039` and `0.192 ± 0.029`). Oracle SafeRL policies, while achieving lower cost violation (`0.013 ± 0.001` on even, `0.039 ± 0.002` on rough), required significantly more training data (8 times) and resulted in higher tracking errors.
- **Tracking Error:** CNCP also demonstrated the best tracking accuracy among conditioned policies, with `0.298 ± 0.007 m/s` on even terrain and `0.575 ± 0.014 m/s` on rough terrain. Oracle SafeRL showed substantially higher tracking errors (`0.659 ± 0.076 m/s` and `0.874 ± 0.053 m/s`).
These results indicate that CNCP effectively balances noise reduction and velocity tracking, outperforming other conditioned policies in both metrics without the increased data requirements of oracle policies.

**B. Pareto Optimality**
The concept of Pareto dominance and Pareto fronts was used to assess the trade-off between normalized cost and tracking error.
- **Pareto Dominance (Figure 3):** On both even and rough terrains, CNCP generated a superior Pareto front, with solutions concentrated in the lower-left corner (minimizing both cost and tracking error). CNCP's solutions dominated a significant portion of the solutions generated by CONC and R&C, particularly at higher velocities where naive conditioning methods struggled. This suggests that the successor feature decomposition effectively enables adaptive noise-aware control by modulating state representations based on constraint-dependent weight functions.
- **Hypervolume and Sparsity (Table II):** Quantitative measures of Pareto front quality, hypervolume (higher is better) and sparsity (lower is better for uniform distribution), were used.
 - CNCP consistently achieved the highest hypervolume on average (`10.416 × 10^-2`) across most target velocities, indicating superior cost-performance trade-offs compared to CONC (`7.132 × 10^-2`) and R&C (`8.199 × 10^-2`).
 - CNCP also demonstrated the lowest sparsity on average (`1.138 × 10^-2`) and in half of the evaluation cases, remaining competitive in the rest. This indicates a more uniformly distributed set of Pareto-optimal solutions.

**C. Real World Adjustable Noise Reduction**
Real-world experiments on a Unitree Go2 robot provided validation of CNCP's effectiveness.
- **Adaptive Noise Reduction (Figure 4):** CNCP successfully reduced noise levels (measured in decibels) as the commanded noise reduction level (`ϵ`) increased. This adaptability was consistent across different conditions, including 1 m/s on a rubber surface, 1.5 m/s on a rubber surface, and 1 m/s on a wooden surface. As anticipated, higher velocities and wooden surfaces (compared to rubber) resulted in higher baseline noise levels, highlighting the importance of a conditional policy for diverse terrains and speeds.
- **Comparison to Baselines (Figure 5):** When compared to CONC, R&C, and an unconstrained policy at 1.5 m/s on rubber terrain, CNCP achieved a more substantial noise reduction while maintaining relatively low velocity tracking error. All constrained policies were quieter than the unconstrained policy, but CNCP demonstrated a wider range of noise adjustments and a better balance between noise reduction and tracking accuracy.
- **Dynamic Adjustment (Figure 6):** The robot's performance was evaluated as it followed an increasing target velocity on both wooden and rubber surfaces. Higher noise reduction levels consistently led to lower decibel levels, showcasing the policy's ability to adjust noise output in real-time. While stricter noise constraints could cause slight deviations from the target velocity, CNCP generally maintained stable tracking performance.

In summary, the results from both simulation and real-world experiments indicate that CNCP effectively addresses the trade-off between locomotion performance and noise minimization. The method's use of Successor Feature decomposition enables a flexible, continuously adjustable noise reduction policy that generalizes across varied conditions, leading to superior Pareto-optimal solutions and demonstrating practical utility in real robotic systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces the Conditional Noise-Constrained Policy (CNCP), a framework designed to enable adaptive and versatile noise-aware locomotion in quadrupedal robots. The significance of this work lies in its direct address of a critical yet often overlooked aspect of robotic deployment: noise generation in human-centric environments.

The potential impact of CNCP is multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Real-World Deployability:** By providing a mechanism for flexible and continuously adjustable noise control, CNCP directly contributes to making quadrupedal robots more socially acceptable and deployable in sensitive environments such such as hospitals, homes, and offices. This capability could broaden the range of applications for these robots beyond industrial or hazardous settings.
- **Improved Efficiency and Adaptability:** The framework eliminates the need for retraining policies for every new noise preference or environmental condition. This feature significantly reduces the computational overhead and data requirements associated with adapting robot behavior, making the deployment process more efficient and scalable. The ability of a single policy to generalize across diverse noise constraints, facilitated by Successor Feature decomposition, represents a notable advancement in adaptable robotics.
- **Advancement in Safe and Socially Aware Robotics:** CNCP demonstrates a method for achieving a superior Pareto-optimal trade-off between traditional locomotion performance metrics (like agility and tracking accuracy) and social constraints (like noise minimization). This advancement contributes to the broader field of safe reinforcement learning and the development of robots that can operate not only robustly but also considerately in human environments.
- **Generalizable Methodological Contribution:** The decomposed value estimation approach, leveraging Successor Features, offers a generalizable method for integrating diverse and dynamic constraints into reinforcement learning policies. This technique could be applied to other complex, multi-objective robotic control problems where continuous adjustment of trade-offs is required, extending beyond noise control.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work lays a foundation for future exploration into more complex environmental interactions, integrating additional human-centric constraints (e.g., visual intrusiveness, energy efficiency with noise trade-offs), and incorporating human-in-the-loop steering for personalized noise adaptation.

In conclusion, QuietPaw provides a practical and effective solution for a pertinent problem in robotics, demonstrating a pathway towards more versatile, efficient, and socially integrated quadrupedal robot systems.
