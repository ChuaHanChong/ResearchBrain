# FlexiTac: A Low-Cost, Open-Source, Scalable Tactile Sensing Solution for Robotic Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> FlexiTac introduces a low-cost, open-source, and scalable piezoresistive tactile sensing solution for robotic end-effectors, integrating flexible sensor pads with compact readout electronics for real-time, synchronized data. This system costs approximately $30 per unit and simplifies manufacturing, enabling improved repeatability and integration into various robot learning pipelines, including robust real-to-sim-to-real training.

## Problem

- Robotic end-effectors exhibit limited dexterity compared to human capabilities, largely due to a lack of dense tactile feedback.
- Existing high-resolution tactile sensors are often expensive, hindering widespread adoption and scalability across diverse robotic platforms.
- Complex manufacturing processes and integration challenges limit the ease of deployment and robustness of current tactile sensing solutions.

## Method

- **FlexiTac** is an open-source, modular ==plug-in tactile sensing platform== comprising thin flexible ==piezoresistive sensor pads== and a compact multi-channel readout board.
- Utilizes a ==Flexible Printed Circuit (FPC)-based design==, directly integrating electrodes to improve manufacturing throughput, repeatability, and customization.
- Employs a compact readout board with ==Arduino Nano== and multiplexers, streaming synchronized tactile measurements at **100 Hz**.

## Results

- Complete **FlexiTac** unit costs approximately **$30** per unit, with further reductions at higher production volumes.
- Integrates onto diverse end-effectors (rigid and soft grippers); supports configurations including **8x16, 16x16, 32x12, 32x32**.
- Supports visuo-tactile fusion, cross-embodiment skill transfer, and improved real-to-sim-to-real for tasks like nut-and-bolt assembly.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The FPC-based sensor design dramatically enhances manufacturing efficiency and repeatability, reducing fabrication time per pad to approximately five minutes.
> - Incorporating narrow slots between FPC electrodes improves mechanical compliance and localizes deformation, thereby increasing sensor sensitivity.
> - Consistent calibration and normalization between real and simulated tactile signals, using a Kelvin-Voigt model, enable stable reinforcement learning fine-tuning and effective sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressing rapidly, particularly in learning-based manipulation and hardware development; however, robotic end-effectors currently exhibit less dexterity compared to human capabilities. This disparity is often attributed to the limited tactile feedback available to robots. Recent advancements in robotic manipulation have been driven by two primary trends: the development of data-driven learning techniques, such as imitation learning and reinforcement learning fine-tuning, coupled with scalable data collection methodologies, and the increasing integration of tactile sensing. Tactile sensing serves to complement visual information, mitigate occlusions, and enable closed-loop, contact-aware manipulation, providing the necessary "last-millimeter" precision for fine-grained tasks.

While existing robotic tactile sensors do not replicate the full range of signals perceived by biological skin (e.g., vibration, temperature, texture), even partial tactile feedback, such as pressure, has been demonstrated to improve key robotic capabilities. These include enhancing grasp outcome prediction, facilitating grasping and regrasping operations, enabling effective contact-rich insertion tasks, and supporting visuo-tactile policy learning.

The research community has explored a diverse array of tactile sensing technologies. These include vision-based tactile sensors (e.g., GelSight, DIGIT), magnetic tactile sensors (e.g., ReSkin, AnySkin), capacitive tactile arrays, and flexible piezoresistive tactile sensor arrays (e.g., conformal tactile textiles, tactile gloves). Despite this exploration, a single dominant sensing modality has not emerged. Among these options, flexible tactile sensors have gained traction due to their compact form factor, ease of integration onto existing grippers, straightforward fabrication processes, simple mechanical structures, and demonstrated robustness in challenging contact-rich manipulation scenarios.

FlexiTac contributes to this landscape by introducing an open-source, low-cost, and scalable piezoresistive tactile sensing solution specifically designed for robotic end-effectors. It aims to address the challenge of making dense tactile sensing more accessible and easier to deploy, reproduce, and scale across various robot platforms. The work focuses on improving sensor robustness, simplifying manufacturing and assembly procedures, and providing robust simulation support, thereby building upon and advancing the capabilities of flexible piezoresistive tactile sensors for robotic applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to facilitate advanced research in complex, contact-rich manipulation by making dense tactile sensing more readily deployable, reproducible, and scalable across a variety of robotic platforms. To achieve this overarching goal, the authors were motivated by several specific challenges and requirements in current robotic tactile sensing:

- **Cost Reduction:** Existing high-resolution tactile sensors can be expensive, limiting their widespread adoption in research and development. A key motivation was to create a low-cost solution to democratize access to advanced tactile sensing.
- **Open-Source Development:** The lack of standardized, open-source tactile sensing platforms often hinders collaboration and rapid iteration within the research community. An open-source approach was adopted to encourage wider use, customization, and community contributions.
- **Scalability:** Many tactile sensing solutions are difficult to scale in terms of manufacturing volume, integration onto diverse robot morphologies, or adaptation to varying sensor resolutions. The project aimed to deliver a scalable solution for both production and deployment.
- **Improved Robustness:** Tactile sensors must withstand repeated physical contact and potential wear in robotic manipulation tasks. Enhancing sensor robustness was a crucial design consideration to ensure long-term functionality.
- **Simplified Manufacturing and Assembly:** Complex fabrication processes or labor-intensive assembly can limit the production and widespread availability of tactile sensors. A core motivation was to simplify these processes to improve throughput and repeatability.
- **Simulation Support:** Integrating tactile feedback into robot learning often requires effective simulation environments for data generation and policy training. Providing robust simulation support for the tactile sensor was identified as essential for modern learning pipelines.
- **Versatility and Modularity:** Robotic tasks and platforms vary widely, requiring tactile sensors that can be easily adapted to different end-effectors, sizes, and compliance requirements. The design aimed for a modular "plug-in" system with customizable configurations.

The main contributions of this work, as explicitly stated by the authors, are:
- The presentation of FlexiTac, an open-source and low-cost tactile sensing platform that integrates flexible tactile sensor pads, compact readout electronics, and unified software interfaces for real-time, synchronized tactile data acquisition.
- The proposal of a manufacturing-friendly Flexible Printed Circuit (FPC)-based design for piezoresistive tactile sensors, which is shown to improve repeatability, scalability, and ease of customization across different robotic embodiments.
- The demonstration that FlexiTac effectively supports modern tactile learning pipelines, including visuo-tactile learning, cross-embodiment skill transfer, and real-to-sim-to-real training workflows that leverage tactile simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FlexiTac is designed as a modular, "plug-in" tactile sensing solution composed of two primary components: thin, flexible tactile sensor pads and a compact multi-channel readout board. These components collectively stream synchronized tactile measurements for real-time control and large-scale data collection.

**Sensor Principle and Pad Manufacturing:**
FlexiTac sensor pads operate on a piezoresistive principle, wherein mechanical pressure applied to the sensor causes a change in its electrical resistance. These resistance changes are then converted into voltage signals by the readout electronics. The sensing matrix employs force-sensitive films addressed through orthogonal electrodes to obtain spatially organized pressure measurements. The pads are engineered to be thin (total thickness less than 1 mm), lightweight, and conformable, facilitating integration onto various robotic end-effectors, including rigid parallel-jaw grippers (e.g., Robotiq 2F-140, xArm) and compliant or deformable interfaces (e.g., soft, fin-shaped grippers).

A key innovation in FlexiTac V2 is the utilization of Flexible Printed Circuits (FPCs) with integrated, patterned copper openings that directly serve as electrodes. This design replaces earlier manual alignment methods involving conductive threads, substantially improving manufacturing throughput and repeatability. The sensor pad follows a sealed three-layer laminate stack: a piezoresistive film (Velostat) is sandwiched between a top FPC and a bottom FPC, with the entire assembly encapsulated by laminating sheets. The fabrication process is streamlined, typically completed within approximately five minutes per pad. A desktop cutting machine (Silhouette Cameo 5) is employed to rapidly and consistently cut components like the Velostat and laminating sheets, reducing manual labor and enhancing uniformity across batches. The latest design (FlexiTac V2) features a 2 mm pitch between adjacent sensing units.

**Flexible Printed Circuit Design:**
The FPCs are central to the sensor's design, enabling rapid, repeatable manufacturing and providing a substrate for dense electrode patterns. The electrode layout involves two orthogonal FPC layers: the top FPC features vertically extending traces, and the bottom FPC has horizontally extending traces. Each intersection of these traces defines one sensing unit (taxel) in the tactile array. For instance, a 12 × 32 tactile array uses 32 horizontal and 12 vertical electrodes.

To enhance mechanical compliance and sensitivity, narrow slots are incorporated between adjacent electrodes on the FPCs. These slots allow the FPC layers to conform better to curved or soft surfaces and locally concentrate deformation onto the Velostat film, thereby increasing the effective pressure applied under contact. Structural integrity is maintained by incorporating a supporting beam in the middle of the bottom FPC (which has longer electrode traces) to prevent warping, and 0.2 mm polyimide stiffeners are added to the bottom layer of the FPCs for increased reliability.

**Readout Board PCB Design:**
The readout board is responsible for acquiring the tactile signals and streaming them to a host computer via serial communication at a rate of 100 Hz. Sensor pads connect to the board using standard 0.5 mm flexible flat cable (FFC) connectors. The electronic circuit design prioritizes lightweight construction and the use of widely available, low-cost components. An Arduino Nano serves as the microcontroller unit (MCU), chosen for its extensive adoption, community support, and ease of integration.

The board employs a combination of multiplexers and shift registers to enable scalable addressing of high-dimensional sensing matrices with minimal wiring and General Purpose Input/Output (GPIO) usage. For example, a 32 × 16 readout board uses one 16-channel multiplexer for analog signal routing and four 8-bit shift registers for digital addressing. This design is backward-compatible with lower-resolution pads by allowing the configuration of a subset of channels, improving reproducibility and facilitating replication. The compact PCB footprint also allows for integration into space-constrained robotic hands or end-effectors.

**Cost Breakdown and Scalability:**
The FlexiTac system prioritizes low cost and scalability. A complete FlexiTac unit is estimated to cost approximately $30. The primary cost contributors are the sensor pad and the readout board. FPCs for the sensor pad cost $3.55 per pair at a 30-unit order volume, decreasing to $1.36 at 1000 units. The readout board PCB costs $3.10 per board at 30 units and $2.61 at 1000 units, excluding the microcontroller. An off-the-shelf Arduino Nano costs approximately $25. The authors note that further cost reduction could be achieved by integrating the microcontroller directly onto the main PCB, though the current modular design with a separate Arduino balances cost with ease of assembly, debugging, and replication.

**System-Level Integration for Robot Learning:**
FlexiTac is designed to function as a practical tactile interface for robot learning systems. Its thin, flexible pads can be mounted on various end-effectors, and its readout electronics provide synchronized tactile streams that can be integrated with vision, proprioception, and control pipelines. The paper demonstrates three representative capabilities:

1. **Visuo-Tactile Representation and Decision Making:** FlexiTac can be integrated into bimanual manipulation setups on fin-shaped grippers, complemented by multi-view RGB-D cameras. After a one-time calibration of the pad-to-gripper transform, each taxel's 3D location on the end-effector is known. Visual observations are converted into a 3D point cloud, and tactile readings are "lifted" into 3D as points with tactile magnitude as a feature. These are merged into a unified 3D visuo-tactile representation, enabling learning-based decision modules (e.g., diffusion policies) for contact-aware control and correction.

2. **Cross-Embodiment Tactile System:** FlexiTac can be deployed on both portable human data-collection devices and robotic platforms (e.g., xArm). This shared sensing stack ensures consistent tactile signal formats across embodiments, which is crucial for transferring manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robots. The dense array structure provides stable, spatially organized contact measurements for learning contact-dependent skills like grasp stabilization and pressure modulation.

3. **Tactile Simulation with Real-to-Sim-to-Real Pipeline:** FlexiTac's matrix-based pressure sensing is amenable to GPU-parallel tactile simulation. Each pad is modeled as a dense set of taxel contact points. During simulation, a Kelvin–Voigt penalty model (linear spring plus viscous damper) is applied based on querying the signed distance field (SDF) of contacted objects to generate normal force tactile signals. Sim-real alignment is achieved through calibration, where normal stiffness and damping parameters are tuned to match force-response curves between real and simulated pads. Consistent normalization rules are applied to both real and simulated signals, leading to overlapping tactile histograms that improve the stability of downstream reinforcement learning fine-tuning and reduce sim-to-real degradation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents FlexiTac as a functional, low-cost, open-source, and scalable piezoresistive tactile sensing solution for robotic end-effectors.

- **System Realization:** FlexiTac has been successfully developed as a "plug-in" module, demonstrating its design as an integrated system of thin, flexible tactile sensor pads and a compact multi-channel readout board capable of streaming synchronized measurements at 100 Hz.
- **Manufacturing Efficiency and Repeatability:** The FPC-based design, which directly integrates electrode patterns into flexible printed circuits, substantially improved fabrication throughput and repeatability compared to prior methods relying on manual alignment of conductive threads. The use of a desktop cutting machine further enhanced fabrication uniformity and reduced manual labor.
- **Mechanical Performance and Integration:** The sensor pads exhibited a thin (<1 mm), lightweight, and conformable form factor, maintaining mechanical compliance. This allowed for successful deployment on diverse robotic platforms, including both rigid grippers (e.g., Robotiq 2F-140, xArm) and soft, deformable grippers, without requiring significant mechanical redesign. The inclusion of narrow slots between electrodes contributed to improved conformability and enhanced sensitivity by concentrating deformation on the piezoresistive film.
- **Readout Electronics Functionality:** The compact readout board, built with widely available, low-cost components including an Arduino Nano, multiplexers, and shift registers, effectively acquires and streams tactile signals. The design demonstrated backward compatibility with lower-resolution pads, enhancing its versatility.
- **Cost-Effectiveness:** A complete FlexiTac unit was shown to cost approximately $30, affirming its low-cost nature. The component pricing indicates that costs further decrease with higher order volumes, contributing to its scalability.
- **Versatile Deployment:** The system's conformability, modularity, and plug-and-play integration were demonstrated through its successful deployment across multiple robot end-effectors and a wearable collector, spanning various applications such as tabletop manipulation, bimanual coordination, mobile manipulation, and in-the-wild data acquisition (as illustrated in Figure 1). The system supports various pad configurations, including fingertip pads and larger tactile mats with resolutions such as 8x16, 16x16, 32x12, and 32x32.
- **Support for Modern Tactile Learning Pipelines:**
 - **Visuo-Tactile Fusion:** FlexiTac demonstrated its utility in forming a unified 3D visuo-tactile representation by integrating its signals with multi-view RGB-D camera data and robot proprioception. This enabled the development of learning-based decision modules for contact-aware policies (e.g., 3D-ViTac).
 - **Cross-Embodiment Skill Transfer:** The system was deployed on both a portable human data-collection device and an xArm robotic platform, providing a consistent tactile interface across embodiments. This facilitated the transfer of manipulation skills from human demonstrations to robotic execution, including grasp stabilization and pressure modulation.
 - **Real-to-Sim-to-Real Training:** FlexiTac's matrix-based pressure sensing proved suitable for GPU-parallel tactile simulation. Through a calibration process of tuning normal stiffness and damping, and consistent signal normalization, the system achieved close alignment between real and simulated tactile histograms. This alignment measurably improved the stability of downstream reinforcement learning fine-tuning and reduced sim-to-real degradation in complex tasks like nut-and-bolt assembly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FlexiTac carries several significant implications and potential impacts on robotics research and application:

- **Democratization of Tactile Sensing:** By offering a low-cost and open-source platform, FlexiTac substantially lowers the barrier to entry for researchers to integrate dense tactile sensing into their robotic systems. This can enable broader participation in tactile robotics research, including institutions and individual researchers with limited budgets.
- **Acceleration of Robot Learning Research:** The platform's compatibility with advanced robot learning paradigms—such as visuo-tactile fusion, cross-embodiment skill transfer, and real-to-sim-to-real pipelines with tactile simulation—can accelerate progress in contact-rich manipulation. Researchers can more easily leverage tactile data for training robust and adaptable manipulation policies.
- **Enhanced Robot Dexterity and Performance:** The provision of reliable, dense, and synchronized tactile feedback allows robots to achieve higher levels of dexterity. This is crucial for tasks requiring fine-grained precision, delicate object handling, and continuous contact-aware control, complementing visual sensing and addressing "last-millimeter" challenges.
- **Improved Manufacturing Paradigms for Sensors:** The FPC-based design with integrated electrodes represents an advancement in sensor manufacturing. Its improved repeatability, throughput, and ease of customization could serve as a model for developing other types of flexible and scalable sensors, moving away from labor-intensive manual processes.
- **Scalability for Large-Scale Data Collection:** The low unit cost and simplified fabrication make it feasible to equip multiple robot systems or create large arrays of tactile sensors for extensive data collection. This is vital for data-driven learning approaches that require vast datasets for training complex models.
- **Facilitating Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The demonstrated ability to achieve strong alignment between real and simulated tactile signals is a critical enabler for sim-to-real policy transfer. This can allow robots to learn complex contact-rich behaviors in simulation, where data collection is safer and faster, and then transfer those skills effectively to the real world.
- **Community-Driven Innovation:** As an open-source project, FlexiTac encourages a community-driven approach to innovation. Other researchers can freely replicate, modify, and extend the platform, fostering collaborative development and potentially leading to unforeseen applications and improvements.
- **Versatility in Applications:** Its conformability and modularity, allowing deployment on diverse rigid and soft grippers, broaden its potential application scope across various robotic tasks, from industrial assembly to mobile manipulation and human-robot interaction.
