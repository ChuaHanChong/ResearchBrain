# PAKE: Learning Whole-Body Loco-Manipulation with Partial Kinematic Embeddings

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from HKUST, KAIST, and ETH Zurich developed PAKE, a hierarchical control framework leveraging Kinematic Normalizing Flows for high-dimensional redundancy management in legged loco-manipulation. The framework achieved superior end-effector pose and chassis velocity tracking, with mean simulation errors of 0.0402m for position and 0.0609 rad for orientation, and demonstrated robust real-world performance on diverse tasks.

## Problem

- Learning high-precision, coordinated whole-body loco-manipulation for high-DoF robots is challenging due to extremely large search spaces.
- Existing methods struggle to fully exploit the kinematic redundancy inherent in high-DoF systems, often searching for only a single feasible solution.
- Achieving precise 6D end-effector pose tracking typically compromises the robot's legged mobility and dynamic chassis velocity control.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical control== framework, **PAKE**, decouples the problem into partial kinematic reference generation and low-level dynamic execution.
- A ==Kinematic Normalizing Flow (KNF)==, trained on a large-scale kinematic dataset, learns to model the distribution of feasible partial kinematic solutions, enabling exploration of ==kinematic redundancy==.
- A ==high-level controller (HLC)== learns to navigate the KNF's ==latent space== to select optimal redundancy-aware references, while a ==low-level controller (LLC)== is pretrained and then fine-tuned to ensure dynamic execution and physical feasibility.

## Results

- In simulation, PAKE achieved mean end-effector position and orientation errors of **0.0402 m** and **0.0609 rad**, respectively, significantly outperforming state-of-the-art baselines like VBC and Umi-on-Legs.
- The framework expanded the feasible workspace for high-precision tasks, demonstrating a volume of **2.3867 m³** for targets within **0.1m** position and **0.2rad** orientation error, compared to **0.4228 m³** for VBC.
- Real-world experiments on a quadruped robot across **8** diverse loco-manipulation tasks maintained high precision, with mean end-effector position error of **0.0449 m** and orientation error of **0.1437 rad**, demonstrating robust generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing whole-body control into separate stages for redundancy-aware kinematic planning and dynamic execution simplifies the learning process for high-dimensional robotic systems.
> - Utilizing a data-driven Kinematic Normalizing Flow to represent the solution space allows for systematic exploration and exploitation of kinematic redundancy, enhancing coordination and precision.
> - Explicitly leveraging the redundancy through a high-level controller guiding the KNF's latent space enables more sophisticated whole-body motions that balance manipulation precision and locomotion stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has seen significant advancements in mobile manipulation, particularly with manipulators integrated into legged chassis. These systems offer expanded workspaces and enhanced obstacle negotiation capabilities compared to fixed-base manipulators. A key area of interest is whole-body control (WBC) for floating-base platforms, which introduces additional degrees of freedom (DoFs) that can be leveraged to extend manipulation workspace and potentially assist with locomotion tasks.

Traditional approaches to locomotion and manipulation have often relied on model-based control, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), which provides real-time computation but can be limited by model inconsistencies and predefined contact sequences. More recently, model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) has gained prominence, demonstrating success in learning complex locomotion behaviors like velocity tracking, fall recovery, and navigation in challenging terrains. However, applying RL to combined loco-manipulation tasks presents distinct challenges.

Existing methods for loco-manipulation often face difficulties in achieving high-precision control, managing the large search space associated with high-DoF systems, and fully exploiting the inherent redundancy of whole-body robots. Many studies have focused primarily on position tracking, with some attempting simultaneous position and orientation tracking, often resulting in significant errors. Higher precision has been achieved in certain cases, but sometimes at the cost of chassis mobility, by treating the robot as a fixed-base manipulator or by adding wheels to simplify locomotion. A notable limitation of much prior work is the tendency to search for a single feasible solution, thereby overlooking the vast space of redundant solutions that could potentially improve coordination, precision, and task performance.

This work addresses these gaps by proposing a hierarchical framework that explicitly separates redundancy-aware kinematic reference generation from dynamic execution. It introduces a data-driven model, the Kinematic Normalizing Flow (KNF), to leverage kinematic redundancy for improved coordination and precision in high-DoF loco-manipulation systems, aiming to overcome the limitations of single-solution approaches and enhance both end-effector tracking accuracy and chassis mobility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel whole-body control framework that enables high-precision, generalized, and dynamic loco-manipulation for high-DoF robotic systems. The authors identify several specific challenges in current loco-manipulation research that motivate their approach:

1. **High-Dimensional Search Space**: Whole-body systems possess many DoFs, leading to an extremely large action space. This complexity makes it difficult for traditional RL methods to efficiently identify meaningful and coordinated actions for both manipulation and locomotion simultaneously. The consequence is often that existing methods are evaluated in simplified scenarios, such as when the robot is stationary during manipulation.
2. **Coordination Complexity**: Locomotion and manipulation objectives can be distinct and sometimes conflicting. Improper coordination can lead to adverse effects, such as magnified vibrations from the torso compromising end-effector precision, or shifts in the robot's center of mass (CoM) affecting locomotion stability. Achieving harmonious whole-body movement that balances these conflicting demands is a significant challenge.
3. **Exploitation of Redundancy**: High-DoF systems inherently offer redundant solutions for achieving a specific end-effector pose. Many existing approaches, however, tend to search for only a single feasible solution, thereby failing to fully exploit this redundancy. The authors are motivated to leverage this redundancy to enhance tracking precision, improve coordination, and potentially enable more sophisticated downstream tasks like collision avoidance.
4. **Simultaneous High-Precision Tracking and Mobility**: While some methods achieve high-precision pose tracking, they often compromise the robot's mobility by fixing its base or simplifying its locomotion. The objective is to achieve precise 6D end-effector pose tracking *while* maintaining full legged mobility and accurate chassis velocity control, even during dynamic motions.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes a decoupled control framework that simplifies the exploration process by separating kinematic planning, which accounts for redundancy, from dynamic execution, which ensures physical feasibility. This approach aims to provide a practical and powerful solution for accurate and generalized whole-body loco-manipulation in complex robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Partial Kinematic Embeddings (PAKE), introduces a hierarchical control framework comprising three distinct training stages designed to achieve generalized, high-precision, dynamic whole-body loco-manipulation.

#### 3.1. A. Overview of the Framework

The framework decouples the problem into partial reference motion generation and low-level imitation control. The core idea is to first learn a kinematic model that understands the redundancy of the system, then train a high-level controller (HLC) to navigate this redundancy for task objectives, and finally, train a low-level controller (LLC) to execute these motions dynamically and physically feasibly.

#### 3.2. B. Partial Kinematic Embedding (PAKE)

1. **Partial Kinematic Dataset Generation**:
 - **Partial Reference Motion**: The reference motion `q_i` focuses on critical upper-body DoFs: torso roll (ξ), pitch (θ), height (h), and the 6 DoFs of the robotic arm. The torso's x, y, and yaw components are intentionally excluded to preserve the robot's mobility, leaving their control to the low-level policy. All references and end-effector poses are represented in the robot's heading frame.
 - **Data Collection**: A large-scale dataset (`M = {q_i, χ_e^i}`) of 170 million data pairs is generated. Instead of inverse kinematics, the authors utilize parallel forward kinematics (χ_e = χ_e(q)). Joint configurations `q_i` are uniformly sampled within their limits, and the corresponding end-effector poses `χ_e` are computed. This approach allows for rapid dataset generation.

2. **Learning Kinematic Motion Embedding with Kinematic Normalizing Flow (KNF)**:
 - **Normalizing Flows (NF)**: An NF model is trained on the kinematic dataset `M` to represent a distribution of feasible solutions conditioned on the target end-effector pose. NF models are generative models capable of representing multimodal distributions and complex nonlinear relationships. They employ invertible coupling layers to map a simple base distribution (e.g., normal distribution) to a desired target distribution.
 - **KNF Architecture**: The KNF model is based on the framework by Ames et al., incorporating stabilization techniques from Kim et al.. It uses a normal distribution as its base distribution. Coupling layers, structured as per the Glow architecture with three-layer fully connected neural networks (1024 hidden neurons) as coefficient functions, encode both input variables and conditional information. The KNF comprises 12 coupling layers, each with a dimensional width of 12 (exceeding the solution space dimension) to effectively model multimodal target distributions. The KNF efficiently represents the solution space with minimal reconstruction error and allows for efficient sampling during inference.

#### 3.3. C. Pretraining Low-level Imitation Policy (LLC)

The low-level controller policy (`π_llc`) is pretrained to track 3D torso reference motions (roll, pitch, height) and 3D base velocity commands (linear x, y, angular z). This is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). This pretraining is crucial because KNF-generated references are purely kinematic and may violate physical constraints (e.g., joint limits, leg reachability, locomotion stability).

1. **Network Architecture**:
 - The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used with an asymmetric actor-critic architecture.
 - **Actor**: A fully connected network ([256 × 128]) maps proprioceptive observations to action outputs, which are offsets from nominal joint positions for 12 actuators. These offsets are then fed into a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller (k_p = 100, k_d = 1) operating at 500 Hz (sim) / 4000 Hz (real) to track target joint positions. The actor runs at 100 Hz.
 - **Critic**: A fully connected network ([512 × 256 × 128]) estimates value functions based on the current state.
 - Nominal leg joint positions are based on the robot’s stance, while nominal arm positions are sampled from a normal distribution.

2. **Curriculum-Based Command Sampler**:
 - Commands (`c_vel`, `c_base`) are sampled per episode and remain fixed.
 - Torso roll (ξ) and pitch (θ) are uniformly sampled, with ranges progressively increasing up to (±0.26 rad).
 - Torso height (h) is uniformly sampled, with its lower bound gradually decreasing (from 0.3 m to 0.5 m).
 - Velocity commands (`c_vel`: v_x, v_y, w_z) are uniformly sampled, with ranges progressively expanding to maximums: v_x ∈ (-1.0, 2.0) m/s, v_y ∈ (-1.0, 1.0) m/s, w_z ∈ (-1.0, 1.0) rad/s.

3. **Policy Input**:
 - **Actor**: Receives proprioceptive states (`o_p`), including 10-step histories of leg, arm, and torso observations, 3-step histories of previous actions, 10-step histories of end-effector targets, and a 9-dimensional partial reference motion. Gaussian noise is added for realism.
 - **Critic**: Receives privileged state information (`s_p`), which includes noise-free proprioceptive states and additional simulation-derived data (e.g., end-effector states, foot-contact states, linear torso velocity, torso height, roll, pitch, foot-end time-of-flight, stance durations).

4. **Reward Function**:
 - The reward encourages command tracking, end-effector pose tracking, stable contact behavior, and smooth whole-body motion. It combines common locomotion RL terms with specific additions for end-effector and torso posture tracking, and arm motion regularization. Most penalties are scaled by curriculum factors.

5. **Domain Randomization**:
 - To facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, simulation parameters like ground friction, joint friction, and observation noise are randomized at the start of each episode, sampled from normal distributions within predefined ranges.

#### 3.4. D. Training High-level Controller Policy (HLC) and Fine-tuning LLC

In the final stage, the pretrained KNF model is combined with the training of the high-level controller (`π_hlc`). The HLC navigates the KNF's latent space to leverage redundancy, while the LLC is simultaneously fine-tuned to track dynamically changing torso references and convert them into physically consistent full-DoF actuator commands.

1. **Implementation Details**:
 - The HLC uses the same asymmetric actor-critic architecture and network structures as the LLC. Observation and reward designs are similar, promoting collaborative goal achievement.
 - **Action Space**: The HLC outputs actions `a_hlc_t` which are transformed into latent variables `z_t` for the KNF: `z_t = λ_latent_scale × tanh(a_hlc_t)`. The hyperparameter `λ_latent_scale` is set to 0.7 to balance solution diversity and quality. The KNF decodes `z_t` into partial reference motions (torso and arm trajectories).
 - **Rewards**: In addition to the LLC rewards, the HLC receives a smoothness reward based on the difference between consecutive partial reference motions.

2. **Command Sampling**:
 - **End-Effector Trajectory**: Unlike prior methods that interpolate sampled points, end-effector target trajectories are directly sampled using third-order rational Bézier curves. Control point positions are uniformly sampled within the kinematic range (from Section III-B.1), and control point weights are uniformly sampled within. Orientation trajectories are derived via spherical linear interpolation (slerp).
 - **Base Command Sampling**: Torso velocity commands are uniformly sampled from their maximum ranges, with curriculum scaling factors initialized at their maximum (1). Crucially, torso reference motions (`c_base`) are no longer independently sampled but are generated directly by the KNF model, guided by the HLC's latent space exploration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates PAKE through extensive simulation and hardware experiments, addressing its ability for accurate tracking, performance relative to baselines, and redundancy utilization.

#### 4.1. A. Implementation Details

- **Simulation Environment**: RaiSim.
- **Software**: PyTorch.
- **Hardware**: Unitree Z1 manipulator integrated with an in-house quadruped platform (18 actuators).
- **Training Resources**: AMD Ryzen 9 7950X CPU, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 GPU.
- **Training Time**: LLC pretraining: ~14 hours (~9 billion simulation timesteps). HLC training and LLC fine-tuning: ~15 hours (~1 billion simulation timesteps).

#### 4.2. B. Performance Comparison (Simulation)

Experiments compare PAKE against two state-of-the-art methods, VBC and Umi-on-Legs, and an ablation study of PAKE without the High-Level Controller (HLC). Each method tracked 4000 randomly sampled 6D end-effector pose targets and 3D base velocity targets, fixed per episode.

1. **Mean Tracking Performance**:
 - PAKE (HLC-large) achieved the lowest mean tracking errors across all four metrics:
 - Position error: 0.0402 ± 0.0255 m
 - Orientation error: 0.0609 ± 0.0747 rad
 - Planar velocity error: 0.1502 ± 0.0989 m/s
 - Angular velocity error: 0.0563 ± 0.0538 rad/s
 - **VBC** exhibited significantly higher errors, particularly for end-effector position (0.4431 m) and orientation (1.0816 rad).
 - **Umi-on-Legs** showed better end-effector position accuracy (0.2883 m) than VBC but significantly larger orientation errors (1.9722 rad).
 - The **HLC** ablation showed degraded performance compared to PAKE, with higher mean errors in position (0.0515 m) and orientation (0.0884 rad), indicating the HLC's contribution to leveraging redundancy.
 - PAKE's tracking accuracy is competitive with concurrent approaches (0.022 m, 0.041 rad and 0.026 m, 0.064 rad, respectively), which often operate under different assumptions (e.g., wheeled-legged locomotion or stationary base manipulation). PAKE maintains full legged mobility and coordinates locomotion and manipulation simultaneously.

2. **Tracking Error Distribution**:
 - Violin plots (Figure 4) illustrated that PAKE exhibited more centralized error distributions with lower mean errors for end-effector position and orientation, alongside excellent torso velocity tracking.
 - Umi-on-Legs showed lower position errors but clustered high orientation errors.
 - VBC exhibited concentrated lower orientation errors (due to analytical Jacobian) but broadly distributed position errors, suggesting local minima issues. PAKE mitigated both these issues.

3. **Feasible Workspace Comparison**:
 - The feasible workspace was defined as the minimal convex hull of reachable points meeting specific error thresholds (position < 0.1m, position < 0.05m, orientation < 0.2rad, and combined).
 - PAKE demonstrated superior feasible workspace coverage across all stringent conditions (Table III, Figure 5). For instance, for position error < 0.1m and orientation error < 0.2rad, PAKE achieved a workspace volume of 2.3867 m³, significantly higher than VBC (0.4228 m³) and Umi-on-Legs (0.0174 m³). The HLC ablation also showed reduced workspace volume (2.2999 m³), further supporting the HLC's role in exploiting redundancy to expand the high-precision operational space.

#### 4.3. C. Case Study: High Dynamic Tracking with Simultaneous Movement (Simulation)

PAKE was evaluated under highly dynamic conditions, involving rapidly varying chassis velocity commands and dynamic end-effector trajectories (using Lissajous equations for torso velocity). Results (Figure 6) indicated that the robot effectively followed both torso velocity and partial reference motions (height, roll, pitch). End-effector tracking error converged within 5 cm and 5 degrees approximately 2 seconds after new trajectory commands were introduced, affirming the robustness of the PAKE pipeline.

#### 4.4. D. Hardware Experiments (Real-World)

To validate robustness and applicability, PAKE was deployed on a real quadrupedal robot with a manipulator across 24 episodes involving 8 diverse mobile loco-manipulation tasks (e.g., cart_pull, sweep_broom, plug_in, pick-and-place).

- **Aggregate Performance (Table IV)**:
 - Mean end-effector position error: 0.0449 ± 0.0100 m
 - Mean orientation error: 0.1437 ± 0.0164 rad
 - Mean planar base-velocity error: 0.0961 ± 0.0355 m/s
 - Mean angular velocity error: 0.0145 ± 0.0123 rad/s
- These results demonstrate that PAKE maintained stable whole-body behavior and high-precision tracking across varied object interactions, motion patterns, and mobile manipulation demands, suggesting robust task-level control rather than overfitting to specific demonstrations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a control framework that addresses several challenges in whole-body loco-manipulation for high-DoF robotic systems. By decomposing the complex problem into partial reference motion generation and low-level imitation control, the framework simplifies the exploration process and enables more effective control.

The introduction of the Kinematic Normalizing Flow (KNF) represents a significant contribution. The KNF, trained on a large-scale kinematic dataset, allows for the generation of diverse yet feasible partial reference motions. This data-driven approach to modeling kinematic redundancy facilitates the effective selection of solutions from a rich solution space, a capability often overlooked in prior work that focuses on single solutions. The high-level controller's ability to navigate the KNF's latent space explicitly leverages this redundancy, which contributes to improved coordination between locomotion and manipulation and enhanced tracking precision. The low-level controller then ensures that these kinematically generated references are physically feasible and executed accurately.

The experimental results demonstrate that PAKE achieves low mean tracking errors for both end-effector pose and chassis velocities, outperforming several state-of-the-art baselines in simulation. The ablation studies confirm the importance of the high-level controller and the redundancy exploitation provided by the KNF in achieving these performance gains and expanding the feasible workspace. Furthermore, the successful deployment on real-world hardware across a diverse set of mobile manipulation tasks, with consistent high-precision tracking, indicates the practical applicability and robustness of the proposed method.

The framework's ability to maintain omni-directional chassis mobility while simultaneously performing precise 6D end-effector pose tracking positions it as a foundation for advanced robotic applications. This work has the potential to facilitate future research in more complex and diverse downstream robotic tasks that require agile and precise whole-body coordination, such as advanced manufacturing, service robotics in unstructured environments, or disaster response. The rational decomposition of whole-body control into kinematic planning and dynamic execution, coupled with a data-driven approach to redundancy management, offers a versatile paradigm for high-DoF robot control.
