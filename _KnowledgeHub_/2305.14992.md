# Reasoning with Language Model is Planning with World Model

> [!summary] Summary
> The Reasoning via Planning (RAP) framework from UC San Diego and University of Florida integrates Large Language Models (LLMs) with an internal world model and Monte Carlo Tree Search to enable deliberate, multi-step reasoning. This approach allows LLaMA-33B to outperform GPT-4 on plan generation and achieves higher accuracy on math and logical reasoning tasks compared to existing LLM reasoning methods.

## Problem

- Current Large Language Models (LLMs) often lack an explicit internal 'world model' to simulate the consequences of actions or anticipate future states in complex reasoning tasks.
- Existing LLM reasoning paradigms, such as Chain-of-Thought, generate steps autoregressively without explicit feedback mechanisms to evaluate intermediate steps or balance exploration versus exploitation in reasoning paths.
- LLMs struggle with problems requiring deliberate planning and foresight, leading to high error rates in tasks like multi-step plan generation or complex logical deduction.

## Method

- The ==Reasoning via Planning (RAP)== framework repurposes LLMs to serve dual roles: as a reasoning agent to generate actions and as a ==world model== to predict subsequent states.
- ==Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)== is employed as the core planning algorithm to strategically explore the reasoning space, enabling the LLM to balance exploring novel paths and exploiting promising options.
- A flexible ==reward mechanism== is introduced, incorporating action likelihood, state confidence, LLM self-evaluation, and task-specific heuristics, to quantitatively assess and guide the quality of each reasoning step.

## Results

- On Blocksworld plan generation, LLaMA-33B with RAP achieved an average success rate of **64%**, surpassing GPT-4 with Chain-of-Thought by **33%** relative improvement.
- For GSM8K math reasoning, RAP achieved **48.6%** accuracy, with RAP-Aggregation further boosting it to **51.6%**, outperforming strong baselines like Chain-of-Thought with Self-Consistency.
- In PrOntoQA logical reasoning, RAP achieved **94.2%** prediction accuracy and **78.8%** proof accuracy, demonstrating improved performance over Chain-of-Thought and its self-consistency variants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs can be effectively repurposed to function as internal 'world models,' simulating environmental dynamics for planning and predicting state transitions.
> - Integrating principled search algorithms like Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) with LLMs enables more deliberate and robust multi-step reasoning beyond autoregressive generation.
> - Explicitly modeling world states and incorporating diverse reward signals are crucial for enhancing LLM performance on complex planning tasks, allowing for guided exploration and evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical challenge in the field of Large Language Models (LLMs): their limitations in complex, multi-step reasoning and deliberate planning, despite their remarkable emergent capabilities in tasks like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting.

**Current Landscape & Limitations:**
The field has witnessed significant advancements in LLM reasoning through methods like Chain-of-Thought (Wei et al., 2022), Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2022), and Least-to-Most prompting (Zhou et al., 2022). These techniques encourage LLMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps, improving performance on various tasks. However, as the authors highlight, LLMs still struggle with problems that require a human-like internal "world model" – a mental representation of the environment – to simulate actions and their long-term effects. This deficiency prevents LLMs from performing deliberate planning, which involves exploring alternative reasoning paths, anticipating future states and rewards, and iteratively refining steps. Existing LLM reasoning is largely autoregressive, generating a sequence of steps without explicitly modeling underlying world states or considering future consequences.

**Bridging the Gap: World Models and Planning:**
The concept of a "world model" is well-established in other areas of AI, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) and robotics (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018a,b; Hafner et al., 2019, 2020; Schrittwieser et al., 2020). These models learn to predict environmental dynamics, enabling agents to plan more effectively. This paper innovatively bridges this gap by introducing the notion of a world model *within the context of LLM reasoning*.

**Distinguishing Features from Related Work:**
- **LLM-based Search/Planning:** While some concurrent works (e.g., Yao et al., 2023, "Tree of Thoughts") explore heuristic-based search for better reasoning paths, and others like CoRe (Zhu et al., 2022) use MCTS for decoding in specific domains, RAP offers a *more principled and unified framework*. It explicitly formalizes the roles of the LLM as both a reasoning agent and an internal world model, integrating reward mechanisms and advanced planning algorithms like Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).
- **Classical Planning with LLMs:** Other works (e.g., LLM+P by Liu et al., 2023) translate natural language instructions into formal planning languages (like PDDL) and then apply classical planning algorithms. In contrast, RAP operates directly within the LLM's natural language domain, making it applicable to open-domain problems (math, logical reasoning) where formal PDDL descriptions might not be feasible or easily extractable.
- **Self-Correction/Evaluation:** The work builds upon ideas of self-evaluation (Welleck et al., 2022; Shinn et al., 2023), but integrates it as a *reward signal* within a broader planning framework, enabling proactive refinement rather than just post-hoc correction.

In essence, "Reasoning via Planning (RAP)" represents a significant step towards endowing LLMs with more human-like cognitive abilities for planning and deliberate reasoning, moving beyond purely instinctive, autoregressive generation by formalizing the internal simulation of states and outcomes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the Reasoning via Planning (RAP) framework stems from the observed limitations of current Large Language Models (LLMs) in complex reasoning tasks, particularly those requiring deliberate planning and foresight, despite their impressive emergent capabilities.

**Motivation:**
The authors identify three key deficiencies in current LLM reasoning paradigms (e.g., Chain-of-Thought):
1. **Lack of an internal world model:** Unlike humans, who form mental representations to predict the consequences of actions and anticipate future states, LLMs generate reasoning traces instinctively, often failing to simulate the evolving "world state" (e.g., environment status, intermediate variable values in a math problem). This prevents LLMs from performing grounded and coherent inference over multiple steps.
2. **Absence of a reward mechanism:** Current LLM reasoning lacks an intrinsic or explicit mechanism to assess the feasibility or desirability of intermediate reasoning steps. Without feedback, LLMs cannot effectively guide their reasoning towards a desired end state or identify suboptimal paths.
3. **Incapability of balancing exploration vs. exploitation:** Due to the lack of a world model and reward, LLMs struggle to efficiently navigate vast reasoning spaces. They typically generate a single path autoregressively, without systematically exploring alternative routes or exploiting promising sub-solutions.

These limitations make LLMs prone to errors in tasks that are often straightforward for humans, such as generating action plans (e.g., in Blocksworld where GPT-3 achieved only 1% success vs. human 78%) or solving multi-step math and logical problems.

### 3. Key Objectives

To address these fundamental limitations, the paper proposes RAP with the following objectives:
1. **Repurpose LLMs as both a World Model and a Reasoning Agent:** To enable LLMs to explicitly predict state transitions (i.e., how the world changes after an action) and generate appropriate actions, thereby providing the foundation for deliberate planning.
2. **Incorporate a Principled Planning Algorithm:** To leverage established search algorithms, specifically Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), for strategic exploration of the reasoning space, allowing LLMs to balance exploring novel paths and exploiting known high-reward options.
3. **Introduce a Flexible Reward Mechanism:** To design various reward functions (e.g., action likelihood, state confidence, self-evaluation, task-specific heuristics) that can assess the quality of reasoning steps and guide the planning process effectively across diverse problems.
4. **Demonstrate General Applicability and Superiority:** To apply RAP to a wide range of challenging reasoning problems—including plan generation (Blocksworld), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K), and logical inference (PrOntoQA)—and empirically show its significant performance improvements over strong existing LLM reasoning baselines, even surpassing more powerful models like GPT-4 in certain scenarios.

In essence, the goal of RAP is to move LLM reasoning from an "instinctive" to a "conscious planning" paradigm, mirroring human cognitive processes for complex problem-solving.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The Reasoning via Planning (RAP) framework meticulously integrates Large Language Models (LLMs) with core concepts from planning and reinforcement learning, specifically world models, reward functions, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).

**Core Framework Idea:**
RAP views the reasoning process as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), where the LLM acts as both the *reasoning agent* generating actions and the *world model* predicting the resulting states. This dynamic interaction is then guided by MCTS to efficiently explore the vast reasoning space.

#### 4.1. Language Model as World Model (Section 3.1)

The fundamental innovation is repurposing the LLM itself to serve a dual role:
- **State Definition:** A "state" ($s_t$) is defined as a textual representation of the current situation or intermediate variables in a problem. This definition is flexible and problem-specific:
 - **Blocksworld (Plan Generation):** State = configuration of blocks (e.g., "the orange block is on the table").
 - **Math Reasoning (GSM8K):** State = values of intermediate variables (e.g., "the answer is 60").
 - **Logical Reasoning (PrOntoQA):** State = a fact currently being focused on.
- **Action Definition:** An "action" ($a_t$) is a textual instruction or sub-question that transitions the state. Like states, actions are problem-specific:
 - **Blocksworld:** A movement command (e.g., "pickup the orange block").
 - **Math Reasoning:** A sub-question (e.g., "How old is Mohamed?").
 - **Logical Reasoning:** Selecting a logical rule for deduction.
- **State Transition:** Given a current state $s_t$ and a chosen action $a_t$, the LLM (as the world model) is prompted to generate the next state $s_{t+1}$. This explicit modeling of state transitions (s_t, a_t, s_t+1) is a key differentiator from Chain-of-Thought, which only produces action sequences (a_0, a_1...). This internal simulation allows the LLM to "look ahead" without real-world interaction.

#### 4.2. Reward Design (Section 3.2)

RAP introduces a mechanism to quantitatively assess the quality of each reasoning step (action $a_t$ taken in state $s_t$) using a reward function $r(s_t, a_t)$. These rewards guide the MCTS search:
- **Likelihood of the action:** The log-probability of the LLM generating a specific action, reflecting its "instinctive" preference. Useful as a prior.
- **Confidence of the state:** For problems where state prediction involves answering, multiple samples are drawn from the world model, and the proportion of the most frequent answer (e.g., for math problems) serves as a confidence score.
- **Self-evaluation by the LLM:** The LLM is prompted to critically evaluate its own reasoning step (e.g., "Is this reasoning step correct?"), and the probability of "Yes" is used as a reward. This allows for internal error recognition.
- **Task-specific heuristics:** Domain-specific knowledge can be incorporated (e.g., in Blocksworld, comparing the new state to the goal for progress).
Rewards can be combined (e.g., weighted geometric mean). For some tasks, the Q-value (expected future reward) is calculated as the maximum of average rewards in future steps, rather than a sum, to account for path length.

#### 4.3. Planning with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) (Section 3.3)

MCTS is employed as the core planning algorithm due to its effectiveness in balancing exploration and exploitation in large search spaces. Each node in the MCTS tree represents a state, and each edge represents an action. An MCTS iteration involves four phases:
1. **Selection:** Starting from the root (initial state), the algorithm iteratively selects child nodes (actions) that balance high Q-values (exploitation) with high uncertainty (exploration), using the UCT (Upper Confidence bounds applied to Trees) formula. This phase navigates through the existing tree.
2. **Expansion:** When a leaf node is reached, the LLM (as agent) is prompted to sample a fixed number ($d$) of possible actions. For each action, the LLM (as world model) predicts the corresponding next state, expanding the tree with new child nodes.
3. **Simulation (Rollout):** From each newly expanded node, a "rollout" is performed. The LLM (using a lightweight policy based on local rewards) generates a sequence of actions and predicts states until a terminal state is reached. This estimates the long-term reward from that node.
4. **Back-propagation:** The total reward collected during the simulation phase is propagated back up the tree, updating the Q-values and visit counts for all state-action pairs along the selected path. This updates the LLM's "beliefs" about the value of different reasoning steps.

The MCTS process continues for a predetermined number of iterations. The final reasoning trace is then selected, typically as the path that yielded the highest cumulative reward during any iteration. Adaptations for LLMs include sampling a fixed number of actions (due to open-domain nature) and using lightweight rewards during simulation for efficiency.

#### 4.4. RAP-Aggregation (Section 3.4)

For tasks where only a single final answer is required (e.g., math reasoning), RAP can generate multiple reasoning traces and their corresponding answers from different MCTS iterations. These answers are then aggregated (e.g., via majority voting) to produce a more robust final result, similar to the Self-Consistency technique but applied to the diverse paths generated by MCTS.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that the Reasoning via Planning (RAP) framework consistently and substantially outperforms strong existing LLM reasoning baselines across diverse and challenging problem domains.

#### 5.1. Plan Generation (Blocksworld)

- **Task Setup:** LLMs are asked to generate sequences of actions to rearrange blocks into target configurations. States are block configurations, actions are movement instructions. Rewards combine action likelihood and task-specific progress towards the goal.
- **Key Results (LLaMA-33B vs. GPT-4):**
 - Chain-of-Thought (CoT) with LLaMA-33B performs poorly, failing almost completely on 4-step and 6-step problems (e.g., 0% success on 6-step).
 - RAP with LLaMA-33B achieves significant improvements, with an average success rate of 64% across 2/4/6-step problems. It nearly solves all 2-step and 4-step problems (100% and 88% respectively).
 - **Crucially, LLaMA-33B with RAP surpasses GPT-4 with CoT** (which achieved 40-63% on these tasks) by a remarkable 33% relative improvement in the plan generation setting. This highlights that algorithmic improvements can outweigh sheer model scale for specific reasoning tasks.
- **Extended Blocksworld (Llama-2 70B):** On the full, more complex Blocksworld dataset, RAP consistently outperforms CoT with Llama-2 70B in both "Easy" (using demonstrations with similar step counts) and "Hard" (random demonstrations) settings. For problems requiring 6+ steps, CoT's success rate severely drops (e.g., 0% on 12-step in Hard), whereas RAP maintains a relatively high success rate (e.g., 9% on 12-step in Hard, 32% in Easy), further validating its ability to handle complexity.
- **Case Study Insights:** RAP's superiority stems from its ability to maintain an explicit world state (preventing illegal actions), backtrack and explore alternatives when initial paths are unpromising, and focus computational budget on harder initial decisions by treating subsequent states as new, simpler problems.

#### 5.2. Math Reasoning (GSM8K)

- **Task Setup:** Solving grade school math word problems. States are intermediate variable values, actions are sub-questions. Rewards are a weighted geometric mean of self-evaluation (helpfulness of sub-question) and confidence of the state (consistency of the sub-answer). RAP-Aggregation is applied here.
- **Key Results:**
 - RAP (48.6%) outperforms strong baselines like Chain-of-Thought with Self-Consistency (46.8%) and Least-to-Most with Self-Consistency (42.5%).
 - RAP-Aggregation further boosts accuracy to 51.6%.
 - The performance gain is consistent across varying numbers of MCTS iterations/samples, demonstrating RAP's efficiency in finding reliable reasoning paths.

#### 5.3. Logical Reasoning (PrOntoQA)

- **Task Setup:** Verifying hypotheses based on facts and logical rules, requiring both a final true/false answer and a detailed proof. States are facts, actions are rule selections. Reward is based on LLM self-evaluation.
- **Key Results:**
 - RAP achieves 94.2% prediction accuracy and 78.8% proof accuracy.
 - It significantly surpasses CoT (64.8% proof accuracy) and CoT + Self-Consistency (89.8% prediction accuracy).
- **Insights:** RAP's planning allows it to recognize dead ends and backtrack, while the self-evaluation reward helps in avoiding incorrect deduction steps, leading to more robust logical proofs.

#### 5.4. Reward Choice Analysis

Ablation studies confirm that combining multiple reward types generally improves performance, though the optimal combination is task-dependent. For plan generation, action likelihood and task-specific rewards are crucial. For math reasoning, self-evaluation and state confidence rewards are particularly effective, showing that self-evaluation can be a powerful and efficient prior for guiding exploration, especially when reasoning steps are more complex and less structured.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that explicitly modeling world states, incorporating principled planning (MCTS), and using diverse reward signals enables LLMs to achieve significantly higher reasoning capabilities than traditional autoregressive methods, even allowing smaller models to surpass larger ones on specific, complex tasks.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Reasoning via Planning (RAP)" framework represents a significant conceptual and practical advancement in the field of artificial intelligence, particularly concerning the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs).

#### 6.1. Conceptual Breakthrough and Paradigm Shift

The most profound contribution of RAP is its novel paradigm for LLM reasoning. It moves beyond the prevalent autoregressive generation by formally endowing LLMs with an "internal world model" and integrating principled planning algorithms. This bridges two historically distinct areas of AI: large language models (focused on pattern recognition and generation) and classical AI planning (focused on symbolic reasoning and state-space search). By repurposing the LLM itself as both the reasoning agent and the world model, RAP demonstrates that LLMs can go beyond mere pattern matching to actively simulate, explore, and refine complex multi-step reasoning processes, akin to human conscious planning.

#### 6.2. Substantial Performance Gains

The empirical results are striking. RAP consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance across diverse and challenging reasoning tasks (plan generation, math, logical inference). The most compelling evidence is RAP's ability to enable a LLaMA-33B model to outperform GPT-4 (a significantly larger and more powerful model) on a challenging plan generation benchmark. This underscores a crucial insight: algorithmic and architectural innovations can unlock reasoning capabilities that are not simply achieved by scaling up model size. This finding is highly significant for the development of more efficient and capable AI systems.

#### 6.3. Enhanced Generalizability and Flexibility

RAP's framework is shown to be flexible, allowing problem-specific definitions of states, actions, and rewards. This adaptability makes it a general framework applicable to a wide array of open-domain reasoning problems, unlike some prior planning-with-LLMs approaches that are restricted to formal or code-based domains. The ability to customize rewards and interpret LLMs as world models for various contexts broadens the scope of LLM applications.

#### 6.4. Towards More Robust and Interpretable Reasoning

By explicitly maintaining world states and building a reasoning tree, RAP potentially offers more robust and transparent reasoning processes compared to opaque chain-of-thought methods. The tree structure allows for backtracking and exploring alternative paths, making the reasoning process less brittle to initial errors. While not explicitly discussed as an interpretability tool in the paper, the structured nature of RAP's reasoning could lend itself to better analysis and debugging of LLM thought processes.

#### 6.5. Future Research Directions

RAP opens several exciting avenues for future research:
- **Fine-tuning World Models:** The paper utilizes frozen LLMs; future work could explore fine-tuning LLMs specifically to enhance their performance as world models, potentially leading to even greater efficiency and accuracy.
- **Integration with External Tools:** Combining RAP with external tools (e.g., calculators, knowledge bases, code interpreters) could further augment LLMs' ability to solve highly complex real-world problems.
- **Beyond MCTS:** While MCTS is powerful, exploring other advanced planning and search algorithms within the RAP framework could yield further improvements.
- **Cognitive Science Inspiration:** The success of RAP reinforces the idea that explicit modeling of internal states and deliberative planning, inspired by human cognition, can significantly boost AI capabilities.

#### 6.6. Ethical Considerations

The authors briefly touch upon ethical considerations, noting that the immediate applications (plan generation, math, logical reasoning) pose no significant concerns. However, they prudently caution that as such reasoning capabilities become more sophisticated and applied to real-world scenarios, careful consideration of safety and potential misuse will be paramount.

In conclusion, RAP represents a pivotal contribution to the quest for more intelligent and versatile AI, demonstrating that by integrating LLMs with classical AI planning concepts, we can unlock a new level of reasoning and problem-solving abilities, moving AI closer to human-like cognitive processes.
