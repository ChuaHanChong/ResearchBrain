# A Novel Plug-in Module for Fine-Grained Visual Classification

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from National Taiwan Normal University developed a Plug-in Module (PIM) for fine-grained visual classification, which integrates with diverse backbone networks to identify and fuse discriminative pixel-level features. The PIM achieved 92.8% Top-1 accuracy on both CUB200-2011 and NABirds datasets, outperforming prior state-of-the-art methods and consistently enhancing performance across CNN and Transformer architectures.

## Problem

- Fine-grained visual classification (FGVC) is challenging due to high similarity between subcategories and high intra-class variation, which standard classification models struggle with.
- Existing FGVC methods often rely on complex multi-stage architectures that are not end-to-end trainable or are limited to specific backbone types (CNNs vs. Transformers).
- Current approaches frequently produce broad prediction regions, failing to precisely identify and leverage the subtle, pixel-level discriminative features crucial for fine-grained distinctions, while also often requiring costly, expert-level annotations.

## Method

- A novel **Plug-in Module (PIM)** is proposed, designed to be backbone-agnostic and integrate seamlessly with both CNN-based (ResNet, EfficientNet) and Transformer-based (ViT, Swin-T) architectures.
- The **PIM** includes a ==Weakly Supervised Selector (WSS)== that classifies individual feature points and selects a predefined number of top-confidence "helpful" features, while encouraging "less helpful" features to have flat probability distributions.
- A ==Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)==-based ==Combiner== fuses the selected discriminative features from different blocks and scales (enhanced by an ==FPN==) into a final classification, enabling end-to-end training using only image-level annotations.

## Results

- The PIM achieved Top-1 accuracy of **92.8%** on the CUB200-2011 dataset and **92.8%** on the NABirds dataset, both with a Swin-T backbone, surpassing previous state-of-the-art methods.
- The module consistently improved accuracy across all tested mainstream backbones: **+1.3%** for ResNet-50 (**88.2%** to **89.5%**), **+1.7%** for EfficientNet-B7 (**88.2%** to **89.9%**), **+0.9%** for ViT (**90.1%** to **91.0%**), and **+0.9%** for Swin-T (**91.9%** to **92.8%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed the GCN Combiner's superiority over MLPs (**92.77%** vs **92.06%**), and visualizations showed the PIM-enhanced model's ability to focus more precisely on key discriminative object regions while filtering out background noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pixel-level classification of feature points, coupled with a weakly supervised selection mechanism, can effectively identify highly discriminative regions without explicit bounding box annotations.
> - Graph Convolutional Networks are effective in fusing selected multi-scale, discriminative features, outperforming simpler concatenation approaches like MLPs in fine-grained visual classification.
> - The proposed approach successfully pushes background or less discriminative features towards a uniform probability distribution, allowing the model to focus on subtle object details and mitigate irrelevant information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual classification is broadly categorized into coarse-grained and fine-grained tasks. Coarse-grained classification involves distinguishing categories with significant dissimilarity, such as cats from dogs. In contrast, fine-grained visual classification (FGVC) focuses on differentiating highly similar subcategories, such as specific bird species, dog breeds, or vehicle models. This inherent similarity, coupled with high intra-class variations (e.g., different angles or lighting for the same bird species), presents a significant challenge for standard classification models like ResNet, EfficientNet, and Vision Transformer (ViT), which perform effectively in coarse-grained tasks. Furthermore, FGVC often necessitates expensive, expert-level annotations, in contrast to coarser classifications that rely on more readily available image-level labels.

The current research landscape for FGVC largely revolves around strategies to identify and leverage highly discriminative local regions within images, without requiring costly bounding box or part annotations. Existing approaches generally fall into three categories:
1. **Region Proposal Network (RPN)-based methods:** These approaches, such as NTS-Net, FDL, and StackedLSTM, employ RPNs to locate salient regions. However, they often result in two- or multi-stage architectures that are not end-to-end trainable.
2. **Attention mechanism-based methods (for CNNs):** Techniques like CAL, MA-CNN, MAMC, API-Net, and WS-DAN enhance feature maps by strengthening discriminative areas through attention mechanisms. These are primarily built upon Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).
3. **Self-attention map-based methods (for Transformers):** With the advent of Vision Transformers, methods such as TransFG and FFVT utilize the attention maps generated by the self-attention mechanism to identify discriminative regions. While these offer end-to-end training and avoid feedback-based architectures, their applicability is often limited to Transformer-based models, restricting their generalizability to CNNs.

A common limitation across many of these existing methods is the reliance on multi-stage processes (e.g., initial region localization followed by a separate classification stage on cropped or enhanced regions), which complicates end-to-end training. Additionally, localization methods tend to generate broad prediction regions, potentially missing subtle local features crucial for fine-grained distinctions. The paper highlights that weakly supervised object detection (WSOD) offers a relevant parallel, demonstrating that feature maps can contain rich semantic and location information without explicit region annotations. WSOD methods (e.g., WSDDN, OICR, WCCN) leverage feature map responses to infer object locations, often through multi-stage architectures. However, FGVC's goal is to find *critical discriminative features* for recognition, not necessarily complete object detection.

This work positions itself within this context by aiming to develop a novel, end-to-end trainable plug-in module that can seamlessly integrate with diverse backbone architectures (both CNN-based and Transformer-based). The module is designed to provide pixel-level feature discrimination and fusion, addressing the limitations of existing multi-stage or architecture-specific approaches and enhancing the focus on subtle, discriminative local features.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the accuracy of fine-grained visual classification (FGVC) by developing a robust and versatile plug-in module. The authors are motivated by several challenges inherent in current FGVC methodologies:

1. **Limitations of Existing Architectures:** Many state-of-the-art FGVC methods, particularly those based on CNNs, employ multi-stage architectures involving region proposal, cropping, resizing, and subsequent re-input to the network. This complexity hinders end-to-end training, making the overall training process less efficient and more difficult to optimize. Transformer-based methods mitigate this by leveraging attention maps for region selection, enabling end-to-end training. However, these methods often lack scalability to CNN backbones, limiting their general applicability.
2. **Focusing on Discriminative Regions:** Fine-grained classification demands the identification of subtle, highly discriminative features rather than general object presence. Existing localization methods frequently produce large prediction regions, which may not adequately emphasize these crucial fine-grained details. The authors seek a mechanism that can pinpoint and exploit pixel-level discriminative features more precisely.
3. **Reducing Annotation Costs:** Similar to weakly supervised object detection, FGVC often benefits from methods that can automatically identify important regions using only image-level annotations, thereby reducing the reliance on costly, expert-labeled bounding boxes or part annotations. The proposed module aims to achieve this by inferring discriminative importance from classification predictions.
4. **Generalizability Across Backbones:** The research aims to create a plug-in module that is compatible with a wide range of popular backbone networks, including both established CNN architectures (e.g., ResNet, EfficientNet) and modern Transformer-based models (e.g., ViT, Swin-T). This broad compatibility would allow for flexible integration and leverage the strengths of various pre-trained models.
5. **Mitigating Background Noise:** The presence of irrelevant background information can obscure discriminative object features, particularly when intra-class variation is high. The motivation includes a strategy to effectively filter out background noise and focus the model's attention on the object's salient parts. This is based on the observation that background patches tend to have flat prediction probability distributions, while object patches exhibit sharper distributions.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a novel plug-in module designed with three core operations: division, competition, and combination. This module aims to automatically segment feature maps into object and background candidate regions based on predicted class scores, fuse the most discriminative object features, and integrate them into a single, end-to-end trainable framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a novel Plug-in Module (PIM) designed for integration into various mainstream backbone networks (e.g., ResNet, EfficientNet, ViT, Swin-T) to enhance fine-grained visual classification. The core idea is to treat each pixel or patch feature on the feature map as an independent entity, classify it, and use its prediction confidence to identify and fuse discriminative regions.

**A. Module Design:**

The PIM's design comprises three main components: a Weakly Supervised Selector (WSS), the integration of Feature Pyramid Network (FPN), and a Combiner.

1. **Weakly Supervised Selector (WSS):**
 - **Feature Point Classification:** The WSS operates on the feature maps output by different blocks (`f_i`) of the backbone network. Each feature point (of dimension `R^C`) within a feature map is passed through a linear classifier to predict class probabilities.
 - **Discriminative Region Selection:** After obtaining class prediction probabilities for each feature point via softmax, the WSS selects a predefined number of feature points that exhibit the highest confidence scores (i.e., highest maximum softmax probability). These are considered "helpful" features for classification. The remaining features are deemed "less helpful" or background-related.
 - **Implementation:** Algorithm 1 outlines this selection process, where `num_selects` top-scoring feature points are chosen from the input feature map `x` (reshaped to `[B, H*W, C]`).

2. **Feature Pyramid Network (FPN):**
 - To improve the detection of key features at various scales, an FPN is integrated into the backbone network. This allows for the mixing of spatial features from different scales, enriching the local representation and potentially enhancing the accuracy of fine-grained recognition, similar to its use in object detection tasks.

3. **Combiner:**
 - The Combiner's role is to fuse the selected discriminative feature points obtained from different blocks and scales of the backbone network.
 - **Architectural Exploration:** Two architectures were considered:
 - **Fully Connected Layer (MLP):** Selected features from all blocks are concatenated along the feature dimension (`R^(N*C)`) and passed through an MLP to generate a final prediction.
 - **Graph Convolutional Network (GCN):** This was the chosen architecture. It models the selected feature points as nodes in a graph, where nodes represent features at different spatial locations and scales. The GCN learns the relationships between these nodes. Following graph convolution, pooling layers aggregate features into "super nodes," whose features are then averaged and fed into a linear classifier for final prediction. The GCN approach was favored for its ability to integrate features more efficiently without corrupting backbone outputs.

**B. Forward and Backward Propagation (Loss Function):**

The training is end-to-end and relies solely on image-level annotations. The overall loss function (`L`) is a weighted sum of four components, designed to achieve specific training goals:

1. **Block-level Classification Loss (`L_b`):**
 - For each block `l`, the predictions of all feature points (`f_l,s`) are averaged to obtain a block-wise prediction `z_l`.
 - Cross-Entropy loss is applied to `z_l` to ensure that each block's feature map generally possesses classification capabilities. `L_b = - Σ_l log(z_cls_l)`.

2. **Selected Category Loss (`L_s`):**
 - This loss targets the sum of features from regions identified as "selected" (`h_l`, where `Mask` indicates selected areas).
 - Cross-Entropy loss is applied to `h_l`. `L_s = - Σ_l Σ_i log(h_i_l)`.
 - *Note:* In the actual training, `L_s` was set to 0 and not explicitly used, as its function is implicitly covered by the Combiner's prediction loss (`L_c`).

3. **Flattening Loss for Dropped Areas (`L_n`):**
 - This loss encourages features from regions identified as "dropped" (background or less discriminative, `n_l`) to have a flat probability distribution, indicating that they are not strongly associated with any particular class.
 - `L_n = - Σ_l Σ_i log(1 - n_i_l)`. This effectively pushes their individual class probabilities towards a uniform distribution.

4. **Combiner Category Prediction Loss (`L_c`):**
 - The final prediction from the Combiner (`f_comb`), after averaging features on super nodes and passing through a linear classifier, is subject to Cross-Entropy loss. This is the primary classification loss for the final output.

The total loss is calculated as: `L = λ_b L_b + λ_s L_s + λ_n L_n + λ_c L_c`.
During training, the weights used were `λ_b=1, λ_s=0, λ_n=5, λ_c=1`.

**C. Implementation Details:**

- **Datasets:** CUB200-2011 (200 bird categories) and NABirds (555 bird species), using only image-level annotations.
- **Backbones:** ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B7, ViT, Swin-T.
- **Input Image Sizes:** 448x448 for ResNet, EfficientNet, ViT; 384x384 for Swin-T.
- **Data Augmentation:** Scaling (to 510x510 or 600x600), Random Crop, Random HorizontalFlip, Random GaussianBlur for training; Center Crop for testing.
- **Training Parameters:** Learning rate 0.0005 with cosine decay, weight decay 0.0005, SGD optimizer, batch size 8, 50 epochs.
- **Hardware:** Single Nvidia GeForce RTX 3090. Training times: 3 hours for CUB200-2011, 15 hours for NABirds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed Plug-in Module (PIM) in enhancing fine-grained visual classification across various backbones and achieving competitive performance against state-of-the-art methods.

**A. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Approaches:**

- **CUB200-2011 Dataset:** The PIM, when integrated with a Swin-T backbone pre-trained on ImageNet22K, achieved a Top-1 accuracy of 92.8%. This performance surpassed previous state-of-the-art methods, including TransFG (91.7%), FFVT (91.6%), and CAP (91.8%), by an margin of 1.0% compared to the previously best reported result.
- **NABirds Dataset:** On the NABirds dataset, the PIM with Swin-T also achieved a Top-1 accuracy of 92.8%. This result exceeded the prior best methods like TransFG (90.8%) and CAP (91.0%) by 1.8%.

These results indicate that the PIM effectively fuses features across different scales, enabling more accurate identification of fine-grained categories.

**B. Improvement Across Diverse Backbones:**

To assess the generalizability of PIM, it was tested on four different mainstream backbones: ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B7, ViT, and Swin-T. In all cases, the addition of PIM resulted in improved accuracy:
- ResNet-50: Improved from 88.2% to 89.5% (+1.3%).
- EfficientNet-B7: Improved from 88.2% to 89.9% (+1.7%).
- ViT: Improved from 90.1% to 91.0% (+0.9%).
- Swin-T: Improved from 91.9% to 92.8% (+0.9%).
These findings suggest that PIM is a versatile module capable of enhancing the performance of both CNN-based and Transformer-based architectures, even those already demonstrating strong baseline performance.

**C. Ablation Study:**

An ablation study was conducted to understand the contribution of individual components and design choices within the PIM.

1. **Evaluation Methods:** Testing different ways to derive the final prediction score (e.g., average score of individual blocks, combiner score, or average of top-K prediction scores) revealed that averaging the top-5 prediction scores yielded the best accuracy of 92.77%.
2. **Number of Selections:** The study investigated the impact of the number of selected regions by the Weakly Supervised Selector (`num_selects`) from each of the four backbone blocks. While variations in selection numbers (e.g., vs.) resulted in minor accuracy differences (e.g., 92.77% vs. 92.67%), the number of selections significantly influenced the computational load of the Combiner. A balance between accuracy and computational cost was considered.
3. **Selected vs. Dropped Areas:** Analysis of classification accuracy for selected versus dropped areas showed that selected regions consistently exhibited higher accuracy than dropped regions. For instance, with a selection size of, selected areas had accuracies ranging from 91.44% to 92.72% across blocks, while dropped areas registered very low accuracies (around 9-10%), supporting the PIM's ability to isolate irrelevant features.
4. **Combiner Architecture:** Comparing a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) for the Combiner, the GCN consistently outperformed the MLP. A single-layer GCN was identified as the most effective structure, yielding 92.77% accuracy, which was 0.71% higher than the single-layer MLP (92.06%).
5. **GCN Pooling Number:** Within the GCN Combiner, experiments on the number of layers and the ratio of output to input nodes showed that a single-layer GCN with an output node ratio of 1/32 achieved the highest accuracy of 92.77%. Variations in GCN architecture affected accuracy by approximately ±0.6%.
6. **Component Contribution:** A breakdown of accuracy improvement by adding components sequentially:
 - Backbone (Swin-T): 91.9%
 - Backbone + FPN: 92.0% (+0.1%)
 - Backbone + FPN + Selector: 92.1% (+0.1%)
 - Backbone + FPN + Selector + Combiner: 92.8% (+0.7%)
This incremental analysis highlights the cumulative benefits, particularly the significant impact of the GCN-based Combiner on accuracy.

**D. Visualization:**

Grad-CAM heatmaps provided visual evidence that the PIM-enhanced model focuses more precisely on discriminative key points within the object and effectively filters out background noise compared to the baseline Swin-T model. For example, in cases like the Acadian Flycatcher and Philadelphia Vireo, the proposed method not only focused more intently on the object but also identified a greater number of key positions. In instances with clean backgrounds, such as the Common Tern, the difference in focus from the baseline was less pronounced.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method for improving fine-grained visual classification that exhibits several noteworthy aspects and potential implications:

1. **Advancement in FGVC Performance:** The achievement of state-of-the-art accuracy on two widely recognized fine-grained bird datasets, CUB200-2011 and NABirds, indicates a tangible improvement over existing methodologies. This performance gain, demonstrated through quantitative metrics, contributes to the ongoing progress in the field of fine-grained recognition.
2. **Architectural Flexibility and Generalizability:** The design of the Plug-in Module (PIM) as a backbone-agnostic component is a significant technical contribution. Its proven ability to integrate and improve performance across diverse backbone architectures, including both conventional CNNs (ResNet, EfficientNet) and modern Transformers (ViT, Swin-T), suggests broad applicability. This flexibility can allow researchers and practitioners to leverage the PIM with a wide array of existing or future models without requiring extensive architectural redesign.
3. **End-to-End Trainability:** By achieving end-to-end training, the PIM addresses a common limitation of many multi-stage FGVC methods. This simplification of the training pipeline can lead to more efficient model development, reduced complexity in optimization, and potentially faster convergence.
4. **Weakly Supervised Feature Localization:** The PIM's approach of inferring discriminative regions from image-level annotations, without requiring explicit bounding box or part annotations, offers practical benefits. This method reduces the reliance on costly expert-level labeling, which is a major bottleneck in fine-grained datasets, thereby making the development of FGVC systems more accessible and scalable.
5. **Enhanced Focus on Subtle Details:** The visualization results demonstrate the PIM's capacity to refine the model's attention, enabling it to concentrate more precisely on subtle, discriminative regions within an object and effectively filter out background noise. This capability is crucial for distinguishing highly similar subcategories in FGVC tasks.
6. **Potential for Broader Impact:** The principles underlying the PIM's design—specifically, the weakly supervised selection and fusion of discriminative features—could potentially be adapted to other computer vision tasks that require fine-grained understanding or robust localization with limited supervision. This might include tasks such as medical image analysis (e.g., differentiating subtle disease manifestations), quality control in manufacturing, or detailed content analysis in visual media.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The success of the GCN-based Combiner suggests avenues for further exploration into graph-based methods for feature integration in visual tasks. Additionally, the approach of explicitly differentiating and managing "helpful" versus "less helpful" features opens up possibilities for more nuanced attention mechanisms or curriculum learning strategies in deep learning.

Overall, the proposed Plug-in Module represents an advancement in the field of fine-grained visual classification by offering a flexible, efficient, and effective solution for enhancing model accuracy while simplifying the training process and reducing annotation dependency.
