# Benchmarking World-Model Learning with Environment-Level Queries

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, WorldTest, and its instantiation, AutumnBench, evaluate AI world-model learning using diverse environment-level queries, revealing that current state-of-the-art AI models significantly underperform humans across tasks requiring flexible reasoning and planning.

## Problem

- Existing world-model evaluations primarily measure properties observable from interactions (e.g., next-frame prediction, task-specific RL returns), failing to assess comprehensive understanding of an environment's underlying dynamics.
- Benchmarks are often task-specific or require predefined output representations, limiting generalizability and fair comparisons between diverse AI agents and human cognitive capabilities.
- A lack of diverse "environment-level queries" prevents evaluation of an agent's grasp of global structure, hidden mechanisms, or counterfactual reasoning, which extend beyond single observed trajectories.

## Method

- Introduced **WorldTest**, a ==two-phase, behavior-based framework== where agents first interact in a reward-free base environment (M) to build an internal model, then face diverse ==environment-level queries== encoded as derived challenge environments (M').
- Developed **AutumnBench**, a concrete instantiation of WorldTest, comprising ==43 interactive grid-world environments== and ==129 problems== across three task types: ==Masked Frame Prediction (MFP)==, ==Change Detection (CD)==, and ==Planning==.
- The framework assesses an agent's ability to answer questions about global environmental structure and dynamics through ==behavioral responses== in challenge environments, enabling representation-agnostic and automatic scoring.

## Results

- **Humans achieved significantly higher scores** than all five frontier reasoning models across every environment and task type in AutumnBench.
- AI model performance correlated with computational cost in only **58%** of environments, suggesting inherent reasoning limitations in other cases, with **Claude Opus 4.6** showing a substantial gap to human performance.
- Humans utilized the `reset` action more frequently and systematically (mean LCS ratio **0.637 vs. 0.188–0.360** for models) and achieved higher **causal rule coverage (92.5%)** during exploration, demonstrating more effective learning strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evaluating world models with environment-level queries reveals a substantial performance gap, with humans consistently outperforming state-of-the-art AI models in flexible reasoning and planning.
> - Human exploration strategies are more structured and purposeful, involving systematic use of resets for hypothesis testing and rapid transition to focused behavior, contrasting with less strategic AI exploration.
> - Even with perfect next-state prediction, a simulator agent underperforms humans, indicating that advanced inference under uncertainty and long-horizon reasoning are crucial for true world-model understanding beyond simple prediction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of AI agents capable of flexible reasoning and planning is considered a critical area for artificial intelligence advancement, with world models identified as a central component. Current methods for evaluating world models in AI primarily focus on properties measurable through observed interactions, such as next-frame prediction or task-specific returns in reinforcement learning (RL) benchmarks. These evaluations often assess an agent's ability to optimize a task-specific objective rather than its comprehensive understanding of an environment's underlying dynamics.

Existing evaluation paradigms exhibit several limitations:
- **RL-based benchmarks** (e.g., OpenAI Gym, Atari, Procgen) measure task success, but high performance may stem from memorized policies rather than a generalized understanding of environment structure. They do not explicitly probe an agent's inferred dynamics.
- **Non-interactive benchmarks** (e.g., Bongard problems, ARC-AGI) assess rule inference from static examples, representing environment-level queries about non-interactive settings. However, they do not test an agent's capacity to learn structure through active exploration in dynamic environments.
- **Representation-based approaches** (e.g., DiscoveryWorld, CLEVRER variants) allow exploration but restrict agent outputs to specific formats like symbolic logic, programs, or natural language descriptions. Such constraints can limit the types of models that can be evaluated and hinder fair comparisons, especially with human performance.
- **Two-phase RL benchmarks** (e.g., URLB, in-context RL) separate learning from evaluation, but both phases typically occur within the same environment, and evaluation remains constrained to properties observable from action-reward sequences. This limitation precludes assessment of an agent's understanding of structural properties, such as regional connectivity or counterfactual variations, that cannot be inferred solely from its own trajectories.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing an evaluation framework that focuses on "environment-level queries." These queries require reasoning about the global structure and dynamics of an environment, rather than being confined to properties derivable from a single observed trajectory or relying on predefined output representations. The work seeks to provide a more comprehensive and representation-agnostic method for assessing whether AI agents build general-purpose world models comparable to human cognitive capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel and comprehensive framework for evaluating the learning of world models in AI agents. This framework aims to assess whether agents acquire general-purpose models of an environment that support flexible reasoning and planning, analogous to human cognitive abilities.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations identified in existing world-model evaluation methodologies:

1. **Incomplete Evaluation Scope:** Current benchmarks primarily test properties that are directly measurable from observed interactions, such as the accuracy of next-frame predictions or an agent's performance in a specific task. They often fail to probe deeper understanding, such as an agent's grasp of global environmental structure or its ability to reason about counterfactual consequences. This narrow focus may lead to agents that excel at specific tasks but lack a transferable, generalizable understanding of the world.
2. **Lack of Diverse Querying:** Humans construct versatile internal models that enable them to answer a wide array of questions about an environment, including those requiring an understanding of hidden mechanisms, system-wide connectivity, or the effects of interventions that extend beyond direct experience. Existing AI evaluation protocols do not adequately test for this breadth of understanding through diverse "environment-level queries"—questions whose answers rely on properties of the entire environment dynamics rather than just isolated observed trajectories.
3. **Constraints on Agent Representation:** Many interactive benchmarks require agents to produce answers in predefined symbolic or natural language formats. This imposes restrictions on the agent's internal representations and output interfaces, complicating automated evaluation and hindering fair comparisons across different types of agents, including humans, whose internal models may not be directly inspectable or easily formalized into such formats.
4. **Reward-Centric Interaction:** Traditional reinforcement learning benchmarks typically embed learning within a reward-driven paradigm. While agents might implicitly learn aspects of environment structure, their primary objective is to maximize reward. This can lead to the acquisition of task-specific policies rather than generalizable world models, as the learning process is optimized for a particular objective rather than a comprehensive understanding of dynamics.
5. **Limited Generalization Testing:** Existing two-phase evaluation protocols often limit both the exploration and evaluation phases to the same environment or to properties observable through action-reward sequences. This approach may not sufficiently test an agent's ability to generalize its learned model to novel situations, detect environmental changes, or plan over long horizons.

To address these motivations, the authors propose an evaluation framework, WorldTest, and its instantiation, AutumnBench, designed to:
- Directly measure an agent's capacity to answer diverse environment-level queries.
- Support a representation-agnostic evaluation approach, relying purely on behavioral responses in derived challenge environments.
- Promote the learning of general-purpose environment dynamics through reward-free interaction.
- Provide a robust benchmark for comparing AI agents with human performance on tasks requiring flexible, predictive, and counterfactual understanding.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **WorldTest**, a behavior-based framework designed for evaluating world-model learning, and **AutumnBench**, a concrete instantiation of this framework.

#### 3.1. WorldTest Framework

WorldTest operates through a two-phase protocol:
1. **Interaction Phase:** An agent is placed in a reward-free base environment (M) with unknown dynamics. The agent interacts autonomously, selecting actions without external goals. During this phase, it can reset the environment to its initial state to facilitate hypothesis testing and systematic exploration. The agent's objective during this phase is to build an internal world model based on its interactions. This phase concludes when the agent opts to proceed.
2. **Test Phase:** An environment-level query is posed to the agent. WorldTest encodes this query as a *derived challenge environment* (M'), which is a modification of the base environment M (e.g., changes to transitions, observations, or actions), combined with an explicit objective function (R) and an evaluation horizon (H). The agent's performance is then measured solely by its behavior and success in achieving this objective within the challenge environment. This design ensures representation-agnostic scoring, as internal representations are not inspected.

Key characteristics of WorldTest:
- **Representation-agnostic, automatic scoring:** Evaluation is purely behavioral, eliminating the need to interpret internal representations or rely on human judgment, thus enabling fair comparison across diverse agents (including humans).
- **Goal-free interaction:** The absence of rewards in the interaction phase encourages agents to learn transferable environment dynamics rather than optimizing for a specific, potentially narrow, task.
- **Query-driven generalization:** The test phase directly assesses whether the learned world model supports downstream inferences and tasks that extend beyond the initial observation trajectories, such as predicting masked states, detecting dynamic changes, and planning complex action sequences.

#### 3.2. AutumnBench: Design and Implementation

AutumnBench instantiates WorldTest using 43 interactive grid-world environments, each paired with three types of test-phase tasks, resulting in 129 distinct problems.

**Interaction-Phase Environments:**
- Environments are 2D grid worlds, defined using the Autumn domain-specific language (DSL). Autumn enables succinct and expressive specification of causal interactions in grids.
- Grid sizes vary from 3x3 to 25x25. Environments feature up to five object types and 1 to 12 colors.
- 17 of the 43 environments are stochastic, meaning actions can lead to different outcomes due to inherent randomness.
- Environments are categorized by their dynamics and reasoning demands, including "Board & Puzzle Games," "Atari-Style," "Multi-step Reasoning," "Emergent Dynamics," "Physical Analogue," "Visible/Latent Autonomous Dynamics," "Boolean Logic," and "Clickable Cells."
- The action space includes directional movements (up, down, left, right), clicking on grid cells (click x y), and no-op. A `reset` action is available during interaction.

**Test-Phase Challenges:**
Three types of tasks operationalize environment-level queries:

1. **Masked Frame Prediction (MFP):** The agent observes a trajectory where parts of the final observation frame are masked. It must predict the missing content by selecting one of six candidate options. A correct selection yields a score of 1, otherwise 0. This evaluates predictive understanding of hidden states.
2. **Change Detection (CD):** The agent interacts with a version of the base environment where one of the underlying rules has been altered at an unknown timestep. The agent's task is to identify the earliest timestep at which this rule change occurs. Scoring is 1 for exact or one-step-early detection, with partial credit for late detection and 0 for premature detection. This assesses the ability to detect shifts in environmental dynamics.
3. **Planning:** The agent is presented with a target configuration for a specified subgrid. The goal is to produce an action sequence that transforms the subgrid into the target configuration. The `reset` action is unavailable in this phase. A score of 1 is awarded for reaching the goal, 0 otherwise. This evaluates long-horizon reasoning and action sequencing.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Setup

- **Human Evaluation:** 517 English-speaking participants were recruited via Prolific, undergoing screening for color-blindness and attention. Each problem was attempted 20 times. "Human" performance is represented by the 80th percentile score per problem to reflect engaged human capability. Participants used a web-based graphical user interface (GUI) with interactive tutorials.
- **Reasoning Model Evaluation:** Five frontier models were tested: Claude 4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash, o3, and Qwen3-235b-a22b-thinking-2507. Each model was given one AutumnBench problem at a time, with a single trajectory completion per problem due to cost. Models received grid states as 2D arrays of color strings and a text description of the task type. Models could choose `go-to-test` or `reset` during interaction.
- **Baseline Agents:**
 - **Autumn-Simulator Agent:** This agent has direct access to the ground-truth Autumn program. For MFP and CD, it represents an upper bound for next-state prediction approaches. For planning, it uses BFS (capped search depth/breadth), serving as a reference.
 - **Random Agent:** A memoryless policy that selects actions uniformly at random (clicks, directional, no-op). This baseline quantifies the inherent difficulty of each task under unguided exploration.

Behavioral metrics were also developed to quantify exploration and learning, including normalized perplexity (capturing action predictability), Area Under the Curve (AUC) of normalized perplexity (overall learning trajectory), and Longest Common Subsequence (LCS) ratio for reset sequence analysis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved 517 human participants and five frontier AI models on the AutumnBench benchmark.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Human Superiority:** Humans consistently outperformed all five reasoning models across every environment and task type. The aggregated 80th percentile human score significantly exceeded that of all models. Claude Opus 4.6, a newer model, also showed a substantial gap compared to human performance, indicating AutumnBench remains challenging for state-of-the-art AI.
- **Stochasticity:** Humans maintained consistent performance in both deterministic and stochastic environments. Conversely, reasoning models exhibited higher scores in stochastic environments compared to deterministic ones.
- **Computational Cost vs. Performance:** An analysis of performance against the increasing computational cost of the five reasoning models revealed that performance improved monotonically with cost in 25 out of 43 environments (58%). However, in the remaining 18 environments (42%), additional computational resources did not yield a performance benefit, suggesting inherent reasoning limitations not overcome by scaling. Task-specific breakdown showed improvements in 37% of MFP and Planning tasks, and 33% of CD tasks.
- **Simulator Agent Performance:** The Autumn-simulator agent, with direct access to ground-truth environment dynamics, significantly underperformed humans across all three task types. This indicates that even perfect next-state prediction capabilities are insufficient, highlighting the need for advanced inference under uncertainty and long-horizon reasoning.

#### 4.2. Exploration Patterns

- **Action Distribution:** Humans allocated approximately 12.5% of their actions to resets and 12.5% to no-ops. In contrast, reasoning models dedicated less than 7% of their combined actions to resets and no-ops, prioritizing clicks and directional movements.
- **Reset Frequency:** Humans utilized the `reset` action more frequently, resetting at least once in every environment and exceeding the per-environment model mean in 33 of 43 cases. Models often avoided resets, with Claude skipping resets in 31 environments.
- **Reset Structure (LCS Ratio):** Human exploration strategies around resets were more structured. The longest common subsequence (LCS) ratio between pre- and post-reset action windows was significantly higher for humans (mean 0.637) compared to reasoning models (mean 0.188–0.360). This suggests humans employ resets more systematically for hypothesis testing.
- **On-Clause Coverage:** Humans achieved the highest mean coverage of an environment's causal rules (on-clauses in the Autumn program) at 92.5%. Model coverage correlated positively with test-phase scores, but humans were near-saturated, showing no within-human correlation.

#### 4.3. World-Model Learning Trajectories

- **Normalized Perplexity:** Humans demonstrated lower Area Under the Curve (AUC) for normalized perplexity and lower final normalized perplexity values compared to reasoning models. This indicates that humans transition more rapidly from random exploration to focused, targeted behavior, and maintain more deterministic and purposeful actions by the end of the exploration phase.

#### 4.4. Prompt Robustness and Context Usage

- **Prompt Sensitivity:** Testing with alternative prompt formulations revealed modest performance differences, suggesting that model capabilities, rather than specific prompt engineering, were the primary determinant of performance.
- **Context Length:** Reasoning models generally used a small fraction of their available context windows (e.g., Claude 1.3%-6.3%, Gemini Pro 1.5%-16.5%). No significant benefit was observed from longer context lengths within the tested ranges for the zero-shot reasoning models.

#### 4.5. Correlation with External Benchmarks

- **AutumnBench Correlation:** Overall AutumnBench scores exhibited a strong rank correlation with SWE-bench Verified (Spearman ρ = 0.89, p < 0.05), indicating a plausible transferability of world-modeling abilities to real-world software engineering tasks. Masked Frame Prediction (MFP) showed a particularly strong correlation with SWE-bench (ρ = 0.99, p < 0.001) and GPQA Diamond (ρ = 0.82). Planning correlated with SWE-bench (ρ = 0.90, p < 0.05) and TerminalBench (ρ = 1.00, n=3). Change Detection (CD) showed weak or negative correlations, potentially due to its reliance on counterfactual reasoning. AIME 2025 showed negligible correlation with AutumnBench.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework and benchmark that offer a method for evaluating world-model learning in AI agents. The key significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Novel Evaluation Framework:** WorldTest introduces a behavior-based, representation-agnostic framework for assessing whether AI agents learn general-purpose world models. By decoupling the interaction phase from the test phase and framing evaluation as a challenge within derived environments, WorldTest moves beyond task-specific reward optimization and internal representation inspection. This design facilitates a more direct measurement of an agent's ability to reason about global environmental properties and counterfactuals, aligning more closely with human cognitive capabilities.
2. **Concrete and Extensible Benchmark:** AutumnBench provides a concrete instantiation of WorldTest in an interactive grid-world setting. Comprising 43 environments and 129 tasks across masked-frame prediction, change detection, and planning, it offers a diverse and structured environment for evaluating core world-modeling capabilities. The design emphasizes extensibility, allowing for the future integration of new environments, dynamics, and challenges (e.g., tool use, analogical reasoning) into richer domains like physics-rich environments or robotics.
3. **Exposure of Human-AI Gaps:** The empirical evaluation, involving both human participants and state-of-the-art AI models, consistently demonstrates a substantial gap in world-model learning capabilities. Humans significantly outperform current frontier models across all environments and task types. This finding indicates that existing AI models do not yet possess the flexible, predictive, and counterfactual understanding characteristic of human-like world models.
4. **Insights into Model Limitations:** The behavioral analysis of exploration patterns and world-model learning trajectories provides insights into specific limitations of current AI models. The observation that humans strategically utilize resets for hypothesis testing, alongside their more focused exploration (lower normalized perplexity), suggests deficiencies in AI agents' strategic experimental design, uncertainty quantification, and flexible belief updating. Models appear less adept at adapting their understanding in the face of contradictory evidence, particularly in masked-frame prediction tasks.
5. **Guidance for Future AI Research:** The identified human-AI gaps suggest that achieving human-level performance in world-model learning will likely require advancements not only in fundamental components like priors over world models and memory architectures but also in higher-level cognitive processes such as strategic information acquisition and dynamic belief revision. The framework provides a diagnostic tool to guide research efforts in these areas.
6. **Publicly Available Resources:** The public release of the AutumnBench dataset, the web-based GUI, the Autumn interpreter, and the reasoning-model baselines ensures reproducibility and supports community-wide engagement. This open access facilitates further research, allowing other researchers to build upon this work, develop new agents, and continue to push the boundaries of world-model learning.

In summary, this work provides a framework and benchmark that offer a standardized method for evaluating the generality and robustness of world models. It highlights the current limitations of AI in developing flexible understanding and provides a foundation for future research aimed at closing the observed performance gaps.
