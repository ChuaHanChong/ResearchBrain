# Geometric Red-Teaming for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and NIST introduced Geometric Red-Teaming (GRT), a framework that systematically discovers object geometries causing pre-trained robotic manipulation policies to fail. The framework generates physically plausible 'CrashShapes' which induce significant performance degradation, and demonstrates that fine-tuning policies on these failure cases significantly improves robustness across various tasks in both simulation and real-world settings.

## Problem

- Standard robotic manipulation evaluations often use controlled test sets, failing to reveal how systems fail under realistic, subtle geometric object variations.
- Existing methods for generating geometric variations in robotics are typically static and task-specific, unable to dynamically respond to policy behavior or systematically search for failure modes.
- Manipulation policies are often brittle to minor object geometric changes, impacting both sensory input and physical interaction dynamics in unpredictable ways.

## Method

- **Geometric Red-Teaming (GRT)** is a modular, black-box, simulator-in-the-loop framework that identifies minimal, failure-inducing geometric perturbations.
- ==VLM-guided (Vision-Language Model) mesh deformation== generates plausible and task-relevant object shape variations, prioritizing physical realism and potential for policy failure.
- ==Gradient-free, population-based black-box optimization== is used to efficiently search the high-dimensional deformation space for **CrashShapes** that cause catastrophic policy failures during embodied simulation rollouts.

## Results

- GRT consistently induced catastrophic policy failures, with a mean relative final drop of **76.3%** in success rate for grasping and **over 70%** drops for insertion and articulated manipulation tasks, often with low increases in shape complexity.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both VLM-guided handle selection and the gradient-free optimization strategy significantly contributed to higher failure severity and faster convergence, outperforming heuristic and simple perturbation baselines.
- Blue-teaming experiments recovered success rates on CrashShapes from **20-45%** to **80-95%** in simulation without degrading nominal performance, and real-world validation on xARM 6 and Franka Emika Panda robots demonstrated robust transferability of both failure discovery and policy improvements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Manipulation policies exhibit high brittleness; even small, plausible geometric changes in objects can consistently lead to catastrophic task failures.
> - Combining VLM-guided handle selection with selective perturbation optimization is crucial for efficiently discovering impactful, semantically coherent 'CrashShapes' that reveal policy vulnerabilities.
> - The 'CrashShapes' discovered through red-teaming are actionable; fine-tuning (blue-teaming) policies on these specific failure-inducing geometries can significantly improve robustness without degrading performance on nominal objects, and these improvements transfer to real-world robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Standard evaluation methodologies in robotic manipulation typically assess policy performance on controlled, in-distribution test sets. This approach often provides limited information regarding how robotic systems might fail under realistic variations, particularly those stemming from subtle changes in object geometry. Such geometric variations can unpredictably influence object affordances, alter contact dynamics, and lead to task failures.

In contrast, fields such as computer vision and natural language processing have developed systematic methods, including adversarial testing and red-teaming, to probe model robustness against controlled input perturbations. While nascent efforts in developing analogous robustness evaluation tools for robotic manipulation exist, they often target symbolic parameters, language instructions, or predefined failure taxonomies. Existing methods for generating geometric variations in robotics, such as DoorGym, ManiSkill, and EGAD, typically produce static, task-specific datasets that do not dynamically respond to policy behavior.

This work addresses a gap by introducing a formal framework for systematically evaluating policy performance under plausible, task-relevant geometric perturbations. It focuses specifically on object-centric robustness, considering 3D object meshes as a critical axis of potential failure that impacts both sensory input and physical interaction dynamics. By actively searching for failure-inducing geometries, this research aims to provide a dynamic and policy-conditioned approach to uncover vulnerabilities missed by static benchmarks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to ascertain whether an automated system can discover object geometries that cause manipulation policies to fail, treating the policies as black boxes. This challenge is framed as a "red-teaming" problem, drawing inspiration from cybersecurity practices where systems are subjected to realistic stress tests to uncover vulnerabilities.

The core motivation is to generate "CrashShapes"—structurally valid, physically plausible, and semantically coherent deformations of 3D objects—that consistently trigger catastrophic failures in pre-trained manipulation policies. The identification of such shapes necessitates evaluating policy performance through embodied simulation rollouts, as policy success is inherently linked to the physical interaction between the deformed object and the task environment.

Operationalizing this objective presents two main challenges:
1. **Non-differentiable Evaluation**: Policy performance is assessed via simulator rollouts, which are typically non-differentiable. This characteristic precludes the direct use of gradient-based optimization methods for generating failure-inducing geometries.
2. **High-dimensional, Constrained Deformation Space**: The space of possible object deformations is high-dimensional. Exploring this space requires enforcing structural constraints to ensure that generated objects maintain mesh integrity and remain physically plausible and semantically coherent for real-world relevance.

The research also has a secondary objective: to demonstrate the utility of the discovered CrashShapes for improving policy robustness. This is explored through a process referred to as "blue-teaming," where policies are fine-tuned on these failure-inducing geometries to recover performance without degrading capabilities on nominal objects. The overarching goal is to establish a general-purpose framework for structured, object-centric robustness evaluation that can both diagnose and facilitate the correction of failure modes in robotic manipulation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Geometric Red-Teaming (GRT), is a modular, policy-agnostic, simulator-in-the-loop system designed to discover minimal, failure-inducing geometric perturbations. GRT integrates a physically-grounded deformation model with gradient-free optimization, consisting of four primary components:

#### 3.1. VLM-Guided Mesh Deformation

To generate plausible deformations, GRT first establishes a deformation interface on the input mesh $M = (V, F)$ by selecting handle points $H \subseteq V$ (vertices to be displaced) and anchor points $A \subseteq V$ (fixed vertices). Instead of manual selection, which can be time-consuming and prone to human bias, GRT employs a Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically ChatGPT-4o, to guide handle point selection. This is achieved through a hierarchical prompting strategy:

1. **Geometric Reasoning Template**: The VLM receives a canonical view panel (a 2x2 grid of rendered object views with overlaid, indexed keypoints generated by a heuristic sampler) and a JSON file containing the 3D coordinates of these keypoints. The VLM is prompted to analyze this information to propose subsets of handle points, each with a semantic description of the intended deformation.
2. **Task-Critical Ranking Template**: In the second stage, the VLM is provided with the previous interaction history and asked to Pareto-rank the candidate handle point subsets. This ranking prioritizes two objectives: (i) the physical plausibility and real-world existence of the resulting deformed objects, and (ii) their hypothesized potential to induce policy failure in a given manipulation task. This process distills the VLM's output into a compact set of task-relevant handle points. A fallback to manual selection is available for cases where the VLM may struggle (e.g., specialized manufacturing components).

The deformation itself is performed using a Jacobian field-based model, adapted from the first stage of the APAP framework. This model computes smooth, structurally coherent perturbations of the nominal mesh based on the specified handle and anchor constraints. Notably, the method omits the 2D diffusion prior used in the original APAP, which reduces computational overhead (27x speedup, 10x memory reduction) and was found to better preserve local geometric fidelity for task-relevant regions, especially for objects underrepresented in the diffusion prior's training data (e.g., a USB plug).

#### 3.2. Red-Teaming via Black-Box Optimization

GRT casts the search for failure-inducing deformation parameters ($\theta$) as a black-box optimization problem, as policy performance from simulator rollouts is non-differentiable. It utilizes a gradient-free, population-based method inspired by TOPDM, which employs a selective perturbation strategy:

1. **Population Management**: A population of deformation candidates is maintained, sampled from a Gaussian distribution over handle displacement parameters.
2. **Selective Perturbation**: In each iteration, only a random subset (typically half) of the handle point displacements for each candidate is perturbed with Gaussian noise. This approach is designed for high-dimensional spaces, allowing for local refinement of critical object features (e.g., contact surfaces) without globally destabilizing the mesh structure.
3. **Simulator-in-the-Loop Evaluation**: Each candidate deformed mesh $D_\theta(M)$ is evaluated using a pre-trained black-box manipulation policy $\pi$ within a physics-based simulator (NVIDIA IsaacGym). Task-specific success metrics $J(\pi, D_\theta(M))$ are computed via parallel rollouts, which can be binary (e.g., grasping, insertion success) or continuous (e.g., drawer opening distance). The objective is to minimize this metric.
4. **Distribution Update**: The highest-performing samples (elites) that induce the most significant policy degradation are used to update the proposal distribution for the next iteration, promoting further exploration in promising failure regions.
5. **Constrained Search**: An optional "Smoothness Score" (SS) constraint allows users to bound the average handle displacement (in metric units), ensuring that the discovered CrashShapes represent small, realistic deviations from the nominal geometry. Any candidate exceeding this budget is uniformly scaled back to the budget surface.

Prior to simulation, all deformed meshes undergo convex decomposition using CoACD to ensure compatibility with collision detection and stable simulation dynamics.

#### 3.3. Blue-Teaming for Policy Refinement

To demonstrate the actionable utility of CrashShapes, the framework includes a "blue-teaming" phase. This involves fine-tuning the original pre-trained policies on subsets of the discovered failure-inducing geometries. The fine-tuning is conducted using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with early stopping, without additional task augmentation. The goal is to improve policy performance on the specific CrashShapes while preserving, or even enhancing, performance on the original nominal object.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research addressed three primary questions through experiments across rigid object grasping, high-precision insertion, and articulated drawer manipulation tasks, validated in both simulation and real-world robotic setups.

#### 4.1. RQ–1: Failure Discovery

GRT consistently identified catastrophic policy failures, characterized by significant reductions in success rates, even with minimal geometric perturbations.

- **Grasping**: For the Contact-Graspnet policy on 22 YCB objects, VLM-guided handle selection led to a mean relative final drop of 76.3% in success rate, reaching a 50% drop in 7.3 iterations on average, with a low median increase in shape complexity (∆Complexity) of 0.041. This suggests VLM guidance effectively targeted critical contact regions.
- **Insertion**: For a state-based insertion policy, manual handle selection resulted in a 73.95% final drop. For a point-cloud-initialized policy, VLM-guided selection proved more effective, causing a 77.7% final drop, reaching a 50% drop in 6.0 iterations.
- **Articulated Manipulation**: Manual handle selection achieved a 98.9% final drop in success for a drawer-opening policy, reaching 50% degradation in 6.0 iterations.
- **Smoothness Score (SS) Constraint**: When constrained to small handle displacements, GRT still induced significant failures (e.g., 58.3% drop for grasping, 60.9% for state-based insertion), demonstrating that the framework does not rely on large, unrealistic deformations to find vulnerabilities, and typically resulting in lower ∆Complexity.
- **Qualitative Evolution**: Visualizations of the optimization process (Appendix A) demonstrated that subtle deformations could lead to catastrophic failures, highlighting the brittleness of learned affordances. The final CrashShapes maintained structural realism while causing severe task failures.

#### 4.2. RQ–2: Component Contribution (Ablation Study)

An ablation study on the grasping task evaluated the impact of handle selection strategy (Heuristic vs. VLM-guided) and deformation search method (Gaussian Perturbation vs. Optimization).

- **Optimization vs. Gaussian Perturbation**: Optimization using TOPDM's selective perturbation significantly improved both failure severity and convergence speed. For instance, "Heuristic + Optimization" achieved a 68.4% final drop with an average of 8.95 iterations to 50% failure, compared to "Heuristic + Gaussian Perturbation" which yielded a 63.3% drop in 10.0 iterations.
- **VLM-Guided vs. Heuristic Handle Selection**: VLM-guided selection consistently outperformed heuristic methods. "VLM-Guided + Optimization" (the full GRT approach) achieved the highest final drop of 76.3% and fastest convergence (7.32 iterations to 50% failure).
- **"All Handles + Optimization"**: While effective (71.4% final drop), this baseline resulted in a higher ∆Complexity (0.179), suggesting it achieved failures through larger, less localized deformations.

These results indicate that both VLM-guided handle selection and the gradient-free optimization strategy contribute to efficient and effective failure discovery.

#### 4.3. RQ–3: Actionability (Blue-Teaming and Real-World Validation)

The research demonstrated that CrashShapes are actionable for targeted policy improvement and transfer to real-world robots.

- **Simulation Blue-Teaming**: Fine-tuning (blue-teaming) insertion policies on discovered CrashShapes resulted in substantial performance recovery. Success rates on CrashShapes, which initially ranged from 20-45%, increased to 80-95% after fine-tuning. Crucially, this improvement did not degrade the policies' performance on the original nominal objects, which remained between 87.3% and 96%.
- **Real-World Validation**:
 - **Insertion (xARM 6)**: 3D-printed CrashShapes for USB plugs caused task success to drop from a nominal 90% to as low as 22.5% and 55% respectively. Deploying the blue-teamed policies (trained only in simulation) on the real robot recovered success rates to 90% and 82.5% on the corresponding physical CrashShapes, closely matching simulation results and preserving nominal performance.
 - **Grasping (Franka Emika Panda)**: 3D-printed CrashShapes for objects like a mustard bottle and screwdriver caused real-world success rates to drop from 80-90% (nominal) to 30-35%, consistent with simulation trends.

These real-world validations confirm the physical realism of the simulated CrashShapes and underscore their utility as both diagnostic tools for identifying vulnerabilities and corrective signals for enhancing policy robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces Geometric Red-Teaming (GRT), a framework that addresses a critical gap in robotic manipulation research by providing a systematic, object-centric approach to evaluating policy robustness against plausible geometric variations.

The significance of GRT lies in several key aspects:
- **Novelty**: It represents the first red-teaming framework that specifically targets 3D geometric deformations to probe robotic manipulation policies, moving beyond symbolic parameters or scene-level taxonomies.
- **Policy-Agnostic and Scalable**: GRT operates as a black-box, simulator-in-the-loop tool, making it applicable across diverse manipulation policies and tasks without requiring internal policy access or gradient information.
- **Discovery of Brittle Failure Modes**: The framework consistently discovers CrashShapes—physically plausible object variants—that induce catastrophic failures missed by traditional evaluation protocols, revealing the inherent brittleness of learned manipulation affordances.
- **Actionable Insights for Robustness**: Beyond diagnostics, GRT demonstrates that the discovered CrashShapes can serve as effective training signals. The "blue-teaming" approach allows for targeted policy refinement that significantly improves performance on these challenging geometries while preserving or enhancing performance on nominal objects, offering a practical pathway to more robust robotic systems without overfitting.
- **Real-World Transferability**: The validation of both red-teaming and blue-teaming results on physical robots provides evidence for the fidelity of the simulation environment and the practical utility of the framework in real-world deployment scenarios.

The potential impact of GRT includes fostering the development of more robust and reliable robotic manipulation systems. By enabling automated, systematic discovery and correction of geometric failure modes, GRT can contribute to building safer and more dependable robots for various applications, particularly in industrial settings, household tasks, and complex assembly operations where object variations are inherent.

The work also highlights current limitations, such as the requirement for watertight mesh inputs, the inherent approximations in simulation dynamics affecting transferability across all tasks, and the need for future work in automatic failure diagnosis and causal attribution to further enhance the framework's utility.
