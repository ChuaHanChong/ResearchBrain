# Defeating the Training-Inference Mismatch via FP16

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Sea AI Lab and the National University of Singapore demonstrates that using FP16 precision for both training and inference fundamentally resolves the numerical discrepancies causing instability in Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning of Large Language Models. Employing FP16 drastically reduces the training-inference mismatch, leading to more stable optimization, faster convergence, and superior performance compared to existing BF16-based methods and complex algorithmic corrections.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs) often suffers from instability, training collapse, and sub-optimal performance.
- The 'training-inference mismatch,' caused by numerical discrepancies between fast inference and gradient computation engines, leads to biased gradients and a 'deployment gap'.
- Existing solutions, such as importance sampling corrections or manual engineering alignments, are computationally expensive, complex, or only address symptoms without eliminating the fundamental mismatch.

## Method

- The paper hypothesizes and confirms that low floating-point precision, specifically ==BF16==, is the root cause of the ==training-inference mismatch== due to accumulated rounding errors.
- The core solution involves switching from BF16 to ==FP16 precision== for both the inference policy and the training engine in RL fine-tuning.
- FP16's higher numerical precision (more ==mantissa bits==) ensures that minor implementation differences are absorbed, preventing policy divergence, with standard ==loss scaling== addressing its limited dynamic range.

## Results

- FP16 reduced the training-inference mismatch in sequence-level log-probability ratios by approximately **24 times** compared to BF16.
- Switching to FP16 precision enabled traditional RL algorithms (e.g., GRPO, PG-Seq-IS) to converge stably and achieve nearly **100%** training accuracy on a challenging dataset, while BF16-based methods frequently collapsed or converged slowly.
- The benefits of FP16 generalized across diverse settings, including Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, LoRA-based fine-tuning, larger dense models, and different LLM architectures, consistently leading to higher training rewards and validation performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - BF16's lower precision (7 mantissa bits) is a primary source of numerical instability in RL fine-tuning, causing policies to diverge even with identical model weights.
> - FP16's significantly higher precision (10 mantissa bits) effectively eliminates the training-inference mismatch by ensuring numerical consistency between inference and training policies.
> - By addressing the root cause of the mismatch, FP16 simplifies RL fine-tuning, allowing even simple, unbiased policy gradient estimators to perform robustly and effectively, rendering complex algorithmic corrections less necessary.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical and persistent challenge in the field of LLM fine-tuning: the instability and sub-optimal performance often encountered when applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) techniques. RL fine-tuning has proven to be a powerful paradigm for enhancing LLM capabilities, particularly in areas like reasoning and alignment with human preferences. However, the process is notoriously fragile, prone to training collapse, and highly sensitive to hyperparameters.

A key source of this instability, as identified by recent works, is the "training-inference mismatch." This discrepancy arises because modern RL frameworks typically employ different computational engines for fast inference (generating responses or "rollouts") and for training (computing gradients). While these engines are mathematically designed to be identical, numerical discrepancies due to precision errors and hardware-specific optimizations cause the policies used in inference ($\mu$) and training ($\pi$) to diverge. This divergence leads to biased gradients during optimization and a "deployment gap," where the model optimized under the training engine is not truly optimal for the inference engine used in deployment.

Prior research has attempted to mitigate this mismatch through two main avenues:

- **Algorithmic Corrections:** These involve introducing patches based on importance sampling (IS) to correct the biased gradients. Examples include token-level Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS) and sequence-level Masked Importance Sampling (MIS), often applied to algorithms like Group Reinforcement Policy Optimization (GRPO). While these methods can prolong training stability, they suffer from significant drawbacks:
 - **Computational Inefficiency:** They often require an extra forward pass to compute importance sampling ratios, adding substantial overhead (e.g., 25% to training cost).
 - **Inherent Limitations:** Token-level corrections can be insufficient and lead to biased gradients, while sequence-level ratios, though more principled, suffer from high variance and slow convergence.
 - **Deployment Gap Persistence:** These corrections only address the bias *during* training; they do not eliminate the fundamental mismatch, meaning the final model may still not be optimal for deployment.
- **Engineering Alignments:** These approaches attempt to manually align the training and inference implementations. Efforts include using FP32 for the language model head or custom alignment procedures. However, these are often insufficient, require deep domain knowledge, substantial engineering effort, and lack generalizability across different frameworks or models.

This paper critically re-evaluates these existing strategies, arguing that they are either insufficient, inefficient, or overly complex because they address the *symptoms* rather than the *root cause* of the mismatch. By identifying floating-point precision itself as the fundamental problem, the authors propose a much simpler, more efficient, and universally applicable solution that directly tackles the source of instability. This work shifts the focus from complex algorithmic or engineering workarounds to a foundational aspect of numerical computation, potentially redefining the best practices for RL fine-tuning of LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **fundamentally defeat the training-inference mismatch** that plagues Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs). The authors aim to achieve this by pinpointing the root cause of the numerical discrepancies and offering a simple, universally applicable solution.

The key motivations driving this work are:

- **Addressing Instability and Collapse:** The current state of RL fine-tuning is characterized by high instability, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and frequent training collapses. The authors are motivated to find a robust method that enables more reliable and consistent performance improvements.
- **Identifying the Root Cause:** Instead of building upon complex algorithmic or engineering fixes that merely alleviate symptoms, the authors seek to identify the core reason for the training-inference mismatch. Their hypothesis, which they subsequently prove, is that floating-point precision itself is the culprit.
- **Simplifying the RL Fine-Tuning Process:** Existing solutions are computationally expensive (e.g., requiring extra forward passes for importance sampling) or demand extensive engineering effort for manual alignment. The motivation is to introduce a solution that is simple to implement, requires minimal code changes, and avoids architectural or algorithmic modifications.
- **Achieving Superior Performance and Efficiency:** The ultimate goal is to enable more stable optimization, faster convergence, and stronger performance across diverse RL tasks and LLM architectures. By eliminating the mismatch at its source, the authors aim to restore RL to its "purest importance weighted policy-gradient form," thereby closing the "deployment gap" where models optimized for training do not perform optimally during inference.
- **Challenging the Status Quo:** The widely adopted BF16 precision, while excellent for pre-training due to its wide dynamic range, is identified as problematic for RL fine-tuning due to its lower precision. The paper aims to motivate a broader reconsideration of precision trade-offs specifically for the RL fine-tuning phase of LLMs.

In essence, the paper seeks to provide a clean, efficient, and universally applicable solution to a critical challenge in RL-based LLM alignment, paving the way for more robust and high-performing models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this paper revolves around a hypothesis-driven investigation into the numerical root cause of the training-inference mismatch, followed by extensive empirical validation across diverse settings.

#### 3.1. Hypothesis: Floating-Point Precision as the Root Cause

The central hypothesis is that the widely adopted BFloat16 (BF16) precision, despite its large dynamic range, introduces significant rounding errors that accumulate and cause numerical divergence between the training and inference policies. The authors propose that **reverting to Float16 (FP16)**, with its higher numerical precision (more mantissa bits), can effectively eliminate this mismatch.

#### 3.2. Technical Justification (FP16 vs. BF16)

- **Floating-Point Formats:** The paper provides a clear comparison of FP16 and BF16.
 - **FP16 (IEEE 754 half-precision):** Allocates 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits. Offers high precision but has a limited dynamic range, making it susceptible to overflow and underflow.
 - **BF16 (bfloat16):** Allocates 8 exponent bits (matching FP32 range) and only 7 mantissa bits. Provides a wide dynamic range but sacrifices precision.
- **Rationale for FP16's Efficacy:** The authors argue that in RL fine-tuning, the model's weights and activations already have a stable dynamic range established during pre-training. Thus, BF16's wide range is less critical, while its low precision becomes a dominant drawback. FP16's 8 times higher precision (2^10 vs. 2^7 mantissa values) ensures that minor implementation differences between training and inference engines (e.g., CUDA kernel optimizations, parallelization strategies) are absorbed, preventing rounding errors from accumulating and diverging the policies.
- **Addressing FP16's Limitations (Loss Scaling):** The paper acknowledges FP16's limited dynamic range and its historical need for "loss scaling" to prevent gradient underflow. However, it emphasizes that loss scaling is a mature, standard technique in modern deep learning frameworks (e.g., PyTorch, Megatron, DeepSpeed) and can be enabled with minimal code changes, making FP16 practical.

#### 3.3. Experimental Validation Strategy

The authors employ a multi-faceted experimental strategy to validate their hypothesis:

1. **Offline Analysis of Mismatch:**
 - They first quantitatively measure the training-inference mismatch under BF16 and FP16.
 - This involves generating responses using an inference policy and then evaluating token-level log-probabilities and sequence-level log-probability ratios using the *same model weights* within a training engine, but under different precision settings.
 - Metrics include KL divergence and visual inspection of probability distributions.

2. **Sanity Test for RL Algorithms:**
 - **Novel Dataset:** A "perfectible" dataset is constructed from the MATH dataset, filtering for problems where the initial model accuracy is between 20% and 80%. This ensures problems are solvable but not trivial, allowing for clear evaluation of an RL algorithm's ability to learn.
 - **Evaluation Criterion:** An algorithm passes if it converges above a high training accuracy threshold (e.g., 95%) on this dataset.
 - **Experimental Setup:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B model, 8 NVIDIA A100 80G GPUs, batch size 64 questions (8 rollouts/question), 4 gradient steps per policy iteration.
 - **Algorithms Tested:** Vanilla GRPO, GRPO with token-level TIS, GRPO with sequence-level MIS, standard Policy Gradient with Importance Sampling (PG-Seq-IS), and GSPO.
 - **Frameworks:** Experiments are conducted on two independent RL frameworks: VeRL and Oat, to ensure robustness and rule out implementation-specific artifacts.

3. **Ablation Study on Precision Combinations:**
 - To isolate the impact of inference and training precision, an ablation study is performed using vLLM for inference and PyTorch FSDP for training.
 - Combinations tested include various pairings of FP16, BF16, and FP32 for both inference and training.

4. **Generalization Across Diverse Settings:**
 - **MoE RL:** Experiments are conducted on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base) with different RL algorithms (GRPO-Seq-MIS, GRPO-Token-TIS, PG-Seq-TIS), known for increased instability due to complex parallelization and expert selection.
 - **LoRA RL:** Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) based RL (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) is tested to see if precision affects efficient fine-tuning methods.
 - **Large Dense Models:** Experiments with larger dense LLMs (Qwen3-14B-Base) using DAPO algorithm.
 - **Other Model Families:** Validation on a model mid-trained from Llama3.2-3B (OctoThinker-3B) to confirm generalizability beyond Qwen-based architectures.

Across all experiments, the primary comparison involves training reward curves (e.g., accuracy, validation scores) and analysis of training stability, convergence speed, and the observed training-inference mismatch under BF16 versus FP16 precision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a clear and consistent set of findings demonstrating the superior efficacy of FP16 precision in addressing the training-inference mismatch in RL fine-tuning of LLMs.

#### 4.1. FP16 Dramatically Reduces Training-Inference Mismatch (Offline Analysis)

- The offline analysis (Figure 2) vividly shows that FP16 significantly reduces the numerical discrepancy between the inference policy ($\mu$) and the training policy ($\pi$).
- **Token-level Probability:** With FP16, data points representing token probabilities are much more tightly concentrated around the diagonal (representing perfect alignment) compared to BF16, indicating higher numerical consistency.
- **Sequence-level Log-Probability Ratios:** BF16 exhibits an exponentially larger mismatch, with log-probability ratios diverging significantly, especially for longer sequences, due to cumulative autoregressive errors. In contrast, FP16 maintains the mismatch at a much milder level, approximately 24 times smaller. This finding directly supports the hypothesis that BF16's lower precision is the root cause of divergence.

#### 4.2. FP16 Stabilizes Training and Outperforms Existing Algorithmic Corrections (Sanity Test)

- **Instability of BF16 Algorithms:** On the "sanity test" dataset, traditional RL algorithms and their algorithmic corrections based on BF16 precision largely failed:
 - Vanilla GRPO collapsed early.
 - Token-level TIS (Yao et al.) prolonged training slightly but still collapsed, failing to achieve high accuracy.
 - GSPO showed better stability than token-level TIS but eventually faced issues (e.g., NaN gradients in VeRL).
 - Sequence-level MIS (Liu et al. [2025a]) maintained stability but suffered from slow convergence due to high variance and exhibited a significant "deployment gap," achieving lower peak accuracy (95% vs. 99% in FP16) and evaluation scores.
- **Efficacy of FP16 Precision:** Simply switching to FP16 for both training and inference provided a dramatic and uniform improvement across all tested algorithms (Figure 1, Figure 3, Figure 6).
 - FP16 training runs were significantly more stable, often reaching near 100% training accuracy on the perfectible dataset without collapse.
 - They converged much faster and achieved substantially higher final rewards and evaluation scores (e.g., AIME 2024).
 - Crucially, even the simple, unbiased policy gradient estimator (PG-Seq-IS, Equation 5), when powered by FP16, dramatically outperformed all existing BF16 algorithmic corrections, demonstrating that FP16 fundamentally improves the behavior of importance sampling by stabilizing the sequence-level ratio.

#### 4.3. Ablation Confirms FP16 for Both Training and Inference is Optimal

- The ablation study (Figure 5) showed that increasing inference precision (e.g., to FP32) when training with BF16 *could* prolong stability but at a prohibitive computational cost (FP32 inference was ~3x slower).
- The combination of **FP16 for both training and inference** yielded the best results, achieving the lowest training-inference mismatch and the most stable training dynamics, reaching nearly 100% training accuracy without any loss of speed.

#### 4.4. Generalization Across Diverse Models, Data, and Training Regimes

The benefits of FP16 consistently generalized across a wide range of challenging scenarios (Figure 1, Figure 6):

- **MoE RL:** For Mixture-of-Experts models (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base), FP16 led to greater stability and consistently higher training accuracies and validation rewards across GRPO-Seq-MIS, GRPO-Token-TIS, and PG-Seq-TIS.
- **LoRA RL:** BF16-based LoRA training (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) collapsed early, while FP16 maintained stable training throughout.
- **Large Dense Models:** For large dense models (Qwen3-14B-Base) using DAPO, FP16 resulted in faster increases in training rewards and higher validation accuracy.
- **Other Model Families:** FP16 consistently stabilized training for OctoThinker-3B (Llama3.2-3B variant), where BF16 training destabilized quickly.

#### 4.5. Impact on Algorithm Performance Differences

- Under FP16 precision, the performance differences between various RL algorithms (GRPO, TIS, MIS, GSPO, PG-Seq-IS) became almost indistinguishable (Figure 4). This suggests that by fundamentally reducing the training-inference mismatch, FP16 transforms the optimization problem into a nearly on-policy setting where complex algorithmic corrections offer little additional benefit.

In summary, the results overwhelmingly indicate that FP16 is a simple, highly effective, and universally applicable solution to the training-inference mismatch, leading to more stable, faster, and higher-performing RL fine-tuning for LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in "Defeating the Training-Inference Mismatch via FP16" carry significant implications for the field of Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning, particularly for Reinforcement Learning (RL) based alignment. The potential impact is multi-faceted:

#### 5.1. Paradigm Shift in Precision Trade-offs for RL Fine-Tuning

The most profound impact is a direct challenge to the prevailing wisdom of using BF16 as the default floating-point precision for all stages of LLM training. While BF16's wide dynamic range is invaluable for the initial, highly volatile pre-training phase, this work demonstrates that for the more constrained and numerically sensitive RL fine-tuning phase, FP16's higher precision is paramount. This necessitates a fundamental **reconsideration of precision choices based on the specific training stage**, suggesting that a "one-size-fits-all" approach to precision is suboptimal.

#### 5.2. Simplification and Democratization of RL Fine-Tuning

The proposed solution is remarkably simple: a few lines of code change to switch from BF16 to FP16, leveraging existing and mature loss-scaling techniques. This simplicity contrasts sharply with the complex algorithmic corrections (requiring extra forward passes and intricate variance reduction strategies) or arduous engineering alignments. This ease of implementation can significantly **lower the barrier to entry** for researchers and practitioners, making stable and high-performing RL fine-tuning more accessible.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Stability, Faster Convergence, and Superior Performance

The empirical results consistently show that FP16 leads to:
- **Elimination of Training Collapse:** A major headache for RL practitioners is effectively resolved, enabling robust and long-term training.
- **Accelerated Convergence:** Models fine-tune much faster, reducing computational time and resource consumption.
- **Higher Final Performance:** The resulting models achieve stronger performance and generalization, closing the "deployment gap" that existing solutions failed to address. This directly translates to more capable and reliable LLMs in real-world applications.

#### 5.4. Resolution of the Bias-Variance Tradeoff in RL

The paper highlights how BF16 exacerbates the bias-variance tradeoff in RL algorithms: low-variance methods are unstable, while low-bias methods suffer from high variance. By fundamentally reducing the training-inference mismatch, FP16 effectively lowers both the bias (from the mismatch) and the variance (of importance sampling ratios). This creates a more benign optimization landscape where even simple, unbiased policy gradient estimators become highly effective, allowing all algorithms to perform well and simplifying algorithmic choices.

#### 5.5. Guidance for Future Research and Development

- **Hardware and Software Optimization:** The findings could spur new optimizations in AI hardware and software frameworks to better support FP16 for RL workloads, ensuring its efficiency while managing its dynamic range limitations.
- **Foundation for Lower Precision Training:** While the paper advocates for FP16, it also acknowledges the industry's push towards even lower precisions like FP8. This work provides valuable context on the critical role of precision for specific phases of training, which will be vital for successfully adopting FP8 and other formats in complex pipelines. The lessons learned about mantissa bits and rounding errors will be crucial for designing robust low-precision training strategies for RL.
- **Broader Impact on LLM Alignment:** Stable and efficient RL fine-tuning is critical for aligning LLMs with human values, enhancing their reasoning capabilities, and improving their safety. By offering a robust foundation for this process, the work indirectly contributes to the development of more beneficial and reliable AI systems.

In conclusion, "Defeating the Training-Inference Mismatch via FP16" provides an elegant, effective, and widely applicable solution to a critical problem in LLM fine-tuning. Its significance lies not just in a technical fix but in prompting a fundamental re-evaluation of how precision is chosen and managed across the intricate lifecycle of large model development, promising more stable, efficient, and performant AI systems.
