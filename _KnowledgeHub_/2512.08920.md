# OSMO: Open-Source Tactile Glove for Human-to-Robot Skill Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> OSMO is an open-source tactile glove platform designed to capture both shear and normal forces from human demonstrations, facilitating direct transfer of these skills to robots. Policies trained using OSMO achieved 71.69% success in a wiping task, outperforming vision-only baselines (55.75%) by eliminating contact-related failures.

## Problem

- Robots lack detailed tactile feedback, particularly shear and normal forces, essential for human-like dexterity in contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- Learning from human demonstrations, predominantly vision-based, fails to capture critical force information, leading to an "information gap" in skill transfer.
- Existing wearable tactile sensors either restrict human movement, offer limited force data, or face challenges like sensor crosstalk and embodiment gap when transferring to robots.

## Method

- Design **OSMO**, an open-source tactile glove, using magnetic sensors to capture comprehensive 3-axis normal and shear forces from human hands without restricting dexterity.
- ==Incorporate MuMetal== shielding and dual magnetometer differential sensing to effectively mitigate multi-sensor crosstalk in the dense wearable array.
- Develop a `Glove2Robot` diffusion policy pipeline to directly train robot policies from multimodal human demonstration data (tactile, vision, and proprioception).

## Results

- The combined MuMetal shielding and dual magnetometer differential sensing approach reduced average RMS noise by **57%** in dense tactile arrays, validating the crosstalk mitigation.
- Robot policies trained with OSMO's tactile feedback achieved a **71.69%** success rate on a wiping task, significantly outperforming vision-only (**55.75%**) and proprioception-only (**27.12%**) baselines.
- Tactile-aware policies eliminated common contact-related failures (e.g., uneven pressure, losing grip) observed in baselines, directly demonstrating the impact of rich tactile data on robust manipulation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rich tactile feedback, including both shear and normal forces, is essential for robots to learn and execute contact-rich manipulation tasks, overcoming the limitations of vision-only data.
> - Multi-sensor crosstalk, a common challenge in dense magnetic tactile arrays, can be significantly reduced through integrated MuMetal shielding and dual-magnetometer differential sensing.
> - Employing a common tactile hardware platform for both human demonstrators and robot hands effectively bridges the embodiment gap, simplifying the transfer of learned policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is heavily influenced by the aspiration to achieve human-level dexterity. A significant bottleneck in this endeavor is the lack of rich, proprioceptive, and exteroceptive data, particularly tactile feedback, which is crucial for contact-rich tasks. Humans instinctively leverage nuanced contact forces to perform delicate manipulations, a capability that most robots, largely reliant on vision, currently lack. This paper directly addresses this fundamental challenge.

Historically, robot learning from human demonstrations has relied predominantly on video data. While human video demonstrations are abundant and accessible, they inherently suffer from a critical information gap: they cannot capture the intricate contact forces, shear, and normal forces that are vital for mastering physical interaction. The visual appearance of an object under different applied forces can be nearly identical, rendering crucial force control invisible to vision-only systems.

Existing approaches to incorporate tactile information or improve human-to-robot transfer have faced various limitations:
- **Wearable Devices for Data Generation:** Some research attempts to attach robot sensors directly to human fingertips, but this often constrains natural human dexterity and limits applications to simple grasps. Exoskeleton gloves integrate tactile sensors and address kinematic retargeting by mechanically matching human motion to robot kinematics, but they are often specific to a particular robot design, requiring a new exoskeleton for each target robot hand. Flexible, glove-based devices, while preserving human dexterity, typically provide only kinematic data or are limited to sensing normal forces, neglecting the equally critical shear forces.
- **Tactile Sensors for Robotics:** A variety of tactile sensing technologies exist. Optical tactile sensors (e.g., GelSight) offer high spatial resolution but are often bulky and difficult to integrate into complex geometries like a human hand. Resistive tactile sensors provide thin and flexible form factors but primarily measure normal forces. Magnetic tactile sensors, which this work builds upon, can capture both shear and normal forces in a thin, skin-like form factor. However, previous magnetic sensor implementations generally used single sensor-magnet pairs rigidly affixed to robots, failing to address the complexities of a dense, multi-sensor wearable array on a human hand, particularly the issue of multi-sensor crosstalk.
- **Learning from Human Demonstration (Vision-only):** Recent advances have allowed robots to learn transferable representations from human videos using techniques like latent skill spaces or explicit representations of hand-object interactions (e.g., affordances, point tracking, hand-object masks). While these methods can capture high-level skills or produce transferable action representations, mapping them to executable robot actions often remains non-trivial, and they fundamentally cannot infer detailed force information for contact-rich manipulation.

OSMO positions itself as a significant advancement by overcoming these limitations. It is presented as the **first flexible platform to capture both shear and normal forces in a wearable glove while preserving full human dexterity**. By minimizing both the visual and tactile embodiment gap—using the same glove for human demonstration and robot deployment—OSMO enables the direct transfer of continuous shear and normal force feedback, obviating the need for complex vision-based force inference or image manipulation (like inpainting) to bridge appearance differences. Furthermore, the paper specifically tackles the engineering challenge of multi-sensor crosstalk in a dense wearable array, a problem largely unaddressed by prior magnetic tactile sensing research. This work directly pushes the boundaries of human-to-robot skill transfer, particularly for tasks requiring fine-grained contact control, by making rich tactile data readily available and transferable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for developing OSMO stems from the inherent limitation of vision-only systems in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring precise contact and force control. Humans leverage tactile feedback extensively to understand object properties, maintain stable grasps, and execute delicate movements, capabilities that robots struggle to replicate when relying solely on visual cues. The ambiguity where different applied forces result in visually identical outcomes highlights a fundamental challenge for robot learning from human demonstrations.

The paper explicitly outlines five key design objectives for the OSMO tactile glove, which collectively form its core motivation:

1. **Unrestrained Human Dexterity during Demonstration Collection:** The goal is to avoid imposing physical constraints on human demonstrators, allowing for natural, "in-the-wild" data collection. This maximizes the realism and diversity of collected human skills, which can then be kinematically retargeted to advanced dexterous robot hands without compromising human movement.
2. **Rich Normal and Shear Force Sensing:** Recognizing that human manipulation involves both normal (perpendicular to surface) and shear (parallel to surface) forces, the glove is designed to capture full 3-axis force feedback. This goes beyond existing wearable sensors that often focus only on normal forces, thus providing a more complete picture of contact interaction.
3. **Full Hand Tactile Coverage:** Dexterous manipulation often involves contact across the entire hand surface, not just the fingertips. OSMO aims to provide comprehensive tactile coverage on both the fingertips and the palm, crucial for tasks requiring stabilization and varied grasping strategies, while also accommodating different hand sizes.
4. **Broad Compatibility with In-the-Wild Hand Tracking Methods:** For human demonstrations to be useful for robot learning, accurate hand and wrist pose information (action labels) is required. The glove is designed to be visually low-profile and appropriately colored to minimize disruption to state-of-the-art vision-based hand tracking systems, including those used in AR/VR headsets and generic RGB videos. It also supports integration with other tracking gloves (e.g., Manus) without electromagnetic interference.
5. **Deployable on Both Human and Robot Hands:** A major hurdle in human-to-robot skill transfer is the "embodiment gap" – differences in physical form, kinematics, and sensory feedback between human and robot hardware. By designing a glove that can be worn by both human demonstrators and biomimetic robot hands (like the Psyonic Ability Hand), OSMO aims to minimize this gap, enabling a more direct and seamless transfer of learned policies.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to create an accessible, open-source tool that enriches human demonstrations with high-fidelity tactile data, thereby enabling robots to learn and execute complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks with human-like dexterity, all while minimizing the technical overhead associated with traditional human-to-robot transfer pipelines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in the paper encompasses both the detailed hardware design of the OSMO tactile glove and a robust algorithmic pipeline for human-to-robot skill transfer.

**Hardware Design (OSMO Tactile Glove):**
- **Sensing Principle:** OSMO utilizes magnetic tactile sensors, building upon prior work but adapting it for a wearable glove form factor. Each sensor consists of two 3-axis magnetometers (Bosch BMM350) and a 6-axis IMU (Bosch BHI360, though the IMUs are not used in this specific work). These magnetometers detect changes in magnetic flux caused by the deformation of nearby soft magnetic elastomers. The elastomers are made from silicone mixed with magnetic microparticles and are axially magnetized. When the elastomer is pressed or sheared, its magnetic field changes relative to the magnetometers, and these changes are directly correlated to applied XYZ forces. The raw magnetic flux signals (μT) are used as tactile data.
- **Sensor Layout and Coverage:** The glove is equipped with 12 taxels: one on each of the five fingertips and seven distributed across the palm. This layout is chosen to provide comprehensive coverage of primary contact regions while allowing for flexible finger lengths to accommodate various hand sizes.
- **Assembly:** Magnetic patches are adhered to small sensor PCBs. Crucially, a **MuMetal magnetic shield** is integrated between the sensor PCB and the glove fabric. The PCBs are wired in series using tinsel wire to an STM32 microcontroller at the wrist, which aggregates data and streams it via USB-C. An outer beige glove provides a uniform appearance and protects the electronics.
- **Multi-Sensor Crosstalk Mitigation:** A key innovation for scaling magnetic sensors to a dense array is addressing crosstalk. The paper introduces two techniques:
 1. **MuMetal Shielding:** MuMetal, a high-permeability alloy, is used to attenuate external magnetic fields, preventing magnetic interference between adjacent sensors.
 2. **Dual Magnetometer Differential Sensing:** Each taxel incorporates two magnetometers. By taking the differential reading between these two, common-mode noise, such as interference from Earth's magnetic field or distant magnets, can be significantly reduced.
 These mitigation strategies were experimentally validated by measuring RMS noise during simulated finger motion and adjacent contact deformation on a robot hand, comparing single unshielded, dual unshielded, and dual shielded configurations.

**Glove2Robot Policy Pipeline:**
The pipeline aims to train robot policies directly from human demonstrations collected with the OSMO glove, without requiring any real robot data for policy learning.
- **Data Collection:** Human demonstrations for a contact-rich wiping task are recorded. A user wears the OSMO glove, and the scene is captured by a RealSense D435 camera (RGB and IR). All data (vision, tactile, hand pose) is logged at 25 Hz as ROS2 bag files. A total of 140 demonstrations (approximately two hours) were collected.
- **Processing Human Demonstration Data:**
 1. **Hand Estimation:** RGB images are processed to estimate human hand pose. SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2) extracts a hand mask, which is then fed to HaMeR (Reconstructing Hands in 3D with Transformers) to obtain 3D keypoints, wrist pose, and a full hand mesh. To refine global position accuracy, stereo depth from the RealSense IR cameras (using FoundationStereo) is combined with the SAM2 mask to create a hand point cloud. The HaMeR mesh is then aligned to the median height of the point cloud around the wrist. Finally, fingertip and wrist trajectories are smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter.
 2. **Kinematics Retargeting:** The refined human fingertip and wrist trajectories are converted into robot joint commands for a Franka Emika arm equipped with a Psyonic Ability Hand. An off-the-shelf inverse kinematics (IK) solver (Mink in MuJoCo) is used. Human fingertip positions serve as direct IK targets for the robot hand, and the human wrist trajectory guides the robot arm. For the contact-rich wiping task, collision detection for the robot hand fingertips and wrist against the ground plane is intentionally disabled to avoid prematurely terminating demonstrations due to minor contact.
- **Policy Training:**
 - **Policy Representation:** A **diffusion policy** is chosen. This model generates action sequences (target joint positions for arm and hand) by iteratively denoising latent trajectories, conditioned on observations. This approach is well-suited for capturing the variability and multi-modality common in human demonstrations.
 - **Network Architecture:** The policy incorporates a multimodal architecture. Robot states (joint positions) and tactile signals are processed by separate MLPs. RGB image features are extracted using a frozen DINOv2 encoder. These encoded features are concatenated to form a global conditioning vector. A U-Net denoising network, conditioned via FiLM, then predicts action sequences.
 - **Data Processing:** Robot states and tactile signals are normalized to a clipped range. Crucially, for tactile data, **differential sensing** is applied by subtracting the readings of the two magnetometers on each sensor PCB *before* feeding them to the MLP encoder, further leveraging the hardware crosstalk mitigation. RGB images are cropped.
 - **Training:** The policy is optimized using the Adam optimizer for 2000 epochs.
- **Policy Evaluation:**
 - **Setup:** Policies are deployed on the real robot (Franka arm + Psyonic Ability Hand, wearing the OSMO glove) in the same environment used for data collection.
 - **Task:** Wiping various marker patterns on a whiteboard.
 - **Metrics:** Success is quantified as the percentage of marker pixels erased. Each policy variant is evaluated over 12 rollouts.
 - **Baselines:** The tactile-aware policy (tactile glove + vision + proprioception) is compared against vision-only (vision + proprioception) and proprioception-only baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded significant findings in both hardware design and policy performance for human-to-robot skill transfer:

**1. Effectiveness of Multi-Sensor Crosstalk Mitigation:**
- **Quantitative Improvement:** Experiments demonstrated that the combined approach of **MuMetal shielding and dual magnetometer differential sensing** significantly reduced crosstalk noise. Compared to a single unshielded magnetometer, this method achieved an average RMS noise reduction of 57%. When compared to a dual-magnetometer but unshielded configuration, it still showed an 18% improvement in average noise reduction across various scenarios (finger motion and adjacent contact deformation).
- **Axis-Specific Effects:** The MuMetal shielding was particularly effective in attenuating magnetic flux in the X-Y plane. While an expected increase in Z-axis noise was observed (due to field line concentration), the overall average noise reduction across all axes justified this trade-off.
- **Signal Fidelity:** Given that a 1 Newton applied force generates magnetic signals in the range of 300 μT, the measured RMS noise in the tens of μT indicates a good signal-to-noise ratio, affirming the practical utility of the sensing system. This confirms that the hardware design effectively addresses the challenges of densely integrating magnetic tactile sensors in a wearable form factor.

**2. Superior Performance of Tactile-Aware Policies in Contact-Rich Tasks:**
- **Enhanced Success Rate:** The robot policy trained exclusively on human demonstrations collected with the OSMO tactile glove (utilizing tactile glove data, vision, and proprioception) achieved an average success rate of **71.69%** on the challenging real-world wiping task. Success was measured by the percentage of marker pixels erased.
- **Outperforming Vision-Only Baselines:** This tactile-aware policy significantly outperformed its vision-only counterpart (vision + proprioception), which achieved 55.75% success. The proprioception-only baseline performed the worst, with a mere 27.12% success rate, highlighting the importance of rich sensory input.
- **Elimination of Contact-Related Failures:** A crucial qualitative finding was that policies *without tactile feedback* frequently failed due to issues directly related to insufficient or inconsistent contact, such as:
 - Uneven or no pressure applied to the sponge.
 - Losing grip on the sponge during the wiping motion.
 The integration of continuous shear and normal force feedback from the OSMO glove **eliminated these specific contact-related failure modes**, showcasing the direct benefit of tactile sensing for robust manipulation.
- **Non-Tactile Failure Modes:** All policies, including the tactile-aware one, still experienced some failures related to not wiping the entire marker pattern. These failures were attributed to accumulated position errors arising from human hand pose estimation, kinematic retargeting, and camera calibration inaccuracies, rather than a deficiency in tactile feedback.

**3. Successful Bridging of the Embodiment Gap:**
- The shared glove platform (OSMO worn by both human demonstrator and robot) successfully minimized the visual domain gap. This avoided the need for complex preprocessing steps like image inpainting or hand-masking, which are commonly required to reconcile visual differences between human and robot hands.
- The direct transfer of continuous tactile feedback from human to robot, without requiring real robot tactile data for training, was successfully demonstrated.

**4. Open-Source Contribution:**
- The release of complete hardware designs, firmware, and assembly instructions represents a significant contribution to the robotics community, lowering the barrier for others to adopt and build upon this technology.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that OSMO is a robust, open-source hardware solution that effectively captures and transfers critical tactile information from humans to robots. This capability leads to significantly improved robot performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks by enabling force-aware policies and eliminating common contact-related failure modes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OSMO tactile glove represents a significant advancement in the field of human-robot interaction and dexterous manipulation, with broad implications and potential impact:

**1. Enabling Direct Human-to-Robot Skill Transfer for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The most profound impact of OSMO is its ability to facilitate the transfer of complex, contact-rich manipulation skills from humans to robots **without requiring any real robot data for training**. This directly addresses a long-standing challenge in robotics: how to imbue robots with the nuanced dexterity that humans possess for tasks requiring delicate touch, variable pressure, and compliant interaction. By providing continuous shear and normal force feedback, OSMO allows robots to learn skills that are fundamentally difficult, if not impossible, to infer from vision alone.

**2. Lowering Barriers to Robot Learning:** By open-sourcing the complete hardware designs, firmware, and assembly instructions, OSMO significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners in robot learning. This accessibility can democratize access to advanced tactile sensing technology, enabling more widespread experimentation and data collection with rich contact information. This is crucial for accelerating progress in areas like dexterous manipulation, where hardware often remains expensive and proprietary.

**3. Minimizing the Embodiment Gap:** The innovative design choice of using the *same glove* for both human demonstration and robot deployment is highly impactful. It effectively minimizes both the visual and tactile embodiment gaps, simplifying the data processing pipeline by eliminating the need for complex domain adaptation techniques (e.g., image inpainting, hand masking) and ensuring direct compatibility of sensory feedback. This streamlined approach makes the transfer process more robust and efficient.

**4. Accelerating Research in Tactile Robotics and Human-in-the-Loop Learning:**
- **New Datasets:** OSMO will likely spur the creation of new, large-scale human tactile demonstration datasets. Such datasets are currently scarce but are critical for training more capable and generalizable robot policies, especially with the rise of large behavior models in robotics.
- **Benchmarking:** It provides a standardized platform for benchmarking different robot learning algorithms in contact-rich scenarios, allowing for more direct comparisons and identification of superior approaches.
- **Fusion of Modalities:** The work highlights the power of multimodal learning (vision + proprioception + tactile). Future research can explore more sophisticated methods for fusing these modalities, perhaps incorporating the onboard IMUs for improved hand pose tracking under occlusion.

**5. Advancing Dexterous Manipulation and Generalization:**
- **Robustness:** Policies trained with tactile feedback are demonstrably more robust to contact-related failures, leading to higher success rates in tasks requiring sustained pressure or delicate interaction. This increased robustness is essential for deploying robots in unstructured, real-world environments.
- **Generalization:** The ability to learn from human intuition and dexterity, enriched by tactile data, could lead to policies that generalize better across variations in object properties, textures, and environmental conditions.
- **New Task Domains:** Beyond wiping, OSMO could unlock capabilities for robots in numerous other domains requiring fine motor skills, such as object assembly, manipulating deformable materials (e.g., cloth folding), precise surgical tasks, or even delicate handling of fragile items.

**6. Inspiration for Future Hardware and Algorithms:** The paper itself identifies several avenues for future work, including hardware improvements like denser sensor layouts (e.g., wraparound fingertip coverage) and the integration of IMU data for improved pose estimation. Algorithmically, it opens doors for exploring multi-finger and bimanual coordination tasks, and how tactile feedback can improve data efficiency and generalization across visual variations. This self-reflection demonstrates the generative potential of OSMO for future research.

In conclusion, OSMO is more than just a tactile glove; it is a foundational open-source system that addresses a critical gap in robot learning from human demonstrations. By making high-fidelity tactile data accessible and directly transferable, it paves the way for robots to achieve unprecedented levels of dexterity and autonomy in contact-rich manipulation, significantly advancing the capabilities of robotic systems.
