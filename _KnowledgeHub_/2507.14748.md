# Skill Learning via Policy Diversity Yields Identifiable Representations for Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers provided the first theoretical identifiability guarantee for learned features in reinforcement learning, demonstrating that methods like Contrastive Successor Features (CSF) can recover ground-truth environmental features up to a linear transformation. Empirical validation showed high R² scores in diverse environments and highlighted the importance of skill diversity for this identifiability.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents face challenges with sparse rewards, inefficient exploration, and the need for complex reward function engineering.
- Self-supervised skill discovery methods (MISL) exhibit inconsistent performance, lacking clear theoretical explanations for why certain architectures or mutual information parametrizations are more effective.
- A theoretical gap existed in providing identifiability guarantees for learned representations in RL, particularly in partially observable environments.

## Method

- The research models the Reinforcement Learning problem as a ==Data Generating Process== (DGP) to apply identifiability theory from nonlinear Independent Component Analysis (ICA).
- It theoretically analyzes Contrastive Successor Features (CSF), demonstrating its alignment with conditions required for identifiable representation learning.
- The approach leverages specific mutual information objectives (I(s, s'; z)) and an inner product parametrization for the critic to learn representations that are linearly identifiable.

## Results

- CSF achieved high R² scores (up to **0.99**) for learned features and feature differences in MuJoCo and DeepMind Control environments, empirically validating linear identifiability.
- Sufficient skill diversity, particularly continuous sampling from the skill distribution, was demonstrated as crucial for high identifiability (R² scores) and increased state coverage.
- The dimensionality of the learned latent space directly impacts identifiability, with dimensions lower than the true state space acting as an information bottleneck, and a latent space equal to state dimensionality often maximizing zero-shot task transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Under specific conditions, Contrastive Successor Features (CSF) provably recover the environment's ground-truth features up to a linear transformation.
> - Optimizing the mutual information I(s, s'; z) (between consecutive states and skill) enforces a "locality" constraint, yielding a more meaningful and identifiable latent space geometry compared to I(s; z).
> - Maximum entropy skill-conditioned policies do not promote the necessary skill diversity for identifiability, as they break the unique dependence between state transitions and skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) faces challenges such as sparse rewards, environment exploration, and the need for reward function design. Self-supervised learning (SSL) techniques, including curiosity-driven exploration, intrinsic motivation, and unsupervised skill discovery (USD), have been proposed as solutions. Many of these methods employ information-theoretic principles, often termed mutual information skill learning (MISL), to learn representations and encourage exploration. However, the theoretical underpinnings of why some MISL methods perform better than others, particularly regarding the role of representations and mutual information parametrization, have been less clear.

This work integrates insights from the fields of self-supervised learning (SSL), nonlinear Independent Component Analysis (ICA), and Causal Representation Learning (CRL) into the domain of RL. Specifically, it builds upon recent theoretical advancements in identifiability, which guarantee the recovery of underlying latent factors (ground-truth features) from high-level observations, even in complex scenarios like partially observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs). Previous work in nonlinear ICA has demonstrated that estimating mutual information alone is insufficient for learning "useful" representations without additional assumptions. This paper aims to identify the distinguishing factors of successful MISL methods by applying identifiability theory, thereby contributing to a deeper theoretical understanding of self-supervised representation learning in RL. It connects the concept of diverse policies in RL to diversity and variability conditions found in ICA and CRL literature, suggesting that the success of MISL stems from learning identifiable features.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the theoretical foundation of mutual information skill learning (MISL), particularly focusing on the Contrastive Successor Features (CSF) method, through the lens of identifiable representation learning. The motivations are multi-faceted:

- **Addressing the performance variability of MISL methods:** Despite similar information-theoretic objectives, MISL methods exhibit wide-ranging performance. The theoretical reasons for these discrepancies, and for empirical guidelines suggesting specific parametrizations (e.g., action-value functions), are not well understood. This work aims to provide a theoretical explanation for these observed differences.
- **Providing identifiability guarantees for RL representations:** In partially observable environments, it is crucial to infer ground-truth states from observations. The paper aims to establish the first identifiability guarantee for representation learning in RL, specifically proving that CSF can recover the environment's ground-truth features up to a linear transformation. This guarantee is significant because it implies that the learned representations capture the fundamental properties of the environment.
- **Explaining the role of mutual information parametrization and skill diversity:** The research seeks to elucidate how specific design choices in MISL, such as the inner product parametrization of features and the encouragement of skill diversity (in a discriminative sense), contribute to learning identifiable representations. It also aims to explain the implications of different mutual information objectives and the potential drawbacks of entropy regularizers.
- **Connecting RL with SSL and Causal Representation Learning (CRL):** The paper is motivated by growing connections between RL (where agents perform interventions/actions), SSL (which often relies on information-theoretic principles), and causality (which models cause-effect relationships and requires interventions for identification). By demonstrating identifiability in MISL, the authors aim to strengthen these conceptual links and show how RL agents, through diverse skill learning, can identify environmental ground-truth states.
- **Formulating practical recommendations:** Beyond theoretical understanding, the research aims to derive practical insights and recommendations for designing more effective MISL algorithms, such as quantifying what constitutes a diverse policy and analyzing the role of feature dimensionality and skill space coverage.

In essence, the work seeks to provide a rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirical success of certain MISL architectures and objectives, thereby advancing the understanding of self-supervised representation learning in reinforcement learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research combines theoretical proofs, drawing heavily from existing identifiability theory in nonlinear ICA, with empirical validation in standard reinforcement learning environments.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Approach

The core theoretical approach involves analyzing the Contrastive Successor Features (CSF) method by Zheng et al. (2024) through the framework of identifiability, specifically adapting results from Reizinger et al. (2024) on self-supervised learning identifiability. The paper posits that the success of MISL methods stems from a combination of mutual information estimation under diverse policies and an inner product parametrization of the model.

1. **Modeling the MISL Problem as a Data Generating Process (DGP):** To apply ICA theory, the partially observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) of the RL problem is mapped to a DGP. Skills (zᵢ) are modeled as high-dimensional unit vectors sampled from a prior distribution (e.g., uniform on a hypersphere). Observations (o) are generated by a function `g(s)` of the latent state `s`. The objective is to recover `s` from `o`. Crucially, the paper assumes a specific form for the conditional distribution `p(s' - s | z)`, where feature differences `s' - s` are defined on a hypersphere.
2. **Matching CSF's Assumptions to ICA Identifiability Conditions:** The authors demonstrate that CSF's design aligns with the assumptions required for identifiability in nonlinear ICA. These assumptions (Assumption 1) include:
 - Skills (zᵢ) are unit-normalized and form an affine generator system of `Rᵈ`.
 - Skill-conditioned feature differences `p(s' - s | z)` follow a von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution with mean `z`.
 - The marginal distribution of `(s' - s)` is uniform on the hypersphere.
 - Each pair of features `(s' - s)` uniquely corresponds to one skill, implying diverse skills.
 - The critic `q(z | ϕ(o), ϕ(o'))` uses an encoder `ϕ` to learn features `ϕ(o)` and `ϕ(o')` with an inner product parametrization `[ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)]ᵀzᵢ`, and optimizes a contrastive objective (Equation 1, equivalent to a cross-entropy loss).
 - Observations `o` are generated by an injective generator function `g(s)`.
 The feasibility of these assumptions is discussed, drawing on empirical observations from prior work on CSF.
3. **Proving Identifiability:** Building on these matched assumptions, Theorem 1 (adapted from Reizinger et al., 2024) is invoked, stating that if Assumption 1 holds and the objective is globally minimized, the composite function `h = ϕ ◦ g` is a linear map from `Sᵈ⁻¹` to `Rᵈ`. This implies that `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o) = A[s' - s]` for some linear map `A`. Proposition 1 extends this, arguing that due to the inner product parametrization of the critic, the individual features `ϕ(o)` and `ϕ(o')` are also identified up to the same linear transformation `A`.
4. **Deriving Insights from ICA Theory:**
 - **Mutual Information Formulation:** The paper analyzes the implications of optimizing `I(s, s'; z)` versus `I(s; z)`. It argues that `I(s, s'; z)` promotes distinct yet close embeddings for consecutive states by incentivizing `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)` to be parallel to `z`, avoiding collapse or antipodal features.
 - **Maximum Entropy Policies:** Lemma 1 demonstrates that a maximum entropy skill-conditioned policy is not diverse. Such a policy breaks the dependence of state transitions on the skill, making it impossible for a discriminative model to infer the skill from state pairs, thus preventing optimal reward maximization.

#### 3.2. Empirical Approach

The empirical section aims to validate the theoretical claims regarding feature identifiability.

1. **Environments:** Experiments are conducted using the MuJoCo and DeepMind Control (DMC) environments, specifically Half Cheetah, Ant, Quadruped State, and Quadruped Pixel. This allows testing with both direct state observations and pixel-based observations.
2. **Algorithm:** The CSF algorithm is used for self-supervised pretraining, with monitoring of learned successor features.
3. **Metrics:**
 - **Identifiability (R² score):** A linear map `A` is fit between the learned features `ϕ(o)` (or feature differences `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)`) and the ground-truth states `s` by minimizing the squared error. The coefficient of determination (R²) is reported to quantify the linear relationship, with higher scores indicating stronger identifiability.
 - **State Coverage:** This metric assesses the extent of environmental exploration, indicating the diversity of behaviors learned by the agent.
 - **Oracle Return:** This evaluates zero-shot task transfer performance by taking the best rollout among sampled skills, measuring the utility of the diverse skill set for downstream tasks without explicit reward training.
4. **Experimental Variations:**
 - **Number of Skills:** The effect of varying the number of fixed `zᵢ` vectors (discrete skill set) versus continuous sampling from `p(z)` is investigated to understand the impact of skill diversity on identifiability and coverage.
 - **Latent Space Dimensionality:** The dimensionality of the learned feature space is varied to examine its impact on identifiability and zero-shot task transfer, particularly in relation to the true state space dimensionality.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both theoretical proofs and empirical validations supporting the claim that skill diversity in MISL, particularly in CSF, leads to identifiable representations.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Identifiability of CSF Features (Theorem 1 and Proposition 1):** The core theoretical finding is that under specified assumptions (Assumption 1), CSF can provably recover the environment's ground-truth features. Specifically, the learned feature differences `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)` are identified up to a linear transformation of the true state differences `[s' - s]`. Furthermore, due to the inner product parametrization of the critic, the individual features `ϕ(o)` and `ϕ(o')` are also identified up to the same linear transformation. This provides a formal explanation for the effectiveness of CSF in learning useful representations.
- **Implications of Mutual Information Objective (Figure 2):** The analysis reveals that the choice of mutual information objective influences the geometry of the learned latent space. Optimizing `I(s, s'; z)` (using `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)` in the reward/critic) imposes a desirable "locality" constraint, ensuring that embeddings of consecutive states are distinct yet close. In contrast, optimizing `I(s; z)` (using `ϕ(o)` or `ϕ(o')` directly) could lead to collapsed or antipodal features, which are not realistic modeling assumptions for consecutive states.
- **Suboptimality of Maximum Entropy Policies (Lemma 1):** The study demonstrates that a maximum entropy skill-conditioned policy is not diverse. Such a policy renders the skill-conditioned actions independent of the skill `z`, thereby preventing a discriminative model from uniquely inferring `z` from state transitions. This implies that strong entropy regularizers for policies can hinder skill diversity and, consequently, representation identifiability.

#### 4.2. Empirical Results

- **Strong Identifiability of CSF (Figure 3):** CSF exhibits high R² scores for both `ϕ(o)` (individual features) and `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)` (feature differences) in state-based MuJoCo and DMC environments (Half Cheetah, Ant, Quadruped State). This empirically validates the theoretical claim of linear identifiability. In the pixel-based Quadruped environment, `ϕ(o)` still shows high R² scores, while feature differences `ϕ(o') - ϕ(o)` show a more moderate, but still substantial, linear relationship. The high R² scores are accompanied by increased state coverage and oracle return, suggesting a correlation between identifiability and effective exploration and zero-shot task transfer.
- **Crucial Role of Skill Diversity (Figure 4, Figure 6):** Experiments show that a sufficient number of diverse skills is essential. When a small, fixed set of skills is used, it violates Assumption 1(i) (skills spanning `Rᵈ`), leading to lower R² scores for identifiability and reduced state coverage. As the number of fixed skills increases, both R² and coverage improve, peaking when skills are continuously sampled from `p(z)`, which emulates an infinite and diverse skill set. This confirms that skill diversity is not merely a theoretical requirement but a practical necessity for learning identifiable representations and comprehensive exploration.
- **Impact of Latent Space Dimensionality (Figure 5, Figure 7):** The dimensionality of the latent feature space influences both identifiability and task transfer. A latent space with lower dimensionality than the true state space acts as an information bottleneck, preventing linear decoding of the ground-truth states and resulting in lower R² scores. However, a smaller latent space can sometimes aid task transfer, though the relationship is non-monotonic. The study finds that a latent space dimensionality equal to the state dimensionality can serve as a local maximizer for the oracle return, indicating a balanced trade-off.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the theoretical understanding of reinforcement learning and self-supervised representation learning.

- **First Identifiability Guarantee in RL:** The paper provides the first theoretical identifiability guarantee for learned features in reinforcement learning environments. By proving that methods like CSF can recover ground-truth environmental features up to a linear transformation, it offers a fundamental explanation for their empirical success. This moves beyond mere performance observations to a deeper understanding of what these methods are actually learning.
- **Explanation for MISL Success and Design Choices:** The work explains why specific design choices in MISL methods, such as the use of an inner product parametrization for the critic and the emphasis on skill diversity, are critical. It clarifies that these elements are not arbitrary but align with conditions necessary for identifiable representation learning. This insight can guide future algorithm design, allowing practitioners to make more informed decisions about model architectures and objectives.
- **Insights into Mutual Information Objectives and Regularizers:** By analyzing different mutual information objectives, the research highlights the importance of `I(s, s'; z)` over `I(s; z)` for promoting a meaningful geometry in the latent space. Furthermore, the demonstration that maximum entropy policies hinder skill diversity provides a theoretical basis for avoiding strong entropy regularizers in skill learning, which has practical implications for policy optimization.
- **Bridging RL, SSL, and Causal Representation Learning:** The paper strengthens the conceptual links between these fields by showing how agents taking diverse actions (interventions) in RL naturally generate data that satisfy the conditions for causal and identifiable representation learning. This interdisciplinary connection opens avenues for cross-pollination of ideas and methods, potentially leading to more robust and interpretable RL systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The theoretical framework and empirical validations established in this work provide a foundation for further research into the identifiability of representations in more complex or varied RL scenarios. It prompts questions about extending these guarantees to a broader range of environments and algorithms, investigating the interplay between identifiability, generalization, and transfer learning, and exploring how to effectively quantify and optimize for policy diversity. The insights could also lead to new metrics for evaluating learned representations in RL beyond task-specific performance.
