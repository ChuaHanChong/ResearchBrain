# Deriving Hyperparameter Scaling Laws via Modern Optimization Theory

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at King Abdullah University of Science and Technology, ETH Zürich, and Mila derived explicit power-law scaling predictions for learning rate, momentum, and batch size by minimizing a convergence bound for modern optimizers. The analysis consistently shows an optimal performance decay rate of T^(-1/4) with respect to the total token budget T, offering a theoretical basis for empirically observed hyperparameter rules.

## Problem

- Empirical hyperparameter tuning for large-scale deep learning models is computationally expensive and resource-intensive.
- Existing empirical hyperparameter scaling laws often lack a unified theoretical explanation and can sometimes appear contradictory.
- Prior theoretical work on hyperparameter transfer typically assumes fixed batch sizes, momentum, and training horizons, leaving a gap for other critical dimensions.

## Method

- The study leverages a ==non-convex convergence bound== from modern optimization theory for ==Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO)== methods as a proxy for optimizer performance.
- The proxy objective is analytically minimized across various ==tuning regimes== (fixed momentum, fixed batch size, or joint tuning) with respect to ==learning rate==, ==momentum==, and ==batch size==.
- Asymptotic ==power-law schedules== for optimal hyperparameters and the resulting performance rate are derived as functions of training iterations or total token budget.

## Results

- Under fixed momentum, optimal batch size scales as **T^(1/2)** and learning rate scales as **T^(-1/4)** for a fixed token budget, recovering empirically observed relationships.
- When batch size is fixed, optimal momentum scales as **b * T^(-1/2)** and learning rate as **b * T^(-3/4)**, aligning with timescale preservation arguments.
- With joint tuning of learning rate, momentum, and batch size, the optimal scalings are **b*T^(1/6)**, **eta*T^(-7/12)**, and **alpha*T^(-1/3)**, leading to the consistent **T^(-1/4)** performance rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal performance for LMO-based optimizers consistently scales as O(T^(-1/4)) with respect to the total token budget T across different hyperparameter tuning strategies.
> - Momentum tuning is crucial for achieving optimal performance when batch size growth is constrained by hardware, preventing a non-vanishing noise floor.
> - The batch-size landscape is relatively flat; multiple batch-size scaling laws can achieve the T^(-1/4) optimal rate, with the specific optimal batch size heavily influenced by lower-order terms and the assumed noise model.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep learning, particularly the development and training of large language models (LLMs), increasingly relies on massive computational resources. State-of-the-art performance is often achieved through extensive scaling of model sizes, datasets, and training budgets. Within this context, accurately predicting model performance and optimizing key hyperparameters (such as batch size, learning rate, and momentum) before or during costly training runs is of critical importance.

Historically, the understanding of optimal hyperparameter settings and their scaling behavior has largely been driven by empirical observations. Pioneering works have established scaling laws for neural language models based on quantities like model size, dataset size, and compute budget. However, these empirical findings often lack a unified theoretical explanation, and some recommendations can appear contradictory.

Concurrently, theoretical research has focused on hyperparameter transfer. Methods like μP enable learning rate transfer across different model sizes. Nevertheless, these theoretical results typically assume fixed batch sizes, momentum, and training horizons, forcing practitioners to resort to empirical scaling rules for these other dimensions. These empirical rules are often guided by heuristic arguments, including quadratic analyses of the loss landscape, stochastic differential equation (SDE) approximations of optimizers, timescale preservation arguments, or norm-based views of optimization dynamics.

This paper addresses a gap in the theoretical understanding of hyperparameter scaling for *modern first-order optimizers* across varying batch sizes and training horizons, at a *fixed model scale*. While some theoretical studies have investigated the agreement between convex optimization theory and LLM training, this work aims to align optimization theory more closely with current deep learning practices, particularly for optimizers like Adam and Muon. It seeks to provide a principled, unified framework for deriving these scaling laws, moving beyond purely empirical observation or disparate theoretical insights.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the substantial cost and practical challenges associated with empirical hyperparameter tuning for large-scale deep learning models. Deriving scaling rules through extensive experimentation is resource-intensive and often yields results that are poorly understood or inconsistent across studies. The authors are driven by the need for theory-informed hyperparameter transfer mechanisms that extend beyond merely adapting to different model sizes.

Specifically, the paper identifies the following key objectives:

1. **Re-examine Hyperparameter Scaling Laws**: The work aims to revisit and reformulate hyperparameter scaling laws through the lens of modern optimization theory, leveraging recent advances in convergence bounds for non-convex settings and non-Euclidean geometry. This contrasts with earlier theoretical analyses that often focused on convex problems or simpler optimization landscapes.
2. **Focus on Modern Optimizers**: The research is motivated by the prevalence of adaptive and norm-based optimizers in contemporary deep learning, such as Adam and Muon. It utilizes the Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) framework, which encompasses these optimizers, to ensure the theoretical analysis is directly relevant to current practice.
3. **Derive Explicit Scaling Predictions**: A core objective is to derive explicit, closed-form power-law schedules for key hyperparameters—learning rate ($\eta$), momentum parameter ($\alpha = 1 - \beta$), and batch size ($b$)—as functions of the training iteration ($K$) or total token budget ($T=bK$). These predictions are intended to unify and theoretically underpin scaling aspects that have previously been observed only empirically.
4. **Provide a Unified and Principled Perspective**: The authors seek to offer a cohesive theoretical framework that explains various empirical observations from the literature. This involves treating established convergence bounds as a proxy for optimizer performance and minimizing this proxy across different tuning regimes to systematically derive optimal scaling strategies. The goal is to make this methodology accessible and demonstrate its predictive potential.
5. **Investigate Hyperparameter Interactions**: A specific objective is to understand the interplay between different hyperparameters, particularly the interaction between momentum and batch-size scaling. The study aims to uncover how these parameters should be jointly tuned to achieve optimal performance, identifying whether multiple scaling strategies can be near-optimal.
6. **Inform Practical Hyperparameter Transfer**: Ultimately, the research aims to provide practical insights and theoretical guidelines for hyperparameter transfer, especially when scaling training across different compute budgets (training horizons). This includes understanding when and how momentum tuning becomes critical, particularly under hardware constraints on batch size.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this study is primarily theoretical, centered on minimizing a proxy objective derived from a recent convergence bound for a class of first-order optimization methods.

1. **Problem Formulation and Optimizer Class**: The authors consider the general optimization problem `min f(x)` for a potentially non-convex function `f(x)`, assuming access to mini-batch gradient estimates `g_b`. The focus is on a family of optimizers based on the Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO) method, also known as the Unconstrained Stochastic Conditional Gradient method. This framework includes:
- Normalized SGD with momentum (using the Euclidean norm).
- SignSGD with momentum (using the $L_\infty$ norm), which is theoretically and empirically linked to Adam.
- Muon (using the spectral norm).
 This choice is motivated by the LMO framework's relevance to modern deep learning optimizers and its capacity to demonstrate a non-trivial role for momentum in non-convex stochastic optimization.

2. **Convergence Bound as Proxy**: The core of the methodology is the utilization of a non-convex convergence bound for LMO methods, specifically Theorem 2 from Kovalev. This bound provides an upper limit on the minimum expected dual gradient norm, `min E[||∇f(x_k)||_*]`, over `K` iterations. The bound is formulated as:
 `min E[||∇f(x_k)||_*] ≤ C₁/(ηK) + C₂/(α√(bK)) + C₃(α/b) + C₄η + C₅(η/α)`.
 Where:
- `η` is the learning rate (step size).
- `α = 1 - β` is related to the momentum parameter `β`.
- `b` is the batch size.
- `K` is the number of iterations.
- `C₁, C₂, C₃, C₄, C₅` are constants derived from problem parameters (e.g., initial suboptimality `∆₀`, Lipschitz constant `L`, gradient noise variance `σ`, and norm equivalence constant `ρ`).
 The right-hand side of this inequality is defined as the "proxy objective" or "risk", denoted `risk_K(η, α, b)`. For analyzing token budgets, `K` is substituted with `T/b` to create `risk_T(η, α, b)`.

3. **Minimization of the Proxy Objective**: The central approach involves analytically minimizing this proxy objective with respect to the hyperparameters (`η`, `α`, `b`) under various constraints and assumptions about the training budget. The minimization process aims to find the optimal power-law schedules for these hyperparameters. Three main tuning regimes are investigated:
- **Fixed Momentum and Large Horizon (Simplified Proxy)**: Initially, the momentum parameter `α` is held constant, and a simplified version of the proxy (assuming a large-horizon regime where some terms are dominated) is minimized with respect to `η` and `b`. This is done separately for fixed iteration count (`K`) and fixed token budget (`T=bK`).
- **Fixed Batch Size and Large Horizon (η and α Tuned)**: Here, the batch size `b` is fixed, and `η` and `α` are tuned. The optimization involves minimizing the proxy (again, a leading-order approximation) first over `η` and then over `α` for a fixed token budget `T`.
- **Joint Tuning (η, α, b) under Fixed Token Budget**: This is the most comprehensive scenario, where all three hyperparameters are optimized simultaneously for a given token budget `T`. This involves a multi-step minimization process: first over `η`, then over `b`, and finally over `α`. This analysis requires retaining all terms in the full proxy objective, leading to more complex algebraic conditions (e.g., a cubic equation for `α`).

4. **Asymptotic Analysis**: For each minimization scenario, the authors derive asymptotic power-law scalings for `η`, `α`, `b`, and the resulting minimum risk as `K` or `T` approaches infinity. This reveals the dominant trends and exponents.

5. **Comparison and Discussion**: The derived scaling laws are then compared against existing empirical observations and other theoretical predictions in the literature. Insights into phenomena like the token-optimal batch size, the critical token budget, and the interaction between momentum and batch size are extracted.

6. **Numerical and Empirical Verification**: The theoretical derivations are numerically validated by computing the proxy objective across wide ranges of hyperparameters and token budgets, confirming the predicted power-law trends. Furthermore, qualitative empirical support is provided through a constant-η training experiment on a transformer model, demonstrating alignment with certain predicted trends.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study successfully derives explicit power-law scaling predictions for learning rate, momentum, and batch size across varying training horizons and token budgets, consistently finding an optimal performance decay rate of $T^{-1/4}$ across multiple tuning regimes.

1. **Overall Performance Scaling**: Across all major tuning regimes, the minimum expected dual gradient norm (the proxy objective) scales asymptotically as $O(T^{-1/4})$ with respect to the total token budget $T$. This indicates a fundamental limit on how quickly performance can improve with increasing computational resources under the LMO framework.

2. **Fixed Momentum (Theorem 1)**:
- **Iteration Scaling**: For a fixed batch size $b$ and momentum $\alpha$, the optimal learning rate scales as $\eta^*_K \propto K^{-1/2}$. The performance improves with batch size, $risk^*_K \propto K^{-1/2} + b^{-1/2}$.
- **Token-Budget Scaling (Fixed $b$)**: When the token budget $T=bK$ is fixed and $b$ is constant, the optimal learning rate scales as $\eta^*_T(b) \propto b^{1/2} T^{-1/2}$. This finding recovers the empirically known square-root scaling of learning rate with batch size and its inverse square-root dependency on the token budget.
- **Joint $(\eta, b)$ Tuning (Fixed $\alpha$)**: When both learning rate and batch size are tuned for a fixed token budget $T$, the optimal batch size scales as $b^*_T \propto T^{1/2}$, and the corresponding optimal learning rate scales as $\eta^*_T(b^*_T) \propto T^{-1/4}$. This identifies a non-trivial, token-optimal batch size that grows with the square root of the token budget. The analysis also suggests that the optimal batch size exceeds one only after a certain momentum-dependent critical token budget is reached.

3. **Fixed Batch Size (Theorem 2)**:
- When the batch size $b$ is fixed, and learning rate $\eta$ and momentum $\alpha$ are tuned, the optimal momentum parameter scales as $\alpha^*_T(b) \propto b \cdot T^{-1/2}$. This implies that as the token budget increases, the optimal momentum coefficient $\beta = 1 - \alpha$ should increase towards 1.
- The corresponding optimal learning rate scales as $\eta^*_T(b) \propto b \cdot T^{-3/4}$.
- This result aligns with insights derived from timescale preservation arguments and stochastic differential equation (SDE) literature regarding momentum and learning rate co-adaptation.

4. **Joint Tuning of $(\eta, \alpha, b)$ (Theorem 3)**:
- When all three hyperparameters are jointly tuned for a large token budget $T$, the asymptotic scalings are found to be:
- Optimal batch size: $b^*_T \propto T^{1/6}$.
- Optimal learning rate: $\eta^*_T \propto T^{-7/12}$.
- Optimal momentum parameter: $\alpha^*_T \propto T^{-1/3}$.
- The resulting performance rate remains $O(T^{-1/4})$, consistent with the other regimes. The $T^{1/6}$ batch growth is notably milder than the $T^{1/2}$ observed under fixed momentum.

5. **Robustness and Flatness of Batch Size Landscape (Corollary 1 & 2, Appendix C.2)**:
- **Momentum Tuning Impact**: If batch-size growth is unconstrained, tuning momentum (varying $\alpha$) only marginally improves the rate constant (up to a factor of $2^{1/4} \approx 1.19$) but does not change the $T^{-1/4}$ exponent. However, if the batch size is capped by hardware, fixed momentum leads to a non-vanishing noise floor. Allowing $\alpha$ to decrease with $T$ (momentum tuning) is necessary to recover the $T^{-1/4}$ rate and remove this floor.
- **Near-Optimal Batch Scalings**: Multiple batch-size scaling laws can achieve the $T^{-1/4}$ optimal rate. Specifically, any batch scaling $b(T) = T^\phi$ with $\phi \leq 1/2$ can maintain the $T^{-1/4}$ rate if $\eta$ and $\alpha$ are retuned accordingly. Batch growth faster than $T^{1/2}$ leads to a slower rate.
- **Sensitivity to Noise Model**: The analysis shows that the "flatness" of the batch-size landscape (where leading-order terms cancel the dependence on $b$) is sensitive to the assumed noise model. The derived $T^{1/6}$ scaling for $b^*_T$ in the joint tuning regime is primarily influenced by lower-order terms, implying that many batch-growth paths can remain near-optimal. Deviations from the standard $b^{-1/2}$ noise scaling (e.g., heavy tails or correlations) can significantly alter the predicted optimal batch size behavior.

6. **Comparison with SGD**: In contrast to LMO-based methods, the classical analysis of vanilla SGD does not predict a non-trivial token-optimal batch size. For SGD, the optimal performance after tuning depends solely on the token budget $T$, making the batch size largely a trade-off between iterations and parallelism without affecting the leading-order performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work offers a significant theoretical contribution to the understanding of hyperparameter scaling in deep learning, particularly for large-scale training.

1. **Unified and Principled Framework**: The study provides a unified optimization-theoretic framework for deriving hyperparameter scaling laws for modern first-order optimizers (LMO-based methods like Adam approximations and Muon). This moves beyond empirical observations and heuristic arguments, offering a principled basis for understanding why certain scaling behaviors are observed.
2. **Recovery of Empirical Insights**: The analysis successfully recovers and provides theoretical grounding for several established empirical trends, including:
- The square-root learning rate scaling with batch size.
- The learning rate's dependency on the token budget.
- The emergence of a non-trivial token-optimal batch size.
- The hyperbolic relationship between step count and batch size for a given performance target.
 This strengthens the confidence in both the empirical observations and the theoretical framework.
3. **Insights into Momentum's Role**: The research clarifies the non-trivial role of momentum in non-convex stochastic optimization within the LMO framework. It demonstrates that while momentum tuning might offer only marginal constant-factor improvements when batch size is unconstrained, it becomes essential for achieving optimal performance (avoiding a noise floor) when batch-size growth is capped by hardware limits. The predicted decreasing optimal momentum as batch size increases also aligns with contemporary empirical findings.
4. **Practical Hyperparameter Transfer Rules**: The derived power-law schedules translate into explicit hyperparameter transfer rules (summarized in Appendix A). These rules can guide practitioners in extrapolating optimal hyperparameters from smaller tuning runs to much larger training budgets, potentially saving significant computational resources.
5. **Challenges to Common Practices**: The findings implicitly challenge common practices such as fixing batch size purely based on hardware throughput or extending training horizons without re-tuning hyperparameters. The analysis suggests that such choices can lead to inefficiencies, motivating further quantification of these performance gaps.
6. **Limitations and Future Research Directions**: The paper also clearly articulates its limitations, which in turn open several avenues for future work:
- **Scope of Analysis**: The analysis assumes fixed model size, a constant learning rate, matched initialization, and does not incorporate complexities like weight decay, learning-rate scheduling, or warmup phases, which are crucial in real-world training.
- **Mismatch with Some Empirical Trends**: While many empirical trends are recovered, the framework currently does not fully capture all observed phenomena, such as certain cases where optimal learning rates are reported to increase with token budget under joint batch/token scaling. Resolving this discrepancy could involve investigating protocol constraints, mismatches in theoretical assumptions (e.g., noise models, heavy-tailed gradients, or specific properties of real-world loss landscapes), or the looseness of the bounds for modern large models.
- **Statistical Generalization**: The focus is solely on optimization efficiency (gradient norm reduction), without explicitly addressing statistical generalization, which is a key aspect of deep learning performance.
- **Extension to Other Parameters**: Future work could extend the framework to include model size (`N`), effective smoothness, various gradient noise models, and parametrization choices like μP.

In summary, this paper provides a robust theoretical foundation for hyperparameter scaling in modern deep learning, offering both confirmatory evidence for empirical rules and novel insights, while also clearly delineating areas for further theoretical and empirical investigation.
