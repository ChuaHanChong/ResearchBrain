# Quadratic Programming-Based Posture Manipulation and Thrust-vectoring for Agile Dynamic Walking on Narrow Pathways

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northeastern University and Caltech developed a Quadratic Programming-based control framework that enables a multi-modal legged-aerial robot to perform agile dynamic walking on narrow pathways. Their approach integrates posture manipulation with thrust-vectoring, allowing the simulated Husky β robot to recover from significant lateral pushes and maintain stable locomotion by actively regulating ground reaction forces to prevent slippage.

## Problem

- Legged robots face inherent limitations in stability and locomotion plasticity, especially in challenging environments with severely restricted foot placement options like narrow pathways.
- Traditional posture manipulation through ground contact forces alone is often insufficient for maintaining frontal dynamic stability and regulating contact forces to prevent slippage on confined surfaces.
- Dynamic walking on narrow beams or pipes presents a significant challenge for purely legged systems due to limited stability margins and difficulty in counteracting external disturbances.

## Method

- A ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework was designed to integrate both ground reaction forces and thrust forces from propeller motors attached to the robot's knee joints.
- A Quadratic Programming (QP) problem is solved at 100 Hz to determine optimal ground reaction forces and thrust actions, using a linearized centroidal dynamics model and minimizing state deviations from references and control input magnitudes.
- Low-level joint control employs a PD controller with feedforward torques derived from the MPC-determined ground reaction forces, while a Raibert heuristic guides swing leg trajectory generation.

## Results

- The simulated Husky β robot, with thruster assistance, successfully recovered from a 40N lateral push applied for **0.5** seconds during a narrow-path trotting gait, demonstrating enhanced robustness compared to thruster-deactivated trials.
- The robot successfully performed stable dynamic walking on a 0.1m wide narrow beam for 10 seconds, maintaining stable lateral position, stance height, and attitude.
- Thrusters were utilized at relatively low magnitudes (not exceeding 7N during beam walking) primarily for subtle, continuous adjustments to stabilize roll dynamics and ensure ground friction cone constraints were consistently satisfied to prevent slippage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating thrust-vectoring with conventional posture manipulation significantly enhances a legged robot's ability to maintain stability and improve locomotion plasticity in highly constrained environments.
> - Thrusters can actively regulate ground reaction forces to ensure no-slip conditions and compensate for limited ground contact, providing robust stability beyond what purely legged systems can achieve.
> - Bio-inspiration from multi-modal animals like birds suggests that combining legged and aerial capabilities offers a promising paradigm for expanding robotic agility and versatility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot locomotion has seen advancements in agility, with bipedal and quadrupedal robots demonstrating complex maneuvers such as gymnastic routines and parkour. However, these feats primarily rely on posture manipulation through ground contact forces, which present inherent limitations in terms of stability and locomotion plasticity, particularly in challenging environments.

This study positions itself within the context of expanding robot capabilities beyond conventional legged systems by integrating multi-modal locomotion. The authors draw inspiration from biological systems, specifically birds, which combine bipedalism and flight to achieve exceptional locomotion plasticity and agile maneuvers, such as ascending steep inclines using both legs and wings. This biological paradigm, termed "Multi-Modal Mobility Morphobot (M4)" in related work, suggests that augmenting legged locomotion with thrust-vectoring capabilities can overcome the limitations of ground-contact-only systems.

The research builds upon a foundation of previous work by the same group on control design for thruster-assisted walking, specifically with the Northeastern Husky robot. This paper is presented as a continuation, applying and refining these multi-modal control concepts to a more specific and challenging scenario: dynamic walking on narrow pathways. Navigating narrow paths represents a significant challenge for legged robots due as it severely restricts available foot placement options and limits the effectiveness of posture manipulation alone for contact force regulation and stability maintenance. By addressing this specific problem, the work contributes to the broader goal of developing more versatile and robust robotic platforms capable of operating in diverse and complex environments where traditional locomotion methods may be insufficient. The integration of real-time optimization techniques, specifically Quadratic Programming (QP) solvers, for control further aligns this research with contemporary advancements in robot control, where computational efficiency and robust constraint handling are critical.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a control design methodology that integrates the posture manipulation capabilities of a legged robot with its thrust-vectoring functionality. This integration aims to enable the robot, specifically the Husky β platform, to perform dynamic traversal of narrow pathways stably. The motivation behind this specific goal stems from several considerations:

- **Overcoming Limitations of Ground Contact Forces:** Conventional legged locomotion relies heavily on manipulating ground contact forces through limb movements and posture adjustments. While effective for many tasks, this approach faces significant limitations when foot placement options are severely constrained, such as on a narrow beam or a pipe. In such scenarios, the ability to generate sufficient stabilizing torques and forces through ground contact alone becomes difficult, leading to instability.
- **Leveraging Multi-modal Capabilities:** The Husky β robot is designed as a legged-aerial platform, equipped with thrusters in addition to its legs. The study is motivated by the desire to fully exploit these multi-modal capabilities. The thrusters are strategically placed at the sagittal knee joints, allowing them to exert lateral forces that can influence the robot's frontal dynamics, particularly its roll stability. The objective is to determine how these additional external forces can be effectively recruited alongside joint accelerations to achieve stable locomotion in challenging conditions.
- **Bio-inspiration for Enhanced Plasticity:** Observing agile biological systems like birds, which seamlessly combine bipedalism and flight, provides strong motivation. Birds demonstrate impressive locomotion plasticity by utilizing both legs and wings for tasks such as ascending steep inclines or rapid maneuvers. This biological precedent suggests that integrating thrust-vectoring can significantly expand a robot's stability and locomotion capabilities, allowing it to perform feats beyond those achievable by purely legged systems.
- **Addressing the "Narrow Path" Problem:** Dynamic walking on a rigid pipe is identified as a particularly interesting and challenging problem. The confined walking surface makes traditional posture manipulation for contact force regulation largely ineffective. The thrusters are intended to compensate for this inherent limitation by providing an alternative means of stabilization.
- **Step Towards a Broader Ambitious Goal:** While focused on narrow-path walking, the authors state that their overarching objective is to achieve dynamic walking on a flexible rope. The findings and control strategies developed in this work for rigid narrow paths are considered a significant and necessary stride toward that more complex ambition, indicating a progressive research agenda.
- **Validation of Robotic Design:** The study also serves to validate the design philosophy of multi-modal robots like Husky β by demonstrating how its unique features (thrusters) can be effectively controlled to enhance performance in specific, difficult scenarios.

In summary, the core motivation is to enhance the agility, stability, and locomotion plasticity of legged robots by strategically integrating thrust-vectoring, inspired by biological counterparts, to overcome the inherent limitations of purely ground-contact-based systems, with a specific focus on the challenging problem of narrow-path locomotion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on a simulation-based approach to design and evaluate a model predictive control (MPC) framework for a multi-modal legged-aerial robot.

**A. Robot Platform and Simulation Environment:**
The study utilized a simulation model of the **Husky β** robot, developed in the PyBullet simulator. Husky β is a quadrupedal robot with three actuated degrees of freedom per leg (hip frontal abduction-adduction, hip sagittal swing, and knee flexion-extension). A key feature of this platform is the attachment of a propeller motor to the outside of each knee joint, allowing for the generation of thrust forces. This design enables the robot to potentially morph into a quadrotor configuration. The hardware components, such as Dynamixel XH540-W270-T servomotors and SunnySky X4112S BLDC motors with propellers, are incorporated into the high-fidelity simulation model, allowing for realistic thrust generation (maximum 10.6 kg of thrust, providing a thruster-to-weight ratio of approximately 1.6).

**B. Centroidal Dynamics Model with Thrusters:**
The control strategy is based on a centroidal dynamics model that accounts for both traditional ground reaction forces ($u_{g,i}$) and the additional thrust forces ($u_{t,i}$) generated by the propellers.
1. **Formulation:** The model describes the robot's linear and angular acceleration in the world frame. Ground reaction forces ($u_{g,i} \in \mathbb{R}^3$) are applied at points defined by vectors $d_i$, and thrust forces ($u_{t,i} \in \mathbb{R}$) are applied at points defined by vectors $r_i$ and oriented by unit vectors $\hat{e}_i$.
 - Linear acceleration: $\ddot{p} = M^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^4 (\hat{e}_i u_{t,i} + u_{g,i}) + [0, 0, -g]^T$.
 - Angular momentum rate: $\frac{d}{dt}(I\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^4 (r_i \times \hat{e}_i u_{t,i} + d_i \times u_{g,i})$.
 - Rotation matrix dynamics: $\dot{R} = [\omega]_\times R$.
2. **Linearization:** For computational efficiency within the MPC framework, the nonlinear dynamics are linearized. Under typical walking conditions involving small roll and pitch angles, the Euler angle rates ($\dot{\theta}$) are approximated as $\dot{\theta} \approx R_z^T \omega$, and the angular momentum rate approximation $I\dot{\omega} \approx R_z^B I R_z^T \dot{\omega}$ is used. This allows the system to be represented in a linear state-space form: $x = A x + B u + h_g$, where $x$ is the state vector (attitude, position, angular velocity, linear velocity) and $u$ is the control input vector (ground reaction forces and thrust forces).

**C. Low-level Joint Control:**
For lower-level control, a hybrid joint state input is used. This involves a PD controller for joint position tracking, supplemented by feedforward joint torques. The desired joint torques ($\tau_{j}$) are primarily contributed during the stance phase and calculated from the ground reaction forces ($u_{g,j}$) determined by the MPC, using the leg Jacobian ($J_j$): $\tau_j = J_j^T u_{g,j}$. The Pinocchio library is employed for real-time calculation of forward kinematics and joint Jacobians.

**D. Model Predictive Control (MPC) Framework:**
The core of the control strategy is an MPC approach that finds optimal ground reaction forces and thrust actions at each time step.
1. **Locomotion Phases:** The walking gait is divided into stance and swing phases.
2. **Swing Phase Control:** A Raibert heuristic is used to determine the desired foot-end location for the swing leg. This heuristic calculates the target foot placement based on a nominal position, the robot's forward velocity, and a gain-based correction for velocity error: $p_{d,i} = p_{ref,i} + \frac{vT_s}{2} + k(v_i - v_{d,i})$. A fourth-order Bézier curve is then used to generate a smooth swing trajectory, ensuring zero velocity at both lift-off and touchdown.
3. **Stance Phase/Force Optimization (QP):** In the stance phase, the control inputs ($u$) are calculated by solving a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem at each time step. The linearized and discretized system dynamics are used as equality constraints within the QP.
 - **Objective Function:** The QP minimizes a cost function over a prediction horizon ($n_h=5$). This cost function balances two objectives:
 - Minimizing the deviation of predicted states ($x_k$) from desired reference states ($x_{r,k}$), weighted by matrix $Q$.
 - Minimizing the magnitude of the control inputs ($u_k$), weighted by matrix $R$.
 - $\min_{u_k} \sum_{k=1}^{n_h} (x_k - x_{r,k})^T Q (x_k - x_{r,k}) + u_k^T R u_k$
 - **Constraints:**
 - **System Dynamics:** $x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k + h_g$.
 - **Thruster Force Limits:** For stance legs, thrust forces ($u_{t,i}$) are constrained to be positive (0 < $u_{t,i}$) and below a maximum value ($u_{t,i} < u_{max_t}$). This ensures single-direction thrust.
 - **Ground Reaction Force Limits:** Friction cone constraints are applied to the ground reaction forces to prevent slippage. These constraints are linearized: $u_{g,i,x} < \mu_s u_{g,i,z}$, $-u_{g,i,x} < \mu_s u_{g,i,z}$, $u_{g,i,y} < \mu_s u_{g,i,z}$, and $-u_{g,i,y} < \mu_s u_{g,i,z}$. Additionally, normal forces are constrained to be positive ($-u_{g,i,z} < 0$).
4. **QP Solver:** The `quadprog` solver, identified as a fast off-the-shelf QP solver for small problems, is used to solve the optimization problem at a rate of 100 Hz.

**E. Simulation Scenarios:**
Two main simulation experiments were conducted to evaluate the controller's performance:
1. **Push Recovery:** A continuous lateral force of 40 N was applied to the robot's center of mass for 0.5 seconds (from t=1s to t=1.5s) during a narrow path trotting gait. This test compared the robot's ability to recover with and without thruster assistance.
2. **Beam Walking:** The robot was tasked with walking on a narrow beam (0.1m width, 0.1m height) to demonstrate continuous stable locomotion on a constrained pathway, utilizing both ground reaction forces and thrust forces.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation studies demonstrated the effectiveness of the QP-based posture manipulation and thrust-vectoring control for stabilizing the Husky β robot during narrow-path locomotion and against external disturbances.

**A. Push Recovery:**
The push recovery test involved applying a significant 40N lateral disturbance to the robot's center of mass for 0.5 seconds (from t=1s to t=1.5s) while it was performing a narrow-path trotting gait.

- **Without Thruster Assistance:** When the thrusters were deactivated, the robot exhibited instability and failed to recover from the lateral push. The locomotion failed approximately at t=2s after the disturbance was applied. This result indicated that traditional ground contact forces alone were insufficient to maintain frontal dynamic stability during such a gait on a narrow path under external perturbation.
- **With Thruster Assistance:** With the thrusters activated, the robot successfully recovered from the disturbance and re-stabilized its gait. The Model Predictive Control (MPC) system generated high opposite thruster forces to immediately counteract the external push (evident from t=1s to t=1.7s in the thruster force plot). Following this initial counteraction, smaller forces in the same direction were applied by the thrusters (from t=1.7s to t=2.8s) to fine-tune the robot's balance and re-stabilize its gait. The comparison plots of the robot's center of mass (CoM) position and orientation further illustrate the successful stabilization of the robot's position and roll angle. The thruster forces, as shown in Figure 6, indicate that the right pair of thrusters activated to counterbalance the initial disturbance, followed by the left pair of thrusters activating to correct for residual effects, all while satisfying the specified 20N inequality constraint for thruster output.

**B. Beam Walking:**
The beam walking test assessed the robot's ability to maintain stable locomotion on a continuously narrow pathway (0.1m width, 0.1m height).

- **Stable Locomotion:** The simulation demonstrated that the robot successfully performed narrow path walking for 10 seconds.
- **Position and Attitude Stability:** Throughout the simulation, the robot maintained a stable lateral position (y-axis), stance height (z-axis), and attitude (roll, pitch, yaw) while moving forward (x-axis) (Figures 8 and 9). This indicates that the integrated control strategy effectively managed the robot's overall state despite the constrained environment.
- **Thruster Force Contribution:** The thrusters were utilized for stabilization, but at relatively low magnitudes. The thruster forces did not exceed 7 N during the beam walking maneuver (Figure 9). This value is significantly less than the thruster's maximum capacity of approximately 100 N, suggesting that the thrusters provided subtle, continuous adjustments primarily to stabilize the roll dynamics, rather than large, impulsive forces.
- **Ground Friction Cone Satisfaction:** A key finding was the role of the sagittal propellers (thrusters) in ensuring that the ground friction cone constraint was satisfied on each leg throughout the entire walking process (Figures 9 and 10). This indicates that the thrusters did not merely provide external balancing forces, but actively assisted in regulating the ground reaction forces to prevent slippage, thereby maintaining robust foot-ground contact. The plot of the friction-to-normal force ratio (Figure 10) remained below the no-slip limits, confirming successful adherence to friction constraints.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the QP-based controller effectively integrates thrust-vectoring with legged locomotion, enabling the Husky β robot to recover from significant lateral disturbances and stably navigate narrow pathways, with thrusters playing a crucial role in enhancing roll stability and ensuring no-slip conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents significant contributions to the field of legged robotics and multi-modal locomotion, with several potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Stability and Locomotion Plasticity:** The primary significance lies in demonstrating a method to significantly enhance the stability and locomotion plasticity of legged robots in challenging environments. By successfully stabilizing the robot against lateral pushes and enabling dynamic walking on narrow pathways, the work expands the operational envelope of quadrupedal systems. This allows robots to traverse terrains that are traditionally difficult or impossible for purely legged platforms due to limited ground contact area or inherent instability.
- **Validation of Multi-Modal Robotic Design:** The study validates the utility of multi-modal robot platforms like the Husky β, which combine legged mobility with thrust-vectoring. It shows that the thrusters, strategically placed at the knee joints, are not merely ancillary components but integral parts of a sophisticated control system that can actively contribute to dynamic balance and force regulation. This encourages further development and exploration of hybrid locomotion systems inspired by biological agility.
- **Ground Reaction Force Regulation and No-Slip Conditions:** The finding that thrusters can be used to regulate ground reaction forces and ensure no-slip conditions is crucial. On narrow or slippery surfaces, maintaining sufficient normal force and managing tangential forces to prevent slippage is critical. The ability of the thrusters to actively assist in this force regulation provides a novel mechanism for improving traction and stability, thereby enhancing the robot's robustness in diverse terrains.
- **Pathway Towards Advanced Locomotion Tasks:** The successful simulation of dynamic walking on a narrow beam is explicitly stated as a significant step toward the more ambitious goal of dynamic walking on a flexible rope. This highlights the foundational nature of the current work, laying the groundwork for more complex and dynamic behaviors requiring continuous and adaptive stabilization. Such capabilities could be vital for future robots operating in unstructured or precarious environments.
- **Potential for UAV Mode Transition:** The integrated thrust-vectoring capability not only aids legged locomotion but also points towards the robot's potential for seamless transitions into a UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle) mode. The authors note that the thrusters could be used to transform the robot into a quadrotor configuration to fly over obstacles or rough terrains. This multi-modal flexibility could make such robots exceptionally versatile for tasks like inspection, search and rescue, or environmental monitoring in complex, varied landscapes.
- **Advancement in Real-Time Control Techniques:** The application of Model Predictive Control (MPC) with a Quadratic Programming (QP) solver demonstrates the continued relevance and effectiveness of optimization-based control for complex, dynamic robotic systems. The choice of `quadprog` for its speed at 100 Hz indicates progress towards real-time implementable solutions, crucial for dynamic tasks.
- **Future Impact on Robotics Applications:** Robots capable of agile dynamic walking on narrow pathways and exhibiting enhanced stability have numerous potential applications. These include navigating confined spaces in urban search and rescue, performing infrastructure inspection on beams or pipes, or exploring dangerous environments where human access is limited. The enhanced robustness against disturbances also makes these robots more reliable for long-duration or mission-critical tasks.
- **Challenges for Hardware Implementation:** While the simulation results are promising, the authors acknowledge the significant challenges associated with implementing this controller on a physical robot. Factors like computing power limitations, sensor noise, and communication delays in real-world hardware will necessitate further development of robust controllers and estimation algorithms. This perspective highlights the practical impact of the simulation work as a critical first step towards real-world deployment.
