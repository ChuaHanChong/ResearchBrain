# SafeFlowMPC: Predictive and Safe Trajectory Planning for Robot Manipulators with Learning-based Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> SafeFlowMPC integrates a flow matching model with an online Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework, enabling robot manipulators to perform safe and reactive trajectory planning in dynamic environments. This method guarantees continuous safety through a projection onto a safety manifold and terminal constraints, while learning complex behaviors from demonstrations, and demonstrating real-time performance and high success rates across various manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional robot motion planners lack flexibility, generalization, and real-time reactivity for dynamic, unstructured environments and human-robot interaction.
- Learning-based methods, while flexible, typically struggle to provide rigorous safety guarantees critical for real-world robotic deployment due to their 'black-box' nature.
- Designing explicit numerical objective functions for complex robotic tasks, such as human-robot handovers, is challenging, motivating the use of learning from demonstrations which often lacks safety integration.

## Method

- **SafeFlowMPC** combines a ==flow matching== model, which learns desired trajectory distributions from demonstrations, with an online Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulation.
- It projects proposed trajectories onto a 'safety manifold' using a non-convex optimization problem that enforces kinematic, collision avoidance, and crucial terminal safety constraints in real-time.
- A two-step training process for the flow model includes initial training on demonstrations and subsequent finetuning on a safety dataset, where all target trajectories are projected onto the safety manifold.

## Results

- SafeFlowMPC achieved high success rates (**86%** in static grasp, **82%** in reactive grasp) and zero obstacle collisions, outperforming purely learning-based methods (e.g., Behavior Cloning at **2%** success) and traditional MPC (**76%** success).
- The method maintained real-time planning times (average 62-63 ms per step) on a 7-DoF robot manipulator while adhering to all physical and collision constraints.
- In human-robot object handovers, SafeFlowMPC produced trajectories closest to human demonstrations (0.12m average distance) while ensuring terminal safety constraints, demonstrating its ability to learn complex, non-numerically defined task objectives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a learning-based flow model with an online optimization solver allows for combining the performance benefits of learned policies with rigorous, continuous safety guarantees.
> - The use of a terminal safety set, enforced by constraints within the MPC formulation, enables provable safety beyond the immediate planning horizon by ensuring a low-level controller can stabilize the system.
> - Employing a suboptimal, real-time iteration (RTI) scheme for solving the non-convex projection problem ensures computational efficiency crucial for real-time reactivity in dynamic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of robots into unstructured and dynamic environments, particularly in everyday life and human-robot interaction, presents challenges for traditional robot motion planning. Conventional optimization-based and sampling-based planners can generate trajectories that adhere to constraints but often lack the flexibility, generalization capabilities, and real-time reactivity needed for dynamic settings. They typically require explicit numerical objective functions, which can be difficult to design for complex tasks such as human-robot handovers.

Learning-based methods, in contrast, offer advantages in terms of fast inference times and the ability to learn complex behaviors from demonstrations, thereby bypassing the need for explicit reward function design. Techniques like flow matching and diffusion models have shown promise in learning distributions over desired trajectories. However, a significant limitation of these "black-box" learning models is the inherent difficulty in providing rigorous safety guarantees, which are critical for real-world deployment where collisions or constraint violations can lead to damage or harm.

The existing approaches to incorporate safety into learning-based methods include:
- **Safety filters:** These project potentially unsafe control inputs into a safe set. However, they can alter the system's behavior, potentially degrading the performance of the learned policy.
- **Learning-based safety:** Methods that integrate safety directly into the learning process or use techniques like Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) or Hamilton-Jacobi reachability. Many of these approaches struggle with scalability to complex systems like robot manipulators, or only offer probabilistic safety bounds rather than guaranteed constraint satisfaction.
- **Cost guidance in flow/diffusion models:** These techniques guide the iterative generation process towards desired behaviors, but often cannot guarantee hard constraint satisfaction.

This work positions itself by addressing the gap between the flexibility and generalization of learning-based policies and the rigorous safety guarantees of optimization-based approaches. It aims to develop a method that can learn complex behaviors from demonstrations while ensuring continuous safety, real-time reactivity, and applicability to robot manipulators in dynamic environments. The paper extends existing flow matching applications by tailoring them specifically for robot manipulators and deeply integrating safety enforcement through an online optimization framework, rather than relying solely on post-hoc filtering or probabilistic guarantees.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and reliable method for online trajectory planning for robot manipulators that can operate safely and reactively in dynamic, unstructured environments, while also leveraging the generalization capabilities of learning-based policies.

The motivation for this work arises from several key challenges in modern robotics:

- **Non-linear Kinematics and Physical Limits:** Robot manipulators possess complex non-linear kinematics, singularities, and physical limits on joints and velocities. Trajectory planning must account for these factors to ensure feasibility and prevent damage.
- **Dynamic and Changing Environments:** Robots are increasingly deployed in environments where conditions change during operation. This necessitates online adaptation of motion to react to moving obstacles, changes in task objectives initiated by human operators, or the presence of other dynamic actors. Traditional global planners are insufficient for such reactive demands.
- **Difficulty in Encoding Task Objectives:** Many real-world robotic tasks, especially those involving human interaction (e.g., human-robot handovers), have complex objectives that are difficult to express as simple numerical reward functions for optimization-based methods. Learning from demonstrations offers a solution but often lacks safety considerations.
- **Critical Safety Considerations:** For physical interaction with the world and collaboration with humans, safety is paramount. The robot must consistently avoid collisions with the environment, other objects, and humans to prevent damage and ensure reliable operation. Probabilistic safety guarantees are often insufficient for these critical scenarios.
- **Real-time Performance:** Online planning in dynamic environments requires algorithms with high computational efficiency to enable real-time execution and rapid reaction to changes.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:

- **Develop SafeFlowMPC:** A novel procedure that integrates a flow-matching model with an online optimization solver. This integration is designed to combine the performance benefits of learning agents with rigorous constraint enforcement.
- **Guarantee Safety at All Times:** Implement a mechanism, specifically through the enforcement of a safe terminal constraint within the optimization, to ensure that the robot remains within a designated safe operating manifold throughout its operation.
- **Demonstrate Reactive Motion Planning:** Validate the method's ability to perform reactive motion planning on a real-world 7-DoF robot manipulator in challenging scenarios, including dynamic object grasping in obstructed environments and human-robot object handovers. This aims to showcase its real-time applicability and ability to adapt.
- **Leverage Learning from Demonstrations:** Utilize flow matching to learn desired behaviors from demonstrated trajectories, thereby enabling the robot to generalize tasks without requiring manual design of complex numerical reward functions.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to provide a solution that reconciles the flexibility of learned behaviors with the absolute safety requirements essential for deploying robots in complex and interactive real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SafeFlowMPC method combines a learning-based flow matching model with an online, optimization-based model predictive control (MPC) formulation. The core idea is to leverage flow matching for generating desired, high-level trajectories while using an embedded optimization problem to project these trajectories onto a "safety manifold," thereby guaranteeing constraint satisfaction.

**A. Problem Formulation and Safety Manifolds**

The motion planning problem is defined as finding an optimal joint trajectory $q^*(t)$ that minimizes an objective function $J(q(t))$ while satisfying all motion constraints. These constraints define a "safety manifold" $M_{safe}$, where $M_{safe} = \{q(t): h(q(t)) \le 0 \land g(q(t)) = 0\}$. Here, $h(q(t))$ represents inequality constraints (e.g., collision avoidance, joint limits), and $g(q(t))$ represents equality constraints (e.g., reaching a target pose). An additional "performance manifold" $M^*$ represents trajectories in $M_{safe}$ that also minimize $J(q(t))$. The method uses an iterative procedure to move a current safe trajectory $q_{safe}(t)$ towards $M^*$ by applying a modification $\Delta q_{safe}(t)$ and then projecting the result back onto $M_{safe}$ using a projection operator $P_{safe}^q(\cdot)$.

**B. Flow Matching on Manifolds**

The trajectory modification $\Delta q_{safe}(t)$ is computed by a flow matching model. Flow matching learns a conditional flow $u_\phi^s(q(t)|O(t))$ that transforms trajectories from an initial distribution $p_0(q(t)|O(t))$ (safe trajectories on $M_{safe}$) to a target distribution $p_1(q(t)|O(t))$ (safe trajectories on $M^*$). The observations $O(t)$ condition these distributions.

1. **Mathematical Formulation:** The transformation is described by an integral: $q_1(t) = q_0(t) + \int_0^1 u_\phi^s(q(t)|O(t))ds$. The flow $u_\phi^s$ is learned by minimizing a loss function $L_{FM}$ that measures the difference between the learned flow and a target flow $u_s$. The target flow itself is defined by the derivative of the projection operator $P_{safe}^q$ applied to an interpolated path between $q_0(t)$ and $q_1(t)$. This formulation ensures that the path generated by the flow matching process always remains on $M_{safe}$ as long as the initial trajectory $q_0(t)$ is safe.

2. **Training Procedure:** A two-step training procedure is employed:
 - **Initial Training:** A flow model is trained to match demonstrated trajectories without explicit safety considerations (standard flow matching).
 - **Finetuning on Safety Dataset:** The pre-trained model is finetuned on a "safety dataset." This dataset consists of the original demonstrations, but each trajectory is projected onto $M_{safe}$ to ensure all target trajectories are safe. The initial distribution $p_0$ is approximated by sampling and projection. The derivative of the projection operator, needed for the target flow, is computed using finite differences.

**C. Trajectory Projection ($P_{safe}^q$)**

The projection operator $P_{safe}^q(q_{init}(t))$ is formulated as a non-convex optimization problem:
$\min_{q(t)} D(q(t), q_{init}(t))$
subject to:
$h(q(t)) \le 0$ (inequality constraints during trajectory and at terminal point)
$g(q(t)) = 0$ (equality constraints during trajectory and at terminal point)
where $D(\cdot)$ is a distance measure, and $q_{init}(t)$ is the trajectory proposed by the flow model.

Crucially, the constraints include **terminal constraints** $h_T(q(t))$ and $g_T(q(t))$ that define a **terminal safety set $S_T$** at the end of the planning horizon ($t_0 + T$). The method relies on **Assumption 1**, which states that a controller exists capable of maintaining safety if the robot reaches $S_T$. **Theorem 1** then guarantees safety for all times beyond the planning horizon if the projected trajectory ends within $S_T$.

**D. Inference (Receding Horizon Control)**

During inference, SafeFlowMPC operates in a closed-loop, receding horizon fashion, similar to MPC:
1. At each time step $i$, the model predicts a trajectory $q_i(t)$ for a finite horizon $T$.
2. The trajectory generation for step $i$ involves multiple iterative steps (Algorithm 1), where the flow matching model proposes a motion, and the $P_{safe}^q$ operator projects it back onto $M_{safe}$. A guidance cost term $J_{guide}$ can be added to bias the flow towards specific task objectives (e.g., reaching a goal pose).
3. Only the first segment of the optimized trajectory (for time $T_s$) is executed.
4. The process then repeats: the initial trajectory for the next step $i+1$ is derived from the executed portion of $q_i(t)$ and the terminal part of $q_i(t)$ (Equation 10).

**Assumption 2** states that a safe initial trajectory exists. **Theorem 2** establishes that this iterative procedure maintains safety at all times, provided the projection in (9) converges. In cases where the non-convex projection fails to converge optimally within the allotted time, the current (still safe due to prior projection) trajectory segment is executed, and the terminal safety set $S_T$ ensures continued safety.

**E. Practical Implementation for Robot Manipulators**

- **State Definition:** The state $x(t)$ includes joint positions, velocities, accelerations, and jerks.
- **Constraints:**
 - Kinematic limits: Joint position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk bounds.
 - Initial state continuity: Ensures smooth transitions between planning horizons.
 - Terminal state: Zero velocity, acceleration, and jerk at the end of the horizon, ensuring the robot ends in $S_T$ and can be stabilized by a low-level controller.
 - Collision avoidance: Utilizes a convex-set-based formulation around key points of the manipulator and environment obstacles.
- **Non-convex Optimization:** Since the projection problem (Equation 8) is non-convex due to non-linear forward kinematics and collision avoidance, an optimal solution is computationally intensive. The paper adopts a suboptimal approach using the Real-Time Iteration (RTI) scheme within the `acados` framework. This involves solving a single Quadratic Program (QP) approximation of the non-convex problem at each iterative flow step. This design prioritizes real-time execution, with $N_s=7$ flow steps performed within each time step.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The SafeFlowMPC method was evaluated against several baseline planners in three real-world experiments using a KUKA 7-DoF manipulator.

**A. Experiment 1: Global trajectory planning made local**

- **Task:** Grasping an object in an environment with obstacles, learning from a global planner (VP-STO).
- **Performance Metrics:** Trajectory duration ($T_{traj}$), planning time ($T_{plan}$), obstacle collisions ($c_{obs}$), and success rate ($r_{success}$).
- **Key Results:**
 - **Efficiency and Safety:** SafeFlowMPC achieved low $T_{traj}$ (5.12 s), comparable to the offline global planner VP-STO (4.46 s), while maintaining real-time execution ($T_{plan}$ of 62 ± 5 ms) and guaranteed safety ($c_{obs}=0$).
 - **Success Rate:** It demonstrated a high success rate (86%) for reaching the final pose without collisions.
 - **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **BoundMPC:** While safe ($c_{obs}=0$), it had longer $T_{traj}$ (6.16 s) and a lower $r_{success}$ (76%) due to local planning limitations.
 - **Behavior Cloning (BC) & Flow Matching (FM):** Both had faster $T_{plan}$ (1-33 ms) but significantly lower success rates (2% and 22%, respectively) and exhibited obstacle collisions ($c_{obs}=0.08$ m), indicating a lack of robust safety.
 - **Ours with NL-Opt:** Solving the projection optimally incurred much higher and more variable $T_{plan}$ (93 ± 178 ms), often violating real-time constraints, despite a similar $r_{success}$ (87%). This validates the choice of the suboptimal RTI approach for real-time applicability.
 - **Ours w/o finetuning:** This variant showed a lower $r_{success}$ (58%), highlighting the importance of the finetuning step on the safety dataset.
 - **Constraint Adherence:** Analysis of joint velocities showed SafeFlowMPC effectively utilized the full range of limits without violation, contributing to shorter trajectory times. Terminal state norms demonstrated that trajectories ended close to zero velocity/acceleration/jerk, satisfying the terminal safety constraints.

**B. Experiment 2: Online replanning for object grasps**

- **Task:** Reactive replanning when the object's pose dynamically changes multiple times during the motion.
- **Key Results:** SafeFlowMPC maintained its safety ($c_{obs}=0$) and achieved the highest success rate (82%) among the real-time capable methods in this more challenging, dynamic scenario. This indicates its strong reactive capabilities while adhering to safety constraints.

**C. Experiment 3: Dynamic human-robot object handover**

- **Task:** A robot handing an object to a human walking past, learning from human-human handover demonstrations.
- **Objective:** To demonstrate SafeFlowMPC's ability to learn complex, non-numerically defined task objectives while maintaining safety in human-robot interaction.
- **Key Results:**
 - **Demonstration Fidelity and Safety:** SafeFlowMPC produced trajectories that were closest to the demonstrated human-human handovers (average position distance $d_{demo}=0.12$ m) compared to BC ($d_{demo}=0.28$ m) and FM ($d_{demo}=0.21$ m). It did so while respecting terminal constraints ($c_T=0$), which enforced the end-effector to a safe set during potential handover failures.
 - **Real-time Performance:** $T_{plan}$ remained within real-time limits (63 ± 6 ms).
 - **Comparison to Baselines:** BC and FM either violated terminal constraints or produced trajectories significantly different from demonstrations.
 - **Qualitative Validation:** Supplementary video demonstrated successful real-world handovers, indicating practical applicability in a collaborative setting.

Overall, the findings consistently demonstrate that SafeFlowMPC effectively combines the strengths of learning-based trajectory generation with rigorous, real-time safety enforcement through an integrated online optimization framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SafeFlowMPC framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robot motion planning by effectively bridging the gap between learning-based approaches and traditional optimization-based control for robot manipulators. Its primary significance lies in providing a method that can simultaneously achieve:

- **Guaranteed Safety:** Through the deep integration of a safety manifold and terminal constraints enforced by an online optimization solver, SafeFlowMPC offers rigorous, continuous safety guarantees, which are critical for robot deployment in shared or human-centric environments. This moves beyond probabilistic safety bounds often associated with purely learning-based systems.
- **Real-time Reactivity:** The receding horizon control strategy combined with the efficient, suboptimal real-time iteration scheme for projection enables the robot to adapt its motion in real-time to dynamic changes in the environment, such as moving obstacles or changing task requirements.
- **Flexible Behavior Learning:** By leveraging flow matching, the method can learn complex and nuanced behaviors directly from demonstrations, eliminating the need for arduous manual design of task-specific numerical reward functions. This is particularly impactful for tasks where objective functions are hard to define, such as natural human-robot interaction.
- **Generalizability:** Learning from a diverse dataset of demonstrations allows the robot to generalize learned behaviors to similar, previously unseen situations.

The potential impacts of SafeFlowMPC are broad, particularly in areas where robots need to operate autonomously and interact safely with humans and dynamic environments:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy in Unstructured Environments:** Robots can be deployed in more complex and less controlled settings (e.g., logistics, domestic assistance, healthcare) where environmental changes are common, without compromising safety.
- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ability to learn intuitive and safe interaction behaviors from human demonstrations can lead to more seamless and trustworthy human-robot collaboration, particularly for tasks like object handovers, co-manipulation, and shared workspace operations. This could accelerate the adoption of robots in collaborative manufacturing and service industries.
- **Reduced Development Effort for Complex Tasks:** For tasks with ill-defined objective functions, learning from demonstrations, coupled with safety guarantees, can significantly reduce the engineering effort and time required to program robots.
- **Foundational Work for Future Safe AI in Robotics:** The framework provides a principled approach for integrating interpretable safety mechanisms with powerful, non-interpretable learning models, offering a pathway towards more trustworthy and certifiable AI-driven robotic systems.

While the method currently relies on differentiable constraints and a finetuning step, it lays a foundation for future research. The authors suggest future work on integrating learning-based safety filters and terminal constraints, as well as predicting the trajectory distributions of other actors in interactive scenarios. This indicates that SafeFlowMPC can serve as a building block for increasingly sophisticated and robust safe planning systems in robotics.
