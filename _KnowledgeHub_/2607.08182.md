# LEEVLA: Seeing What Matters in Latent Environment Evolution for Vision-Language-Action

> [!summary] Summary
> LEEVLA proposes a Vision-Language-Action framework that directs model attention to task-critical visual information and models structured latent environment evolution. This approach achieves an average success rate of 98.2% on the LIBERO benchmark and demonstrates improved performance in real-world robotic manipulation compared to baselines.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle with complex dynamic scenarios by treating all visual tokens uniformly, leading to diluted attention on task-critical elements.
- Existing VLA methods often rely on human-selected context clues or auxiliary models, which restricts the search space and overlooks potentially important, 'unknown but task-relevant' factors.
- Over-reliance on explicit pixel-level reconstruction or externally supplied conditions can lead to fragility against modality variations and limit the model's exploratory capacity in latent space.

## Method

- Introduces **Drift-Guided Dynamic Prioritization (DGDP)** to dynamically identify and prioritize ==visual regions== that are both spatially active and semantically relevant to the language instruction during training.
- Implements **Structured Feature Flow Generation (SFFG)** through ==Prototype-to-Periphery (P2P) prediction== and ==Mutual-Neighborhood Contrastive (MC) loss== to model the structured evolution of latent environment representations.
- Develops a task-aware 'where-how' training framework, integrating **DGDP** and **SFFG**, where both components are only active during training to avoid additional inference overhead.

## Results

- LEEVLA-large achieved an average success rate of **98.2%** on the LIBERO benchmark, outperforming other large-scale VLA baselines.
- In real-world robotic manipulation tasks, LEEVLA-large exhibited an average success rate of **78.5%**, substantially higher than OpenVLA's average of **40%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that Drift-Guided Dynamic Prioritization provided the largest individual performance gain (**1.5%** increase), with all proposed components contributing to the overall **97.0%** success rate on LIBERO-Goal.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically prioritizing visual regions based on their dynamism and instruction-relevance significantly enhances model focus and efficiency, concentrating supervision on interaction-centric areas.
> - Modeling the structured evolution of latent features using prototype-to-periphery prediction and mutual-neighborhood contrastive loss preserves semantic continuity and topological consistency, leading to more stable future feature predictions.
> - Reasoning directly in an abstract latent feature space allows the model to exploit entangled semantic, appearance, and geometric cues without requiring auxiliary pixel-level reconstruction or externally supplied conditions during inference.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which aim to enable robots to interpret multi-modal inputs (visual observations, proprioception, natural language instructions) and translate them directly into low-level control actions in a closed-loop system. This domain is experiencing rapid advancements, particularly with the integration of large language models (LLMs) and visual-language models (VLMs) to create generalist robotic policies.

Current VLA models often rely on explicit intermediate reasoning steps, which typically involve processing specific context clues such as subgoal images, segmentation masks, or depth maps, often selected by human designers. While these methods can enhance spatial perception and interpretability, they are noted for several limitations. These include a restricted search space due to dependence on external conditions or priors, which requires auxiliary models and incurs additional computational overhead. Such reliance can also lead to an overemphasis on "known" factors while potentially overlooking "unknown but task-relevant" factors, resulting in fragility when objects vary across modalities or appear similar within a single modality. This design choice can inadvertently constrain the model's exploratory capacity in latent space.

Parallel to VLA, the broader field of world models focuses on learning to forecast future states of an environment. While some world models generate future video frames for control, LEEVLA distinguishes itself by using latent-space future prediction primarily as auxiliary training supervision for the action policy, rather than for direct video generation.

LEEVLA positions itself as an advancement that addresses the limitations of current VLA approaches by advocating for reasoning directly within the latent feature space. It treats actions as drivers of environment state evolution in a high-dimensional space, aiming to exploit entangled semantic, appearance, and geometric cues without requiring explicit pixel-level reconstruction or externally supplied condition pipelines during inference. By introducing mechanisms to prioritize task-critical information and model structured latent environment evolution, LEEVLA seeks to develop VLA agents that are more robust, scalable, and capable of handling complex dynamic scenarios, thereby pushing the boundaries of generalist robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to enhance the capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly in complex dynamic scenarios, by addressing limitations identified in existing approaches.

The core motivations for this work stem from several challenges faced by contemporary VLA models:

- **Struggle with Complex Dynamic Scenarios:** Many existing VLA models treat all visual tokens uniformly, implying that every part of a visual observation holds equal importance. This uniform treatment can lead to a dilution of attention and computational resources across static backgrounds or instruction-irrelevant objects, preventing the model from focusing on the task-critical elements that drive dynamic changes in the environment.
- **Reliance on Human-Selected Factors and Lack of Underlying Factor Discovery:** Prior methods frequently rely on specific context clues (e.g., sub-goal images, segmentation, depth maps) that are either human-selected or derived from auxiliary models. This dependence can restrict the model's reasoning to a narrow, pre-defined set of "known" factors, while "unknown but task-relevant" underlying factors, which might be crucial for robust performance, are under-explored. This dependency also introduces additional computational overhead and limits the model's ability to discover emergent patterns or critical features in a flexible manner.
- **Fragility to Modality Variations and Limited Exploratory Capacity:** The reliance on specific context clues can make models fragile. For instance, if an object's appearance varies across different modalities, or if different objects look similar within a single modality, such models may struggle. This approach also limits the model's capacity to explore and learn in the latent space, hindering its ability to generalize to novel situations.
- **Inefficient High-Dimensional Prediction:** When operating directly in high-dimensional latent space, naive prediction can inadvertently divert the model's attention towards static background elements or objects not relevant to the current instruction, thereby undermining effective reasoning and action generation.

To address these motivations, LEEVLA sets out with the following key objectives:

- **Explicitly Guide Attention to Informative Regions:** Develop a mechanism to dynamically identify and prioritize regions within the visual input that are most salient and instruction-relevant for the current task. This aims to ensure that the model concentrates its supervision and capacity on scene components causally tied to the intended manipulation.
- **Preserve Structured Evolution of Latent World Representations:** Move beyond flat, sequential token prediction to model how latent environment states evolve in a structured manner. This objective seeks to maintain the local semantic continuity and topological consistency of visual features, ensuring that features belonging to the same semantic unit are processed coherently.
- **Enable Reasoning in Abstract Latent Feature Space:** By operating directly in latent space, the model can jointly exploit semantic, appearance, and geometric cues that are often entangled within objects, without the need for auxiliary pixel-level reconstruction or externally supplied conditions during training. This aims to reduce overhead and enlarge the policy's search space for discovering both known and unknown task-relevant factors.
- **Develop a Task-Aware "Where–How" Training Framework:** Integrate the guidance on "where to attend" (informative regions) with the modeling of "how these prioritized features should evolve" (structured latent reasoning) into a unified training framework. This framework is designed to make the VLA agent more adaptable, robust, and capable of long-horizon reasoning.

By pursuing these objectives, LEEVLA aims to establish a more scalable and effective approach to VLA, capable of handling the complexities of real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LEEVLA (Latent Environment Evolution for Vision-Language-Action) is designed to guide a VLA agent to attend to task-critical evidence and reason about the structured evolution of latent world representations. The architecture integrates two primary mechanisms: Drift-Guided Dynamic Prioritization (DGDP) and Structured Feature Flow Generation (SFFG).

#### 3.1. Overall Architecture

The LEEVLA model processes multi-modal inputs: third-person images ($I_p^t$), wrist-camera images ($I_w^t$), proprioception ($s_t$), and a language instruction ($l$).
1. **Visual Encoding:** Pretrained visual backbones (DinoV2 and SigLIP) act as a visual encoder ($E_v$) to produce patch features ($f_p^t, f_w^t$) from the images. A projector ($P$) then maps these features to a model-dimension token space ($x_p^t, x_w^t$).
2. **Language Encoding:** Language instructions ($l$) are encoded by a tokenizer ($\Psi$) into language tokens ($c$).
3. **Proprioception Embedding:** Proprioception ($s_t$) is embedded by an encoder ($E_s$) into a feature vector ($x_s^t$).
4. **Action Prediction:** A Parallel Continuous Action Predictor (action policy), an MLP head, generates continuous action chunks ($a_{1:T}$) from the last-layer hidden states of a large language model.
5. **Future Feature Decoding:** A Future Feature Decoder ($D$) maps the current vision embedding to a future feature space, predicting features at time $t+T$. This decoder is crucial for the SFFG module.

#### 3.2. Drift-Guided Dynamic Prioritization (DGDP)

DGDP aims to identify salient and instruction-relevant regions in the feature space during training, guiding the model on "where to attend." It consists of two components:

- **Dynamic Position Prioritization (DPP):** This mechanism quantifies the dynamism of each spatial position by measuring the change in cosine similarity between a patch's feature at time $t$ and its feature at time $t+T$. The dynamism score ($\theta_i$) for patch $i$ is calculated as:
 $\theta_i = 1 - \cos(v_{t,i}, v_{t+T,i})$
 Higher scores indicate regions with significant visual feature changes, suggesting task relevance.

- **Semantic Drift Guidance (SDG):** To ensure focus on task-relevant dynamics, SDG assigns an instruction-relevant score ($r_{t,i}$) to each patch feature at time $t$ and $t+T$. This score is the maximum Euclidean inner product between the normalized vision token ($\tilde{x}_{t,i}$) and normalized language tokens ($\tilde{c}_j$) from the instruction.
 $r_{t,i} = \max_{1 \le j \le M} \langle \tilde{x}_{t,i}, \tilde{c}_j \rangle$
 The instruction-relevant semantic drift ($\Delta_i$) is then defined as the clipped difference between future and current instruction-relevant scores, normalized by a temperature $\tau$. This drift is mapped to a semantic drift guidance factor ($\omega_i$) using an exponential function:
 $\omega_i = \exp\left(\frac{\tilde{\Delta}_i}{\tau}\right)$
 where $\tilde{\Delta}_i$ is a normalized version of $\Delta_i$. This factor amplifies regions where semantics evolve towards instruction relevance.

- **Prioritization Weight:** The DGDP combines DPP and SDG by multiplying $\omega_i$ and $\theta_i$, and then applying a sigmoid function to yield the final prioritization weight ($\beta_i$) for each token:
 $\beta_i = \sigma(\omega_i \cdot \theta_i)$
 This weight prioritizes dynamic regions whose semantic evolution aligns with the language instruction, while down-weighting static or instruction-irrelevant regions. DGDP is only used during training, incurring no inference cost.

#### 3.3. Structured Feature Flow Generation (SFFG)

SFFG models "how" the latent environment representations should evolve, addressing issues of semantic fragmentation and preserving local structure. It involves:

- **Prototype-to-Periphery (P2P) Prediction:**
 1. **Clustering:** Joint clustering is performed on the multi-view future features ($f_p^{t+T}$; $f_w^{t+T}$) to obtain a set of clusters ($C$), each with a centroid ($\mu_{\ell}$).
 2. **Ordering:** Within each cluster, members are sorted by their Euclidean distance to the cluster centroid, from nearest (prototype) to farthest (periphery), forming an ordered sequence ($\vec{c}_{\ell}$).
 3. **Forecasting Loss:** The P2P forecasting loss ($L_{P2P}$) is calculated over these ordered sequences. It is a cosine embedding loss between the predicted future feature ($\hat{v}_{t+T,j}$) and the ground-truth future feature ($v_{t+T,j}$), modulated by a global factor $\alpha$ and the DGDP prioritization weight $\beta_j$. This enforces a structured prediction flow, ensuring features from the same semantic unit are processed cohesively.
 $L_{P2P} = \frac{1}{|C|} \sum_{\ell=1}^{|C|} \frac{1}{|\vec{c}_{\ell}|} \sum_{j \in \vec{c}_{\ell}} (\alpha + \beta_j) \cdot (1 - \cos(\hat{v}_{t+T,j}, v_{t+T,j}))$

- **Mutual-Neighborhood Contrastive (MC) Loss:** To maintain topological consistency and address noisy clustering, MC loss builds high-confidence neighborhood relations.
 1. **Neighbor Set Construction:** For each anchor feature $i$, a first-order neighbor set ($G^{(1)}_i$) is formed by selecting the K most similar tokens. A second-order neighbor set ($G^{(2)}_i$) is then constructed from the M nearest neighbors of each first-order neighbor.
 2. **Positive Set Definition:** The positive set ($G^+_i$) for anchor $i$ includes only those tokens $j$ that are mutual neighbors (i.e., $j$ is a neighbor of $i$, and $i$ is also a neighbor of $j$) from the combined first and second-order sets.
 3. **InfoNCE Loss:** The MC loss ($L_{MC}$) applies the InfoNCE loss over these mutual-neighborhood positives, pulling them closer in the feature space while pushing other tokens away, thereby preserving reliable semantic neighborhood consistency.
 $L_{MC} = - \frac{1}{|I|} \sum_{i \in I} \frac{1}{|G^+_i|} \sum_{j \in G^+_i} \log \frac{\exp(S_{ij}/\tau_c)}{\sum_{\ell \in V \setminus \{i\}} \exp(S_{i\ell}/\tau_c)}$
 where $S_{ij}$ is the cosine similarity between features $i$ and $j$, and $\tau_c$ is a temperature parameter.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

The overall training objective combines three loss components:
1. **Action Prediction Loss ($L_{action}$):** An L1 regression loss between the predicted continuous actions and ground-truth actions, to filter noise.
2. **P2P Forecasting Loss ($L_{P2P}$):** As described above, for structured future feature prediction.
3. **Mutual-Neighborhood Contrastive Loss ($L_{MC}$):** As described above, for maintaining latent space topology.

The total loss is a weighted sum:
$L_{total} = \lambda_1 L_{action} + \lambda_2 L_{P2P} + \lambda_3 L_{MC}$
where $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ are hyperparameters. Like DGDP, SFFG components are used only during training.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

LEEVLA models are implemented at two scales:
- **LEEVLA-large:** Initialized from OpenVLA-7B and pretrained on the Open X-Embodiment dataset. Trained for 50k to 150k steps with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-4}$.
- **LEEVLA-mini:** Initialized from miniVLA and pretrained on LIBERO-90. Trained for 20k to 50k steps with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$.
All models use the AdamW optimizer. Real-world experiments are conducted using a Universal Robots UR5 arm for tasks like object placement, button pressing, and drawer closing, with 20 consecutive trials per setup.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

LEEVLA's performance was evaluated across simulation environments and real-world scenarios, with comprehensive ablation studies to assess the contribution of each proposed component.

#### 4.1. Simulation Environment Results

- **LIBERO Benchmark:** LEEVLA consistently achieved competitive performance across various manipulation tasks grouped into Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long categories.
 - **LEEVLA-mini (0.5B parameters):** Achieved an average success rate of 97.5%, outperforming other small-scale baselines such as Octo (75.1%), UniACT (77.8%), Seer (87.7% on Long), DreamVLA (92.6%), and FLOWER (95.7%). This indicates the effectiveness of LEEVLA's reasoning mechanisms even at a smaller scale.
 - **LEEVLA-large (7B parameters):** Achieved an average success rate of 98.2%, surpassing large-scale baselines including OpenVLA (76.5%), CoT-VLA (81.1%), π0 (94.1%), π0.5 (97.5%), OpenVLA-OFT (97.1%), and UniVLA (95.2%). LEEVLA-large demonstrated optimal or near-optimal performance across all LIBERO task suites.
- **CALVIN Benchmark:** LEEVLA-large showed strong performance on CALVIN ABC-D tasks, achieving an average length success rate of 4.34, which was higher than OpenVLA (3.80), π0 (3.87), π0.5 (4.02), and UniVLA (3.80).
- **Instruction-Relevance Visualization:** Visualizations (Fig. 4) of cosine similarity between vision features and instruction embeddings demonstrated that LEEVLA achieved a tighter coupling between visual observations and action generation compared to a baseline model without future state inference. This suggests that the DGDP and SFFG modules contribute to better utilization of visual embeddings for task guidance.

#### 4.2. Real-world Results

- **Quantitative Analysis:** In real-world manipulation tasks using a UR5 robotic arm (Placing an object, Pressing a button, Closing a drawer), LEEVLA-large exhibited higher success rates compared to OpenVLA. For example, LEEVLA-large achieved 70% for "Place," 80% for "Press," and 65% for "Drawer," with an average of 78.5%, significantly outperforming OpenVLA's average of 40%.
- **Qualitative Insights:** Qualitative results (Fig. 5) illustrated LEEVLA's ability to perform complex tasks in a real-world setting, demonstrating its generalization performance.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study on LIBERO-Goal using LEEVLA-mini investigated the contribution of each component:
- **Baseline (without P2P, MC, DPP, SDG):** Achieved 94.8% average success.
- **Adding P2P:** Improved performance to 95.2% (+0.4% over baseline), indicating the benefit of enforcing an ordered feature flow.
- **Adding MC Loss (P2P + MC):** Further boosted success to 95.6% (+0.8% over baseline), suggesting that preserving local semantic topology stabilizes future feature prediction.
- **Adding DPP (P2P + MC + DPP):** Yielded the largest single gain, reaching 96.3% (+1.5% over baseline), highlighting the importance of focusing supervision on interaction-centric regions.
- **Adding SDG (P2P + MC + DPP + SDG):** Led to the best overall performance of 97.0% (+2.2% over baseline), demonstrating the complementary nature of both SFFG and DGDP components for robust action policies.

#### 4.4. Discussion Points

- **Structured Latent Space Reasoning:** The paper argues that reasoning in a structured latent space, as facilitated by SFFG, allows the model to leverage relational structures already encoded in visual backbones, avoiding narrow human-selected hypotheses. Clustering visualization (Fig. 6) showed that patches with similar semantics were grouped into the same feature clusters.
- **Feature Reordering:** Reordering visual features (as part of P2P) improved success rates (95.2% with reorder vs. 94.7% without reorder), indicating that addressing fragmented semantic spaces caused by flat token prediction enhances generalization.
- **Global Factor ($\alpha$):** The global modulation factor $\alpha$ in the P2P loss proved beneficial. Using both $\alpha$ and prioritization weights $\beta$ (97.0% success) outperformed relying solely on $\beta$ (95.8% success). This suggests that $\alpha$ helps maintain global scene awareness, providing crucial spatial context from background tokens, while $\beta$ sharply highlights task-critical regions, preventing over-selectivity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LEEVLA framework introduces a method for enhancing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models through explicit task-evidence guidance and structured latent reasoning. This approach addresses several identified limitations in previous VLA systems, potentially influencing the development of more capable and generalist robotic policies.

The primary significance of this work lies in its two core contributions: Drift-Guided Dynamic Prioritization (DGDP) and Structured Feature Flow Generation (SFFG).
- **Enhanced Task Relevance and Efficiency:** DGDP enables the model to dynamically identify and prioritize regions of visual input that are both spatially active and semantically relevant to the language instruction. This mechanism redirects the model's focus from static backgrounds or irrelevant objects towards task-critical elements, potentially leading to more efficient learning and more precise actions by concentrating supervision where it is most impactful. This is a move towards a more human-like attention mechanism in robotic perception.
- **Robust Latent Space Reasoning:** SFFG introduces a structured approach to modeling the evolution of latent environment representations. By employing prototype-to-periphery (P2P) prediction, LEEVLA encourages coherent processing of features belonging to the same semantic units, thereby mitigating issues of semantic fragmentation. The mutual-neighborhood contrastive (MC) loss further strengthens this by preserving local topological consistency, leading to more stable and reliable future feature predictions. This structured reasoning in latent space, leveraging inherent relationships within visual features, offers an alternative to relying on human-engineered features or auxiliary models.
- **Reduced Inference Overhead:** A notable aspect of LEEVLA is that both DGDP and SFFG components are exclusively employed during training. This design ensures that the model's enhanced capabilities do not incur additional computational costs or memory overhead during inference, making it potentially more practical for real-world robotic deployments where latency and efficiency are critical.
- **Improved Generalization and Long-Horizon Reasoning:** The consistent performance gains observed across various benchmarks (LIBERO, CALVIN) and real-world tasks, at both mini and large model scales, indicate that explicit task-relevance guidance and structured latent reasoning contribute to improved generalization and the ability to handle longer-horizon tasks. This suggests a step towards more robust and adaptable robot behaviors in diverse and dynamic environments.

The potential impact of LEEVLA extends to:
- **Advancing Generalist Robot Policies:** By improving how VLA models perceive, reason about, and interact with complex environments, LEEVLA contributes to the development of more generalist robot policies that can perform a wider range of tasks with greater adaptability.
- **Reducing Data Dependency:** By operating in latent space and identifying task-critical features dynamically, LEEVLA may allow for more efficient learning from existing demonstration datasets, potentially reducing the need for highly specialized or exhaustively labeled datasets that explicitly highlight task-relevant features.
- **Inspiration for Future VLA Architectures:** The "where-how" training framework and the specific mechanisms of DGDP and SFFG could inspire future research in VLA model design, particularly in developing more intelligent attention mechanisms and structured world models that directly support action generation.

Overall, LEEVLA presents a method that addresses specific challenges in VLA, demonstrating improved performance and offering insights into the benefits of integrating task-aware feature prioritization and structured latent environment evolution.
