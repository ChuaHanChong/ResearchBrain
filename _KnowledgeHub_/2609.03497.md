# BRIDGE: An Open-Source Humanoid Platform via Morphology-Control Co-Design for Physical AI

> [!summary] Summary
> BRIDGE is a data-driven morphology-control co-design framework that jointly optimizes humanoid body structure and whole-body control for human-like motion. It compresses SMPL-derived degrees of freedom, instantiates candidates with calibrated actuator envelopes and torque-speed limits, evaluates them via closed-loop policy tracking, and upgrades saturated joints. A combined kinematic-plus-dynamic human-likeness score guides the loop, yielding an open-source 88cm, 21-DoF, $1.5K humanoid outperforming Bumi, K1 and ToddlerBot.

## Problem

- Humanoid hardware design and whole-body control are developed as isolated processes, producing bodies chosen by mechanical heuristics before any controller performance is known.
- Control research assumes a fixed embodiment, so policies must compensate for unfavorable joint topologies, limited ranges of motion and insufficient dynamic capability.
- Prior co-design work stays in simulation, ignoring actuator envelopes, calibrated torque limits and manufacturability, so discovered designs are not physically realizable.

## Method

- ==Two-stage DoF compression==: screen waist-axis pairs by kinematic retargeting error, then pick the final one-DoF waist by closed-loop dynamic tracking error, fixing a 21-DoF SMPL-derived topology.
- ==Actuator-aware instantiation== initializes smallest-volume actuators, enforces experimentally calibrated torque-speed curves, resolves compound-joint stacking by offsetting roll/yaw axes distally, and recomputes link mass and inertia.
- ==Failure-guided refinement== measures per-joint peak utilization, upgrades genuinely saturated actuators, rebuilds the URDF and retrains policies, optimizing kinematic error subject to height and motion-coverage constraints.

## Results

- Bridge scores **0.5252** human-likeness versus **0.4321** (Bumi), **0.4198** (K1), **0.3883** (ToddlerBot), with the lowest kinematic (**0.0260**) and dynamic (**0.0384**) errors.
- On merged LaFAN1 plus bones benchmark with SONIC policies, Bridge reaches **94.83%** success and best MPJPE **0.0711**, MPJVE **0.5167**, RootVelErr **0.1671**, MPKPE **38.43**.
- Category breakdown gives **95.00%** balance, **94.50%** highly dynamic, **94.99%** daily motion, beating K1 by **4.70 points** on dynamic motions; body scaling **1.02** versus SMPL, and fastest BeyondMimic convergence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Controller performance is a usable design signal: judging morphology by whether retargeted motion executes under closed-loop control, not just retargeting error alone.
> - Kinematic fidelity and dynamic feasibility trade off; compact actuators preserve proportions but saturate, so co-design should search a Pareto front rather than optimize either alone.
> - Local actuator upgrades shift whole-body mass distribution and can break previously successful motions, so refinement demands full re-instantiation and re-evaluation, not isolated limit edits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of humanoid robots capable of leveraging human behavioral data for general-purpose embodiment is a central objective in contemporary robotics, particularly within the field of data-driven Physical AI. Human data offers a comprehensive source of behavioral demonstrations, making robots that can effectively utilize this data critical for advancing general-purpose embodiment.

Historically, humanoid robot development has followed a decoupled paradigm, separating hardware design from whole-body control. Hardware is typically designed using heuristic reasoning and manual tuning, prioritizing mechanical aspects such as actuator placement, structural stiffness, manufacturability, anthropomorphic proportions, and joint arrangements. Concurrently, whole-body control policies are developed assuming a fixed robot morphology, focusing on generating feasible and robust motion profiles for the given hardware. Advances in control often leverage simulation-based reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and sim-to-real transfer, treating the robot's physical embodiment as a static baseline.

This conventional separation often results in suboptimal systems. Mechanical designs may not fully account for downstream operational workspaces or task constraints. Conversely, Physical AI researchers are frequently tasked with compensating for inherent limitations of a predefined morphology, such as non-human-like joint topologies, restricted ranges of motion, suboptimal link proportions, or insufficient dynamic capabilities. This can lead to motions that are dynamically feasible but lack the fluidity observed in human movement, or hardware that is mechanically sound but not ideally suited for the intended locomotion repertoire.

This paper addresses this challenge by proposing a morphology-control co-design framework. This approach represents a shift from sequential, decoupled optimization to a unified, iterative process where hardware design and control policy development are intrinsically linked. Prior work in robot co-design, particularly with evolutionary algorithms and data-driven methods, has largely been confined to simulation environments, often neglecting the practicalities of physical actuation systems, real-world manufacturing, and hardware feasibility. The authors position their work as the first co-design framework for humanoid robots that demonstrates joint effectiveness across both physical hardware design and control. They contrast their approach with existing open-source platforms like ToddlerBot, which, while valuable for ML-compatible locomotion, relies on manually specified and subsequently evaluated morphology.

In the domain of whole-body control, current learning-based methods, especially those employing reinforcement learning and sim-to-real pipelines, typically optimize controllers for pre-existing robot morphologies. The role of learning in these contexts is often to mitigate the limitations of a fixed body. This research differentiates itself by incorporating controller performance as a direct signal within the design process. Specifically, dynamic tracking error under a trained policy is integrated into the "human-likeness" metric, ensuring that a morphology is evaluated not only on its ability to retarget human motion but also on its capacity to physically execute that retargeted motion under closed-loop control. This integration aims to overcome the limitations imposed by a fixed morphology on the feasibility and naturalness of challenging motions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized bottleneck in developing humanoid robots: the conventional decoupled approach to hardware design and control policy development. This separation inhibits the creation of robots that can fluidly and agilely mimic human behavior, which is crucial for achieving general-purpose embodiment and effectively utilizing large-scale human behavioral data.

The key objectives of this work are as follows:

1. **Develop a Data-Driven Morphology-Control Co-Design Framework:** The central objective is to introduce a novel framework that intrinsically couples humanoid morphology optimization with the development of whole-body control policies. This framework is designed to iteratively refine robot morphology to enhance its ability to mimic human movement by jointly considering kinematic feasibility, actuator constraints, and dynamic tracking performance.
2. **Introduce a Novel Quantitative Metric for Human-Likeness:** To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of the co-design process and quantify the "human-likeness" of the resulting robot, the authors aim to define a new metric. This metric is intended to comprehensively assess a morphology–control pair by simultaneously considering both kinematic retargeting fidelity to human motion and dynamic tracking performance under closed-loop control.
3. **Achieve State-of-the-Art Performance:** A specific objective is to demonstrate that the humanoid robot designed using this co-design framework surpasses the performance of existing baseline humanoid platforms, such as Bumi, K1, and Toddlerbot, across the introduced human-likeness metrics.
4. **Realize and Open-Source a Physical Humanoid Platform:** The project aims to instantiate the optimized design into a tangible, open-source physical robot named "Bridge." This 88cm-tall humanoid platform, along with its control policies, is intended to democratize research in humanoid locomotion and manipulation by providing an accessible and validated tool for the broader physical AI research community.
5. **Validate Hardware and Control Capabilities:** The final objective is to experimentally validate that the Bridge platform can capture human motion data with superior fidelity and demonstrate exceptional performance across a diverse range of behaviors. This includes foundational locomotion, robust balance recovery, and highly dynamic maneuvers, showcasing the benefits of the morphology-control co-design approach in real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research treats humanoid morphology and locomotion control as coupled design variables, aiming to synthesize an embodiment that maintains kinematic fidelity to natural human motion while being dynamically realizable under physics-based control. The co-design framework operates through an iterative optimization loop structured across four main stages: Degree-of-Freedom (DoF) Compression, Actuator-aware Robot Instantiation, Motion-based Robot Evaluation, and Failure-Guided Robot Refinement.

#### 3.1. DoF Compression

The initial phase focuses on reducing the complexity of a human body model (specifically, the 23-DoF SMPL model) to a practical robotic design, particularly addressing the waist joint simplification. Human anatomical joints, like spherical 3-DoF joints, are often implemented in robots using 1-DoF rotary actuators, requiring kinematic decoupling sequences.

- **Stage 1: Kinematic Screening of Two-Waist-DoF Candidates:** The process begins by constructing three 22-DoF URDF candidates from the initial 23-DoF model, each retaining a pairwise combination of waist roll, pitch, and yaw. Large-scale human motion data is retargeted to these candidates, and a kinematic retargeting error ($E_{kin}$) is computed. This error quantifies the discrepancy in keypoint-level position and joint-angle alignment. The candidate with the lowest $E_{kin}$ (identified as retaining roll and yaw) is selected for the next stage, excluding the waist pitch DoF.
- **Stage 2: Dynamic Selection of One-Waist-DoF Candidates:** From the selected roll–yaw waist configuration, two 21-DoF URDF candidates are created, one retaining only the waist roll DoF and the other retaining only the waist yaw DoF. For each candidate, the URDF layout parameters are optimized by minimizing $E_{kin}$ with respect to retargeted human motion data. Subsequently, a tracking policy is trained for each optimized 21-DoF URDF using the same reference motion set and training pipeline. These candidates are then compared based on their dynamic tracking error ($E_{dyn}$), which measures the discrepancy between the reference motion and the physically executed robot motion under closed-loop control. The final waist DoF (identified as yaw) is selected based on the lowest $E_{dyn}$. This stage determines the fixed SMPL-derived base topology, designated G, with 21 active DoFs, specifying joint connectivity, rotational functions, and compound joint mechanisms (e.g., serial structures for shoulders and hips, parallel mechanism for ankles).

#### 3.2. Actuator-aware Robot Instantiation

This stage addresses the inherent trade-off between morphological fidelity (human-like joint locations and body proportions) and physical actuation capabilities (torque and speed). It integrates real physical actuator properties into the design process.

- **Compact Actuator Initialization:** To bias the co-design towards a compact, human-like morphology, each of the 21 joints is initially assigned the smallest-volume actuator from its candidate set.
- **Actuator Constraints and Dynamics:** Each selected actuator model incorporates its physical envelope, mounting clearances, mass, inertia, reduction ratio, and experimentally calibrated torque–speed curve. These realistic torque–speed limits are strictly enforced during physics simulation and policy training.
- **Spatial Resolution of Compound Joints:** To resolve physical collisions in compound joints (shoulders, hips) where ideal kinematic models assume intersecting axes, the pitch axis is anchored at the anatomical joint center. Roll and yaw axes are translated distally along the local limb axis, ensuring physical clearance based on actuator lengths and mounting clearance. The ankle's parallel mechanism is excluded from this serial-stacking rule.
- **Structural Optimization and Mass Redistribution:** Any change in actuator assignment necessitates updating visual and collision meshes, relocating actuator inertias, recalculating link lengths, and adjusting downstream joint locations. The overall robot height is constrained around 90 cm, and the mass, center of mass, and inertia of affected links are recomputed. Optimal structural parameters for a fixed actuator assignment are determined by minimizing the kinematic human-motion retargeting discrepancy ($E_{kin}$). This process yields a fully instantiated, physics-ready candidate robot model, $M^*(G, s, \theta)$, complete with geometrically consistent kinematics, collision bodies, inertial properties, and velocity-dependent torque limits.

#### 3.3. Motion-based Robot Evaluation

This stage evaluates the dynamic capabilities of each instantiated design.

- **Policy Fine-tuning:** For every candidate robot $M_s^*$ and reference motion $\tau$, a motion-specific policy $\pi_{s,\tau}^*$ is fine-tuned from a shared 21-DoF base policy. During this process, the candidate's specific mass distribution, inertial properties, and velocity-dependent torque limits are enforced as fixed physical constraints in the simulation.
- **Motion Feasibility Assessment:** A motion is deemed feasible if the policy rollout remains stable, successfully completes the target motion, and achieves a dynamic tracking error ($e_{dyn}$) below a predefined threshold $\epsilon$.
- **Motion Coverage:** The overall motion coverage ($R_{motion}$) for a candidate robot is defined as its success rate across the entire motion dataset. This serves as a functional metric, quantifying which motions are executable under the physical dynamics and actuator limits of the design, complementing the kinematic error $E_{kin}$.

#### 3.4. Failure-Guided Robot Refinement

This iterative process refines the robot design based on performance feedback.

- **Actuator Bottleneck Analysis:** For each failed motion, the peak utilization ratio of every actuator is analyzed. An actuator bottleneck is confirmed if a joint repeatedly saturates its torque or speed boundaries across multiple random seeds within a fixed training budget, and if artificially relaxing these boundaries allows for successful tracking.
- **Hardware Upgrade and Re-evaluation:** Identified saturated joints are upgraded to more capable actuators. Crucially, any actuator replacement necessitates a comprehensive recomputation of compound-joint stacking, regeneration of adjacent links and downstream joint locations, rebuilding of the complete URDF, and regeneration of actuator configuration files. Subsequently, policies are fine-tuned again, and the entire motion dataset is re-evaluated. This re-evaluation is vital as localized actuator upgrades can alter whole-body mass distribution, potentially compromising previously successful motions.
- **Co-design Objective and Termination:** The overarching co-design objective is to minimize $E_{kin}(M)$ while adhering to a height constraint (e.g., ≤ 90 cm) and achieving a minimum required motion coverage ($R_{min}$). The iterative refinement terminates when desired motion coverage rates are achieved, or when further actuator upgrades lead to size constraint violations or morphological penalties that outweigh their dynamic benefits. Upon finalizing the optimal hardware configuration, a single unified policy is trained across the retained motion set for final evaluation.

This methodology departs from conventional robot design by integrating actuator selection and structural adjustments directly into the optimization loop, driven by motion performance and physical constraints, rather than treating them as post-design considerations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that validate the proposed morphology-control co-design framework and the resulting BRIDGE platform.

#### 4.1. Human-likeness Metric and Co-design Outcome

A novel human-likeness score ($S_{HL}$) was introduced, combining kinematic retargeting error ($E_{kin}$) and dynamic tracking error ($E_{dyn}$). This metric, with equal weighting for kinematic and dynamic terms, provides a unified criterion for comparing different humanoid morphologies and their controllers.

- **DoF Compression:** The two-stage DoF compression process successfully identified the optimal waist configuration. Kinematic screening of 2-DoF waist candidates revealed that retaining (roll, yaw) yielded the lowest $E_{kin}$ (0.02570). Subsequently, dynamic selection of 1-DoF candidates demonstrated that retaining only the waist yaw DoF resulted in a lower $E_{dyn}$ (0.02115) compared to retaining only the waist roll DoF. This led to a fixed 21-DoF base topology for BRIDGE.
- **Actuator Assignment:** The actuator-aware instantiation and failure-guided refinement led to a specific actuator assignment (Table 3): 10-Nm class for upper-limb joints, 55-Nm class for bilateral hip-pitch and knee-pitch joints, and 25-Nm class for the remaining joints including the parallel ankle mechanisms. This optimized configuration balances morphological fidelity with dynamic capability.

#### 4.2. Objective Comparison with Baseline Platforms

BRIDGE was objectively compared against three existing humanoid platforms: Bumi, K1, and ToddlerBot, using the proposed human-likeness metric and motion tracking performance.

- **Human-likeness Score:** BRIDGE achieved the highest $S_{HL}$ of 0.5252, outperforming Bumi (0.4321), K1 (0.4198), and ToddlerBot (0.3883). This indicates that BRIDGE's morphology better preserves human motion kinematics and supports more accurate dynamic tracking under learned control. BRIDGE also exhibited the lowest $E_{kin}$ (0.0260) and $E_{dyn}$ (0.0384) among the compared platforms.
- **Body-Part Scaling:** Analysis of body-part scaling relative to the SMPL human model showed BRIDGE to be closest to the reference scale (mean scaling of 1.021236), suggesting more human-consistent body proportions and fewer morphology-induced retargeting mismatches compared to other platforms (ToddlerBot: 1.17, K1: 1.34, Bumi: 1.37).
- **Motion Tracking Performance:** When training SONIC policies on a unified motion benchmark (LaFAN1 and bones seed), BRIDGE demonstrated superior performance (Table 5). It achieved the highest success rate (SR 94.83%) and the lowest values across all reported tracking metrics, including Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE), Mean Per-Joint Velocity Error (MPJVE), Root Velocity Error (RootVelErr), and Mean Per-Keypoint Position Error (MPKPE).
 - Further analysis by motion category (Balance, Highly Dynamic, Daily Motion) revealed consistent outperformance by BRIDGE across all categories (Balance: 95.00%, Highly Dynamic: 94.50%, Daily Motion: 94.99%). BRIDGE's largest advantage was observed in highly dynamic motions, exceeding the strongest baseline (K1) by 4.70 percentage points, highlighting its morphology-control compatibility for complex, rapid movements.
- **Control Policy Convergence:** Training BeyondMimic on a dance motion from LaFAN1 showed that BRIDGE converged fastest and reached the lowest value-function loss among the evaluated morphologies, indicating improved learnability and control efficiency.

#### 4.3. Subjective and Qualitative Results

Subjective comparisons (Fig. 6) indicated that BRIDGE better preserved the dynamic consistency of human motions, such as maintaining the center-of-mass projection close to the support foot during toe-touching and single-leg standing, and maintaining reasonable pelvis height and torso orientation during low-center-of-mass turning. This suggests that the co-design approach improves not only pose similarity but also dynamic feasibility.

Qualitative real-world demonstrations (Fig. 7) showcased BRIDGE's capabilities in executing a diverse range of behaviors, including single-leg balance, dance routines (Charleston), backflips, and teleoperation sequences. These demonstrations, supported by accompanying videos, validate the physical platform's ability to perform complex, dynamic motions.

#### 4.4. BRIDGE Platform Specifications

The resulting BRIDGE platform is an 88cm tall, 13kg humanoid robot. Notably, it is open-source (both code and design) and positioned as a cost-effective solution at $1.5K, significantly lower than comparable platforms like Bumi ($3K), K1 ($5.7K), and ToddlerBot ($6K).

These findings collectively indicate that the proposed morphology-control co-design framework successfully produces a humanoid robot that is highly faithful to human kinematics and capable of dynamically executing complex human-like motions, surpassing existing platforms in quantitative and qualitative evaluations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the fields of robotics and physical AI by directly addressing a fundamental limitation in current humanoid robot development. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Hardware-Control Gap:** The primary impact lies in demonstrating a viable and effective morphology-control co-design framework. By treating robot morphology and control policies as intrinsically coupled design variables, the work offers a systematic methodology to overcome the "decoupled paradigm" that has historically led to suboptimal and less human-like robotic systems. This paradigm shift can lead to more inherently capable and agile humanoid robots.

2. **Advancement in Robot Design Methodology:** The introduction of an iterative optimization loop that integrates kinematic feasibility, actuator constraints, and dynamic tracking performance provides a robust computational approach to hardware design. This moves beyond heuristic and manual tuning, offering a principled way to synthesize robot embodiments that are optimized for specific behavioral repertoires. The consideration of real physical actuator properties, including experimentally calibrated torque-speed limits and spatial resolution of compound joints, enhances the practical applicability and realism of the design process.

3. **Novel Quantitative Evaluation Metric:** The proposed human-likeness score ($S_{HL}$) is a novel contribution that offers a more comprehensive and objective way to evaluate humanoid robot designs. By jointly considering kinematic fidelity and dynamic executability, it provides a crucial tool for benchmarking and driving progress in the development of robots that closely mimic human movement.

4. **Enabling General-Purpose Embodiment and Data-Driven AI:** The BRIDGE platform's superior ability to capture human motion data with high fidelity is directly relevant to the pursuit of general-purpose embodiment. By providing a robot whose morphology is optimized for human-like movement, this work facilitates research into leveraging large-scale human behavioral data for robot learning, imitation, and ultimately, more versatile and intelligent physical AI systems.

5. **Democratization of Humanoid Robotics Research:** The decision to open-source the BRIDGE platform, including its hardware design and control policies, significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers. At a cost significantly lower than comparable platforms, BRIDGE makes advanced humanoid robotics research more accessible to a wider academic community and individual researchers. This open approach can accelerate innovation, foster collaboration, and diversify the pool of contributors in a complex and resource-intensive research area.

6. **Potential for Real-World Applications:** The demonstrated capability of BRIDGE to execute foundational locomotion, robust balance, and highly dynamic maneuvers suggests its potential for a range of real-world applications where human-like agility and fluidity are beneficial. This includes tasks in complex environments, human-robot interaction, and service robotics.

7. **Inspiration for Future Work:** The authors acknowledge limitations, such as the reliance on rotary motors and the compact scale limiting workspace/payload. These limitations, along with the co-design framework itself, explicitly point to future research directions, including exploring alternative actuation mechanisms (e.g., tendon-driven systems for volume reduction and mass redistribution) and developing larger-scale implementations for enhanced manipulation capabilities. This work lays a foundation for continued exploration in computational hardware design and integrated robot systems.

In conclusion, this research not only presents a high-performing humanoid platform but also introduces a methodology that addresses a core challenge in the field, with the potential to significantly influence the future direction of humanoid robot design and the development of intelligent physical AI.
