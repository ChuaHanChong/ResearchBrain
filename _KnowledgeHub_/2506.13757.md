# AutoVLA: A Vision-Language-Action Model for End-to-End Autonomous Driving with Adaptive Reasoning and Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UCLA developed AutoVLA, a Vision-Language-Action model for end-to-end autonomous driving that unifies semantic reasoning and direct physical trajectory generation. The model employs an adaptive dual-thinking mode to switch between fast and slow reasoning, and achieves a 10.6% improvement in Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS) while reducing runtime by 66.8% through reinforcement fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Traditional modular autonomous driving systems suffer from error accumulation and suboptimal holistic optimization.
- Early end-to-end methods based on imitation learning lack world knowledge and robust reasoning for complex or long-tail scenarios.
- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models generate abstract actions requiring downstream planners or use inflexible reasoning strategies, leading to inefficiencies or inadequate responses.

## Method

- **AutoVLA** integrates a VLM backbone (Qwen2.5-VL-3B) with novel ==physical action tokenization== to directly generate physically feasible driving trajectories.
- The model employs an adaptive dual-thinking mode (fast for simple scenarios, slow with Chain-of-Thought for complex ones) within a unified autoregressive framework.
- A two-stage training process uses supervised fine-tuning with automatically generated reasoning data from a larger VLM, followed by reinforcement fine-tuning (GRPO) with a reward function that penalizes unnecessary CoT.

## Results

- Reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) on AutoVLA improved PDMS by **10.6%** and reduced inference runtime by **66.8%** on the nuPlan NAVSIM test set.
- AutoVLA achieved competitive performance on nuPlan and Waymo benchmarks, notably ranking highest in the RFS Spotlight metric for Waymo's challenging scenarios.
- In CARLA closed-loop simulations, AutoVLA outperformed existing end-to-end models with a driving score of 78.84 and a **57.73%** success rate on Bench2Drive.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unified autoregressive generation of both reasoning tokens and physical action tokens enables truly end-to-end planning with adaptive capabilities.
> - Discretizing continuous trajectories into specific physical action tokens effectively allows language models to output directly executable, physically compliant maneuvers.
> - Reinforcement learning, with a CoT penalty, can successfully train a VLA model to adaptively select between fast and slow thinking, balancing performance and computational efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous driving research has traditionally followed a **modular paradigm**, where tasks like perception, prediction, and planning are handled by separate, specialized components. While robust, this approach can suffer from error accumulation and a lack of holistic optimization across modules, leading to suboptimal performance in complex or novel situations. In response, **end-to-end autonomous driving** has emerged, aiming to map raw sensor inputs directly to driving actions using a single, unified model. Early end-to-end methods primarily relied on imitation learning from human driving data, but often lacked essential world knowledge and reasoning capabilities, making them vulnerable in long-tail or challenging scenarios.

The advent of **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)** has significantly impacted this landscape by introducing models capable of leveraging extensive world knowledge and powerful reasoning from diverse data sources. Building upon VLMs, **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models** extend this capability to generate physical actions, enabling embodied agents like autonomous vehicles to produce feasible actions based on visual observations and language instructions.

AutoVLA positions itself at the forefront of VLA research for autonomous driving by addressing two critical limitations of existing VLA models:
1. **Physically-infeasible or complex action generation:** Many VLM-based approaches either generate abstract textual actions or waypoints that require downstream planners to produce physically feasible trajectories, breaking the end-to-end paradigm or adding complexity. Others use latent action tokens, which still require decoders and increase model overhead. AutoVLA directly integrates physical action tokens into the language model, enabling direct autoregressive generation of feasible trajectories.
2. **Inflexible and inefficient reasoning:** Most VLM/VLA models employ a fixed reasoning strategy, either always performing elaborate chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning (slow thinking) or only direct action generation (fast thinking). This inflexibility leads to redundant reasoning in simple scenarios and potentially inadequate reasoning in complex ones. AutoVLA introduces an adaptive dual-thinking mode, allowing it to switch between fast and slow thinking as needed, enhancing both performance and efficiency. This contrasts with hybrid approaches like DriveVLM, which rely on separate modules for fast and slow responses, leading to architectural complexity.

By unifying reasoning and action generation within a single autoregressive model and incorporating adaptive thinking capabilities through novel tokenization and reinforcement fine-tuning, AutoVLA contributes to making end-to-end autonomous driving systems more robust, efficient, and intelligent in diverse, real-world scenarios. It represents a significant step towards creating autonomous driving systems that can both understand complex environments and act effectively within them, drawing inspiration from cognitive science's dual-process theory of thinking.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of AutoVLA are driven by the aforementioned limitations in current VLA models for autonomous driving:

1. **To unify reasoning and action generation:** AutoVLA aims to integrate semantic reasoning (understanding complex situations) and low-level trajectory planning (generating physically feasible movements) within a single autoregressive generation model. This seeks to overcome the drawbacks of modular systems and models that rely on intermediate, non-end-to-end representations.
2. **To enable physically feasible action outputs directly:** The model is designed to directly generate trajectories that are physically compliant and avoid mode collapse. This is achieved by tokenizing continuous trajectories into discrete, feasible action tokens, directly integrating them into the language model's output space.
3. **To develop adaptive reasoning capabilities:** A core objective is to allow the model to dynamically switch between "fast thinking" (direct trajectory generation for simple scenarios) and "slow thinking" (enhanced with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning for complex scenarios). This adaptive behavior is crucial for balancing planning accuracy with computational efficiency.
4. **To improve planning performance and efficiency:** Through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and a novel reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) method, AutoVLA seeks to enhance the accuracy of generated trajectories and reduce unnecessary reasoning, thereby boosting both performance and runtime efficiency.
5. **To leverage extensive world knowledge for robust driving:** By using a VLM backbone and incorporating CoT reasoning, AutoVLA aims to tap into a broader understanding of the world, allowing it to handle long-tail and challenging scenarios more effectively than traditional imitation learning methods.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the desire to create more capable and reliable end-to-end autonomous driving systems that can reason about their environment like humans do, while simultaneously executing precise and safe driving maneuvers without the complexities and inefficiencies of multi-module or inflexible VLA architectures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AutoVLA's methodology is built upon a novel architecture and a two-stage training process designed to achieve its objectives:

#### 3.1. Framework Components

- **VLM Backbone:** AutoVLA adopts **Qwen2.5-VL-3B** as its vision-language backbone. This choice balances strong visual understanding capabilities with computational efficiency, making it suitable for potential on-board deployment. The model is an autoregressive Transformer decoder, capable of processing both visual and textual inputs and generating sequences of tokens.
- **Action Tokenization:** To integrate continuous trajectories into the discrete token space of a language model, AutoVLA discretizes vehicle trajectories (P ∈ R^(τ×d)) into a sequence of physical action tokens `a = [a1..., aT]`. Each token `at ∈ A` represents a short-term spatial position and heading movement (∆x, ∆y, ∆θ) over 0.5 seconds, with a planning horizon of 5 seconds (10 action tokens). The action codebook `A = {a1..., aK}` (K=2048) is constructed using a **K-disk clustering method** on real-world (WOMD) and simulation (CARLA-Garage) datasets, ensuring physically feasible and diverse movement patterns. These action tokens are then incorporated as additional vocabulary into the VLM.
- **Unified Reasoning and Action:** The architecture allows for seamless, autoregressive generation of both reasoning tokens (language) and action tokens (physical trajectory). This unification enables adaptive switching between:
 - **Fast Thinking Mode:** Directly predicts action tokens for rapid responses in straightforward scenarios, with minimal or templated reasoning.
 - **Slow Thinking Mode:** Engages in structured Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning (scene analysis, critical object identification, intention reasoning) before generating action tokens, for complex or ambiguous scenarios. This dual capability is managed by system prompts and response formats.

#### 3.2. Training Process

AutoVLA's training proceeds in two main stages:

- **1. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
 - **Data Curation:** Training data includes both **trajectory-only data** (for fast thinking) and **CoT reasoning data** (for slow thinking).
 - **Automated Reasoning Data Generation:** A critical challenge is the lack of high-quality, large-scale driving reasoning datasets. AutoVLA addresses this by proposing an automated pipeline using the advanced **Qwen2.5-VL-72B model**. This larger VLM generates structured CoT annotations (scene description, critical objects, intentions, driving actions) by incorporating ground-truth driving actions as hints to guide causal explanations. This process yields approximately 45.6k CoT annotations for nuPlan and 7.2k for Waymo, augmented by reformatted DriveLM data.
 - **Loss Function:** SFT optimizes a combined loss: `L_SFT = w_i * (L_LM,i + λ_a L_action,i)`.
 - `L_LM`: Standard causal language modeling objective for reasoning capabilities.
 - `L_action`: Auxiliary loss specifically on the action tokens for planning accuracy.
 - `w_i`: A per-sample weighting factor (`λ_cot` if CoT is present, `1` otherwise) to address data imbalance and encourage learning from reasoning-augmented examples.
 - **Implementation:** FSDP strategy, mixed-precision (BFloat16), gradient checkpointing, learning rate warm-up and decay, trained for 5 epochs on 8 NVIDIA L40S GPUs.

- **2. Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT):**
 - **Objective:** To further improve planning performance, align with driving requirements (e.g., safety, comfort), and enable adaptive reasoning by penalizing unnecessary CoT.
 - **Algorithm:** Employs **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, which uses group-based sampling to compute advantage, stabilizing training and aligning with planning's multi-modality.
 - **Reward Function:** `r = r_Driving - λ_r r_CoT`.
 - `r_Driving`: Task-specific reward. For nuPlan, it's the **Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS)**, a comprehensive measure of driving quality (safety, comfort, progress). For Waymo, due to limited RFS data, it's the (normalized) **Average Displacement Error (ADE)**.
 - `r_CoT`: A penalty for CoT length, defined by a sigmoid function, to discourage excessively long reasoning chains and promote fast thinking in simple scenarios.
 - **Implementation:** Uses LoRA adapter for parameter-efficient training, learning rate of 3 × 10^(-5), KL regularization weight β=0.04.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

AutoVLA underwent extensive evaluation across real-world (nuPlan, Waymo, nuScenes) and simulated (CARLA) datasets, demonstrating competitive performance and validating its core design principles.

- **Data Scaling Effect:** Increasing training data consistently improved planning performance. Crucially, while CoT reasoning didn't always outperform action-only training with small datasets, it consistently surpassed it with larger training set sizes, highlighting the scalability and benefits of reasoning-augmented learning for complex scenarios.
- **Reinforcement Fine-tuning (RFT) Performance:** RFT significantly enhanced AutoVLA's capabilities:
 - **Performance Improvement:** RFT yielded a **10.6% improvement in PDMS** on the NAVSIM test set.
 - **Efficiency Gain:** It resulted in a **66.8% reduction in runtime** (average over 500 scenarios), effectively enabling adaptive reasoning by minimizing unnecessary "slow thinking" in straightforward situations due to the CoT length penalty.
 - **Adaptive Thinking:** Qualitative results clearly showcased RFT's ability to switch between fast thinking for simple cases and maintain detailed slow thinking for complex ones.
- **nuPlan Benchmark (NAVSIM):** AutoVLA achieved competitive results, with the RFT-enhanced model showing significant improvement. The "best-of-N" planning strategy, where an oracle selects the optimal trajectory from multiple generated candidates, further boosted PDMS, achieving competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art models like TrajHF, Hydra-MDP, and Centaur.
- **Waymo End-to-End Driving Performance:**
 - Pretraining on a combination of nuPlan and nuScenes (nuX) datasets significantly improved performance on Waymo, indicating enhanced scene understanding from diverse data.
 - Incorporating CoT reasoning further improved planning (RFS metric) compared to action-only training.
 - Post-training with RFT (using ADE as reward) achieved the best overall RFS metric, demonstrating its effectiveness in challenging, long-tail scenarios like construction zones. On the Waymo End-to-End Driving Challenge leaderboard, AutoVLA achieved competitive RFS and ADE scores, notably ranking highest in the RFS Spotlight metric for challenging scenarios.
- **CARLA Closed-loop Performance:** In closed-loop simulations on the Bench2Drive benchmark, AutoVLA outperformed existing end-to-end driving models, achieving a **driving score of 78.84 and a success rate of 57.73%**, which is significantly higher than previous methods like Orion (77.74 driving score, 54.62% success rate) and UniAD-Base (45.81 driving score, 16.36% success rate).
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Physical Action Tokenization vs. Text Waypoints:** AutoVLA's physical action tokenization method significantly outperformed generating text-based waypoints (71.31 vs. 80.54 PDMS, 7.65s vs. 3.95s runtime), demonstrating its superiority in terms of planning quality and computational efficiency, particularly highlighting the limitations of language models for precise numerical reasoning.
 - **Action Tokenization Methods:** The proposed K-disk clustering method consistently achieved higher reconstruction accuracy across various codebook sizes compared to alternatives like RT-1 and FAST, especially at K=2048, which balances accuracy, movement coverage (99.42%), and efficient codebook usage. This also translated to better planning performance on NAVSIM.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AutoVLA represents a significant advancement in end-to-end autonomous driving, particularly in the realm of Vision-Language-Action models. Its key contributions and potential impacts are:

1. **Unified and End-to-End Optimization:** By integrating reasoning and action generation into a single autoregressive model, AutoVLA moves closer to a truly end-to-end learning paradigm, potentially mitigating error accumulation issues inherent in modular systems. This design simplifies the overall autonomous driving stack and allows for more holistic optimization.
2. **Adaptive Reasoning for Efficiency and Robustness:** The introduction of dual-thinking modes (fast and slow thinking) with adaptive switching, enhanced by RFT, is a crucial step towards creating more intelligent and efficient autonomous systems. This allows the vehicle to react quickly in simple situations while engaging in deeper, human-like reasoning for complex and safety-critical scenarios, thereby balancing performance and computational demands.
3. **Physically Feasible and Direct Action Generation:** The novel physical action tokenization method ensures that the language model directly outputs physically viable trajectories, avoiding the complexities and potential infeasibility issues of textual or latent action representations that require downstream processing. This is vital for real-world deployment.
4. **Leveraging Large-Scale Models for Data Generation:** The automated reasoning annotation pipeline, utilizing powerful VLMs like Qwen2.5-VL-72B, addresses a major bottleneck in VLM-based driving research: the scarcity of high-quality, large-scale reasoning datasets. This methodology can accelerate the development of more intelligent driving policies.
5. **Strong Empirical Performance:** AutoVLA's superior performance across multiple diverse and challenging benchmarks (nuPlan, Waymo, CARLA) in both open-loop and closed-loop settings demonstrates its practical efficacy and competitive edge over existing state-of-the-art methods.

**Potential Impact:**
The development of AutoVLA could lead to more reliable, safer, and adaptable autonomous vehicles, especially in urban environments with unpredictable elements and long-tail events. Its ability to reason and adapt could enhance passenger comfort and trust. Moreover, the proposed methodologies, particularly the adaptive reasoning and RL-based fine-tuning with a physically grounded action space, could be generalized to other embodied AI tasks beyond autonomous driving, such as robotics.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
Despite its advancements, AutoVLA faces challenges, primarily related to its computational demands. The model, even with dual-process adaptation, remains highly GPU-dependent, requiring significant memory and computing resources for its current near-real-time inference (1 Hz). Future work will need to focus on optimizing runtime efficiency for true real-time applications and reducing computational overhead, potentially through model quantization or distillation, to enable wider deployment. The broader impact section also wisely highlights the need for robust safeguards against adversarial attacks and proactive filtering of unsafe human instructions, given the safety-critical nature of autonomous driving and the integration of language guidance. These are critical considerations for safe and ethical deployment of such advanced AI systems.
