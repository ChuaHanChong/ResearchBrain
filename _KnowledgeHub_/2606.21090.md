# Self-Improvement Can Self-Regress: The Rise-and-Collapse Failure Mode of LLM Self-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> REINFORCE post-training on code makes pass@1 peak in tens of gradient steps then collapse to near zero inside the same campaign. The paper documents this rise-then-collapse on Qwen-2.5 3B/7B with binary CodeGrader reward and compares three control levels: between-campaign memory and gating (CARE), within-campaign early stopping (ES), and swapping the update rule to GRPO. Which level wins is regime-dependent.

## Problem

- Self-play, iterative DPO, STaR and self-reward pipelines optimize one scalar target metric, recording that a strategy helped but not which capabilities it silently regressed.
- Prior collapse studies name what self-improvement degrades, diversity, OOD generalization, exploration, but not when the degradation becomes actionable inside a chained RL loop.
- Continual-learning remedies target cross-task forgetting, while here the model self-regresses on a fixed distribution, and parameter-level KL or EWC constraints fail to prevent it.

## Method

- **CARE** couples ==Capability-Effect Memory== (Gaussian posterior over capability deltas), a four-action ==transfer gate== (reuse/adapt/pilot/reject) under a regression-probability constraint, and ==regression-aware belief revision==; the deployed gate uses the scalar end/peak pass@1 ratio.
- **ES** is a deployed within-campaign rule: set the next campaign's budget to clamp(peak_step + 3, 8, 20), preserve base hyperparameters, and roll the peak checkpoint forward.
- The algorithm-level arm swaps ==REINFORCE== for ==GRPO== via group-relative reward centering only, and per-step trajectory diagnostics (==phase-transition score==, ==collapse-onset step==, ==peak-to-end gap==) attribute each level's gain.

## Results

- A 7B diagnostic run rises from **25%** to **81%** pass@1 by step **50**, then falls near zero by step **200**; EWC and adaptive KL end at **0-10%**.
- At 3B, CARE v2 reaches **9.5%** end-of-chain pass@1 versus naive **4.9%** (paired bootstrap CI [**+0.4, +8.9**], **4/5** seeds positive) and beats ES at **8.2%**.
- At 7B, CARE is at parity (**13.8%** vs **11.8%**), ES reaches **22.2%** and naive GRPO **20.7%**, yet both update rules leave a **~17 pt** peak-to-end gap.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Match the control loop to the failure's timescale: a collapse that delivers 78% of its drop in one gradient step gives an end-of-campaign gate zero actionable latency.
> - More capable models flip the answer: campaign-level memory pays only when the per-step signal is too fragile for a cheap online stopping rule.
> - A variance-reduced update rule can raise the end-of-chain floor by improving carryover while leaving the within-campaign cliff intact; floor and cliff are separate problems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of self-improving artificial intelligence systems, particularly large language models (LLMs). Contemporary approaches such as iterative Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), self-play, self-reward training (STaR), and autonomous research agents aim to enhance model capabilities by enabling them to learn from their own generated outputs. The underlying premise of these methods is the creation of a positive feedback loop where model improvements lead to better data, which in turn fuels further enhancements.

However, a growing body of concurrent research, particularly in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), has identified a range of failure modes that challenge this virtuous cycle. These include policy entropy collapse, diminished exploration, and the degradation of non-target capabilities, even as the primary optimized metric improves. This phenomenon is sometimes termed "self-improvement reversal" or "reward over-optimization," where models become narrowly optimized and brittle.

This paper specifically documents a "rise-then-collapse" failure mode observed during REINFORCE post-training for code generation. It distinguishes this phenomenon from catastrophic forgetting, arguing that the collapse occurs *within-task* on a fixed distribution due to policy over-optimization, rather than being induced by task-switching. The work complements existing research by focusing on the operational question of where and at what level of intervention control loops should be placed to mitigate this observed collapse and sustain performance over extended training campaigns. It investigates whether parameter-level regularization, campaign-level memory and gating, or fundamental changes to the RL update rule are effective in addressing this challenge.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to document and analyze a specific failure mode, termed "rise-then-collapse," which occurs during the self-training of LLMs, particularly when using REINFORCE post-training for code generation tasks. The paper identifies that a model can initially improve rapidly on the metric it optimizes, only to experience a sharp decline in performance within the same training campaign.

The motivation for this work stems from the observation that while self-improving AI systems are designed to foster continuous gains, they are not inherently immune to detrimental effects. The authors hypothesize that improvement strategies, rather than being simple scalar operators that uniformly increase a target metric, function as "capability tradeoff operators." This means that while a strategy might enhance a target capability, it can simultaneously degrade other, potentially unmonitored, capabilities. The paper terms this phenomenon "capability regression through scalar optimization." Traditional scalar-objective self-improvement pipelines, which only track the target metric, are posited to discard critical information about these tradeoffs, leading to the accumulation of "hidden damage" that eventually results in performance collapse.

Given this observed rise-then-collapse behavior and the hypothesis of capability tradeoffs, the central operational question guiding the research is: "where should the control loop sit?" The paper aims to evaluate the effectiveness of different intervention levels in preventing or mitigating this collapse and sustaining "end-of-chain pass@1" in sequential self-training campaigns. To address this, the authors introduce and evaluate three distinct levels of intervention:
1. **Between-campaign control:** Managed by a meta-scientific memory system called Capability-Aware Research Experience (CARE). This system is designed to record the full capability effects of strategies, gate their transfer, and revise beliefs about strategy effectiveness over time. CARE is specifically developed as a stress test for campaign-level intervention, formalizing how memory and gating could hypothetically manage complex capability vectors.
2. **Within-campaign control:** Implemented through an early-stopping (ES) rule that monitors per-step performance trajectories and intervenes during a campaign to prevent collapse.
3. **Algorithm-level control:** Involves modifying the underlying reinforcement learning update rule itself, specifically by replacing vanilla REINFORCE with Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), to investigate if intrinsic algorithmic changes can inherently reduce the propensity for collapse.

By systematically comparing these intervention levels, the research seeks to provide empirical insights into which control mechanisms are effective, at what scale, and for which aspects of the self-regression problem.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a controlled, multi-seed testbed to investigate the rise-then-collapse failure mode and the efficacy of various interventions.

**Testbed and Base Models:**
Experiments primarily utilized Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct models. A single-seed pilot on Gemma-3-4B was also conducted for cross-family validation. Qwen-2.5 was selected for its stability, open-weight status, and availability of dense checkpoints, facilitating controlled scale comparisons.

**Task and Reward:**
Models were trained on competitive programming tasks, drawing problems from HumanEval, MBPP, and APPS. The reward signal was a binary CodeGrader reward, indicating functional correctness of generated code. The primary capability tracked was `pass@1`. Multi-dimensional capabilities (e.g., diversity, hard-case accuracy, robustness, cross-language transfer) were also assessed post-hoc as diagnostic evaluations, but the deployed CARE gate predominantly used a scalar `pass@1` signal during training.

**Campaign Structure and Length Protocols:**
Three distinct campaign length protocols were used:
1. **Diagnostic 200-step run:** A single-seed, continuous training run for 200 gradient steps to characterize the full rise-then-collapse trajectory.
2. **Module-ablation campaigns:** Three sequential 50-step campaigns per condition, used to isolate the contributions of different CARE modules.
3. **Headline long-chain protocol:** Ten sequential 20-step campaigns, serving as the primary setting for main results, scale comparisons, and intervention evaluations. This protocol maximized the number of between-campaign decisions for CARE.

**Baselines:**
- **Naive (no KL):** Standard REINFORCE without any regularization.
- **EWC (KL=0.05):** REINFORCE with a strong KL penalty to a reference policy.
- **Adaptive KL:** REINFORCE with a progressively increasing KL schedule.
- **Best-checkpoint oracle:** A hypothetical baseline that always selects the peak-performing checkpoint.
- **Random Hyperparameter Optimization (HPO):** Random search over learning rate and KL settings.
- **LLM-agent HPO:** An external LLM (Gemini Pro) proposing hyperparameters based on scalar `pass@1` history.
- **Frozen Qwen-2.5-32B-Instruct:** A larger, untuned model used as a performance reference.

**Intervention Methodologies:**
1. **Capability-Aware Research Experience (CARE):**
 - **Architecture:** Comprises three modules:
 - **Capability-Effect Memory:** Stores structured entries for each (strategy, context) pair, including observed capability deltas (initially `pass@1`), boundary conditions, and confidence scores. It aggregates observations into a linear Gaussian posterior over capability deltas.
 - **Self-Improvement Transfer Gate:** Decides whether to reuse, adapt, pilot, or reject a past strategy. In its deployed form for headline experiments, it used a scalar end/peak `pass@1` ratio as its signal, maximizing target improvement subject to no hidden regression on protected capabilities.
 - **Regression-Aware Belief Revision:** Updates the memory, boundary predicates, posterior, and the gate's regression-probability threshold when observed effects deviate significantly from predictions.
 - **CARE v1 vs. v2:** An earlier version (v1) that cumulatively shrank the learning rate was found counter-productive. CARE v2 preserved the base learning rate and routed collapse responses through step budget adjustments and gate sensitivity, improving its effectiveness.

2. **Early Stopping (ES):**
 - **Mechanism:** A within-campaign control rule. It stops a campaign if `pass@1` exhibits three consecutive declines (each > 0.02 absolute) from its running peak. The checkpoint at the running peak is rolled forward, and the next campaign's budget is set to `peak_step+3` (clamped between 8 and 20 steps). This is an online, deployable rule.

3. **Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):**
 - **Mechanism:** An algorithm-level intervention. Vanilla REINFORCE was replaced with GRPO, involving two key changes: `use_score_centering=True` (group-relative reward normalization) and `num_alter_tokens=4` (off-policy alternation window). Other hyperparameters, orchestrator, and chain length remained identical to isolate the effect of the RL update rule.

**Experimental Protocol:**
All experiments were performed on 1x8 GB200 GPUs. Training used 16 samples per prompt, temperature 0.7, and a learning rate of 10^-6. Checkpoints were stored in FSDP format. Statistical analysis relied on bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mean performance and paired bootstrap CIs for differences between conditions across multiple seeds (typically n=3 or n=5 seeds per condition).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the "rise-then-collapse" failure mode and the effectiveness of different intervention strategies:

#### 4.1. The Rise-Then-Collapse Phenomenon

The "rise-then-collapse" failure mode was consistently observed in REINFORCE post-training for code generation. For instance, Qwen-2.5-7B on competitive programming tasks showed `pass@1` rising from 25% to 81% within 50 gradient steps, then collapsing to near-zero by step 200 (Figure 1). This collapse was determined to be a *within-task* phenomenon due to policy over-optimization on a fixed distribution, rather than catastrophic forgetting across tasks.

#### 4.2. Ineffectiveness of Parameter-Level Regularization

Neither EWC-style KL constraints nor adaptive KL schedules prevented the within-campaign collapse. In fact, KL regularization was sometimes counterproductive, anchoring the model to degraded states and preventing recovery in subsequent campaigns. All tested parameter-level methods consistently ended near zero `pass@1` across multiple 50-step campaigns.

#### 4.3. Scale-Dependent Efficacy of CARE (Between-Campaign Control)

- **Qwen-2.5-3B (Fragile Regime):** CARE v2 nearly doubled the mean end-of-chain `pass@1` from 4.9% (naive) to 9.5% (CARE) over 10 sequential 20-step campaigns (n=5). A paired bootstrap 95% CI of the per-seed difference ([+0.4, +8.9]) excluded zero, indicating a statistically significant improvement. CARE also outperformed a deployed within-campaign early-stop (ES) rule at this scale (8.2%).
- **Qwen-2.5-7B (Rich-Signal Regime):** CARE v2 reached parity with naive REINFORCE (13.8% vs 11.8%), with overlapping confidence intervals. An earlier 3-seed estimate suggested an advantage, but this was not sustained with a larger sample.
- **Compute Efficiency:** At 7B, CARE v2 achieved a higher mean end `pass@1` (13.8%) compared to naive REINFORCE (11.8%) while consuming 14.5% less compute on average (141 vs 165 total gradient steps). This translates to 38% higher `pass@1` per 100 steps.

#### 4.4. Effectiveness of Early Stopping (ES) (Within-Campaign Control)

- **Qwen-2.5-7B:** The deployed ES condition achieved the highest end-of-chain `pass@1` among orchestrated REINFORCE configurations, reaching 22.2% [14.1, 28.0] (n=3) over 10 campaigns. This result demonstrated that a real-time, within-campaign rule could recover a substantial portion of the performance lost to collapse. This performance was achieved using approximately 51 cumulative gradient steps across the full chain, significantly less than naive training.
- **Oracle Bounds:** Simulated online early-stopping on existing traces suggested an achievable upper bound of 34-35% `pass@1`, while a hindsight oracle (maximum `pass@1` at any point) indicated a ceiling of 37.8-47.9%. This implied significant headroom for within-campaign interventions.

#### 4.5. Impact of GRPO (Algorithm-Level Control)

- **Qwen-2.5-7B:** Naive GRPO (without orchestration or early stopping) achieved an end-of-chain `pass@1` of 20.7% [15.7, 25.1] (n=5), nearly matching the performance of REINFORCE with ES (22.2%).
- **GRPO + CARE:** Adding CARE on top of GRPO at 7B yielded no measurable additional improvement (20.4%).
- **GRPO Mechanism:** Trajectory diagnostics revealed that GRPO did *not* eliminate the within-campaign peak-to-end collapse gap (which remained approximately 17 percentage points, similar to REINFORCE). Instead, GRPO's benefit derived from *improved between-campaign carryover*, where each campaign's end checkpoint transferred learning more effectively to the next, preventing gradual degradation across the chain.
- **GRPO + ES (W18):** An additivity test of GRPO with ES at 7B showed mixed results. While 2 out of 3 seeds improved over naive GRPO, a single catastrophic collapse in the final campaign of one seed prevented a statistically significant mean gain. This suggested that while GRPO improved the floor and ES targeted the cliff, neither fully eliminated the possibility of a single-campaign collapse.
- **Qwen-2.5-3B:** At the smaller scale, GRPO underperformed REINFORCE+CARE.

#### 4.6. Multi-Dimensional Capability Tracking and Scale

Scalar `pass@1` improvements on the training distribution did not consistently transfer to held-out cross-language (C++) or out-of-distribution (non-English Python) problems. For example, a 7B CARE chain showed 15.2% `cpp_acc` compared to 16.0% for naive. `ood_acc` remained near 0% across all conditions. A post-hoc audit indicated that the multi-capability information was non-trivial, with different winners on `main pass@1` versus `cpp_acc` in some seeds, suggesting value in a true K-dimensional gate.

#### 4.7. CARE vs. Raw Model Scale

Five or ten campaigns of CARE-orchestrated training on Qwen-2.5-7B did not enable it to surpass a frozen Qwen-2.5-32B-Instruct model (13.8% vs 37.7% end `pass@1`). This indicated that CARE is an orchestration framework for improving training trajectories, not a substitute for larger model scale.

#### 4.8. Structural Findings for Scale-Dependent Benefit

Analysis of per-step `pass@1` trajectories revealed why end-of-campaign interventions have scale-dependent effects:
- **Phase-Transition-Like Collapse:** Approximately 78% of the total peak-to-end `pass@1` drop occurred in a single gradient step, indicating a "cliff-like" rather than smooth decay.
- **Post-Hoc Signal:** Collapse onset typically occurred late in a campaign (mean onset at step 15.6/20 for a 20-step campaign), leaving zero usable post-onset latency for an end-of-campaign gate to react in real-time.
- **Compounding Errors:** While local peak-checkpoint selection offered high leverage within a single step, this leverage did not reliably compound over 10 sequential campaigns, suggesting that subsequent campaign dynamics could partly undo prior recoveries.
- **Root Cause:** These issues manifest within a fixed distribution, pointing to within-task policy over-optimization as the root cause, rather than task switching.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic analysis of a critical failure mode in LLM self-training and evaluates various intervention strategies. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Characterization of a Robust Failure Mode

The paper robustly documents and characterizes the "rise-then-collapse" phenomenon, demonstrating its prevalence in REINFORCE post-training for code generation on established LLM families (Qwen-2.5, and a pilot on Gemma-3-4B). The finding that this is a *within-task policy over-optimization* rather than catastrophic forgetting (task-switching) is important for designing targeted mitigation strategies. The ineffectiveness of conventional parameter-level regularization (KL/EWC) highlights the need for alternative control mechanisms.

#### 5.2. Framework for Analyzing Intervention Levels

The work offers a framework for understanding and addressing self-regression by decomposing the problem into two distinct timescales: within-campaign cliffs and between-campaign carryover problems. By evaluating interventions at three levels (between-campaign, within-campaign, and algorithm-level), the paper provides a structured approach for analyzing where control loops are most effectively placed.

#### 5.3. Practical Guidance for Practitioners

The findings offer actionable insights for practitioners engaged in self-improving LLM training:
- **Small Models (Fragile Regime, e.g., Qwen-2.5-3B):** Between-campaign memory and gating (CARE) can significantly improve end-of-chain performance where naive training is unstable.
- **Larger Models (Rich-Signal Regime, e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B):** Within-campaign early stopping (ES) is highly effective, recovering substantial performance by terminating training before collapse.
- **Algorithm Choice:** Replacing vanilla REINFORCE with GRPO can significantly raise the end-of-chain performance floor by improving between-campaign carryover, potentially matching the performance of orchestrated REINFORCE+ES without explicit campaign-level orchestration.
- **Complementarity:** The analysis suggests that GRPO (raising the floor) and ES (targeting the cliff) are complementary, addressing different aspects of the failure mode.

#### 5.4. Identification of Unresolved Challenges and Future Directions

Despite the progress, the research identifies that even a combination of algorithm-level and within-campaign control (GRPO+ES) does not entirely eliminate the possibility of a single-campaign collapse, particularly late in a training chain. This highlights the need for further research into more robust control mechanisms, such as single-campaign restart or rollback policies. The paper also points towards the importance of developing online collapse predictors that can act on *leading indicators* (e.g., entropy decay, diversity drop, gradient-norm trajectories) before the cliff, rather than relying on post-hoc performance signals. Furthermore, the non-trivial nature of multi-dimensional capability tradeoffs underscores the need for deploying true K-dimensional gates rather than scalar reductions, and for advancing fully autonomous self-improvement where models develop meta-level reasoning capabilities.
