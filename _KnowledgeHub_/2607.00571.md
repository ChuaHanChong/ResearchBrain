# Enhancing Robustness in Robot-Environment Interactions through Passive Compliant Degrees of Freedom: A Hybrid Position-Force Control Approach with Feedback Linearization

> [!summary] Summary
> This study proposes an interaction architecture integrating a physical passive compliant degree of freedom at the robot's end-effector with feedback-linearized hybrid position-force control. The spring-damper configuration notably reduced normal velocity error standard deviation by 41.1% and normal force error standard deviation by 25.4% under variable environmental conditions in simulation, enhancing robustness during contact.

## Problem

- Robot-environment interactions face challenges like impact shocks, vibrations, and instability due to uncertainties in mechanical properties and surface geometry.
- Existing active control methods, including hybrid position-force control and impedance control, are susceptible to strong impacts and rapid environmental changes due to bandwidth limitations and difficulty in gain tuning.
- Prior research has primarily focused on virtual compliance and software-level adaptations, with limited exploration of physically embedded passive compliance for enhancing interaction robustness.

## Method

- A novel hybrid physical-control architecture was developed, combining ==feedback-linearized hybrid position-force control== with a physical ==passive compliant element== (spring or spring-damper) at the end-effector.
- Three end-effector configurations (rigid, spring-only, and spring-damper) were systematically compared in simulation using a ==2-DOF planar manipulator==.
- The approach evaluated performance under both fixed ==viscoelastic== environmental conditions and time-varying sinusoidal normal force disturbances to assess robustness against uncertainty.

## Results

- The spring-damper end-effector consistently reduced contact-induced oscillations, lowered force and velocity error variance, and produced smoother joint-torque responses compared to rigid and spring-only configurations.
- Under fixed environmental conditions, the spring-damper configuration reduced normal position, velocity, and force error standard deviations by **8.0%**, **13.1%**, and **9.8%** respectively.
- In time-varying environments, the spring-damper significantly reduced normal velocity error standard deviation by **41.1%** and normal force error standard deviation by **25.4%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating physical passive compliance, particularly with damping, directly at the contact interface effectively stores and dissipates impact energy, mitigating high-frequency shocks before they propagate to the active control loop.
> - The synergy between inherent mechanical energy management of passive elements and the precision of active control offers superior robustness and stability in dynamic interaction scenarios.
> - Physical damping plays a crucial role in suppressing rebound oscillations and reducing variance-based tracking errors more effectively than purely elastic compliance or rigid interactions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Robot-environment interaction is a fundamental aspect of many modern robotic applications, including assembly, polishing, surface finishing, surgery, and human-robot collaboration. These tasks necessitate precise trajectory following alongside regulated contact forces. However, practical deployment is challenging due to inherent uncertainties in mechanical properties, surface geometry, and the dynamic nature of interaction, which can lead to impact shocks, vibrations, inaccurate force regulation, and potential instability.

Existing control paradigms have attempted to address these challenges:
- **Classical position control** excels in free space but fails to regulate contact forces upon constraint, potentially causing instability.
- **Force control** focuses on force regulation but offers no guarantees for unconstrained motion.
- **Hybrid position-force control**, pioneered by Raibert and Craig, combines these by allocating motion control to unconstrained directions and force control to constrained ones. While widely adopted, it remains susceptible to uncertainties and abrupt impacts, primarily relying on active feedback, sensing, and gain tuning.
- **Passivity-based control** ensures stability through energy exchange, yet its performance can degrade under strong impacts or rapid environmental changes.
- **Virtual compliance methods**, such as impedance and admittance control (Hogan), model interaction as a virtual mass-spring-damper system. While valuable, these are highly dependent on sensing accuracy and may have limited bandwidth during high-impact collisions.
- **Dynamic compensation strategies** like feedback linearization (Slotine and Li) improve trajectory tracking for nonlinear robot dynamics but typically assume smooth interactions, leaving them vulnerable to modeling uncertainties and abrupt forces.

Recent research has advanced compliant force control through:
- **Variable admittance control** for adapting compliance to changing human or environmental stiffness.
- **Safety-critical admittance frameworks** integrating control barrier functions or optimization-based constraints.
- **Robust variable admittance control** for co-manipulation under unknown payloads.
- **Sliding-mode and passivity-related variable admittance strategies** for enhanced robustness and stability.
These approaches primarily leverage *virtual compliance* or *software-level* adaptations.

This paper identifies a critical gap: the limited exploration of the complementary role of *physical passive compliance* directly integrated at the robot's end-effector. Most literature focuses on virtual compliance or software-based solutions. The novelty of this work lies in systematically investigating how physically embedded passive elements (spring-only and spring-damper) at the contact interface interact with a feedback-linearized hybrid position-force controller, thereby providing mechanical energy storage and dissipation *before* contact disturbances fully propagate to the active control loop. This distinction is crucial for enhancing robustness against contact transients and model mismatches beyond what active control alone can achieve.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical limitations of conventional active control strategies when robots interact with dynamic, unstructured, or uncertain environments. Such environments frequently introduce impact shocks, vibrations, and unpredictable variations in contact geometry and mechanical properties. These factors can lead to inaccurate force regulation, system instability, safety concerns, and reduced durability of the robot and its surroundings.

Existing active control methods, including classical hybrid position-force control, impedance control, and passivity-based approaches, rely heavily on sensory feedback, sophisticated algorithms, and precise gain tuning. While effective in well-modeled or predictable scenarios, they often exhibit degraded performance when confronted with strong impacts, rapid changes in environmental stiffness, or unknown contact conditions. The bandwidth limitations of active control loops and the challenge of tuning static gains for diverse operating conditions further exacerbate these issues.

The core objective of this paper is to enhance the robustness and stability of robot-environment interactions by proposing and evaluating an interaction architecture that integrates a *physical passive compliant degree of freedom (DOF)* directly at the end-effector with a feedback-linearized hybrid position-force control scheme.

Specific aims derived from this objective include:
1. **Develop a novel hybrid physical-control architecture:** To combine the benefits of active feedback-linearized hybrid position-force control with the inherent mechanical energy management capabilities of passive compliant elements.
2. **Utilize passive DOF for mechanical energy management:** To enable the physical spring-damper interface to store and dissipate impact energy directly at the contact point, thereby mitigating high-frequency shocks before they propagate to the robot's actuated joints and the active force-control loop. This contrasts with purely active systems that handle transients primarily through feedback regulation.
3. **Systematically compare passive configurations:** To quantitatively assess the distinct contributions of elastic energy storage (spring-only) and viscous energy dissipation (spring-damper) by evaluating three end-effector configurations (rigid, spring-only, and spring-damper) under identical feedback-linearized hybrid control. This comparison is conducted across both fixed and time-varying environmental conditions to gauge robustness against uncertainty.
4. **Demonstrate performance improvements:** To show that the integrated spring-damper interface can reduce contact-induced oscillations, lower force and velocity error variance, and produce smoother joint-torque responses compared to the rigid and spring-only counterparts in simulated interaction scenarios.

This work is motivated by the hypothesis that a synergistic approach, combining the precision of active control with the inherent shock absorption and energy dissipation of physical passive elements, can overcome the brittleness of purely active systems and improve overall interaction performance in real-world, unpredictable settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a simulation-based approach in MATLAB/Simulink to evaluate the proposed robot-environment interaction architecture.

#### 3.1. Robot Dynamics and Model

A 2-DOF planar manipulator was selected as the benchmark model, capturing essential coupling between joint-space dynamics, task-space motion, and normal/tangential contact interaction while maintaining analytical simplicity. The system dynamics were derived using the Lagrange method, incorporating terms for inertia, Coriolis/centrifugal forces, gravity, friction, external disturbance torques, and mapped external contact forces. Task-space dynamics were derived via the Jacobian matrix (or its Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse for non-square cases), assuming operation away from kinematic singularities.

#### 3.2. End-Effector Configurations

Three distinct end-effector configurations were analyzed:
1. **Rigid End-Effector (S1):** A baseline configuration with no passive compliant elements. The robot's end-effector directly interacts with the environment.
2. **Spring-Only Passive Compliant DOF (S2):** An end-effector augmented with a linear elastic spring element. This element is assumed to be translational and mounted along the normal contact direction between the robot wrist and the tool.
3. **Spring-Damper Passive Compliant DOF (S3):** An end-effector augmented with both a linear elastic spring and a linear viscous damper element, also translational and mounted along the normal contact direction.
For the passive elements, their mass and internal inertia were neglected, and their operation was assumed to remain within a linear range. The dynamic equations for each configuration were formulated to include the additional force terms from the spring (K*e) and the damper (B*ė), where 'e' is the end-effector deformation.

#### 3.3. Environment Model

To assess robustness, the environment model evolved through several stages:
- **Rigid Wall:** A baseline representing infinite stiffness.
- **Elastic Environment:** An extension of the rigid wall with linear elastic behavior, modeled by an environmental stiffness coefficient `K_env`, such generating a force `F_env = K_env * (p_eff - p_env)`.
- **Viscoelastic Environment (Kelvin-Voigt):** This model incorporated both elastic and damping properties, with a damping coefficient `D_env`. The total environmental force was `F_env = K_env * (p_eff - p_env) + D_env * (p_dot_eff - p_dot_env)`. This model was used for the "fixed environment" scenario.
- **Time-Varying Environment:** For "variable environment" scenarios, a sinusoidal normal force disturbance was introduced at the contact interface, represented as `F_env = [A*sin(2*pi/T * t + phi) + B, 0]^T`. This aimed to approximate bounded periodic variations in contact loading.
The interaction was assumed to occur with a flat wall, meaning the constrained direction (for force control) was normal to the surface, and the unconstrained direction (for position control) was tangential.

#### 3.4. Control Architecture

The overall control system (depicted in Fig. 1) is a feedback-linearized hybrid position-force control scheme.
- **Feedback Linearization:** The core idea was to transform the nonlinear robot dynamics into a decoupled task-space acceleration model (`p_ddot = a`). This transformation assumed exact knowledge of the robot model, availability of joint measurements, maintained contact, and a nonsingular Jacobian. The joint torque `τ` was derived to achieve this linearization.
- **Hybrid Position-Force Control Framework:** This framework decomposed control into orthogonal directions in the task space.
 - **Position Control:** Applied along the unconstrained tangential direction (`p_y`). A linear controller `a_p = p_ddot_D_T + K_T_V * Delta_p_dot + K_T_P * Delta_p` was designed to drive the tangential position tracking error (`Delta_p`) asymptotically to zero.
 - **Force Control:** Applied along the constrained normal direction (`p_x`). Assuming a spring-like environment for derivation, the controller `a_N = 1/K_env * (f_ddot_N_D + K_N_V * Delta_f_dot + K_N_P * Delta_f)` was formulated to drive the normal force tracking error (`Delta_f`) asymptotically to zero.
 - **Orientation Control:** An orientation controller `a_O` was also designed to ensure asymptotic stability of orientation tracking error.
The stability arguments for these controllers were nominal, based on the assumption of exact feedback linearization and ideal conditions.

#### 3.5. Numerical Simulation and Parameters

- **Software:** MATLAB/Simulink.
- **Scenarios:** Simulations included surface-approaching maneuvers followed by trajectory tracking, comparing the three end-effector configurations under both "fixed" (viscoelastic) and "time-varying" (sinusoidal normal force disturbance) environmental conditions.
- **Robot Parameters:** Link lengths (0.4 m, 0.3 m) and masses (1.0 kg, 0.5 kg) represented a small-scale planar arm.
- **Control Gains:** Selected as diagonal positive definite matrices (e.g., `K_T_P = k_T_P * diag()`) and empirically adjusted to achieve stable tracking without excessive oscillation or unrealistically large actuator torques (values detailed in Appendix Table II). Different normal-force gains were used for fixed and variable environments.
- **Environment Parameters:** For the fixed environment, `K_env = 10^6 N/m` and `D_env = 10^2 Ns/m`. For the time-varying environment, sinusoidal parameters were `A=10 N`, `B=50 N`, `T=22 s`.
- **End-Effector Passive Parameters:** `K_spring` ranged from `10^3 N/m` to `10^4 N/m`, and `D_damper` from `75 Ns/m` to `10^3 Ns/m` for fixed and dynamic cases, respectively.
- **Trajectories:** A jerk-limited seven-segment trajectory was used for smooth motion. The desired force input was a constant `F =^T` (10 N normal, 0 N tangential), and the position trajectory was `p = [0.5, 7-Segment]^T` (fixed normal position, tangential motion).

#### 3.6. Evaluation Metrics

The performance of each configuration was assessed by comparing desired and actual end-effector trajectories, positions, velocities, forces, and joint torques. Key metrics included maximum/minimum values, mean, and standard deviation of position, velocity, and force errors, as well as joint torque efforts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation-based evaluation consistently demonstrated that the passive compliant end-effector, particularly the spring-damper configuration, enhanced robustness in robot-environment interactions under both fixed and time-varying conditions, relative to the rigid and spring-only configurations.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Improvements (Spring-Damper vs. Rigid End-Effector)

The spring-damper configuration consistently provided:
- Stronger attenuation of contact-induced oscillations.
- Lower variance in force and velocity errors.
- Smoother joint-torque responses.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Results (Summarized in Table XIII)

**Fixed Environment Conditions:**
- **Normal Position-Error Standard Deviation:** Reduced by 8.0% (from 0.0301 m to 0.0277 m).
- **Normal Velocity-Error Standard Deviation:** Reduced by 13.1% (from 0.4364 m/s to 0.3792 m/s).
- **Normal Force-Error Standard Deviation:** Reduced by 9.8% (from 5.9508 N to 5.3665 N).
- **Tangential Force-Error Standard Deviation:** Significantly reduced by 36.5% (from 27.7483 N to 17.6107 N).

**Variable Environment Conditions (Sinusoidal Normal Force Disturbance):**
- **Normal Position-Error Standard Deviation:** Reduced by 19.3% (from 0.0311 m to 0.0251 m).
- **Normal Velocity-Error Standard Deviation:** Substantially reduced by 41.1% (from 0.1855 m/s to 0.1093 m/s).
- **Normal Force-Error Standard Deviation:** Reduced by 25.4% (from 17.8709 N to 13.3300 N).
- **Tangential Force-Error Standard Deviation:** Increased by 11.2% (from 4.9703 N to 5.5272 N). This indicates that improvements are not uniform across all metrics and directions.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Observations from Figures (3-18)

- **Rigid End-Effector (S1):** Exhibited significant deviations between desired and actual trajectories, especially in the X-Y plane (Fig. 3a, 11a). It showed large overshoots and prolonged oscillations in position (Fig. 4a, 12a), velocity (Fig. 6a, 14a), and force tracking (Fig. 8a, 16a). High peaks and broad spreads were observed in error distributions (Fig. 5a, 7a, 9a, 13a, 15a, 17a), and joint torques displayed high magnitudes and fluctuations (Fig. 10a, 18a), reflecting undamped responses to environmental interactions.
- **Spring-Only End-Effector (S2):** Showed moderated peaks and reduced deviations compared to the rigid case, indicating the benefit of elastic energy storage. However, persistent oscillations (ringing) were still present in position, velocity, and force responses due to the lack of energy dissipation (e.g., Fig. 4b, 6b, 8b). While better than rigid, it did not fully stabilize the interaction.
- **Spring-Damper End-Effector (S3):** Consistently achieved the tightest agreement between desired and actual trajectories (Fig. 3c, 11c). It demonstrated rapid convergence to steady-state (e.g., within 1 second for position in fixed environment, Fig. 4c), minimal oscillations, and significantly reduced error amplitudes across position, velocity, and force metrics (e.g., Fig. 5c, 7c, 9c, 13c, 15c, 17c). The joint torque efforts were notably smoother and had lower peak values and standard deviations (Fig. 10c, 18c). The presence of damping effectively dissipated kinetic energy, preventing rebounds and promoting stability.

#### 4.4. Interpretation

The findings indicate that physical damping plays a crucial role in suppressing rebound oscillations and reducing variance-based tracking errors during contact. The spring-damper configuration leverages both elastic energy storage (from the spring) and viscous energy dissipation (from the damper), leading to superior transient behavior and overall performance compared to either the rigid or spring-only interfaces in the simulated interaction scenarios. While improvements were observed across many metrics, the degree of improvement varied depending on the specific metric, direction, and environment conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robot-environment interaction by demonstrating the benefits of integrating physical passive compliant elements with active feedback-linearized hybrid position-force control. The findings underscore a complementary relationship where physical hardware addresses challenges that purely algorithmic solutions may struggle with, particularly in dynamic and uncertain contact scenarios.

#### 5.1. Enhanced Robustness and Stability

The primary significance lies in the observed enhancement of interaction robustness. By embedding a passive spring-damper at the end-effector, the robot can mechanically store and dissipate impact energy at the contact point. This mechanism mitigates high-frequency shocks and vibrations *before* they fully propagate to the active control loop and actuated joints. This leads to reduced contact-induced oscillations, lower variance in position, velocity, and force errors, and more stable overall interaction dynamics. Such inherent mechanical compliance offers a robust first line of defense against unmodeled disturbances and uncertainties in environmental properties.

#### 5.2. Improved Safety and Durability

The reduction in contact-induced oscillations and the smoothing of joint-torque responses have direct implications for robot safety and durability. Lower peak torques and smoother torque profiles can reduce mechanical stress on actuators and gearboxes, potentially extending the robot's lifespan and reducing maintenance requirements. In applications involving physical human-robot interaction or delicate objects, mitigating impact forces through passive compliance can significantly enhance safety by preventing sudden, high-force interactions.

#### 5.3. Complementing Active Control

This work highlights that physical passive compliance is not a replacement for, but a powerful complement to, advanced active control strategies. While feedback linearization provides precise control under nominal conditions, the passive interface offers an additional physical energy-management layer, making the overall system more resilient to unexpected contact transients, environmental stiffness variations, and rapid changes in contact conditions. This hybrid physical-algorithmic framework could be particularly beneficial for robots operating near actuator saturation limits, as smoother torque demands may reduce the likelihood of exceeding these limits.

#### 5.4. Application Relevance

The proposed architecture has potential impact across a range of contact-rich robotic applications:
- **Assembly and Manufacturing:** Reducing impact shocks during part insertion or surface contact can improve precision, reduce wear on tools, and prevent damage to components.
- **Polishing and Surface Finishing:** Achieving smoother force regulation can lead to more consistent surface quality and reduce tool chatter.
- **Rehabilitation and Surgery:** Enhancing the compliance and robustness of robots interacting with human bodies can improve patient safety and comfort, particularly where tissues have variable stiffness or movements are unpredictable.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** Facilitating safer and more stable physical interactions between humans and robots in shared workspaces.

#### 5.5. Limitations and Future Directions

Despite its significance, the study acknowledges several limitations:
- **Simulation-Only:** The results are derived from MATLAB/Simulink simulations, lacking experimental validation on physical hardware.
- **Representative Parameters:** Environmental and passive element parameters were chosen as representative values rather than being experimentally identified for specific materials or robots.
- **Simplified Model:** The analysis used a 2-DOF planar manipulator interacting with a known flat surface, simplifying contact geometry and eliminating complexities like multi-point contact or rapidly changing contact normals.
- **Idealized System:** Factors such as actuator saturation, torque limits, sampling delays, force-sensor noise, backlash, and unmodeled friction were not explicitly included.
- **Parameter Optimization:** The passive spring-damper parameters were not systematically optimized for different robot scales, payloads, or contact materials.

These limitations point towards important future work, including experimental implementation and real-time testing, broader sensitivity analysis across various environmental stiffness and damping ranges, optimization of passive element parameters, extension to higher-DOF manipulators, online estimation of contact frames for unknown surfaces, and integration of practical industrial constraints into the control design. Verifying the claimed application-level benefits experimentally remains a crucial step for practical deployment.
