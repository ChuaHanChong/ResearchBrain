# Learning Agile Striker Skills for Humanoid Soccer Robots from Noisy Sensory Input

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Texas at Austin developed a four-stage reinforcement learning pipeline enabling a Booster T1 humanoid robot to acquire agile, robust, and continuous ball-kicking skills despite noisy sensory input. The method demonstrates a 79.5% success rate in simulation and 66.7% on the physical robot, achieving zero-shot transfer and improving energy efficiency.

## Problem

- Developing whole-body tasks for humanoid robots that integrate speed, dynamic balance, and effective perception under real-world noise and disturbances.
- Learning robust ball-kicking skills for humanoid soccer, which requires rapid limb movements, single-foot balance, and accurate perception, often challenging traditional control methods and existing RL approaches.
- Overcoming the sim-to-real gap for complex, dynamic humanoid skills and mitigating undesirable robot behaviors that can emerge during reinforcement learning training.

## Method

- A four-stage reinforcement learning (RL) pipeline including teacher policies trained with ground-truth information for long-distance chasing and directional kicking.
- Distillation of teacher skills into a student policy operating on realistic, noisy perception (velocity-dependent noise, asynchronous updates, occlusions) using ==Dataset Aggregation (DAgger)==.
- Online student adaptation and refinement using constrained reinforcement learning (N-P3O) to improve robustness to partial observability and mitigate undesirable movement patterns by controlling regularization costs.

## Results

- The learned policy achieved a **79.5%** success rate and **0.956** kick accuracy in simulation, and a **66.7%** success rate with high accuracy on the physical Booster T1 robot for varied ball positions.
- Constrained RL (N-P3O) policies reduced average energy cost by over **50%** (**108.6** J/s compared to **255.8** J/s for standard PPO) and maximum angular velocities by approximately **33%**, leading to smoother, more stable motions.
- The online student adaptation stage significantly improved performance from a **52.3%** success rate and **256.2** J/s energy cost to **79.5%** success and **110.8** J/s, demonstrating its criticality for handling partial observability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Realistic modeling of perception noise, including velocity-dependent magnitude, asynchronous updates, and occlusions, is crucial for successful sim-to-real transfer of visuomotor whole-body control for humanoids.
> - Constrained reinforcement learning (N-P3O) effectively refines learned policies, reducing energy consumption and mitigating unsafe or jittery movements by balancing task rewards with regularization costs, without sacrificing overall performance.
> - A multi-stage teacher-student training framework, combined with online adaptation, enables humanoids to learn complex, dynamic skills that generalize to imperfect real-world sensory inputs and maintain high performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of robotic whole-body control, particularly focusing on humanoid robots and their application in complex, dynamic tasks such as robot soccer. Humanoid robots, by virtue of their anthropomorphic morphology, are intended to operate in human-centric environments, requiring capabilities like dynamic balance, whole-body coordination, and interaction with their surroundings. Recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) and simulation have facilitated progress in areas such as robust locomotion and object manipulation for humanoids.

Despite these advancements, the paper identifies a persistent challenge in developing whole-body tasks that integrate speed, balance, and perception effectively. Such tasks demand rapid limb movements, stable postures on a reduced support polygon (e.g., a single foot), and resilience to perceptual noise and external disturbances. The skill of ball-kicking in humanoid soccer serves as a representative example of such a challenging benchmark, requiring the robot to approach a ball, execute a powerful leg swing while maintaining single-foot balance, and accurately direct the ball towards a goal, all under conditions of potential sensor noise, delays, and perception errors.

Robot soccer itself has historically served as a benchmark for integrated systems involving perception, control, and decision-making. While quadrupedal robots have demonstrated advanced soccer behaviors—including cooperative play, precise shooting, dribbling, and goalkeeping—benefiting from their inherent stability, humanoid soccer remains comparatively less explored and presents greater difficulties due to bipedal balance requirements, complex whole-body coordination, and increased perceptual uncertainty. Previous humanoid soccer research has largely concentrated on dribbling or general locomotion skills, leaving agile striking under conditions of noisy perception largely unaddressed. This research aims to fill that gap by developing a framework for robust and adaptive ball-kicking skills, which are considered critical for advancing humanoid soccer capabilities.

Furthermore, this work contributes to the broader literature on humanoid whole-body control. Traditional approaches often rely on model predictive control or optimization, which, while offering stability guarantees, can exhibit fragility when confronted with perception noise, latency, or unmodeled dynamics. Reinforcement learning has emerged as an alternative, enabling humanoids to learn complex behaviors directly from interaction. This paper extends RL-based whole-body control to the specific and demanding soccer striker task, integrating robust perception within a staged RL framework to bridge theoretical control research with the practical demands of humanoid soccer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an RL-based training pipeline that enables humanoid robots to acquire and execute robust, continual ball-kicking skills, demonstrating adaptability across various ball-goal configurations. This overarching goal is motivated by several critical challenges inherent in the task of humanoid ball-kicking:

1. **Complexity of Whole-Body Control:** Ball-kicking requires a combination of rapid leg movements, precise timing, and dynamic balance on a single support foot, which collectively present a significant whole-body control challenge for anthropomorphic robots. The coordination of numerous degrees of freedom while maintaining stability is non-trivial.
2. **Perceptual Uncertainty:** The real-world environment introduces inherent noise, delays, and potential errors in sensory input, particularly for vision-based localization of the ball and goal. An effective ball-kicking policy must be robust to these imperfect perceptions to ensure reliable performance.
3. **Adaptability to Dynamic Environments:** A robot must be able to perform consistently across diverse initial ball and goal positions, and under external perturbations, such as those caused by opponents or uneven terrain. The policy needs to generalize beyond specific pre-programmed scenarios.
4. **Sim-to-Real Gap:** Policies trained in simulation often struggle with deployment on physical robots due to discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics, sensor characteristics, and actuator responses. Overcoming this gap through realistic modeling and robust training strategies is a key motivation.
5. **Addressing Undesirable Behaviors:** During RL training, policies can sometimes develop behaviors that are effective for reward maximization but undesirable for real-world deployment (e.g., jittery movements, sudden turns) due to issues like inhomogeneous credit assignment. The objective includes refining policies to produce smoother, safer, and more efficient motions.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives guiding the development of the training pipeline include:
- Enabling a "teacher" policy with privileged ground-truth information to learn robust ball-chasing and directional kicking skills.
- Successfully distilling these skills into a "student" policy that operates solely on noisy and imperfect sensory inputs.
- Developing and integrating a realistic perception noise model that accounts for velocity-dependent noise, asynchronous updates, and perceptual occlusions.
- Implementing an online adaptation and refinement stage using constrained reinforcement learning to enhance robustness to partial observability and mitigate undesirable movement patterns.
- Demonstrating the transferability and performance of the learned skills on a physical humanoid robot without further fine-tuning in the real world (zero-shot deployment).

By addressing these challenges, the research aims to establish humanoid striking as a benchmark task for visuomotor whole-body control, pushing the boundaries of what humanoid robots can achieve in dynamic, perception-dependent environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents a four-stage reinforcement learning (RL)–based training pipeline designed to enable humanoid robots to perform robust continual ball-kicking. The system learns a control policy that maps a history of observations to joint position targets at 50 Hz, facilitating an iterative cycle of approaching, kicking, and reorienting.

**A. System Overview and Training Stages:**
The pipeline consists of four sequential training stages, followed by a real-world deployment phase:

1. **Long-Distance Chasing (Teacher Policy Training):**
 - **Objective:** Train a "teacher" policy to approach the ball from varied initial positions using ground-truth ball location.
 - **Teacher Policy Input:** Proprioceptive observations, a velocity command, gait cycle information, and privileged observations (ground-truth ball/goal positions, ball velocity, physical parameters like mass/friction).
 - **Environment Design:** Robot initialized in an annular region (0.5m to 4.0m) around the ball. Commanded linear velocity aligns with the robot-ball vector; angular velocity directs the robot to turn towards the ball.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Applied extensively to parameters like joint stiffness, damping, friction, body masses, center of mass, and external perturbations (forces, pushes), as well as ball mass and friction, to enhance robustness.
 - **Reward Design:** Primarily focuses on linear and angular velocity tracking errors relative to the ball, adapted from standard walking-gait designs, alongside various regularization terms for stability and safety.

2. **Directional Kicking (Teacher Policy Training):**
 - **Objective:** Extend the teacher policy to execute precise and robust kicks towards a goal, still using privileged information.
 - **Environment Design:** Simulates a RoboCup Adult Size soccer field. Ball initialized in the front half of the field; robot positioned within an annular region (0.5m to 2.0m) around the ball.
 - **Continual Kicking:** If the ball leaves the field, it is reset to a new position around its last location without terminating the episode, encouraging continuous kicking cycles.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Includes external disturbances to the ball (random linear velocity every four seconds) to induce imperfect states and train recovery behaviors.
 - **Reward Design:** Adds a "ball velocity reward" that encourages high ball velocity projected towards the goal (capped at 6.0) and a "head tracking ball reward" (+1 if the ball is in the camera's field of view).

3. **Teacher Policy Distillation (Student Policy Training):**
 - **Objective:** Transfer the learned skills from the privileged teacher into a "student" policy that operates under imperfect perception.
 - **Student Policy Input:** A history of 50 observations, comprising proprioceptive measurements and noisy estimates of ball and goal positions.
 - **Network Architecture:** A 1D convolutional encoder processes the observation history, producing a latent representation concatenated with the most recent observation, which then feeds into a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to output actions.
 - **Noise Modeling:** Crucially, realistic perception uncertainty is simulated:
 - **Velocity-Dependent Noise:** Gaussian noise is added to ball/goal positions, with its magnitude scaling with the object's velocity in the robot's head frame.
 - **Delays and Dropouts:** Proprioceptive data is at 50 Hz, but ball positions are updated at approximately 10 Hz with random offsets (asynchronous updates). If the ball is outside the camera's field of view or between update intervals, its position is masked with zeros. Goal observations are updated at 50 Hz with noise.
 - **Distillation Algorithm:** Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) is employed to iteratively collect rollouts from the student policy and query the teacher for corrective actions, allowing the student to learn from its own error-induced states.

4. **Student Adaptation and Refinement (Student Policy Fine-Tuning):**
 - **Objective:** Refine the distilled student policy through online RL to adapt to partially observed states caused by imperfect perception and mitigate undesirable movements.
 - **Adaptation:** The student policy undergoes further online RL training under noisy perception, but with reduced physical perturbations to allow focused improvement on accuracy and robustness to sensory uncertainty.
 - **Refinement with Constrained RL:** To address issues like sharp rotations or overly dynamic kicks arising from inhomogeneous credit assignment (where task rewards peak disproportionately at certain moments, leading to excessive regularization penalties), the problem is reformulated as a constrained RL problem. The goal is to maximize aggregated task rewards while ensuring aggregated regularization costs remain below a specified threshold ($h$).
 - **Algorithm:** N-P3O, a constrained policy gradient method, is used to solve this optimization, aiming for smoother, safer, and more stable kicking motions rather than strict hardware safety.

**B. Real-World Deployment Pipeline:**
- **Hardware:** The policy is deployed on a Booster T1 humanoid robot, which is 1.18 meters tall, features 23 degrees of freedom, an IMU, joint encoders, and a ZED 2i Stereo Camera, powered by an NVIDIA AGX Orin GPU and a 14-core CPU. The policy runs at 50 Hz.
- **Perception:**
 - **Ball:** A YOLOv8 model detects the ball's bounding box from the RGB-D camera stream. The bounding box center is unprojected to a 3D point using depth, then transformed to the robot's base frame via forward kinematics.
 - **Goal:** Goal localization relies on a lightweight, data-driven legged-inertial odometry module (inspired by Legolas), which fuses inertial signals (gravity, angular velocity) with leg kinematics to predict relative pose changes in SE(2) space. This enables initial goal positioning and tracking.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed training pipeline was evaluated through extensive experimentation in both simulation and on a physical humanoid robot.

**A. Simulation Results:**
- **Evaluation Setting:** Simulations were conducted using a 9x9 grid of initial ball positions (spanning [−1.5, 6.5] × [−4.0, 4.0] meters at 1-meter intervals) relative to the goal. For each position, 50 independent trials were run with randomized robot initial states.
- **Overall Performance:** The policy achieved an average success rate of 79.5%, defined as the ball crossing the goal line between the posts. The average kick accuracy, measured as the cosine similarity between the post-kick ball velocity vector and the ground-truth ball–goal direction, was 0.956.
- **Ball Velocity and Energy Cost:** The policy resulted in an overall maximum ball velocity of 4.13 m/s. The average energy cost, computed during the periods when the robot was within 0.5 meters of the ball, was 110.8 J/s.
- **Adaptive Behavior:** An emergent energy-saving strategy was observed, where the robot consistently applied lighter kicks and consumed less energy when the ball was positioned closer to the goal, indicating adaptive behavior to balance efficiency with task success.

**B. Real-World Results:**
- **Evaluation Setting:** A smaller-scale evaluation was performed on a physical Booster T1 humanoid robot using five distinct ball positions on a RoboCup Kid-Size soccer field. Three trials were conducted for each position, with the robot starting from a fixed location 6.5 meters in front of the goal.
- **Overall Performance:** The policy demonstrated an overall success rate of 66.7% across the evaluated configurations. Kick accuracies were generally high, with examples such as 0.99 ± 0.01 for certain ball positions.

**C. Ablation Studies:**
Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of key design components:

- **Constrained RL:**
 - Comparison between N-P3O (constrained RL) and standard PPO with fixed regularization coefficients (1.0 and 1.5) demonstrated the benefits of the constrained approach.
 - N-P3O achieved the highest success rate (79.5%). While PPO-1.0 and PPO-1.5 yielded higher maximum ball velocities (1.41 m/s and 1.21 m/s, respectively, compared to N-P3O's 1.05 m/s), they incurred significantly higher energy costs (255.8 J/s and 185.7 J/s versus 108.6 J/s for N-P3O) and larger maximum angular velocities (3.21 rad/s and 2.86 rad/s versus 2.13 rad/s).
 - This indicates that N-P3O effectively maintains smooth and stable motions, reducing energy consumption and mitigating unsafe behaviors, despite a slight reduction in peak ball velocity.

- **Student Adaptation:**
 - The importance of the final online student adaptation stage was highlighted by comparing policies before and after this stage.
 - The student policy after adaptation achieved a 79.5% success rate and 0.956 kick accuracy with an energy cost of 110.8 J/s.
 - In contrast, the student policy before adaptation only attained a 52.3% success rate and 0.807 accuracy, while consuming more than twice the energy (256.2 J/s).
 - For reference, a privileged teacher policy trained without sensor noise achieved 81.1% success and 0.954 accuracy with an energy cost of 240.1 J/s.
 - These results demonstrate that online adaptation is critical for handling partially observed states arising from imperfect perception, significantly improving both performance and efficiency, and bringing the student's capabilities closer to that of a privileged teacher.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of humanoid robotics by addressing the complex problem of learning agile and robust ball-kicking skills under realistic conditions of noisy sensory input. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Advancement in Humanoid Soccer:** The work directly addresses a critical capability gap in humanoid soccer: the ability to perform agile and precise striking while operating under imperfect perception. By enabling robust continual ball-kicking, it provides a foundational skill that is essential for advanced gameplay, moving beyond simpler tasks like dribbling or generic locomotion.
2. **Benchmark for Visuomotor Whole-Body Control:** The paper effectively establishes the task of humanoid striking as a challenging benchmark for visuomotor skill learning in humanoid whole-body control. This task inherently combines demands for dynamic balance, rapid and coordinated limb movements, and accurate perception, making it an ideal platform for evaluating integrated control and perception systems.
3. **Methodological Contributions to Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed four-stage teacher-student training pipeline offers a structured approach to transferring complex, dynamic skills from simulation to real-world humanoid robots.
 - The careful **realistic noise modeling** in the distillation stage (velocity-dependent noise, asynchronous updates, occlusions) is critical for bridging the sim-to-real gap, ensuring that the student policy is prepared for the uncertainties of physical deployment.
 - The inclusion of **online constrained reinforcement learning (N-P3O)** for student adaptation and refinement is a significant innovation. It demonstrates a method not just for strict safety enforcement, but for mitigating undesirable behaviors arising from inhomogeneous credit assignment, leading to smoother, safer, and more energy-efficient motions without compromising task performance. This approach has broader implications for refining policies for other complex, dynamic tasks.
4. **Robustness to Imperfect Perception:** The extensive evaluation in both simulation and on hardware validates the system's ability to maintain high kicking accuracy and goal-scoring success across diverse ball-goal configurations despite noisy perceptual inputs. This robustness is essential for real-world robot deployment where perfect sensing is rarely available.
5. **Practical Deployment and Zero-Shot Transfer:** The successful deployment of the learned policy on a physical Booster T1 humanoid robot without any real-world fine-tuning (zero-shot transfer) underscores the practical utility and effectiveness of the training methodology. This achievement is a strong indicator of the pipeline's ability to generate policies that are truly ready for deployment.

A recognized limitation, stated by the authors, is that the long-distance chasing gaits rely on heavily engineered reward functions adapted from prior walking gait training, and the transferability of this approach to ball-kicking is dependent on the similarity of motions. This suggests that the current method may not generalize broadly to tasks requiring significantly different movement patterns. This limitation motivates future research into more scalable strategies, such as learning from diverse human motions, which could further broaden the applicability and autonomy of humanoid robots in complex, human-like tasks.

In summary, this research provides a comprehensive and validated framework for a challenging visuomotor skill in humanoid robots, significantly advancing the state of the art in robot soccer and whole-body control, with methodological insights that can impact the broader field of robot learning and sim-to-real transfer.
