# Vidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Vidar, an embodied video diffusion model, enables data-efficient generalist bimanual robotic manipulation by leveraging a transferable video prior from large-scale internet and embodied video data, coupled with a masked inverse dynamics model for adaptation to new robot embodiments. The system achieves high success rates with as little as 20 minutes of real-world demonstrations, outperforming leading baselines by significant margins in both seen and unseen tasks and environments.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for generalist robotics require massive, hardware-specific datasets (hundreds of thousands to millions of trajectories), leading to significant data scarcity and collection bottlenecks.
- Policies learned on one robot embodiment often struggle to generalize to new platforms with different morphologies, camera viewpoints, or bimanual complexities, hindering broad applicability.
- Robotic systems frequently suffer from performance degradation due to visual distractors and background clutter in real-world, unstructured environments.

## Method

- A two-stage architecture factorizes the policy into an embodied video diffusion model (G) and a masked inverse dynamics model (MIDM) (I), allowing for a 'one prior, many embodiments' approach.
- The video generation model (G) is pre-trained on internet-scale videos, then fine-tuned on large-scale embodied robotic data in a unified observation space, and finally on minimal target-domain demonstrations, enhanced by a test-time scaling strategy.
- A Masked Inverse Dynamics Model (MIDM) predicts robot-specific actions by learning to spatially mask input frames, focusing exclusively on action-relevant regions without explicit segmentation supervision.

## Results

- Vidar achieved 68.2% success on seen real-world tasks with ~20 minutes of data, substantially outperforming UniPi (36.4%) and VPP (4.5%), and demonstrated robust generalization with 66.7% success on unseen tasks and 55.6% on unseen backgrounds.
- The embodied pre-training on the unified observation space significantly improved generated video quality for the target domain, increasing subject consistency from 0.565 to 0.855 and imaging quality from 0.345 to 0.667.
- The Masked Inverse Dynamics Model (MIDM) improved action prediction generalization accuracy from 24.3% (ResNet baseline) to 49.0% on test data, while successfully localizing action-relevant regions in visually complex scenes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large-scale video diffusion models can serve as effective, transferable priors for robot control, significantly reducing the need for extensive robot-specific action data by learning general interaction dynamics.
> - Decoupling video generation from inverse dynamics, combined with a unified observation space, enables robust generalization across diverse robot embodiments and camera setups with minimal fine-tuning data.
> - An implicitly learned spatial masking mechanism in the inverse dynamics model improves action prediction robustness by selectively attending to salient regions, effectively mitigating distractors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is rapidly advancing, with a significant focus on developing "generalist" robots capable of performing diverse tasks in varied environments. A major paradigm in this pursuit is the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as RT-1, RT-2, OpenVLA, and Octo, which integrate perception, language understanding, and action generation. While these models have shown encouraging progress, they typically rely on massive, often hardware-specific, datasets containing hundreds of thousands to millions of robot trajectories. This data scarcity problem, especially for new or specialized robot embodiments (like bimanual systems), presents a significant bottleneck.

Vidar addresses this fundamental challenge by proposing a "scalable recipe for 'one prior, many embodiments'," positioning itself as a data-efficient alternative to purely data-intensive VLA pipelines. Instead of directly learning pixel-to-action policies from vast quantities of robot data, Vidar leverages the inherent richness and abundance of *video data*. This aligns with a growing trend in embodied AI to utilize video generative models, often pre-trained on Internet-scale video corpora, as a foundation for understanding physical dynamics and interaction cues. Previous works like UniPi, RoboDreamer, and VPP have also explored video generative models for robot control, but often remain limited to single-arm robots, fixed camera views, or require complex end-to-end joint training of video generation and action prediction.

Vidar distinguishes itself by focusing on:
1. **Extreme Data Efficiency:** Aiming for performance with only minimal (e.g., 20 minutes) target-domain demonstrations, a stark contrast to existing VLA models requiring orders of magnitude more data.
2. **Generalizability to New Embodiments:** Explicitly tackling viewpoint and morphology shifts through a novel unified observation space and a decoupled video generation and action prediction architecture. This addresses a major limitation of policies tightly coupled to specific hardware.
3. **Bimanual Manipulation:** Acknowledging and tackling the increased control complexity and combinatorial action spaces associated with bimanual tasks, which is often a challenge for existing methods.
4. **Robustness to Distractors:** Introducing a masked inverse dynamics model (MIDM) that learns to selectively attend to action-relevant regions, thereby improving robustness to background clutter—a common issue in real-world deployments.

By factorizing the policy into a video generation model and an inverse dynamics model, Vidar draws inspiration from "world models" while applying state-of-the-art video diffusion techniques. It builds upon advances in large-scale video generative models (e.g., Sora, Wan, Vidu, HunyuanVideo) to create a transferable prior, then efficiently aligns this prior to new robotic platforms. This approach pushes the boundary of generalist robotics towards practical deployment by significantly lowering the data collection burden for new hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of Vidar is to enable **general-purpose and bimanual robotic manipulation** that can **scale efficiently to new robot embodiments with minimal domain-specific data**. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robotics:** Current state-of-the-art VLA models require enormous datasets (hundreds of thousands to millions of hours of demonstrations) to achieve robust performance. Collecting such data is labor-intensive, expensive, and tightly coupled to specific hardware. Vidar aims to overcome this "scalability bottleneck" by drastically reducing the need for platform-specific demonstrations.
2. **Bridging the Gap from Abundant Video to Robot Control:** Video, unlike robot action data, is abundant on the internet and intrinsically captures temporal dynamics, interaction cues, and physical consistency. The motivation is to effectively leverage this rich, inexpensive source of "embodiment-agnostic interaction knowledge" as a transferable prior for robot learning.
3. **Tackling Embodiment Heterogeneity:** Robots vary significantly in sensing capabilities, morphology, and camera viewpoints. Policies learned on one platform often struggle to transfer to others. Vidar seeks to create a framework that can generalize across different robot platforms and camera setups.
4. **Enabling Bimanual Manipulation:** Many complex real-world tasks require bimanual coordination, which dramatically increases the action space complexity and data requirements. Vidar is motivated to provide effective control for such intricate tasks.
5. **Robustness to Real-World Challenges:** Robotic systems operating in cluttered or dynamic environments often suffer from visual distractions, background shifts, and partial observability. A key motivation is to develop a method that can ground video priors into robust, actionable controls despite these challenges.

In essence, Vidar is motivated by the desire to unlock generalist robot capabilities by making robot learning more data-efficient, transferable, and robust, particularly for complex bimanual tasks and new hardware deployments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Vidar addresses its objectives through a novel two-stage architecture that decouples video generation from action prediction, consisting of an embodied video diffusion model (as the generalizable prior) and a masked inverse dynamics model (MIDM) as the embodiment adapter.

**Overall Problem Formulation:**
The policy $\pi$ is factorized as $\pi = I \circ G$, where $G$ is a video generation model that produces physically plausible future rollouts conditioned on observations and language instructions, and $I$ is an inverse dynamics model that maps short video windows into robot-specific actions.

#### 3.1. Video Generation Model ($G$)

- **Base Architecture:** Vidar adopts rectified flow models (a type of diffusion model) which are known for generating high-quality videos by modeling pixel flow over time. These models learn a velocity function to approximate the constant flow between a noisy video and a target video, minimizing a flow matching loss.
- **Unified Observation Space:** To handle heterogeneous data from multiple robot embodiments, a crucial innovation is the "unified observation space." This space aggregates multi-view RGB images (spatially resized for consistency) and concatenates textual instructions related to the robotic platform ($l_r$), camera setup ($l_c$), and task ($l_t$). This ensures the video diffusion model learns a representation that is consistent across different robot morphologies and camera configurations, yet rich enough to capture essential context. Importantly, this space *does not* include actions, allowing the model to learn world evolution generalizable across robots.
- **Three-Stage Training Pipeline:**
 1. **Internet-Scale General Pre-training:** Utilizes off-the-shelf video diffusion models (e.g., Wan2.2, Vidu 2.0) pre-trained on vast internet video corpora. This provides a strong semantic and temporal prior.
 2. **Embodied Domain Pre-training:** The pre-trained video model is further continuously pre-trained using approximately 750K multi-view bimanual robotic episodes collected from three diverse real-world robot platforms (Agibot-World, RoboMind, RDT). All this data is projected into the unified observation space, promoting physically credible contacts and mitigating viewpoint/morphology gaps.
 3. **Target Domain Fine-tuning:** A small number of human demonstration episodes (as little as 20 minutes) collected from the *unseen* target robot platform (e.g., Aloha) are used for supervised fine-tuning. Data augmentation (clipping variable-length videos) maximizes the utility of this limited data.
- **Test-Time Scaling (TTS):** To enhance the quality, physical plausibility, and task relevance of generated rollouts, Vidar employs a test-time scaling strategy. Given an initial observation, K candidate video trajectories are generated with different random seeds. A pre-trained evaluator (e.g., GPT-4o) ranks these trajectories based on physical accuracy and alignment with the video caption, selecting the best one. This acts as a "rejection sampling" mechanism to improve inference quality without retraining.

#### 3.2. Masked Inverse Dynamics Model (MIDM) ($I$)

- **Motivation:** Standard inverse dynamics models often struggle with generalization due to background noise, visual distractors, and lack of explicit localization of action-relevant regions. Existing segmentation methods are also insufficient for complex bimanual tasks.
- **Architecture:** MIDM consists of two components:
 1. **Mask Prediction Network ($U$):** A U-Net structure that takes an input video frame ($x$) and outputs a spatial mask ($m \in^{H \times W}$).
 2. **Action Regression Network ($R$):** A ResNet structure that predicts the robot action ($\hat{a}$) from the *masked* input frame (Round($m$) $\odot x$, where $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication).
- **Training:** MIDM is trained by minimizing a loss function combining a Huber loss on the predicted action error ($l(\hat{a} - a)$) and an $\ell_1$-norm regularization term on the mask ($\lambda\|m\|_1$). The $\ell_1$-norm encourages spatial sparsity, implicitly pushing the model to focus on minimal, action-relevant regions (e.g., hands, tools, contact points) without requiring explicit pixel-level segmentation labels. It is trained exclusively on the limited fine-tuning dataset from the target platform.

Together, these components create a robust and data-efficient system: the video generation model provides a strong, transferable prior of physical interactions, while the MIDM effectively grounds these visual priors into precise, robot-specific actions by filtering out irrelevant visual information.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Vidar's experimental evaluation successfully verifies its hypotheses, demonstrating superior performance and generalization across various settings with remarkably low data requirements.

1. **Superior Success Rates (H1):**
 - **Simulation (RoboTwin 2.0 Benchmark):** Vidar achieves state-of-the-art average success rates in a challenging multi-task setting. In the low-data clean scenario, Vidar achieves 60.0% success (vs. 25.0% for Pi0.5), and in the standard-data clean scenario, it reaches 65.8% (vs. 44.8% for Pi0.5). Performance is also superior in randomized scenarios.
 - **Real-World Experiments:** With only ~20 minutes of human demonstrations (~3 episodes per task) on an unseen Aloha robotic platform, Vidar significantly outperforms leading baselines:
 - **Seen Tasks & Backgrounds:** 68.2% success rate, outperforming UniPi (36.4%) and VPP (4.5%).
 - **Unseen Tasks:** 66.7% success rate, outperforming UniPi (6.7%) and VPP (13.3%).
 - **Unseen Backgrounds:** 55.6% success rate, outperforming UniPi (22.2%) and VPP (0.0%).
 - **Additional Real-World Evaluation:** When reproduced with Wan2.2 and HunyuanVideo backbones, Vidar consistently surpassed Pi0.5, achieving 35% and 54% higher average success rates on seen and unseen tasks, respectively, demonstrating the backbone-agnostic nature of the approach.

2. **Strong Generalization Ability (H2):**
 - Quantitative results in Table 2 clearly show Vidar's robust generalization to unseen tasks and backgrounds in real-world scenarios, including environments with complex reflective surfaces.
 - In simulation, the model generalized effectively to randomized scenarios despite being trained only on clean ones.
 - Visualizations (Figure 2, 7, 8) illustrate Vidar's semantic understanding for complex instructions and its ability to operate in novel environments.

3. **Effectiveness of Embodied Pre-training on Unified Observation Space (H3):**
 - Using VBench metrics, the study confirms that pre-training the video generation model on large-scale robotic videos under the proposed unified observation space significantly enhances both subject consistency (0.855 vs. 0.565 for raw Vidu 2.0) and imaging quality (0.667 vs. 0.345) of generated videos in the unseen target domain. This improved video quality is critical for robot control tasks.

4. **Effectiveness of Masked Inverse Dynamics Model (MIDM) (H4):**
 - MIDM demonstrates superior generalization in action prediction. While both MIDM and a ResNet baseline achieved 99.9% training accuracy, MIDM achieved 49.0% testing accuracy with a lower $l_1$ error (0.0308) compared to the ResNet baseline's 24.3% testing accuracy and higher error (0.0430).
 - Visualizations (Figure 3) show that MIDM successfully learns to focus on action-relevant regions (e.g., robot arms) even in unseen backgrounds with complex reflective surfaces, without any explicit pixel-level supervision.

5. **Ablation Study:**
 - The ablation study confirms that both the Masked Inverse Dynamics Model (MIDM) and Test-Time Scaling (TTS) are beneficial for Vidar's performance. Removing TTS resulted in a drop in success rates (e.g., from 68.2% to 45.5% in seen tasks), and replacing MIDM with a ResNet baseline also significantly reduced performance (e.g., from 68.2% to 59.1% in seen tasks), highlighting the critical role of each component.

In summary, Vidar consistently achieves high success rates with unprecedented data efficiency for new robot embodiments, demonstrating strong generalization abilities to novel tasks and challenging real-world conditions. The unified observation space, embodied pre-training, test-time scaling, and the masked inverse dynamics model are all crucial contributors to this performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Vidar presents a highly significant advancement in the field of generalist robotic manipulation, with profound potential impacts:

1. **Democratization of Robot Learning:** By drastically reducing the data requirements for adapting to new robot embodiments (down to ~20 minutes of demonstrations), Vidar significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing and deploying advanced robotic systems. This democratizes access to sophisticated manipulation capabilities, making it feasible for researchers and practitioners with limited resources to experiment with and deploy robots for new tasks.
2. **Accelerated Deployment of Bimanual Robots:** The ability to achieve robust bimanual manipulation with minimal data unlocks the potential for faster deployment of dual-arm robots in complex real-world environments, from industrial assembly to healthcare and service robotics. Many human-centric tasks require bimanual coordination, and Vidar's approach makes these tasks more accessible for automation.
3. **New Paradigm for Generalist Robot Policy Learning:** Vidar's "one prior, many embodiments" recipe, which leverages strong, inexpensive video priors from Internet-scale data combined with minimal on-robot alignment, establishes a powerful new paradigm. This approach can serve as a blueprint for future generalist robot policies, emphasizing the strategic use of multimodal foundation models (video diffusion) as transferable knowledge.
4. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The unified observation space and the Masked Inverse Dynamics Model (MIDM) directly contribute to greater robustness against background clutter, viewpoint changes, and morphology shifts. This inherent generalization capacity means robots can operate reliably in dynamic, unstructured environments, extending their utility beyond controlled laboratory settings.
5. **Bridge between Generative Models and Embodied AI:** Vidar effectively demonstrates how advanced video generative models, typically used for content creation, can be repurposed as powerful "world models" for embodied AI. This interdisciplinary success highlights the potential for future synergy between large-scale generative AI and robotics.

**Ethical Considerations:** The authors responsibly acknowledge the ethical implications. As generalist robotic systems become more capable and ubiquitous, particularly in sensitive domains involving human-robot interaction, crucial considerations regarding **privacy, safety, and accountability** arise. Vidar's ability to accelerate deployment necessitates proactive measures to ensure these systems are developed and used responsibly, with clear guidelines for their operation and interaction with humans. The success of Vidar underscores the urgent need for ongoing discussion and policy development around the ethical deployment of advanced AI in robotics.
