# Auditing Instruction-Trajectory Mismatches in Multimodal Robot Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> MMPF is a training-free auditor that finds robot demonstrations whose language instruction contradicts the demonstrated trajectory. Each modality (head camera, wrist camera, proprioception) forms a task-label distribution from kNN neighborhood voting plus prototype similarity, and entropy-derived reliability weights fuse them in a product of experts. It tops detection ranking and correction accuracy on LIBERO and real-robot data, and improves downstream policy grounding.

## Problem

- Robot data curation targets failed rollouts, redundancy, or utility, and assumes instructions are correct, leaving behaviorally valid demos with semantically wrong labels undetected.
- Vision-language relabeling work synthesizes or enriches instructions rather than auditing already-labeled human datasets, so it discards existing annotation effort instead of verifying it.
- Label-error detectors like confident learning and multimodal neighbor methods ignore trajectories, multiple sensor streams, and modalities that go intermittently uninformative through occlusion or noise.

## Method

- **MMPF**'s unimodal evidence per modality: ==RBF-weighted kNN label voting== for local agreement, geometrically mixed with ==class-prototype distance softmax== for global manifold structure, controlled by a mixing weight.
- ==Reliability score== from normalized ==predictive entropy== of each unimodal posterior, gating modalities per episode; near-zero reliability also flags sensor-corrupted episodes independently of the predicted label.
- ==Reliability-weighted product-of-experts fusion== sums weighted log posteriors across modalities; argmax gives the corrected task label, and disagreement with the provided instruction marks a mismatch.

## Results

- On LIBERO the method leads ranking, reaching **99.0** AUROC and **98.0** AUPRC on the long-horizon suite under instance-dependent noise, with **100%** correction accuracy across all eight settings.
- Real-robot auditing hits **91.8** F1 with **97.9%** correction accuracy on the bottle-mug set and **94.3** F1 with **94.9%** correction on the table set, beating all baselines.
- Ablations drop F1 from **91.8** to **89.4** without reliability weighting and to **49.9** for prototype-only; downstream, correction-based curation reaches **90.0%** real-robot success versus **73.8%** unfiltered.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cross-modal disagreement is a usable label-error signal: local neighborhood voting and global prototype geometry are complementary, and either alone loses precision or recall.
> - Predictive entropy doubles as a per-episode sensor-health gate, so one confidence quantity handles both unreliable modalities and corrupted observations without extra machinery.
> - Relabeling beats deleting on small datasets, since discarding flagged demos strips valid low-level control coverage; clean-benchmark success can hide grounding gains that counterfactual evaluation exposes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is evolving towards training generalist policies on increasingly large and diverse datasets of robot demonstrations. This shift introduces challenges related to the quality and reliability of data labels. While previous efforts in robotics data curation have predominantly focused on execution-level issues, such as identifying failed rollouts, low-quality actions, or redundant segments, this work addresses a distinct and subtle type of data imperfection: Instruction–Trajectory Mismatches (ITMs).

ITMs occur when a demonstrated robot trajectory is behaviorally correct but is paired with a semantically incorrect natural-language instruction. Such mismatches can corrupt the language–behavior mapping learned by vision-language-action (VLA) models, despite appearing plausible in action or video space and often bypassing standard quality filters. This issue becomes more pronounced as datasets scale across various tasks, laboratories, and robot embodiments, making manual label verification impractical.

Robot demonstration data is inherently multimodal, incorporating information from multiple camera views (e.g., head and wrist cameras), proprioception, and potentially other sensors. Each modality offers partial evidence about the task, but can also be intermittently unreliable due to factors such as occlusion, poor viewpoint, sensor noise, or task-dependent ambiguity. Consequently, effective auditing of ITMs requires a multimodal approach that can aggregate evidence and account for varying reliability across different sensor streams.

Existing related work includes:
- **Robotics data curation** methods that estimate data utility or quality based on motion consistency, mutual information, or learned success classifiers. These methods typically assume that task instructions are correctly assigned and primarily target trajectory quality or relevance.
- **Task instruction labeling** approaches that leverage pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) or large language models (LLMs) to synthesize, relabel, or refine instructions for robot trajectories. These methods are valuable for generating new annotations or enriching existing ones but are less directly focused on the post-hoc auditing of semantic mismatches within an already labeled dataset.
- **General label error detection** techniques, which include classifier-confidence-based methods (e.g., Confident Learning), nearest-neighbor methods (e.g., Deep kNN), and multimodal label-error detection (e.g., LEMoN). While relevant, these methods are not specifically designed to address the unique characteristics of robot ITM auditing, such as the inherent multimodality, intermittently uninformative observations, and the need to reason over trajectories. This work positions itself by proposing a classifier-free framework that addresses these specific challenges without requiring training a target classifier or running VLM inference during the auditing process.

By addressing ITMs through a multimodal, reliability-weighted fusion approach, this research aims to enhance the integrity of large-scale robot demonstration datasets, thereby improving the robustness and grounding of instruction-conditioned robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work stems from the challenges associated with scaling robot learning datasets and the specific, yet often overlooked, problem of Instruction–Trajectory Mismatches (ITMs). As robot learning shifts towards generalist policies trained on vast, heterogeneous datasets, the quality of supervisory labels becomes a critical bottleneck. Manual verification is infeasible at scale, and even a low rate of systematic annotation errors can significantly impair the learning process of vision-language-action (VLA) models.

While much of the existing data curation research in robotics focuses on execution-level failures (e.g., failed rollouts or poor-quality actions), ITMs represent a distinct and insidious failure mode. An ITM involves a physically correct or plausible robot trajectory that is incorrectly paired with a natural language instruction (e.g., "pick up bottle" when the robot is actually placing it, or "open oven" when it's closing it). These mismatches are subtle because the observed behavior often appears coherent, allowing them to pass standard quality filters that check for execution success or trajectory quality. However, such errors fundamentally corrupt the semantic mapping between language and behavior that VLA policies aim to learn.

Another motivation is the inherent multimodality of robot demonstration data. Robot episodes typically include observations from various sensors, such as head cameras, wrist cameras, and proprioception. Each modality provides unique, albeit partial, evidence about the task. Critically, any single modality can become unreliable at times due to real-world factors like occlusion, poor sensor viewpoint, sensor corruption, or task-dependent visual ambiguity. For instance, a head camera might miss a crucial object, a wrist camera could be occluded by the robot's arm, or proprioception might not sufficiently differentiate between visually similar tasks. An effective auditing framework must therefore be able to aggregate evidence across these modalities while robustly handling their intermittent unreliability.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this research are:

1. **To formalize ITM auditing:** Establish a clear problem definition for detecting and correcting Instruction–Trajectory Mismatches in multimodal robot demonstration datasets.
2. **To introduce Multimodal Probabilistic Fusion (MMPF):** Propose a novel, classifier-free auditing framework that leverages embedding geometry to estimate task-label posteriors and fuses multimodal evidence using a reliability-weighted product-of-experts approach. This framework is designed to operate post-hoc on already-labeled datasets without requiring clean-label supervision or training a new task classifier.
3. **To evaluate ITM detection and correction capabilities:** Empirically assess MMPF's ability to identify and relabel instruction–trajectory mismatches more accurately than existing noisy-label baselines across diverse benchmarks, including both simulated (LIBERO) and real-robot datasets.
4. **To demonstrate robustness to modality corruption:** Investigate whether the reliability-weighted multimodal fusion mechanism in MMPF maintains effectiveness even when individual sensor modalities are degraded or provide ambiguous evidence.
5. **To analyze impact on downstream policy learning:** Determine how improved instruction-label quality, achieved through auditing, translates into better policy behavior, particularly in settings where language is essential for disambiguating tasks and ensuring robust grounding rather than relying solely on visual context.
6. **To demonstrate real-robot policy improvement:** Show practical benefits in real-robot experiments, evaluating the trade-off between filtering mismatched demonstrations versus relabeling them.

These objectives aim to provide a practical and robust solution for improving the data quality in the rapidly expanding domain of instruction-conditioned robot learning, ultimately leading to more reliable and semantically grounded robot policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Multimodal Probabilistic Fusion (MMPF), is a classifier-free framework designed for post-hoc auditing of Instruction–Trajectory Mismatches (ITMs) in multimodal robot demonstration datasets. It operates on existing datasets where each episode *i* comprises multimodal observations *x_i,m* for various modalities *m* ∈ M, and an initial, potentially noisy, task label *ỹ_i* ∈ {1..., C}. The core assumption is a finite set of canonical task labels and the availability of multiple episodes for each task.

The MMPF framework involves three main steps after the initial generation of modality-specific trajectory embeddings:

**A. Create Modality-Specific Embeddings:**
For each episode *i* and modality *m*, an encoder *f_m* maps the raw trajectory observations *x_i,m* to a fixed-dimensional embedding *z_i,m* ∈ R^(D_m).
- For visual modalities (e.g., head camera, wrist camera), the paper uses Cosmos-Embed1, a pretrained vision-language model.
- For robot proprioception (robot states), a custom lightweight trajectory encoder is trained. This encoder concatenates normalized robot-state and action variables at each timestep and processes the sequence using a four-layer Transformer. It is trained transductively on each audited dataset using self-supervised objectives such as masked state-delta reconstruction, masked action reconstruction, inverse dynamics, and forward-versus-reversed trajectory classification. Crucially, task instructions and labels are explicitly excluded from this encoder's training objectives to prevent label leakage. The resulting encoder is frozen before the auditing process.

**B. Unimodal Evidence Estimation:**
For each modality *m* and episode *i*, MMPF estimates a per-modality evidence distribution *p_m(y|i)* over all possible task labels *y*. This distribution is constructed by combining two sources of evidence:

1. **Local Neighborhood Agreement (kNN Evidence):**
 - For each embedding *z_i,m*, the *K* nearest neighbors *N_K(i,m)* are identified in the embedding space of modality *m* (excluding the queried episode itself). Euclidean distance on ℓ2-normalized embeddings is used.
 - Neighbors are weighted using an RBF kernel: *w_ij^(m)* = exp(−*d^(m)(z_i,m, z_j,m)* / *σ_m*), where *σ_m* is a modality-specific bandwidth parameter (set to the mean pairwise distance in experiments).
 - A local evidence distribution *p_m^(kNN)(y|i)* is derived by normalizing the weighted histogram of labels among these neighbors. This captures task consensus within the local region of the embedding space.

2. **Global Prototype Similarity (Prototype Evidence):**
 - For each task class *c* and modality *m*, a prototype *μ_c,m* is computed as the mean embedding of all instances initially labeled *c* in that modality.
 - A global evidence distribution *p_m^(proto)(y|i)* is derived based on the distance between *z_i,m* and these prototypes: *p_m^(proto)(y|i)* ∝ exp(− ||*z_i,m* − *μ_y,m*|| / *τ_m*), where *τ_m* is a modality-specific temperature parameter (set to the mean prototype distance in experiments). This captures the global manifold structure by relating an episode to the central tendency of each task class.

3. **Local and Global Combination:**
 - These two evidence sources are combined using a geometric mixture to form the unimodal evidence distribution *p_m(y|i)*. The logarithm of the probabilities is combined: log *p_m(y|i)* ∝ *λ* log(*p_m^(kNN)(y|i)* + *ε*) + (1 − *λ*) log(*p_m^(proto)(y|i)* + *ε*).
 - *λ* ∈ controls the trade-off (set to 0.5 for equal weighting). A small *ε* prevents zero probabilities from yielding undefined log values. The resulting distribution is then renormalized.

**C. Modality Reliability:**
Since individual modalities can be unreliable, MMPF computes a reliability score *r_i,m* ∈ for each episode and modality. This score is based on the predictive confidence of the unimodal evidence distribution *p_m(y|i)*, measured by its normalized entropy:
*r_i,m* = 1 − *H(p_m(·|i))* / log *C*.
A sharp posterior distribution (low entropy) indicates high confidence and receives a higher reliability score, while a diffuse posterior (high entropy) indicates low confidence and contributes less to the fusion process.

**D. Reliability-Weighted Multimodal Fusion:**
Finally, the unimodal evidence distributions are fused into a single multimodal posterior *p(y|i)* using a reliability-weighted Product of Experts (PoE). This method, inspired by generalized PoE formulations, emphasizes agreement across reliable experts:
log *p(y|i)* ∝ Σ_m∈M *r_i,m* log *p_m(y|i)*.
The final task prediction for episode *i* is *ŷ_i* = arg max_y *p(y|i)*. Additionally, episodes with globally low per-modality reliability (where any or all *r_i,m* are close to zero) can be flagged as noisy, indicating potential inherent issues with the observations themselves, independent of the instruction label.

**Hyperparameters:** Throughout the experiments, *K* is fixed at 30 nearest neighbors. The RBF bandwidth *σ_m* and temperature *τ_m* for each modality are set to the mean pairwise distance within that modality's embeddings. The mixing weight *λ* is set to 0.5. These hyperparameters are not tuned per dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MMPF centered on three primary questions: its effectiveness in detecting and correcting ITMs, its robustness to modality corruption, and its impact on downstream policy learning. Experiments were conducted on both simulated LIBERO benchmarks and real-robot datasets.

**A. Filtering Instruction–Trajectory Mismatches (Detection and Correction)**

- **Overall Performance**: Across various LIBERO suites (LIBERO-10, LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal) with injected instance-dependent and clustered label noise, MMPF consistently provided the strongest overall auditing signal (Table I). It achieved the best AUROC and AUPRC scores, indicating superior ranking of mislabeled episodes above clean ones. For example, on LIBERO-10 with instance-dependent noise, MMPF reached 99.0 AUROC and 98.0 AUPRC, outperforming retrieval (88.2 AUROC, 77.9 AUPRC). On LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Object, MMPF was superior in both ranking and practical filtering metrics (e.g., F1 scores often near 100%).
- **Correction Accuracy**: A significant strength of MMPF was its high correction accuracy. Across all eight LIBERO settings, MMPF achieved 100.0% correction accuracy among detected true ITMs. This indicates that when MMPF flags an episode as mismatched and proposes a new label, that proposal is highly reliable. Baselines showed more variability, ranging from 72.4% to 100.0%.
- **Real-Robot Performance**: The strong performance transferred to real-robot datasets ("Table" and "Bottle-mug") (Table II). MMPF achieved the highest F1 scores (91.8% on Bottle-mug, 94.3% on Table), AUROC (99.9% Bottle-mug, 99.6% Table), and AUPRC (99.8% Bottle-mug, 99.3% Table), along with superior correction accuracy (97.9% Bottle-mug, 94.9% Table). These results suggest MMPF's reliability in practical, noisier environments.

**B. Downstream Policy Learning After Auditing**

- **Impact on Success Rates**: Auditing's benefits for downstream policy learning varied depending on the task's reliance on language for disambiguation (Table III).
 - **Significant Gains**: MMPF substantially improved success on LIBERO-Goal (77.5% vs. 58.7% for unfiltered) and LIBERO-Spatial (76.7% vs. 70.3% for unfiltered), indicating that removing ITMs improves policies where task semantics are crucial and not easily inferred from visual context alone.
 - **Similar Performance**: On LIBERO-10 and LIBERO-Object, MMPF's policy success rates were comparable to or slightly lower than the unfiltered baseline. This suggests that for some tasks, policies might exploit scene layout or object configurations, reducing their dependence on perfect instruction grounding.

**C. Grounding-Oriented Evaluation (CF-Object)**

- To isolate language grounding, policies were evaluated on CF-Object, a counterfactual benchmark designed to test if the policy truly relies on language rather than spurious correlations with visual context.
- **Improved Grounding**: MMPF consistently reduced interaction with "biased" objects (objects visually prominent but not instructed) and maintained or improved interaction with "instructed" objects (Table IV). For example, MMPF increased the faithful grasp rate from 28.3% to 41.4% and reduced the biased grasp rate from 40.3% to 14.3% compared to the unfiltered baseline. This indicates that auditing primarily leads to cleaner alignment between task language and demonstrated behavior, rather than just generic control improvements.

**D. Ablation Study**

- **Multimodality is Key**: An ablation study on the Bottle-mug dataset (Table V) showed that full MMPF (91.8 F1) outperformed variants using only proprioception (90.4 F1) or visual observations (90.7 F1), highlighting the complementary nature of multimodal evidence.
- **Local and Global Evidence Complementarity**: Removing either local (kNN) or global (prototype) evidence sources reduced performance (Local only: 89.5 F1; Global only: 49.9 F1), demonstrating that both contribute to robust ITM detection.
- **Reliability Weighting**: Disabling reliability weighting lowered F1 from 91.8% to 89.4%, primarily by reducing recall while maintaining precision. This supports the role of reliability weighting in dynamically gating uncertain modalities on an episode-by-episode basis.
- **Noisy Episode Detection**: Thresholding the minimum reliability score was shown to be effective in detecting sensor-corrupted episodes. Raising the threshold from 0.0 to 0.2 improved overall recall from 81.3% to 95.1%, largely by better recovering episodes with observation noise (from 13.3% to 77.8%) (Table VI).

**E. Real-Robot Experiments (Downstream Policy Trade-offs)**

- **Filtering vs. Correction**: Real-robot downstream evaluations on the Table and Bottle-mug datasets explored the trade-off between filtering (removing flagged demonstrations) and correction (relabeling flagged demonstrations) (Table VII).
 - **Filtering**: On the Table dataset, MMPF with filtering achieved an average success rate of 78.8%, outperforming the unfiltered baseline (73.8%). It showed strong performance on semantic stages (100% on focus and navigation). However, removing data reduced the dataset size, potentially hurting tasks reliant on low-level control coverage, such as placement (40% success).
 - **Correction**: With correction-based curation, MMPF achieved a higher average success rate of 90.0% on the Table dataset, including improved placement success (65.0%). On the Bottle-mug dataset, MMPF with correction achieved 77.5% average success, surpassing unfiltered (62.5%) and CL (60.0%).
- **Conclusion**: Correction-based curation is often preferable for small real-robot datasets. It allows for preserving valuable control trajectories while improving semantic understanding, demonstrating MMPF's utility not only in detecting but also in accurately correcting ITMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Multimodal Probabilistic Fusion (MMPF), a framework that addresses Instruction–Trajectory Mismatches (ITMs) in robot demonstration datasets. The findings suggest that MMPF has the potential to significantly improve the quality and reliability of data used for training vision-language-action (VLA) policies, thereby impacting several areas of robot learning.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to tackle a subtle yet critical data quality issue that is becoming increasingly prevalent with the scaling of robot datasets. Unlike execution-level failures, ITMs often go undetected because the demonstrated behavior appears plausible, corrupting the semantic mapping between language and robot actions. By providing a training-free, classifier-free auditing mechanism, MMPF offers a practical solution for post-hoc identification and correction of these mismatches.

The empirical results demonstrate MMPF's robustness and accuracy across both synthetic and real-robot benchmarks. Its superior performance in ITM detection and, particularly, its high correction accuracy (100% on LIBERO benchmarks and over 94% on real-robot data among detected ITMs) suggest that it can reliably identify erroneous labels and propose valid replacements. This capability is crucial for reducing the burden of manual data annotation and verification in large-scale data collection efforts.

Furthermore, the research highlights the tangible benefits of auditing for downstream policy learning. By improving the alignment between language instructions and demonstrated behavior, MMPF enhances the grounding of VLA policies, especially in tasks where language is essential for disambiguation. The counterfactual evaluation on CF-Object underscores this by showing that auditing leads to a policy that more faithfully adheres to instructed objects and less to biased visual cues. This implies that policies trained on MMPF-audited datasets are more likely to genuinely understand and respond to linguistic commands.

The real-robot experiments also provide insights into practical data curation strategies. The findings illustrate a trade-off between filtering out mismatched demonstrations and relabeling them. While conservative filtering can improve semantic grounding, it may reduce the overall data volume, which can be detrimental for learning low-level control skills, especially in smaller datasets. MMPF's ability to provide accurate correction labels supports a correction-based curation approach, preserving valuable control trajectories while enhancing semantic consistency. This balance is particularly important for real-world robotics, where data collection is resource-intensive.

Potential impacts include:
- **Improved data quality for large-scale robot learning**: MMPF enables more reliable training of generalist robot policies by ensuring higher fidelity in language-action mappings.
- **Reduced human annotation burden**: Automating ITM detection and correction can significantly reduce the need for meticulous manual inspection of large datasets.
- **More robust and trustworthy robot behavior**: Policies trained on cleaner data are expected to exhibit better instruction following and grounding, leading to more predictable and safer robot operation.
- **Facilitating open-ended robot learning**: By addressing a fundamental data quality issue, this work contributes to the development of more general and adaptable robot systems.

Despite its contributions, the framework has limitations. Its performance is dependent on the quality of the underlying trajectory embeddings. While pretrained vision-language-action embeddings are becoming more available, broadly reusable encoders for robot proprioception are less common, requiring specialized training. Moreover, MMPF currently assumes a finite set of canonical task instructions, which restricts its direct applicability to fully open-vocabulary task descriptions. Future work could explore extensions to open-vocabulary settings, integration with automatic label synthesizing methods, and the inclusion of additional modalities like touch sensing to further enhance auditing capabilities.
