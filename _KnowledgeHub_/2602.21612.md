# Jumping Control for a Quadrupedal Wheeled-Legged Robot via NMPC and DE Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The Chinese University of Hong Kong developed a motion control framework that combines Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) for locomotion and Differential Evolution (DE) for trajectory optimization, enabling a quadrupedal wheeled-legged robot to perform dynamic jumps. The framework was validated through hardware experiments, demonstrating successful forward jumps over a 0.12 m obstacle and vertical jumps reaching approximately 0.5 m.

## Problem

- Achieving dynamic jumping for quadrupedal wheeled-legged robots presents challenges due to the integration of wheels and legs, which introduces additional degrees of freedom.
- There was a lack of hardware demonstrations for model-based planning and control pipelines enabling forward jumping in quadrupedal wheeled-legged robots.
- Effectively leveraging the unique capabilities of wheeled legs, specifically for generating initial velocity, to enhance jumping performance had not been fully explored.

## Method

- A comprehensive motion control framework was developed, integrating Nonlinear ==Model Predictive Control== (NMPC) for generating initial velocities during the rolling locomotion phase.
- Differential Evolution (DE) based trajectory optimization was used to compute optimal jumping trajectories for the take-off, flight, and landing phases.
- A centroidal dynamics model was used for NMPC to accurately represent the robot's significant leg and wheel mass, while a simplified Single Rigid Body Dynamics (SRBD) model was employed for the DE optimization for computational efficiency.

## Results

- Hardware experiments demonstrated the robot performing a forward jump over a **0.12** m obstacle and achieving vertical jumps with a height of approximately **0.5** m.
- During experimental jumps, the wheels reached speeds of approximately 20 rad/s and knee joint torques peaked around 30 Nm, effectively utilizing the robot's hybrid capabilities.
- Simulations revealed the potential for more complex maneuvers like backflips, though these required knee joint torques (57 Nm) exceeding the current hardware's capacity (42 Nm).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating active wheeled legs allows the robot to generate initial velocity through rolling, which significantly aids in achieving greater jump distances compared to purely legged systems.
> - The hybrid control framework, combining NMPC for continuous locomotion and DE for discrete dynamic maneuvers, effectively manages the complex dynamics and additional DoFs of wheeled-legged robots for versatile jumping.
> - Employing different fidelity dynamic models (centroidal for NMPC, SRBD for DE) across the control pipeline strikes a balance between model accuracy for specific tasks and computational tractability for real-time performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of hybrid robotic platforms, such as quadrupedal wheeled-legged robots, is an area of ongoing research aiming to combine the mobility advantages of different locomotion modes. These robots leverage both legs and wheels to enhance adaptability across diverse terrains, capable of movements like walking, rolling, and dynamic maneuvers.

Within this domain, achieving dynamic jumping presents a particular challenge. While legged robots have demonstrated advanced jumping capabilities through various trajectory optimization and control strategies, the integration of wheels introduces additional degrees of freedom (DoFs) that complicate dynamic control. Previous work on wheeled-legged robots has focused on aspects such as active versus passive wheels, with active wheels offering greater efficiency and flexibility. However, challenges persist in achieving agile locomotion for these hybrid systems. Notable examples like Boston Dynamics' Handle, Ascento, Centauro, and ANYmal demonstrate advanced capabilities, often confined to research environments, while robots with passive wheels face limitations in control and mobility efficiency over uneven ground.

Model Predictive Control (MPC), particularly Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC), has been widely adopted in quadruped robotics due to its ability to handle complex dynamics and constraints. Many existing MPC implementations simplify the robot's dynamics to a Single Rigid Body Dynamics (SRBD) model for computational efficiency, as seen in robots like the MIT Cheetah 3 and Mini-Cheetah. However, for wheeled-legged robots, the mass and inertial properties of the legs and wheels are often significant and cannot be accurately represented by an SRBD model, necessitating more sophisticated dynamic models like the centroidal model.

In the area of jumping trajectory optimization for legged robots, various approaches have been explored, including hierarchical planning frameworks and efficient trajectory optimization methods for achieving significant jump heights or distances. However, research specifically addressing dynamic jumping in quadrupedal wheeled-legged robots, particularly with hardware demonstrations of model-based planning and control pipelines for forward jumping, has been limited. One prior simulation-based study attempted driving-jumping with a wheeled-legged quadruped but did not achieve real-world experimental validation due to hardware constraints. This paper positions itself by addressing this gap, presenting a comprehensive control framework that integrates locomotion control with jumping trajectory optimization, validated through hardware experiments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate and propose a comprehensive motion control strategy for enabling dynamic jumping in quadrupedal wheeled-legged robots. This includes developing a framework that can manage the additional degrees of freedom introduced by wheeled legs to execute versatile jumping motions.

The motivation for this work stems from several factors:
1. **Enhancing Robot Versatility:** Jumping is identified as a critical capability that significantly improves a robot's versatility, allowing it to overcome obstacles, traverse gaps, and adapt to dynamic environments that traditional wheeled or purely legged locomotion might struggle with.
2. **Addressing the Challenge of Wheeled-Legged Jumping:** While wheeled-legged robots combine the benefits of both locomotion modes, executing dynamic maneuvers like jumps remains a complex and underexplored area. The additional DoFs from the wheels, while beneficial for general locomotion, add complexity to dynamic control and optimization for jumping. The authors explicitly identify the challenge of "how to deal with the extra DoFs of wheeled-legged robots for aiding the driving-jumping motion."
3. **Leveraging Wheeled Motion for Jumping Performance:** The authors aim to exploit the unique characteristics of wheeled locomotion, specifically the ability to generate initial velocity, to enhance jumping performance. This contrasts with purely legged robots that rely solely on leg mechanics for jump initiation.
4. **Closing a Research Gap in Hardware Demonstration:** A key motivation is the absence of prior research demonstrating hardware forward jumping on a quadrupedal wheeled-legged robot using a model-based planning and control pipeline. The study seeks to fill this gap by presenting a practical, validated solution.
5. **Achieving Versatile Jumping Maneuvers:** The research aims to enable the robot to perform a range of jumping motions, including vertical jumping, forward jumping, and backflips, showcasing the robustness and adaptability of the proposed control framework.

In summary, the core motivation is to develop a functional control strategy that enables a quadrupedal wheeled-legged robot to execute dynamic and versatile jumps by effectively integrating its wheeled and legged capabilities, thereby enhancing its mobility and adaptability in complex real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes a novel motion control framework for a mini-sized quadrupedal wheeled-legged robot, integrating Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) for locomotion and Differential Evolution (DE) based trajectory optimization for jumping.

**Robot Design:**
The robot is a mini-sized quadrupedal wheeled-legged platform with a total mass of approximately 22.5 kg. Each of its four legs has four electric motors: three for joint actuation (hip abduction/adduction, hip flexion/extension, knee flexion/extension) and one for wheel motion. This configuration results in 22 degrees of freedom (6 from the floating base and 16 from actuated joints). Each wheel module has a radius of 0.085 m, and each leg link is approximately 0.2 m long. The joint actuators provide a maximum torque of 42 Nm and a maximum velocity of 40 rad/s.

**Control Framework Overview:**
The overall control framework operates in distinct phases: a rolling phase, a take-off phase, a flight phase, and a landing phase.
- **Locomotion Control by NMPC:** This module is responsible for controlling the robot during the rolling phase, generating the necessary initial velocity for the jump. It operates at 50 Hz.
- **Jumping Trajectory Optimization by DE:** This module activates during the take-off phase to compute an optimal jumping trajectory based on the initial state provided by the NMPC. It is capable of finding a solution within 1 second.
- **Joint PD Controller:** This low-level controller tracks the desired joint positions, velocities, and torques derived from the higher-level controllers. It operates at 1000 Hz.
- **Inverse Dynamics:** Used to calculate the actuated torque commands for the motors from the desired GRFs and joint accelerations, operating at 500 Hz.

**Locomotion Control of Quadrupedal Wheeled-Legged Robot by NMPC:**
1. **Centroidal Dynamics Model:** Unlike conventional legged robots that often use a Single Rigid Body Dynamics (SRBD) simplification for MPC, this work employs a centroidal model. This choice is made because the mass and inertial properties of the legs and wheels in a wheeled-legged robot are substantial and cannot be neglected. The centroidal model captures the floating base dynamics, defining the evolution of the centroidal momentum based on ground reaction forces (GRFs). The system state includes Euler angles, positions, angular velocities, and linear velocities of the robot's body, along with joint positions. Inputs are defined as the GRFs for each end-effector and joint velocities.
2. **NMPC Problem Formulation:** The NMPC formulates an optimal control problem to compute GRFs and joint velocities. It minimizes a stage quadratic cost function over a prediction horizon, subject to the discrete-time centroidal dynamics, initial state, and general equality and inequality constraints. The problem is solved using Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) via the OCS2 library.
3. **Foot Constraints:**
 - **Swing Foot Constraints:** For legs in the swing phase, constraints ensure the foot follows a predefined spline curve to a desired height and exerts no external force.
 - **Contact Foot Constraints:** For legs in contact with the ground, constraints ensure that the foot force remains within the friction cone to prevent slipping. Additionally, foot velocity in the normal and binormal directions (perpendicular to the wheel's rolling direction) must be zero. Crucially, the foot velocity along the wheel's rolling direction is allowed to be non-zero, distinguishing wheeled-legged robots from purely legged systems and enabling the rolling motion.
4. **Joint Control:** A Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller is used for all joints. For wheel joints, the position gain is set to zero, prioritizing velocity tracking accuracy. An inverse dynamics calculation, performed using Pinocchio, determines the desired joint torques from the optimized GRFs. The control law for wheel motors specifically includes a velocity tracking term and a feedforward term from the optimized GRFs.

**Jumping Motion Optimization:**
1. **Optimization Algorithm:** Differential Evolution (DE) is chosen for its search accuracy, robustness, fast convergence, and global optimum finding capabilities. The optimization is based on a three-phase model of jumping: take-off, flight, and landing.
2. **Jumping Trajectory Optimization Formulation:** The objective is to determine control inputs during the take-off phase to reach a target position at the end of the jump while satisfying kinodynamic constraints and minimizing mechanical energy consumption.
 - **Simplified Dynamics:** To reduce computational complexity for online optimization, the jumping optimization uses a Single Rigid Body Dynamics (SRBD) equation of the robot, simplifying the full centroidal model used in NMPC.
 - **Inputs:** Ground Reaction Forces (GRFs) from the hind and front legs are assumed as polynomial curves over time. The take-off phase can be segmented into two steps: one where all legs are in contact, and a second where two legs lift off, allowing for more complex maneuvers like backflips where contact legs may not leave simultaneously.
 - **Optimization Variables:** The optimization variables include the robot's CoM position (x, z) and body rotation angle (θ) at three specific time points (mid-first take-off step, end of second take-off step, and end of flight phase), along with the duration of these phases (t1, t2, t3).
3. **Kinodynamic Constraints:** To ensure feasibility in real-world scenarios, the following constraints are enforced:
 - **Contact Force:** Vertical GRF component must be positive.
 - **Friction Cone:** Prevents contact point sliding.
 - **Joint Limits:** Maximum/minimum joint angles, velocities, and torques.
 - **Body Position:** Minimum height of the robot body (zB > z_min) to avoid ground contact.

**Experimental Setup:**
The control algorithms are executed on an onboard Intel NUC BXNUC10I7FNK. The NMPC operates at 50 Hz with a 1-second prediction horizon and 0.015-second discrete time step. The jumping optimization finds solutions within 1 second.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**Simulations:**
1. **Locomotion Performance:** The robot demonstrated successful locomotion in the Gazebo simulation environment. It navigated a 0.1 m high stair blindly and rolled down a 20-degree slope. The simulation confirmed that the integration of wheel motion allows the robot to slide on the ground during contact, combining rolling and trotting gaits for traversal of challenging terrain at higher speeds compared to traditional legged robots.
2. **Forward Jumping:** The robot successfully simulated a forward jump over a 0.25 m high obstacle, starting with an initial velocity of 3 m/s provided by the locomotion controller. During the jump, the robot's highest base position reached 0.58 m. Analysis of joint data indicated that the knee motor nearly reached its peak torque of 42 Nm, and the wheel velocities approached their maximum value of 40 rad/s during this maneuver.
3. **Backflip:** The simulation successfully demonstrated a backflip motion using the jumping trajectory optimization. However, the simulation data indicated that the maximum torque in the knee joints reached 57 Nm, which exceeded the physical limit of the actual motor (42 Nm). This suggested that such an aggressive motion was not feasible on the current hardware without design modifications or more powerful actuators.

**Hardware Experiments:**
1. **Experimental Setup:** Experiments were conducted on the designed quadrupedal wheeled-legged robot. The onboard Intel NUC processor handled the NMPC (50 Hz, 1s prediction horizon) and the DE-based jumping optimization (solution found within 1s).
2. **Forward Jumping:** The robot successfully performed a forward jump over a 0.12 m obstacle in a real-world experiment. While a slight contact with the obstacle occurred, attributed to human timing of the jump, the experiment validated the framework's ability to execute forward jumps. Before the jump, the wheel speed reached approximately 20 rad/s. During the maneuver, the maximum knee joint torque was measured at approximately 30 Nm, and the maximum joint velocity was around 14 rad/s.
3. **Vertical Jumping:** The robot achieved a vertical jump height of approximately 0.5 m in the experiments, with the target set at 0.7 m. During this motion, the maximum knee joint torque was approximately 30 Nm, and the maximum joint velocity reached about 14 rad/s.
4. **Overall Performance:** Despite the robot's total mass of 22.5 kg, which is relatively heavy due to the 1.5 kg per wheel module, the hardware experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed control framework. The results highlighted the advantage of integrating wheel motion, which allowed for greater jump distances compared to purely legged robots. A discrepancy between simulation and experimental results was noted, attributed to the trade-off relation between motor torque and velocity in real hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of robotics, particularly in the domain of hybrid locomotion and dynamic control.

**Significance:**
1. **Novel Integrated Control Framework:** The paper introduces a novel motion control framework that effectively integrates Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) for continuous locomotion and Differential Evolution (DE) based trajectory optimization for dynamic jumping. This dual-component approach is designed to leverage the distinct advantages of both control paradigms for complex robot behaviors.
2. **Hardware Validation of Wheeled-Legged Jumping:** A key contribution is the first known hardware demonstration of forward jumping by a quadrupedal wheeled-legged robot using a model-based planning and control pipeline. This moves beyond simulation-only results, providing empirical evidence of the feasibility and effectiveness of such dynamic maneuvers in real-world settings.
3. **Leveraging Additional DoFs:** The work successfully addresses the challenge of utilizing the additional degrees of freedom provided by wheeled legs to enhance jumping performance. By using wheel motion to generate initial velocity, the robot achieves more effective forward and vertical jumps, surpassing the capabilities of purely legged systems in certain aspects.
4. **Application of Advanced Control Techniques:** The application of a centroidal dynamics model within NMPC for locomotion, and a DE algorithm for complex, multi-phase jumping trajectory optimization, demonstrates a robust and computationally viable approach for controlling hybrid robot systems. The use of a simplified SRBD model for the DE optimization phase further illustrates a pragmatic balance between model fidelity and computational tractability for online performance.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Enhanced Robot Mobility and Adaptability:** The demonstrated jumping capability significantly enhances the robot's ability to navigate complex, unstructured environments by allowing it to overcome obstacles and gaps that would otherwise be impassable. This has implications for applications in search and rescue, logistics, industrial inspection, and planetary exploration where varied terrains are encountered.
2. **Inspiration for Future Hybrid Robot Design:** The research highlights both the advantages and the current limitations (e.g., motor torque constraints for aggressive maneuvers) of wheeled-legged robot designs. This can inform future hardware optimization efforts, guiding the development of lighter, more powerful, or more energy-efficient hybrid robotic platforms.
3. **Advancements in Dynamic Control of Hybrid Systems:** The control framework offers a template for how to manage the interplay between different locomotion modes in hybrid robots. It contributes to the broader understanding of how to integrate continuous locomotion with discrete, dynamic maneuvers, which is a fundamental challenge in robotics.
4. **Benchmarking for Future Research:** The quantified experimental results for forward and vertical jumps provide benchmark data for future comparisons and further research in wheeled-legged robot control and design. The identified gap between simulation and experiment, particularly regarding motor limitations, also points to important areas for future investigation, such as more accurate motor modeling or co-optimization of hardware and control.
