# Navigating to Objects in Unseen Environments by Distance Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> ByteDance AI Lab researchers introduce a method for Object Goal Navigation in unseen environments by predicting the distance to a target object, reformulating the problem from end-to-end reinforcement learning. The approach leverages learned spatial priors, demonstrating improved sample efficiency over RL-based baselines and achieving an average success rate of 84.7% in real-world robot experiments.

## Problem

- Existing Object Goal Navigation (ObjectNav) methods often rely on inefficient blind exploration in unknown environments without prior guidance.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches for ObjectNav are typically sample-inefficient, requiring extensive training data and interactions.
- Precisely predicting the exact semantics and poses of unobserved objects (semantic scene completion) for navigation guidance is a difficult and less robust learning problem.

## Method

- A modular system integrating a ==semantic mapping module==, a ==target distance prediction module==, and a ==local policy module== is proposed, transforming ObjectNav into a distance estimation problem.
- A ==fully convolutional network== predicts a discretized 'target distance map' indicating the shortest path length to the nearest target object, trained with ==pixel-wise Cross-Entropy loss== on offline collected data.
- The local policy module selects mid-term goals on the exploration frontier by minimizing a combined cost of path length and predicted target distance, then uses a ==Fast Marching Method== for path planning.

## Results

- The method achieved an average Success Rate of **0.436** and SPL of **0.232** in simulation, outperforming the SemExp baseline by **2.6%** in success rate and **3.5%** in SPL.
- It demonstrated significantly higher sample efficiency, requiring **1.2 million** training samples compared to **10 million** for RL-based baselines.
- Deployment on a real-world robot achieved an average success rate of **84.7%** with an average time to goal of **134.8 seconds**, showing strong generalization capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reformulating ObjectNav as a distance prediction task, learning spatial priors via supervised learning, offers a more sample-efficient and robust alternative to direct RL or exact semantic scene completion.
> - Discretizing continuous distance values into bins for classification, rather than regressing continuous values, yields better navigation performance and is a more stable learning target.
> - Despite low quantitative accuracy in precise distance values, the predicted target distance map effectively guides the agent by providing correct directional cues for exploration and target approach.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the Object Goal Navigation (ObjectNav) task, a fundamental problem within embodied artificial intelligence and robotics. ObjectNav requires an intelligent agent to navigate to a target object category in an unknown indoor environment without a pre-built map or specified goal coordinates. This differentiates it from classical navigation tasks, where a map is often pre-constructed, and a goal location is provided as coordinates.

The broader landscape includes several related areas:
- **Active SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping)**: While Active SLAM focuses on efficiently exploring an environment and building maps, ObjectNav specifically aims for efficient search and navigation to a target object category. Traditional Active SLAM methods, such as Frontier-based Exploration, prioritize exploring unknown areas to expand the map.
- **Target-Driven Navigation**: This is a broader category where the goal can be a point, object, or room. ObjectNav is a specific instance of target-driven navigation focusing on object categories.
- **Embodied Navigation Approaches**: Solutions for target-driven navigation generally fall into three groups:
 - **Map-less reactive methods**: These rely on immediate observations to determine actions.
 - **Memory-based methods**: These build some form of internal representation or memory of the environment.
 - **Explicit map-based methods**: These construct and utilize a semantic or occupancy map of the environment.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL)**: Many recent works in ObjectNav formulate the task as an RL problem, where an agent learns a policy through interaction with the environment and reward signals. However, RL methods often face challenges related to low sample efficiency, requiring extensive training data.
 - **Semantic Scene Completion**: Another line of research involves predicting elements out of sight or completing the unexplored scene to guide navigation. This often entails predicting the exact semantics and their poses, which can be difficult to achieve accurately.

This paper positions itself within the explicit map-based methods but proposes an alternative to direct RL or complex scene completion. It draws inspiration from human common sense regarding spatial relationships between objects, suggesting that humans prioritize exploration based on the likelihood of finding a target near semantically related objects. The work addresses the challenge of efficient exploration in ObjectNav by transforming it into a distance estimation problem, leveraging learned spatial priors in a supervised learning framework, rather than relying solely on online interaction and reward signals characteristic of RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable an intelligent agent to efficiently navigate to a specified target object category in unseen indoor environments without a pre-existing map. This task, ObjectNav, requires the agent to explore the environment and locate the target.

The key motivations driving this approach are:
- **Inefficiency of Blind Exploration**: In unknown environments, the agent faces numerous possible areas to explore. Without guidance, exploration can be inefficient. The authors aim to improve exploration efficiency by prioritizing candidate goal areas.
- **Leveraging Prior Knowledge**: Humans often use common-sense knowledge about spatial relationships between objects (e.g., chairs are typically found near tables) to guide their search. The paper is motivated to incorporate this type of prior knowledge into the navigation system. By understanding that certain objects are frequently co-located or in close proximity, the agent can make informed decisions about which directions are more likely to lead to the target.
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Approaches**:
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Sample Inefficiency**: Many state-of-the-art ObjectNav solutions rely on RL, which often requires a large number of interactions with the environment to learn an effective policy. The authors aim to develop a more sample-efficient method.
 - **Difficulty of Exact Semantic Scene Completion**: Predicting the precise location or relative pose of unobserved related objects (e.g., the exact position of a chair relative to a table) is a complex task, even for humans. The paper proposes that predicting the *distance* to a target, based on observed related objects, is an easier and more robust learning problem compared to predicting exact semantics or completing the entire unseen scene. The distance between related objects tends to fall within a reasonable range, making it a more stable target for prediction.
- **Simplifying Goal Selection and Integration**: By predicting a "target distance map" (representing the shortest path length to the nearest target object), the ObjectNav problem can be reformulated. This allows for the use of simpler, human-designed strategies for mid-term goal selection and integration with traditional path planning algorithms, reducing the complexity often associated with end-to-end learning policies. The motivation is to create a system that can implicitly switch between exploration and target approaching phases as more information is gathered.

In essence, the research aims to solve ObjectNav by learning to estimate the "path length to the nearest target object" based on an incrementally built semantic map and the learned spatial relationships between object categories. This distance prediction then directly informs the selection of optimal mid-term goals, guiding the agent efficiently towards the target object.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method for ObjectNav integrates learned distance prediction with a traditional navigation framework. It consists of three primary modules: a semantic mapping module, a target distance prediction module, and a local policy module.

#### 3.1. System Overview

The agent receives RGB-D images and its pose as input. At each step, this input is processed to update an internal representation of the environment. The output of the system is the next action for the agent. The core idea is that as the agent explores, the internal map and predicted distances become more accurate, allowing the agent to transition from general exploration to targeted searching.

#### 3.2. A. Semantic Mapping

The semantic mapping module is responsible for constructing a bird’s-eye view semantic map (`L_S`). This process follows established methods, specifically referencing SemExp.
- **Input**: RGB-D image and agent's current pose.
- **Process**:
 1. A pre-trained semantic segmentation model (e.g., Mask-RCNN) identifies semantic categories within the RGB image.
 2. Depth information and camera intrinsics are used to convert the 2D semantic segments into a 3D semantic point cloud in the camera's coordinate system.
 3. This 3D point cloud is transformed into the global coordinate system using the agent's pose.
 4. The transformed semantic point cloud is projected onto the ground plane to create a 2D bird's-eye view semantic map.
- **Output**: `L_S`, a `k × h × w` tensor, where `h × w` is the map size (e.g., 240x240 cells for a 12m x 12m local map, with 5cm x 5cm cell resolution). `k` represents the number of semantic categories (`c_S`, e.g., 15) plus two additional channels for obstacles and explored areas.

#### 3.3. B. Target Distance Prediction

This module is the core of the proposed system, learning to predict the distance to the target based on the observed semantic map.
- **Target Distance Map Definition**: A target distance map (`L_D`) is defined with the same dimensions as the semantic map. Each cell in `L_D` stores the shortest path length an agent would need to travel from that cell to reach the nearest target object. Cells within the target object are assigned a distance of zero, while cells within obstacles (not containing the target) are assigned an infinite distance.
- **Problem Formulation**: Estimating exact continuous distances is challenging. Therefore, the problem is formulated as a classification task. The continuous distance values are discretized into `n_b` bins, each representing a range category (e.g., `n_b=5` bins: <1m, 1-2m, 2-4m, 4-8m, >8m or infinite).
- **Model Architecture**: A fully convolutional neural network (FCN) is used. It comprises three downsampling ResBlocks and three upsampling ResBlocks, with skip connections (concatenating low-level feature maps with upsampled feature maps) at each level.
- **Input**: The local semantic map `L_S` (e.g., `k × 240 × 240`).
- **Output**: The local target distance map `L_D`. The output channel dimension is `n_b × n_T`, where `n_T` is the total number of target categories (e.g., 6). This design allows `n_b` channels to form a group for predicting the distance map of a specific target, enabling simultaneous training for all target categories.
- **Training**:
 1. **Ground-Truth Generation**: Ground-truth (GT) target distance maps are generated using the GT semantic map and traversable map of the environment. Shortest path lengths are calculated for all traversable cells, and these continuous distances are then categorized into discrete bins.
 2. **Data Collection**: Training data is collected offline by performing random exploration in diverse environments. During exploration, pairs of local semantic maps (`L_S`) and corresponding local GT distance maps are recorded. If the target is observed, the exact distance within the explored area is known. If the target is out of view, the distance map within the explored area is calculated based on the frontier distances.
 3. **Loss Function**: Pixel-wise Cross-Entropy loss is applied. Crucially, the loss is calculated only for cells within a specified distance (e.g., 1m) to the exploration frontier, as this is the area where the model makes predictions for guiding future exploration. Different loss weights are applied to different distance range categories.

#### 3.4. C. Local Policy

The local policy module decides the next action based on the predicted target distance map. It has two components: a mid-term goal selection strategy and a path planner.
- **Goal Selection Strategies**: Three simple strategies are designed to demonstrate the effectiveness of the distance map:
 1. **Integrating with Path Planning (Ours-PP)**: This strategy selects a mid-term goal `p_goal` on the exploration frontier (`B_exp`) that minimizes the sum of the path length from the agent's current position to `p` (`d(p_agent, p)`) and the predicted distance from `p` to the target (`L_Dis(p)`). `d(p_agent, p)` is obtained from the path planner based on the obstacle map. If all predicted distances on `B_exp` are infinite, random exploration is temporarily adopted. If the target is observed, its location becomes the goal.
 2. **Closest-First Strategy (Ours-CF)**: This strategy simplifies goal selection by choosing `p_goal` on `B_exp` that has the smallest *predicted* distance to the target (`L_Dis(p)`), without considering the agent's current position relative to `p`.
 3. **Door-Exploring-First Strategy (Ours-DEF)**: This strategy prioritizes exploration through doors or passages if they are observed and have a small predicted distance to the target. It uses a classification network to detect doors/passages in the RGB image. If detected, it defines an area `B_d` (intersection of a sector around the agent's orientation and `B_exp`) and selects the goal with the smallest `L_Dis(p)` within `B_d`. If no door is detected or `B_d` is empty, it reverts to the Closest-First strategy.
- **Path Planner**: After a mid-term goal is selected, the Fast Marching Method (FMM) algorithm is used to plan a path from the agent's current position to the selected `p_goal` based on the obstacle channel of the semantic map. The first action along this planned path is executed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. A. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted using the Habitat simulator with the Matterport3D (MP3D) dataset. The training set comprised 54 scenes, and the test set 10 scenes. Six target object categories were used: ‘chair’, ‘couch’, ‘plant’, ‘bed’, ‘toilet’, and ‘TV’. Semantic maps had 15 categories, with a global size of 480x480 cells (24m x 24m, 5cm/cell). Mask-RCNN (pre-trained on MS-COCO) was used for semantic segmentation. Training data consisted of 1.2 million samples collected via random exploration. Evaluation involved 1200 test episodes, with a maximum length of 500 steps and a success threshold of 1m. Metrics included Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by normalized inverse Path Length (SPL).

#### 4.2. B. Simulation Results

**Comparison with Baseline Methods (Table I):**
- The proposed method, particularly `Ours-DEF`, demonstrated competitive performance. `Ours-DEF` achieved an average Success Rate of 0.436 and an SPL of 0.232.
- Comparing to the `SemExp` baseline, `Ours-DEF` showed an increase of 2.6% in success rate (from 0.410 to 0.436) and 3.5% in SPL (from 0.197 to 0.232). This indicates that the target distance map effectively guides the agent towards the target object.
- `Ours-PP` (Integrating with Path Planning) had a higher success rate than `Ours-CF` (Closest-First) and `Ours-DEF` for some categories but often lower SPL, which the authors attribute to potential zigzag paths caused by noise in the distance map, increasing path length. `Ours-DEF` achieved a good balance of success rate and efficiency.
- The method showed improved sample efficiency, requiring 1.2 million training samples compared to 10 million for the RL-based `SemExp`.
- All methods, including the proposed ones, exhibited low performance for the target category 'TV' (Success Rate near 0). This was primarily attributed to the limitations of the underlying semantic model and the quality of 3D reconstruction in some MP3D scenes.
- When using ground-truth semantic maps (`*-GT` rows), `Ours-GT` achieved an average SR of 0.768 and SPL of 0.566, surpassing `SemExp-GT` (0.755 SR, 0.522 SPL) by 4.4% in SPL. This suggests that the distance prediction mechanism itself is effective, with semantic mapping errors being a significant performance bottleneck.
- The `SSCExp` (Semantic Scene Completion) baseline performed relatively poorly in the experiments, which the authors suggest might be due to the larger local map size (12m x 12m) making scene completion more difficult compared to the settings in previous works.

**Analysis of Target Distance Map Functionality (Table II, Figures 5, 6):**
- Quantitative evaluation of the distance prediction model revealed low precision (average 0.254) and recall (average 0.278), particularly for smaller distance categories (<8m). However, precision for distances >8m was high (0.844).
- Despite low quantitative accuracy, the model effectively guides the agent because the predicted *directions* to the target are often correct (Figure 5). If the model predicts a comparatively small distance in the general direction of the target, the agent can still converge.
- Failure analysis (Figure 6) indicated several reasons for inaccurate predictions:
 - **Misleading Spatial Relations**: The model might correctly learn spatial relationships (e.g., chairs near beds) but mispredict a target's presence if it's not actually there in an unseen environment.
 - **Uncertainty**: The model struggles with certainty about whether an object is present in a specific region, especially for smaller distances, unless the area is fully explored.
 - **Lack of Semantic Information**: Failures occur when the semantic mapping module cannot detect objects around the agent or in areas critical for prediction, leading to a lack of input for the distance model.

**Continuous vs. Discrete Representation (Table III):**
- Comparison of discrete distance categorization versus continuous distance regression showed that discrete representation yielded better results (higher SR and SPL).
- The best discrete partition achieved an SR of 0.428 and SPL of 0.231.
- This suggests that predicting precise continuous values is harder than classifying into distance ranges. Different discrete partitions also indicated that smaller distance categories are more critical for navigation performance.

**Simulation Episode Example (Figure 7):**
- An example episode demonstrated the agent's behavior: initial random exploration due to uncertain distance predictions, followed by focused search around related objects (e.g., chairs near beds), and finally, target approach upon accurate distance prediction.

#### 4.3. C. Real-World Experiment

**Deployment and Performance (Tables IV, V; Figures 8, 9):**
- The method was deployed on a real-world mobile robot equipped with a Kinect V2 camera, 2D LiDAR (for localization), and an onboard computer.
- Average time consumption per module was reported (Table IV), with Mask-RCNN being the most time-consuming (276.7 ms), and total processing time per step averaging 365.9 ms.
- Experiments in a 120m² house with 5 target categories (excluding 'toilet' due to robot traversability issues) showed strong real-world generalization (Table V).
- The average success rate was 84.7%, with average time to goal of 134.8 seconds.
- Real-world performance was notably higher than in simulation, which the authors attribute to better semantic model performance in the real-world environment compared to the simulation dataset's rendering quality and semantic annotations.

#### 4.4. D. Failure Cases

The primary causes of failure were identified as:
- **Semantic Map Accuracy**: Low accuracy of the semantic mapping module due to incorrect detections, wrong projections (segmentation noise, depth noise).
- **Occlusion/Camera Limitations**: Fixed camera angle and height preventing detection of objects in small or confined spaces (e.g., a toilet in a lavatory).
- **Navigation Bugs**: Instances similar to "Goal Bug" (getting stuck in local minima near an obstacle but not the target) and "Void" (navigating to an empty space) reported in other works.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work introduces a method for Object Goal Navigation based on predicting the distance to the target object, offering an alternative to purely Reinforcement Learning-based approaches. This method contributes to the field of embodied AI and robotics with several points of significance and potential impact:

- **Novel Problem Formulation**: By reframing ObjectNav as a target distance prediction problem rather than an end-to-end RL policy learning task or exact semantic scene completion, the authors propose a distinct and potentially more robust approach. This formulation allows leveraging learned spatial priors in a supervised manner.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency**: A notable impact is the demonstrated significantly higher sample efficiency compared to RL-based baselines (1.2 million samples vs. 10 million). This can reduce the computational resources and time required for training embodied agents, making the development of such agents more accessible.
- **Effective Use of Spatial Priors**: The method successfully integrates human-like common sense about object co-occurrence and spatial relationships into the navigation process. Learning to predict target distance based on observed semantically related objects is shown to be an effective strategy for guiding exploration.
- **Integration with Traditional Navigation Systems**: The output of the distance prediction model (a target distance map) can be easily integrated with conventional path planning algorithms (like FMM) and simple goal selection strategies. This modularity allows for the benefits of learned spatial priors without necessitating complex, learned end-to-end control policies.
- **Real-World Generalization**: The successful deployment and performance of the method on a real-world robot demonstrate its transferability beyond simulation. This suggests its practical applicability in real-world environments, which is a critical step for embodied AI research. The finding that real-world performance surpassed simulation in some aspects highlights the potential for robust application.
- **Direction for Future Research**: The identified failure cases (primarily related to semantic map accuracy and limitations of semantic perception) provide clear avenues for future work. Improving the accuracy of the target distance map, perhaps through auxiliary tasks like room-type prediction, is suggested by the authors. This indicates potential for further performance enhancements by refining the core prediction model and its inputs.

The research's focus on distance prediction as a guiding signal, rather than explicit object pose prediction or heavy reliance on RL, presents a valuable contribution to the ongoing effort to develop intelligent and efficient autonomous navigation systems for complex, unknown environments.
