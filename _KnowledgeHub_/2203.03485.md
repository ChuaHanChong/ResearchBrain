# Self-directed Learning of Action Models using Exploratory Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Parallax Advanced Research, Knexus Research Corporation, Lehigh University, and the Naval Research Laboratory developed a self-directed exploratory planning agent that leverages Lifted Linked Clauses (LLCs) to learn relational action models in unknown domains. This approach enabled the agent to learn action models for 13 actions, with 4 achieving 100% F1 scores, and explore significantly more of the environment compared to agents using non-planning exploration strategies.

## Problem

- Autonomous agents struggle to operate effectively in complex, dynamic, and unmodeled real-world environments without complete, pre-defined action models.
- Relying on knowledge engineers to provide exhaustive and accurate models for all possible domain variants is impractical and prone to failure due to unforeseen environmental changes.
- Traditional planning systems assume a given, perfect domain model, which is often not available in novel or changing settings.

## Method

- Develop an agent architecture that integrates ==self-directed exploration==, planning, and online learning of action models.
- Introduce ==Lifted Linked Clauses (LLCs)==, a formalism for abstract situational contexts, used as goals by an exploratory planning agent and to guide a complementary one-step action selection strategy.
- Utilize the **ILASP** (Inductive Learning of Answer Set Programs) system to infer relational action preconditions and effects from the agent's collected interaction history.

## Results

- The LLC exploratory planning agent learned complete action models (**100%** F1 score) for 4 actions and partial models for a total of 13 actions, significantly surpassing the random (4 actions) and one-step LLC (6 actions) baselines.
- The planning agent explored substantially more of the environment, visiting nearly all **33** unique navigable tiles, compared to the **5-7** tiles visited by baseline agents within 4,000 actions.
- Lifted Linked Clauses (LLCs) guided exploration demonstrated improved learning accuracy and exploration efficiency compared to a random baseline, validating the utility of context-aware action selection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lifted Linked Clauses (LLCs) provide an effective formal mechanism to represent abstract environmental contexts, enabling agents to track and prioritize unexplored situations.
> - Actively planning to reach states that fulfill novel or underexplored LLCs significantly enhances both the breadth of environment exploration and the quality of learned action models.
> - Online inductive learning of symbolic relational action models, using tools like ILASP, is feasible and robust when driven by a self-directed exploration strategy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in artificial intelligence: enabling agents to operate effectively in complex, real-world domains where a complete and accurate model of action capabilities may not be available a priori. Such domains are often dynamic, partially observable, and can change in ways unforeseen by knowledge engineers. The paper positions its contribution at the intersection of several key AI areas:

- **Exploration and Learning in Unknown Domains:** The work focuses on self-directed learning of action models (preconditions and effects) in novel environments. This contrasts with traditional planning systems that assume a given, perfect domain model. It draws inspiration from reinforcement learning (RL) concepts, particularly intrinsically motivated exploration, but diverges by emphasizing the acquisition of symbolic relational action models for goal-driven planning, rather than solely optimizing a reward signal. References are made to prior RL work on action novelty (Hester & Stone, 2017) and emotion-based intrinsic motivations (Sequeira, Melo, & Paiva, 2011), noting a similar objective of taking actions untried in specific contexts.

- **Inductive Learning of Action Models:** The core mechanism for acquiring action models is inductive learning from observed interactions. This builds upon a long history of inductive learning approaches, including heuristic-based methods (Vere, 1980; Hayes-Roth & McDermott, 1978; Watanabe & Rendell, 1990) and systems like FOIL (Quinlan, 1990) and OBSERVER (Wang, 1995). A distinguishing feature of the presented work is its focus on the *acquisition of sufficient and relevant interaction examples* through self-directed exploration, rather than relying on expert-provided plan traces or a simulator, as some earlier systems did. It employs the Inductive Learning of Answer Set Programs (ILASP) system (Law et al., 2015) for its learning component.

- **Planning with Incomplete Knowledge:** The necessity for an agent to plan and act before a complete action model is learned is acknowledged, connecting to research on planning algorithms that reason about action model incompleteness to avoid failures (Weber & Bryce, 2011). While the current implementation assumes a fully observable, deterministic world for learning, the broader context anticipates real-world complexities.

- **Human-Agent Teaming and Explainable AI:** The long-term vision for these agents includes operating as members of human-agent teams, where the ability to explain one's reasoning and behavior is crucial. The paper references challenges in explanation within Goal Reasoning (Molineaux et al., 2018) and systems for generating internal explanations (Aamodt, 1993; Molineaux et al., 2012). The ability to learn explicit, relational action models is presented as a foundational step towards achieving increased explainability for autonomous agents in such collaborative settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this work arises from the inherent limitations of pre-programmed knowledge in complex, dynamic, and potentially novel environments. Relying solely on a knowledge engineer to provide a complete and accurate model of an agent's capabilities (i.e., when actions can be performed and what their effects are) for all possible domain variants is considered unrealistic. Even minor environmental changes, such as weather conditions, can alter action preconditions and effects, making a static, predefined model insufficient.

The primary objective is to enable an autonomous agent to dynamically learn its own action models when operating in previously unseen or changing domains. This learning process is designed to be self-directed, meaning the agent should not require expert-provided domain models, plan traces, or specific goals from an external source to initiate learning. Instead, the agent is motivated to explore its environment to gather the necessary data.

Specifically, the work aims to achieve the following:

1. **Self-directed Acquisition of Action Interactions:** Develop an agent that can actively generate interactions (triples of prior state, action, and post state) through exploration. These interactions serve as the empirical data from which relational action models (preconditions and effects) are learned.
2. **Introduction of Lifted Linked Clauses (LLCs):** Formalize a novel representation for situational contexts, termed Lifted Linked Clauses, which abstractly describe relevant environmental conditions (e.g., "agent is in front of a closed door"). LLCs are intended to guide exploration by identifying generalizable situations where different actions might be tested or new information might be gained.
3. **Novel Exploration Strategies:** Design and implement a new exploratory action selection approach that leverages LLCs to prioritize actions that have been least attempted in currently active contexts. Additionally, develop an exploratory planning agent architecture that uses LLCs as goals to actively seek out and reach novel states or contexts that have not yet been sufficiently explored.
4. **Online Relational Action Model Learning:** Integrate an inductive learning component capable of inferring action preconditions and effects from the acquired interactions in an online fashion, as the agent operates in the domain.
5. **Empirical Validation:** Evaluate the effectiveness of the LLC-guided exploration and planning mechanisms in improving both the rate of environment exploration and the accuracy/completeness of learned action models, compared to non-planning baselines.
6. **Foundation for Explainability:** Establish a basis for learning explicit, relational models that can contribute to improved explainability of an agent's behavior and reasoning, particularly in human-agent teaming scenarios.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond static, expert-defined knowledge bases towards agents capable of autonomous, situated learning and adaptation in dynamic, unknown environments, with an eye towards enhanced interaction capabilities with human teammates.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is a self-directed exploratory planning agent designed to learn relational action models (preconditions and effects) in unmodeled domains. The agent's architecture integrates exploration, learning, and planning functionalities in a domain-independent manner, assuming relational representations for states, observations, and actions.

#### 3.1. Agent Architecture

The agent comprises three main submodules:
- **Controller:** This module orchestrates the agent's behavior. At each time step, it observes the current state, selects an action using the `ActionSelection` procedure, records the action in its `LtoA` counts (tracking LLC-action pairs), and executes the chosen action. The controller prioritizes executing actions from an existing plan, then calls the `leastTakenActions` procedure, then invokes the `explorationPlanner`, and finally resorts to a random action if all other options are exhausted.
- **Exploration Planner:** This module is responsible for generating plans to reach states that offer novel information for learning. It aims to identify and reach states where specific LLCs (contexts) have not yet been sufficiently explored or observed.
- **Transition Model Learner:** This module uses the collected interactions to inductively learn and update the agent’s internal model of action preconditions and effects.

#### 3.2. Lifted Linked Clause (LLC) Formalism

A central concept in the agent's exploration strategy is the **Lifted Linked Clause (LLC)**.
- **Definition:** An LLC `c` is a first-order relational conjunct (e.g., `c₁ ∧ c₂ ∧ ... ∧ c_n`) that includes one or more existentially quantified variables. It represents a general context (e.g., "agent is in front of a closed door") rather than a specific ground state.
- **Structure:** LLC terms can include finite relations, equalities/inequalities between integers, and the "successor" relation. The "size" `n` of an LLC is defined by the number of relations it contains.
- **Connectivity Constraint:** For an LLC with multiple predicates, each predicate must share at least one existentially quantified variable with another term in the clause. This constraint is introduced to reduce the search space of potential LLCs and ensure that learned contexts are relationally connected, rather than arbitrary collections of predicates.
- **Activity:** An LLC is considered `active` if it unifies with the current state `s`.
- **Interaction Tracking:** The agent maintains `LtoA`, a mapping that records the number of times each LLC-action pair `⟨c, a⟩` has occurred in its `Interaction History`. The function `examples(c, a)` quantifies this count.
- **Generation:** LLCs up to a certain size `n` (e.g., `n=2` in experiments) can be generated from the planning domain's predicates.

#### 3.3. Exploration-based Action Selection using LLCs (Algorithm 2)

This procedure, invoked by the `Controller`, guides local exploration:
- If an active plan exists, the next action from the plan is executed.
- Otherwise, it identifies `leastTakenActions`, which are actions that have been tried the least number of times across all currently active LLCs. It calculates for each action how many active contexts it has *not* been taken in.
- The agent then randomly selects one action from this set of `leastTakenActions`.
- If all actions have been attempted in all currently active LLCs (i.e., the current state is considered "fully explored" in terms of immediate action-context novelty), the `Exploration Planner` is called.
- If planning also fails, a purely random action is chosen as a fallback.

#### 3.4. Exploration Planner (Algorithm 3)

When the one-step action selection determines that the current state offers no novel immediate actions, the `Exploration Planner` is activated to seek out new contexts.
- **Learning Update:** First, it calls `learnActionModels(I)` to update its action models using the `Interaction History I`. This involves invoking the ILASP system.
- **Goal Selection:** It iteratively identifies new LLCs that have been encountered the least (or not at all) as potential goals. It maintains a set of `G_done` to track goals already attempted.
- **Planning:** It uses an `ASP-Planner` (Answer Set Programming Planner) with the current (potentially incomplete) learned action models `A`, the current state `s`, and a chosen goal LLC `g`.
- **Plan Execution:** If a plan `π` is found to reach a state satisfying `g`, the plan is returned, and its first action will be executed by the `Controller`.
- If no plan is found for a selected goal LLC, it tries other unexplored goal LLCs. If no plan is found for any remaining goal LLCs, an empty plan is returned, leading to a random action.

#### 3.5. Transition Model Learner

This module performs the inductive learning of action models:
- **Interaction History:** All `⟨s_i, a_i, s_{i+1}⟩` interactions generated by the agent are recorded.
- **Labeling:** In a fully observable, deterministic environment, an interaction is labeled `positive` if `s_i ≠ s_{i+1}` (action succeeded) and `negative` if `s_i = s_{i+1}` (action failed).
- **Learning Algorithm:** The `Inductive Learning of Answer Set Programs (ILASP)` system (Law et al., 2015) is used. It takes positive and negative examples, along with inductive biases (e.g., `#modeb` declarations describing predicate types), to learn Answer Set Programs that represent action preconditions.
- **Effect Learning:** After preconditions are learned, action effects are derived by comparing the `s_i` and `s_{i+1}` states in successful interactions and unifying the observed differences with the variables in the learned precondition clauses.
- **Efficiency Considerations:** ILASP calls are made externally, and their runtime can be substantial. The authors note that future work could focus on optimizing ILASP calls by selecting subsets of interactions or leveraging future performance improvements in ILASP itself.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The methodology was evaluated using an ablation study in a scenario inspired by the exploration-based video game *Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup*. The environment was a static, fully observable grid-world represented in PDDL. Agents were provided with action predicates and argument types but no initial preconditions or effects. Each agent had 24 possible actions: 8 movements, 8 open door, and 8 close door actions, corresponding to 8 cardinal directions.

#### 4.1. Metrics

- **Exploration Progress:** Measured as the cumulative number of unique tiles visited by the agent over time. Scenario 1 contained 33 unique navigable tiles.
- **Learning Accuracy:** Assessed by comparing the learned action preconditions against expert-authored perfect preconditions. For each action, the learned rule was tested on 16 expert test states (8 with a closed door, 8 with an open door). Precision, Recall, and F1 scores (0-100%) were calculated based on whether the learned rule correctly predicted if an action could be executed in a given test state, compared to the perfect rule.

#### 4.2. Hypotheses

The evaluation aimed to test five hypotheses:
- **H1:** All agents (even a random baseline) would learn some action preconditions.
- **H2:** The LLC one-step action selection agent would learn better preconditions for more actions than a random baseline.
- **H3:** The LLC one-step action selection agent would explore more of its environment than the random baseline.
- **H4:** The LLC exploratory planning agent would learn better preconditions for more actions than both the one-step LLC and random agents.
- **H5:** The LLC exploratory planning agent would explore more of its environment than both the one-step LLC and random agents.

#### 4.3. Results (Scenario 1)

The results were averaged over three independent runs for each of three agent types, across 4,000 executed actions.

- **Exploration Progress (Figure 6):**
 - The **Random** agent exhibited the slowest initial exploration rate, eventually reaching parity with the **One-step LLC** agent until approximately 2,500 actions.
 - The **One-step LLC** agent showed a slightly faster initial exploration than the random agent, but its overall progress was not substantially different from the random agent in the later stages. This confirmed **H3 minimally**.
 - The **Planning LLC** agent showed a slight delay in exploration during the initial actions but subsequently demonstrated a significantly accelerated exploration rate. It explored substantially more of the environment than both baseline agents. By the end of 4,000 actions, the planning agent had visited almost all 33 possible tiles, whereas the other agents visited 5-7 tiles. Despite some variance among runs, the planning agent's worst-case performance generally exceeded the other agents' average performance. This result strongly supported **H5**.

- **Learning Accuracy (Table 1 - Precision, Recall, F1 Score):**
 - **H1** was confirmed, as all agents learned partial models for at least some actions.
 - The **Random** agent learned partial action models for 4 out of 24 possible actions.
 - The **One-step LLC** agent learned partial models for 6 actions. In all cases where the random agent also learned a partial model, the one-step LLC agent achieved more accurate models. This confirmed **H2**.
 - The **Planning LLC** agent learned models for 13 actions, with 4 of these achieving perfect (100%) F1 scores, indicating fully correct learned preconditions. This significantly surpassed the learning performance of both the random and one-step LLC agents. An exception was noted for the `move_se` action, where the one-step LLC agent achieved a 1% higher F1 score due to slightly higher precision in its learned rule. However, given the considerably higher number of action models learned by the planning agent, **H4** was considered generally confirmed.

In summary, the empirical evaluation demonstrated that the introduction of Lifted Linked Clauses and their use within an exploratory planning framework significantly improved both the efficiency of environment exploration and the breadth and accuracy of learned action models compared to non-planning exploration strategies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of autonomous agent design, particularly for operation in complex and initially unmodeled environments. The primary significance lies in the introduction and empirical validation of **Lifted Linked Clauses (LLCs)** as a novel mechanism for guiding self-directed exploration and learning of action models.

The key contributions and their potential impact include:

- **Enhanced Autonomous Learning Capabilities:** The work demonstrates that agents can autonomously learn relational action preconditions and effects from scratch, without reliance on expert-provided domain models, pre-existing plan traces, or specific goals. This capability is critical for agents operating in real-world scenarios where environments are dynamic, partially observable, and may change unexpectedly. By generating their own training examples through active exploration, these agents can adapt to novel situations more effectively than those with static, pre-defined knowledge bases.
- **Improved Exploration Efficiency:** The empirical results indicate that using LLCs as goals within an exploratory planning framework leads to significantly more efficient exploration of the state space compared to purely random or local one-step exploration strategies. This means the agent can cover more ground and encounter a wider variety of contexts in fewer actions, accelerating the data collection process necessary for robust learning.
- **More Comprehensive and Accurate Action Models:** The LLC-guided planning approach resulted in the learning of a greater number of action models with higher accuracy (F1 scores) compared to baseline methods. This suggests that actively planning to reach novel contexts (LLCs) provides richer and more diverse interaction data, which is crucial for inferring complete and precise relational rules that govern action feasibility and outcomes.
- **Foundation for Explainable AI in Human-Agent Teaming:** A significant long-term impact is the potential for these learned relational action models to enhance the explainability of agent behavior. Since LLCs and action models are explicit, symbolic representations, they can serve as a basis for generating human-understandable explanations of why an agent performed certain actions or sought particular states. This aligns with the broader goal of developing more transparent and trustworthy autonomous agents, especially in human-agent teaming contexts where mutual understanding is paramount.
- **Generalizable Framework:** The proposed architecture and methodologies are presented in a domain-independent manner, employing relational representations. This suggests that the approach could be applied to a variety of complex domains, provided that suitable relational representations of states and actions can be formulated.

While the current work assumes fully observable and deterministic environments for learning, the demonstrated ability to learn rich relational models from self-generated data opens avenues for future research into more complex, dynamic, and uncertain real-world settings. The challenges identified, such as optimizing the inductive learning process (e.g., ILASP runtime) and extending the approach to handle dynamic environmental changes, represent important next steps. Overall, this research contributes foundational elements for building more adaptive, autonomous, and explainable intelligent agents.
