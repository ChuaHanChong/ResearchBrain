# This&That: Language-Gesture Controlled Video Generation for Robot Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents "This&That", a framework that enables robots to understand and execute tasks through a combination of natural language and 2D pointing gestures. It introduces a method for sparsely-conditioned video generation and a robust behavior cloning policy, resulting in superior video plan quality and more accurate robot task execution in ambiguous environments.

## Problem

- Traditional language-only commands and single image goals for robots often suffer from ambiguity, especially in complex or uncertain environments.
- Existing video generative models require dense spatial or temporal conditioning, making them impractical for intuitive human instruction.
- Translating high-level visual plans into low-level robot actions often relies on complex or less robust methods like inverse dynamics or optical flow.

## Method

- A language-gesture conditioned video diffusion model generates robot action plans as videos, fine-tuning Stable Video Diffusion on robotics datasets and incorporating novel sparse 2D gesture conditioning.
- A self-supervised pipeline is used to annotate existing robotics video datasets with gesture information (gripper and object keypoints) for training the video generation model.
- A Transformer-based Diffusion Video to Action (DiVA) module translates the generated video plans into robot actions, using TokenLearner for image embedding compression and temporal noise for training robustness.

## Results

- "This&That" achieves superior quantitative and qualitative video generation, with FID (17.28) and FVD (84.58) substantially lower than state-of-the-art baselines like AVDC (163.93 and 1512.25 respectively) on robotics datasets.
- A human user study showed that language-gesture conditioning yielded 91.7% user alignment success, significantly outperforming language-only baselines (e.g., AVDC at 12.5%) in resolving ambiguity.
- In simulation, the framework achieved 95% pick and 93% place success on in-distribution scenes, and 87% pick and 80% place success on challenging out-of-distribution scenes, outperforming direct behavior cloning and language-only video planners.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining deictic language with sparse 2D gestures provides an unambiguous and intuitive way for humans to instruct robots, particularly in scenes with identical or visually similar objects.
> - Controllable video diffusion models can be effectively adapted using sparse gesture conditioning and fine-tuned on robotics datasets to generate high-fidelity, user-aligned visual plans.
> - A Transformer-based behavior cloning architecture (DiVA) can robustly translate generated video plans into executable robot actions, even with temporal noise during training, demonstrating a low domain gap between real and generated videos for policy learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: imitation learning, video generative models, and human-robot interaction. It addresses a crucial challenge in robotics: enabling robots to understand and execute tasks from intuitive, human-like instructions.

**a. Imitation Learning and Behavior Cloning (BC):** Imitation learning, particularly behavior cloning (BC), is a prominent paradigm for teaching robots complex skills by mimicking expert demonstrations. While traditional BC often relies solely on robot state and visual observations, recent advancements incorporate goal information, expressed through language instructions, images, or even sketches. This paper directly contributes to this area by proposing that a *sequence of images* (a video plan) generated by a diffusion model serves as a more robust and unambiguous goal representation than a single image. It explicitly contrasts with prior BC methods that use single, potentially ambiguous visual goals, arguing that a dense sequence of images better aids the policy in reaching long-term objectives. The proposed Diffusion Video to Action (DiVA) architecture is a novel behavior cloning approach specifically designed to efficiently process and follow these generated video plans.

**b. Video Diffusion Models (VDMs):** VDMs have revolutionized video generation, producing high-fidelity and temporally consistent outputs. The paper acknowledges significant progress in controlling motion and camera within VDMs (e.g., MotionCtrl, DragAnything, Follow-Your-Click, SparseCtrl). However, it highlights a key limitation of these methods: they typically require *dense spatial or temporal information* (e.g., full object trajectories, segmentation masks) for effective control. "This&That" distinguishes itself by demonstrating controllable video generation from *sparse inputs*—specifically, two 2D gesture points and natural language. This push towards sparse, intuitive conditioning is a significant advancement in making complex generative models more user-friendly and applicable to real-world control scenarios where dense annotations are often unavailable or impractical.

**c. VDMs in Robotics and Embodied AI:** The application of VDMs to robotics is an emerging and highly active research area. Previous works like UniPi, UniSim, and AVDC have explored using generated videos as plans, which are then converted into robot actions via inverse dynamics modeling or optical flow extraction. "This&That" differentiates itself by integrating video prediction directly into a *behavior cloning* framework, offering a potentially more stable and generalizable approach for policy learning. Furthermore, it explicitly tackles the challenge of **ambiguity in robot instructions**, a problem that existing video-based planning methods often struggle with, especially in complex scenes with similar objects. By incorporating language-gesture conditioning, "This&That" offers a more robust solution for directing robot behavior in such uncertain environments, moving beyond language-only or vision-only instruction modalities.

**d. Human-Robot Interaction (HRI):** The core motivation of using "this" and "that" combined with pointing gestures directly aligns with natural human communication patterns. This work pushes the frontier of intuitive HRI by enabling robots to understand instructions in a way that is profoundly human-centric, moving away from verbose, precise verbal commands towards more natural, deictic, and gestural cues. This emphasis on natural communication is crucial for broader adoption of robots in unstructured human environments.

In summary, "This&That" represents a significant step towards creating more capable and intuitively controllable robots by effectively merging powerful generative models with robust robot learning techniques, all while prioritizing natural and unambiguous human-robot communication.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the "This&That" framework is to enable robots to understand and execute a wide range of tasks through **clear, interpretable, and unambiguous human instructions**. This overarching goal stems from recognizing the limitations of current robot instruction methods and drawing inspiration from intuitive human-human communication.

The authors identify three fundamental challenges in video-based planning that "This&That" aims to address:

1. **Unambiguous Task Communication with Simple Human Instructions:** Traditional language-only commands can be verbose, leading to ambiguity, especially in complex or uncertain environments. Single image goals, while visual, often lack the temporal detail to fully specify a task. The motivation here is to enable a more concise and clear way to tell robots what to do, similar to how humans communicate using deictic words like "this" and "that" combined with pointing gestures. This simple, cross-cultural form of communication is inherently less ambiguous for referring to specific objects or locations in a shared environment.

2. **Controllable Video Generation that Respects User Intent:** To leverage video as a plan, the generated video must accurately reflect the user's desired action sequence. Existing video generative models often require dense conditioning information (e.g., pixel-level masks, precise trajectories), which is impractical for human instruction. The objective is to achieve high-fidelity, controllable video generation using sparse, intuitive conditioning modalities (language and gesture), ensuring the generated visual plan truly aligns with the human's intention. This is particularly challenging when dealing with ambiguous objects or complex spatial relationships in a scene.

3. **Translating Visual Plans into Robot Actions:** Once a video plan is generated, it must be effectively translated into executable low-level robot movements. Previous approaches often rely on inverse dynamics models or optical flow, which can be complex or less robust. The motivation is to develop a robust and efficient mechanism to convert these high-level visual plans (videos) into a sequence of robot actions, ideally within a generalizable framework applicable to a wide range of tasks.

In essence, "This&That" is motivated by the vision of enabling robots to act as effective collaborators, capable of understanding and executing tasks from natural, human-centric instructions that mimic how people interact with each other. This involves pushing the boundaries of multimodal learning, controllable generative AI, and efficient policy learning for robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "This&That" framework comprises two tightly integrated components: a **language-gesture conditioned video generation module** and a **video-driven robot execution module** (dubbed Diffusion Video to Action, or DiVA).

**a. Language-Gesture Conditioned Video Diffusion Models (VDMs):**
The core of the visual planning is a video diffusion model built upon a large, open-vocabulary text-to-video diffusion model, specifically **Stable Video Diffusion (SVD)**.

- **Foundation and Fine-tuning:** The pre-trained SVD model, originally trained on general open-domain datasets, is initially fine-tuned on robotics-specific video datasets (Bridge V1 & V2) to adapt its generative capabilities to manipulation tasks. This initial fine-tuning incorporates language descriptions ($C_{text}$) and the initial frame ($I_0$) as conditioning, primarily through Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) and cross-attention between language/image tokens (extracted via a CLIP encoder).
- **Novel Gesture Conditioning:** This is the key innovation for controllable generation.
 - **Gesture Representation:** Gestures are represented as sparse 2D coordinates (pixels) in the first frame, indicating "this" (source) and "that" (target) locations.
 - **Architecture Adaptation:** The VDM architecture is adapted using a supplementary network parallel to the fine-tuned diffusion UNet, inspired by ControlNet. Crucially, the main UNet weights are frozen during this stage, and only the new gesture conditioning branch is trained.
 - **Addressing Sparsity:** A challenge with sparse 2D gesture inputs is that they don't provide dense information across the video or even a single frame. To overcome this, the authors propose a novel technique: within the gesture conditioning branch, before the first convolution layer, they **concatenate the first frame encoded latents ($E(I_0)$), noisy video frames ($\epsilon_t$), and the sparse gesture images ($E(C_{gest})$)**. This ensures dense input signals across the conditioning branch, allowing the sparse gesture to be meaningfully integrated with the current video content. This gesture conditioning branch is also modulated by the language prompt condition.
 - **Self-Supervised Gesture Annotation:** Since existing robotics video datasets lack explicit gesture information, a self-supervised pipeline is developed. This involves:
 1. Using a YoloV8 model to detect the 2D gripper's bounding box at key interaction moments (grasp/release).
 2. Employing the TrackAnything model to track objects' motion over time, identifying target gesture points (e.g., the initial contact point for a "pick" and the final resting point for a "place").
 3. Enhancing spatial signals by applying a 2D Gaussian filter to "dilate" these single-pixel points into small areas.

**b. Video-Conditioned Behavioral Cloning (DiVA):**
DiVA (Diffusion Video to Action) is a behavior cloning model designed to translate the generated video plans into executable robot actions.

- **Architecture:** DiVA employs a Transformer encoder-decoder architecture.
- **Inputs:** It takes a subset of the video plan frames ($\tau$), the current live image observation ($o_t$), and the robot's end-effector pose ($s_t$).
- **Image Processing:** ResNet-18 (ImageNet pre-trained, fine-tuned during training) converts video frames and live observations into latent embeddings.
- **Token Compression:** To manage the potentially large number of images in a video plan, TokenLearner is used to compress all image embeddings (from both the video plan and live observation) into a fixed, small number of tokens (16 tokens per image).
- **Transformer Operations:**
 - A Transformer encoder performs cross-attention between live image tokens and tokens from the video plan.
 - A Transformer decoder converts fixed positional embeddings into a chunk of actions (a_t:t+k) by cross-attending to the encoder output. The robot then executes this action chunk before the model is queried again.
- **Training Robustness:** To counter potential temporal misalignments between fixed-length generated videos and variable-length ground truth action sequences, **temporal noise** is introduced during training. This involves randomly sampling images from N evenly spaced groups of ground truth observations to form the goal sequence, making DiVA robust to slight temporal shifts.
- **Key Design Choices:** The success of DiVA is attributed to (1) compression of latent image embeddings via TokenLearner, (2) effective cross-attention between observation and goal tokens, and (3) the addition of temporal noise during training. Notably, DiVA is *not* trained on generated video frames, demonstrating a low domain gap between ground truth and model-generated videos for policy learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the "This&That" team demonstrate significant advancements in both video generation quality and the efficacy of video-based robot planning.

**a. Video Generation Experiments and Comparisons:**

- **Quantitative Quality (Table II):** Evaluated on the Bridge V1/V2 datasets, "This&That" consistently outperforms state-of-the-art video synthesis methods (SVD, StreamingT2V, DragAnything) and the leading robotics-specific VDM, AVDC. It achieves the best scores across all metrics (FID, FVD, PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS), indicating superior visual realism, temporal consistency, and alignment with ground truth. For instance, its FID (17.28) and FVD (84.58) are substantially lower than AVDC's (163.93 and 1512.25 respectively), highlighting a dramatic improvement in generative quality. This is particularly impressive as AVDC was trained on the entire Bridge dataset, suggesting "This&That" generalizes better despite its training setup.
- **Qualitative Quality (Figures 3, 8, 9):** Visual comparisons show that "This&That" produces high-quality, visually clear videos that faithfully depict the intended action sequences. In contrast, language-only baselines like AVDC often exhibit lower resolution, visual artifacts, or struggle to capture nuanced scene details, hindering accurate interpretation of robot states.
- **User Alignment Study (Table I):** A human study was conducted to assess how well generated videos align with user intent.
 - **Superior Alignment:** "This&That" with vision, language, and gesture conditioning (V.+Lang.+Gesture) achieves an average user alignment success rate of 91.7% (95.8% for regular text, 91.6% for deictic text in "Pick&Place"), significantly outperforming language-only baselines (e.g., V.+Lang. at 51.4%, AVDC at 12.5%).
 - **Power of Gesture:** The study conclusively shows that gesture conditioning is critical for resolving ambiguity, especially in tasks like "Pick&Place" and "Stacking" where objects might be identical or spatial relationships complex. For example, language-only baselines often fail entirely in such ambiguous scenarios (e.g., "Put carrot in pot or pan"), while "This&That" successfully generates the intended action.
 - **Deictic Language Efficacy:** "This&That" maintains high success rates even with simple deictic language ("this" and "that"), performing comparably to baselines that use more verbose, regular text. This validates the premise that gesture, when combined with deictic language, is a powerful and intuitive form of instruction for robots.

**b. Synthetic Robot Rollout Experiments:**

- **Performance in Simulation (Table III):** Experiments in Isaac Gym simulation environment for pick-and-place tasks demonstrate "This&That"'s (language-gesture conditioned VDM + DiVA) superior performance. It achieves 95% pick success and 93% place success on in-distribution scenes, and remarkably, 87% pick and 80% place success on challenging out-of-distribution scenes containing identical blocks.
- **Justifying Video Planning:** "This&That" significantly outperforms direct behavior cloning with ACT conditioned on language and gesture. This result explicitly justifies using video generation as an intermediate representation for task planning, rather than directly mapping observations to actions with complex multimodal inputs.
- **Outperforming Language-Only Planners:** The framework also surpasses AVDC + DiVA and This&That's own language-only VDM + DiVA baseline. This reinforces that gesture conditioning is essential for generating accurate video plans, particularly in scenes with ambiguity that large language models struggle to resolve through text alone (e.g., distinguishing between identical blocks).
- **Qualitative Rollouts (Figures 5, 10, 11):** The simulated rollouts visually confirm that "This&That" effectively translates user intent into robot actions, even with simple commands. Baselines frequently fail to interpret complex or ambiguous text instructions, leading to incorrect or poor-quality actions.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that the novel language-gesture conditioning approach in "This&That" leads to superior, user-aligned video generation and, consequently, more accurate and robust robot task execution, especially in complex and ambiguous real-world-like scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "This&That" framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and human-robot interaction, with several potential impacts:

**a. More Intuitive Human-Robot Communication:** The most profound impact lies in its ability to enable natural, human-like instruction for robots. By leveraging simple deictic language ("this," "that") combined with pointing gestures, "This&That" mirrors how humans intuitively communicate and clarify intent. This could dramatically lower the barrier for non-experts to instruct robots, making them more accessible and user-friendly in domestic, industrial, and service settings. It shifts the burden from precise verbal descriptions to intuitive referential communication.

**b. Enhanced Robustness to Ambiguity:** A critical contribution is "This&That"'s effectiveness in handling ambiguous task environments, especially those with identical or visually similar objects. Language-only instructions often fail in such scenarios due to the inherent limitations of large language models in complex spatial reasoning. The addition of gesture provides a concrete, unambiguous spatial anchor, making robot tasks more robust to real-world uncertainties and complexities. This is a vital step towards robots operating reliably outside of highly structured environments.

**c. Advancing Controllable Generative AI for Embodied Systems:** The paper demonstrates a novel and effective method for sparsely conditioned video generation that significantly improves control over object motion compared to prior art. This technique, by integrating gesture and language, pushes the boundaries of how human intent can be translated into precise visual plans using large generative models. This has implications not just for robotics but for broader applications of controllable video generation where intuitive user input is desired.

**d. Generalizable Visual Planning with Behavior Cloning:** By framing video generation as a generalizable planner and integrating it with a robust behavior cloning architecture (DiVA), "This&That" offers a powerful paradigm for robot control. The DiVA architecture's ability to learn from ground truth videos and effectively execute plans from *generated* videos with minimal domain gap is a strong indicator of its potential for real-world deployment. This approach avoids the complexities and potential instability of inverse dynamics models often used in other video-based planning systems.

**e. Foundation for Future Robotic Capabilities:**
- **Longer-Horizon Tasks:** While currently focused on short, modular tasks, the framework lays a strong foundation for extending to longer, multi-modal tasks (e.g., a cooking sequence) where complex sub-goals and contextual understanding are crucial.
- **Real-World Deployment:** Despite the current limitation of simulator-only robot execution experiments (due to hardware availability), the strong performance on real-world Bridge datasets for video generation, combined with established correlations between simulated and real-world performance in robotics, suggests high potential for successful transfer to physical robots.
- **Cross-Cultural Applicability:** The reliance on pointing gestures, a widely understood form of non-verbal communication, could make this approach broadly applicable across different cultures and languages.

In conclusion, "This&That" represents a significant leap towards more intelligent and human-aware robots. By enabling robots to understand complex tasks through intuitive, multimodal communication, it opens up new avenues for seamless human-robot collaboration and the widespread adoption of robotic systems in diverse real-world applications.
