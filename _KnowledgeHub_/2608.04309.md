# Structured LLM Reasoning for Zero-Shot Human--Robot Coordination Under Hidden Goals

> [!summary] Summary
> A structured Large Language Model (LLM) architecture facilitates zero-shot human-robot coordination in cooperative construction tasks with hidden goals, demonstrating improved task efficiency and human trust compared to reinforcement learning baselines. This system integrates approximate inference and planning with rule-based physical verification, ensuring safe and effective collaboration.

## Problem

- Coordinating human and robot agents effectively in shared tasks when each possesses private, hidden goals or observations.
- The computational intractability of formal decision-theoretic models (e.g., Dec-POMDPs) for real-world problem scales in such scenarios.
- Ensuring physical feasibility and safety of robot actions derived from high-level reasoning, as Large Language Models (LLMs) may generate unexecutable plans.

## Method

- Developing a **structured LLM coordination architecture** guided by a ==Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP)== formulation for cooperative construction tasks.
- Decomposing robot decision-making into modules for hierarchical planning, ==Bayesian Theory-of-Mind (ToM) inference== on human goals, and natural language conversation.
- Integrating a ==rule-based action verifier== to deterministically check physical feasibility and provide structured feedback for LLM replanning, ensuring safe and executable actions.

## Results

- In human-participant experiments, the LLM+ToM architecture achieved a mean of **5.2** interaction steps and **100%** task completion, outperforming an RL baseline (**10.2** steps, **20%** completion).
- The LLM+ToM system fostered higher human trust (mean score **84.9**) compared to the RL baseline (**57.1**), which saw a decrease in trust from pre-experiment levels.
- Simulation studies showed efficient coordination between two RL agents (**100%** success, 5 steps) and moderate success for two LLM agents (**60%** success, 12 steps), but **zero** success for LLM-RL agent interactions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models can act as tractable surrogates for computationally intensive inference and planning within formal decision-theoretic frameworks.
> - Structured decomposition of robot decision-making, including explicit Theory-of-Mind (ToM) for inferring human goals, enhances coordination efficiency and human trust in robot partners.
> - Combining LLM-driven semantic reasoning with deterministic, rule-based physical verification is crucial for ensuring the safety and feasibility of robot actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in human-robot interaction: enabling autonomous systems to coordinate effectively with human partners in mixed teams, particularly when operating under asymmetric information, such as private goals or observations. The paper situates itself within several established and emerging research areas:

**Decentralized Control and Partially Observable Systems:** The work explicitly builds upon the formal framework of Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (Dec-POMDPs), which are well-suited for modeling cooperative decision-making under private information and partial observability. However, the paper acknowledges the computational intractability of exact inference and planning in Dec-POMDPs, especially for problems of realistic scale. This establishes the need for approximate yet structured solutions.

**Large Language Models (LLMs) in Robotics and Multi-Agent Systems:** The study leverages the growing reasoning capabilities of LLMs, which have seen applications in high-level planning, task decomposition, code generation, language-conditioned decision-making, and plan revision using execution feedback. Structured LLM architectures have emerged to organize memory, reasoning, action selection, and recovery, often combining language-based reasoning with knowledge graphs and symbolic planning. In multi-agent contexts, LLMs have functioned as coordinators, communicators, and memory modules, supporting embodied collaboration and learning human-interpretable communication. This work specifically positions LLMs as tractable surrogates for otherwise intractable inference and planning computations within a decision-theoretic framework.

**Intent Inference and Theory of Mind (ToM):** A significant component of the research draws from the field of intent inference, inverse planning, and computational theory of mind. These areas focus on interpreting observed actions as evidence for latent goals, beliefs, or preferences, which is critical for human-robot coordination under private information. The paper notes that standard Bayesian ToM models can be computationally expensive as they might require solving separate decision problems for each candidate goal.

**Safety and Verification in LLM-Enabled Systems:** The research acknowledges the potential for LLMs to generate suboptimal or physically infeasible actions. It integrates with safety-oriented work that places external safeguards around LLM-generated plans, using methods like reachability analysis, temporal-logic constraints, or programmatic verification to validate or modify plans before execution. This highlights a trend towards hybrid systems where LLMs provide semantic reasoning and long-horizon planning, while conventional modules handle grounding, execution, and verification.

**Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL):** The paper references MARL as a complementary computational paradigm where coordination policies are learned through repeated interaction. The proposed LLM-based architecture is compared against an offline-trained MARL policy, establishing a benchmark against a learning-based approach to multi-agent coordination.

In summary, this work contributes to the broader landscape by proposing an integrated, structured LLM architecture that bridges the gap between formal decision-theoretic models (Dec-POMDPs) and the practical application of LLMs, specifically addressing the challenges of private information and physical feasibility in human-robot collaborative tasks. It aims to combine approximate inference and planning with robust rule-based verification, an integration not commonly found in singular prior approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a structured Large Language Model (LLM) architecture that enables zero-shot human-robot coordination in cooperative construction tasks where each agent possesses a private, hidden goal.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and opportunities in the field of human-robot interaction:

- **Prevalence of Mixed Human-Robot Teams:** Autonomous systems are increasingly being deployed in cooperative settings alongside humans. In such scenarios, robots are no longer isolated decision-makers but integral members of a team.
- **Asymmetric Information:** A common characteristic of these mixed teams is asymmetric information, where each teammate may have private goals, observations, or preferences that are not directly accessible to others. For effective coordination, a robot must be able to infer these hidden variables from interaction history, communicate appropriately, and revise its plans as new evidence becomes available.
- **Computational Intractability of Formal Models:** Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (Dec-POMDPs) offer a formal framework for modeling such problems. However, exact inference and planning within Dec-POMDPs quickly become computationally intractable for realistic problem sizes, limiting their direct applicability.
- **Emerging Capabilities of LLMs:** The recent advancements in LLMs, particularly their reasoning and natural language processing capabilities, present an opportunity. The researchers hypothesize that LLMs could serve as tractable surrogates for the computationally intensive inference and planning computations inherent in Dec-POMDPs, thereby preserving the decision-theoretic structure while enabling practical implementation.
- **Need for Physical Feasibility and Safety:** While LLMs can generate complex plans and engage in nuanced reasoning, they may produce physically infeasible actions. Therefore, there is a strong motivation to integrate LLM-based semantic reasoning with conventional, deterministic mechanisms for verifying physical constraints, ensuring that proposed actions are executable and safe.
- **Addressing Specific Task Challenges:** The chosen domain, a cooperative construction task inspired by the La Boca game, highlights these challenges. In this task, a human and a robot jointly construct a 3D structure, each possessing only a private 2D target projection. This setup necessitates robust coordination under uncertainty about the partner's goal, coupled with strict adherence to physical feasibility constraints during construction.
- **Improving Human Experience and Trust:** Beyond task efficiency, a significant motivation is to improve the human experience in collaboration. The research seeks to understand if systematic decomposition of the decision problem and the use of LLMs in a structured manner can lead to not only better task coordination but also higher levels of human trust in the robot partner. The limitations of prior approaches, which often focus on only a subset of necessary capabilities (e.g., only planning or only communication), motivated the development of an integrated framework.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for intelligent, adaptive, and trustworthy robot partners in collaborative tasks with hidden information, seeking to leverage LLMs within a principled, decomposed architecture to overcome the computational hurdles of formal decision-theoretic methods and ensure physical correctness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on developing a structured Large Language Model (LLM) architecture guided by a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP) formulation of a cooperative construction task. This architecture integrates approximate inference and planning with rule-based feasibility verification.

**A. Cooperative Construction Task Formulation (La Boca Game):**
The chosen task involves a human and a robot cooperatively building a 3D structure on a discrete 4x4x4 grid. The team uses a finite set of rigid, colored pieces. Each agent (human and robot) possesses a private 2D target projection (gH, gR) of the desired final structure. A successful construction must be consistent with both private views and use all pieces.
- **Physical Actions:** Agents can 'place', 'remove', or 'wait'. Placement actions require selecting a piece, an anchor cell, and an orientation.
- **Feasibility Constraints:** Physical actions are only admissible if the piece is available, within workspace boundaries, does not overlap with existing pieces, and every newly occupied cell above ground is supported. These constraints are deterministically verified.
- **Communication:** Agents can exchange natural-language messages.
- **Turn-Based Interaction:** The task proceeds in turns, with physical actions, workspace changes, and messages publicly observed.

**B. Dec-POMDP Formalization:**
The task is modeled as a finite-horizon Dec-POMDP.
- **State:** S_t = (x_t, g_H, g_R, ρ_t), comprising the physical construction state (x_t), private target views (g_H, g_R), and the active player (ρ_t).
- **Information:** Physical state and active player are public; target views are private. The robot maintains a belief b_H_t(g) over the human's private target view based on common interaction history (C_t) and its own target (g_R).
- **Belief Update:** Bayes' rule is used to update b_H_t(g), requiring a goal-conditioned model of human behavior q_H_t.

**C. Structured LLM Coordination Architecture:**
The core of the methodology is the proposed architecture (Fig. 2), which decomposes robot decision-making into several modules, with LLMs serving as surrogates for intractable computations and a rule-based verifier for physical feasibility. The modules are:

1. **Hierarchical LLM Task Planner (Fig. 3):**
 - **Context (K_t):** Includes current workspace (x_t), robot target (g_R), human-goal belief (b_H_t), and interaction history.
 - **High-Level Planner (LLM_high):** Identifies the next intermediate objective, generating an 'intent', a natural-language 'subgoal', and a 'rationale'.
 - **Low-Level Planner (LLM_low):** Translates the subgoal into a candidate physical action (operation, piece, cells).
 - **Rule-Based Action Verifier (Python function):** A deterministic module that checks if the candidate action (ea_R_t) is physically feasible (V(x_t, ea_R_t)). If invalid, it returns a structured failure explanation (e_t) indicating the violated constraint.
 - **Feedback-Based Replanner:** If an action fails verification, this module receives the planning context, subgoal, rejected action, and failure explanation. It revises the action (e.g., piece, placement) while trying to preserve the high-level subgoal, and resubmits it for verification. This loop allows for approximate planning while ensuring physical correctness.

2. **LLM-Based Theory-of-Mind (ToM) Module (Fig. 4):**
 - Implements the Bayesian inverse-planning structure for updating the robot's belief about the human's goal.
 - **Goal-Conditioned Action Generator (LLM_act):** For each candidate human goal (g), it predicts plausible human actions given the current state from the human's viewpoint (x_H_t) and interaction history (h_t).
 - **Human-Goal Inference (LLM_compare):** Compares these predictions with the actually observed human action history (h_H_t) to generate compatibility scores.
 - **Belief Update:** These compatibility scores are used to update the probability distribution (b_H_t(g)) over candidate human goals, which is then fed to the planner and conversation modules.

3. **Conversation Module (Fig. 5):**
 - Manages bidirectional natural-language interaction.
 - **Human Speech Processing Pipeline:** Uses OpenAI gpt-4o-mini-transcribe for audio-to-text conversion. A gpt-4.1-nano model processes the transcript to extract human intent, explicit assembly suggestions (structured action type, piece, coordinates).
 - **Robot Response Generation:** Converts generated robot responses into speech.
 - **Functionality:** Supports clarification, explanation of robot actions, communication of subgoals, responses to questions, negotiation, and strategic discussion. Human suggestions are interpreted, evaluated against robot goals and ToM belief, and may lead to accepted, modified, or rejected actions, always subject to rule-based verification.

**D. LLM Implementation Details:**
GPT-5.4 nano was used for the LLM-based components to balance latency and computational cost.

**E. Human-Participant Evaluation:**
- **Participants:** Five graduate students.
- **Conditions:**
 - **LLM+ToM:** The complete structured LLM architecture.
 - **LLM (Ablation):** The same architecture as LLM+ToM but without the explicit ToM module; the robot does not maintain a belief about the human's private goal.
 - **RL (Baseline):** An offline-trained multi-agent Reinforcement Learning (MaskablePPO) policy. This policy was trained via alternating cooperative self-play, with a centralized critic observing both agents' private information during training, but decentralized execution.
- **Measures:** Task completion success, participant-initiated termination, number of interaction steps, completion time, invalid action proposals, replanning attempts, and post-interaction trust ratings using the Trust Perception Scale–HRI.

**F. Simulation Results:**
To further analyze agent-agent interactions, simulations were conducted comparing RL/RL, LLM/RL, and LLM/LLM configurations over 10 episodes, with a maximum of 15 steps per episode. Metrics included success rate and average steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented findings from both human-participant experiments and simulation studies.

**A. Human-Participant Evaluation (Pilot Study, N=5):**

- **Task Efficiency (Interaction Steps):**
 - The **LLM+ToM** condition exhibited the lowest mean number of interaction steps at 5.2.
 - The **LLM ablation** (without ToM) followed with a mean of 6.4 steps.
 - The **RL policy** showed the highest mean interaction steps at 10.2, though this figure includes trials terminated by participants.
 - The difference between LLM+ToM and the LLM ablation suggests that explicit Theory-of-Mind inference contributed to more efficient coordination.

- **Task Completion:**
 - In both the **LLM+ToM** and **LLM ablation** conditions, all five participants successfully completed the construction task.
 - In contrast, only one participant successfully completed the task with the **RL policy**. The remaining four participants terminated the interaction prematurely due to reported frustration with the robot's behavior, indicating a notable difference in task completion robustness.

- **Trust Ratings:**
 - Pre-experiment mean trust score was 68.6.
 - Post-interaction trust scores were highest for the **LLM+ToM** condition, averaging 84.9.
 - The **LLM ablation** also resulted in increased trust, with a mean score of 81.1.
 - Interaction with the **RL policy** led to a decrease in trust, with a mean score of 57.1, which was lower than the pre-experiment baseline. This indicates that while the RL policy might generate physically valid actions, its behavior was less conducive to fostering human trust.

- **Theory-of-Mind Inference Performance:**
 - The ToM module, which started with a uniform belief of 0.25 across four possible human goal views, demonstrated an increase in the average probability assigned to the correct human goal view after each belief update. Updates occurred every two trials. This observation suggests the ToM module was progressively and effectively inferring the human participant's hidden goal.

**B. Simulation Results:**

- **RL/RL Configuration:** When two RL policies interacted, they achieved a 100% success rate with an average of 5 steps per episode. This indicates that when both agents are trained reinforcement learning policies, they can coordinate efficiently.
- **LLM/RL Configuration:** An LLM-based planner interacting with an RL policy resulted in a 0% success rate, with all episodes reaching the 15-step limit and failing. This suggests a significant challenge in cross-policy coordination, where the LLM agent struggled to align with the behavior of the RL agent.
- **LLM/LLM Configuration:** When two LLM-based planners interacted, they achieved a 60% success rate with an average of 12 steps. This indicates that while LLM agents can coordinate and achieve success, their efficiency was lower than that of the RL/RL pairing in this simulated environment.

In summary, the human-participant experiments suggest that the structured LLM architecture, particularly with the explicit ToM module, improved both objective coordination efficiency (fewer steps, higher completion rates) and subjective human experience (higher trust). The RL baseline, despite potentially generating valid actions, failed to coordinate effectively with human partners and led to frustration. Simulation results further illustrate varying coordination capabilities across different agent types, with LLM-RL interaction proving particularly difficult.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research demonstrates a methodology for enhancing human-robot coordination, particularly in scenarios involving asymmetric information. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Validation of Structured LLM Architectures for HRI:** The work provides empirical evidence that systematically decomposing complex team decision problems, guided by formal frameworks like Dec-POMDPs, and implementing these components with LLMs, can lead to effective human-robot coordination. This structured approach leverages LLM capabilities while mitigating their limitations.

- **Bridging Decision Theory and LLM Practicality:** It showcases LLMs as tractable surrogates for otherwise computationally intensive inference and planning computations that arise in decision-theoretic models. This approach allows researchers to retain the theoretical rigor of formal models while enabling practical application in real-world human-robot interaction settings.

- **Improved Human-Robot Teaming and Trust:** The human-participant study indicates that the proposed architecture, particularly with its explicit Theory-of-Mind (ToM) module, resulted in fewer interaction steps, higher task completion rates, and significantly increased post-interaction trust ratings compared to an LLM ablation and a multi-agent reinforcement learning baseline. This suggests that the robot's ability to infer human intent and communicate effectively contributes to a more efficient and positive human experience, which is crucial for the adoption of autonomous systems in collaborative environments.

- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness through Verification:** The inclusion of a rule-based action verifier separates physical feasibility from the LLM's semantic reasoning. This ensures that even if an LLM generates a suboptimal or potentially unsafe action, it is checked and revised before execution. This hybrid approach contributes to the safety and robustness of LLM-enabled robotic systems, preventing physically infeasible or task-violating behaviors.

- **Effective Handling of Asymmetric Information:** The explicit representation and update of the human's private goal estimate via the ToM module represents a tangible method for robots to reason about and adapt to hidden information. This is a critical capability for flexible and intelligent human-robot collaboration where partners often have differing perspectives or knowledge.

- **Foundation for Future HRI Systems:** The architecture's explicit representation of human-goal estimates, current subgoals, verification outcomes, and plan revisions supports runtime adaptation, direct use of natural language, and post-hoc interpretability of robot decisions. These features are foundational for developing more transparent, adaptable, and explainable robotic partners.

- **Insights into LLM-RL Coordination Challenges:** The simulation results highlight that while RL policies can coordinate effectively with other RL agents, and LLM agents can achieve moderate success with other LLM agents, coordination between an LLM agent and an RL agent currently presents significant challenges (0% success rate in simulations). This finding points to a critical area for future research in integrating or aligning different AI paradigms for multi-agent systems.

In conclusion, this research presents a principled and effective approach to human-robot coordination under uncertainty, leveraging LLMs in a structured manner to achieve both task efficiency and improved human trust. It offers a valuable framework for developing more capable and socially intelligent robotic systems for collaborative tasks.
