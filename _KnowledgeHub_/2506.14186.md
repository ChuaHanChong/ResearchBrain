# Differentiable Simulation of Hard Contacts with Soft Gradients for Learning and Control

> [!summary] Summary
> DiffMJX couples MuJoCo XLA's penalty-based contact solver with adaptive-stepsize ODE integration (Diffrax) and smoothed collision routines to recover accurate contact gradients under stiff, realistic settings. Contacts From Distance adds artificial forces between separated bodies only in the backward pass via a straight-through trick, giving informative pre-contact gradients. Together they enable real-world cube system identification and gradient MPC that beats sampling planners.

## Problem

- Penalty-based simulators need stiff solver settings for realistic hard contacts, but automatic differentiation then returns gradients corrupted by time-discretization error, while soft settings widen the sim-to-real gap.
- Time-of-impact correction fixes ideal-elastic simulators but not penalty-based ones, whose collision dynamics are nonlinear and stiff, so they cannot be split into linear segments.
- When bodies are separated, contact gradients vanish, so optimizers get no signal telling a hand to reach an object or a parameter to grow into contact.

## Method

- DiffMJX embeds Diffrax adaptive-stepsize integration (Tsit5 with PID step control) into MuJoCo XLA, with recursive checkpointing cutting backward memory from O(n) to O(1) at O(n log n) compute.
- Smoothed collision detection replaces discrete case distinctions in MJX box, capsule and cylinder routines, plus muscle-actuator branches, with differentiable surrogate functions.
- Contacts From Distance extends MuJoCo's impedance spline and softens the reference acceleration to positive signed distances, injected only in the backward pass by a stop-gradient straight-through estimator.

## Results

- On the ContactNets dataset of 550 real cube tosses, DiffMJX with CFD recovered the 100 mm side length within about 5%, where vanilla MJX stalled from 60 or 140 mm initializations.
- Adaptive integration cut loss and gradient error by several orders of magnitude at equal compute on the cube-bounce benchmark; trajectory error dropped from roughly 0.32 to 0.26 after parameter fitting.
- Gradient MPC over a 256-step horizon solved in-hand ball swapping and cube reorientation on a 39-muscle MyoHand where predictive sampling with up to 1024 rollouts failed; bionic tennis needed CFD.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Bad contact gradients in soft-contact simulators are a numerical integration problem, not an inherent discontinuity problem, so error-controlled solvers fix them where smaller fixed steps are impractical.
> - Applying a physically wrong but informative model only in the backward pass is a general recipe: keep forward realism, buy gradient signal where the true derivative is zero.
> - Caution: accuracy costs wall-clock; overparameterized muscle actuation helped first-order planners escape local minima, and gradient clipping plus best-iterate selection were required for stable optimization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The utilization of gradients has significantly advanced machine learning, including applications in robot control and policy learning through imitation and reinforcement learning. Despite the prevalence of sim-to-real techniques that leverage robot simulators, the direct use of simulator gradients for policy learning and model parameter updates has been limited. This is primarily due to two fundamental challenges: (i) the introduction of discontinuities by contact forces into robot dynamics, leading to erroneous gradients, and (ii) the phenomenon of vanishing contact gradients when objects are not in physical contact. Overcoming these issues could enable more efficient simulator fitting to real-world data, thereby reducing the sim-to-real gap and accelerating policy training.

Current approaches to handling contacts in differentiable simulators vary. Complementary-based solvers compute contact forces precisely, but the resulting sudden jumps in dynamics complicate gradient computation. Research in this area often proposes analytical reformulations (e.g., via the implicit function theorem) or randomized smoothing techniques to address these discontinuities. Penalty-based simulators, such as MuJoCo, soften contact resolution, making them amenable to automatic differentiation. However, achieving realistic "hard" contacts in these simulators requires stiff solver settings, which, in turn, lead to incorrect gradients when using automatic differentiation due to time discretization errors. While reducing the integration step size can improve accuracy, it often results in computationally prohibitive simulations. Time-of-impact (TOI) correction methods, primarily used for impulse-based simulators, target specific piecewise linear dynamics and are not directly applicable to the non-linear dynamics of penalty-based systems during contact.

Regarding the issue of vanishing gradients for non-colliding objects, prior work has explored contact-invariant optimization (CIO), which introduces artificial contact forces between objects to guide optimization. However, a naive application of such forces can significantly alter the simulation's realism, necessitating additional regularization terms. Other methods rely on inter-body distances in optimization objectives.

This research addresses the limitations of existing penalty-based differentiable simulators by focusing on improving gradient accuracy under stiff contact conditions and generating informative gradients for objects that are near but not yet in contact, all while aiming to preserve simulation realism and computational efficiency. It seeks to provide a more robust and practical framework for leveraging simulator gradients in complex robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable the effective and practical use of simulator gradients for gradient-based optimization in robot learning and control. This includes facilitating faster policy learning and more accurate system identification, ultimately contributing to a reduction in the sim-to-real gap. The authors identify two primary challenges that hinder this goal and formulate specific objectives to address them:

**Motivation for Addressing Erroneous Gradients in Hard Contacts:**
Penalty-based simulators like MuJoCo are widely adopted in robotics due to their computational efficiency and ability to model compliant contacts. However, for realistically simulating "hard" contacts (e.g., a rigid cube bouncing off a table), these simulators require stiff solver settings. When automatic differentiation is applied to such stiff simulations, the computed gradients often become inaccurate and oscillate. The authors pinpoint the root cause of these inaccuracies to time-discretization errors that arise from numerically integrating stiff ordinary differential equations (ODEs). While reducing the integration step size can mitigate these errors, it leads to a substantial increase in computational cost and memory usage, rendering it impractical for many applications. The existing time-of-impact (TOI) correction methods are designed for impulse-based simulators with piecewise linear dynamics and are not directly applicable to the continuously smooth but highly stiff dynamics of penalty-based contacts.

- **Key Objective 1:** To analyze why gradients degrade in penalty-based simulators under hard contact conditions and, building on these insights, propose a method that substantially improves gradient accuracy in the presence of stiff contacts without incurring prohibitive computational overhead. This involves developing a differentiable simulation framework that can correctly compute gradients of realistic, hard contacts while maintaining practical simulation speeds.

**Motivation for Addressing Vanishing Contact Gradients for Non-Colliding Objects:**
A second challenge in gradient-based optimization for robotics is that contact gradients are zero when objects are not in contact. For instance, if a robot's gripper is not touching an object it needs to manipulate, there is no gradient signal to guide the gripper to initiate contact. This lack of informative "pre-contact" gradients restricts the ability of gradient-based optimizers to discover complex behaviors that involve initiating or precisely managing contacts, which are crucial for many robot tasks (e.g., grasping, pushing, hitting). While position-based costs can be used, they often require precise specification of target contact locations, which can be cumbersome and limit the discovery of novel interaction strategies. Existing contact-invariant optimization (CIO) techniques introduce artificial contact forces, but applying these forces in the forward simulation can alter the physical realism and lead to non-physical behaviors, requiring additional loss terms to minimize their use.

- **Key Objective 2:** To introduce a mechanism that provides informative pre-contact gradients (i.e., gradients for objects that are close but not yet touching) without compromising the physical realism of the forward simulation. This mechanism should facilitate the discovery of contact-rich behaviors and enhance policy generation and system identification.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces two main contributions, DiffMJX and Contacts from Distance (CFD), to address the identified challenges in differentiable simulation.

**DiffMJX: Differentiable Simulation with Adaptive Time Integration**

- **Analysis of Gradient Degradation:** The authors analyze penalty-based simulators to identify that gradient errors during hard contacts primarily arise from time-discretization errors in the numerical integration of stiff ordinary differential equations (ODEs). They illustrate this with a toy example of a point mass colliding with a surface, showing that for penalty-based models, gradient oscillations decrease with smaller step sizes, unlike ideal-elastic models.
- **Adaptive Stepsize Integration:** To mitigate these errors without resorting to universally small (and slow) fixed step sizes, DiffMJX incorporates adaptive stepsize integration. This technique, well-established in numerical ODE solving, dynamically adjusts the integration step size based on an error estimate. Two numerical integrators of different orders are used to compute the next state; their difference provides an error estimate. If the error is below a threshold, the step is accepted; otherwise, it is rejected, and a new step size is chosen.
- **Implementation with Diffrax:** DiffMJX is implemented by coupling MuJoCo XLA (MJX), a JAX-based reimplementation of MuJoCo, with Diffrax, a JAX library for efficient numerical integration. Diffrax provides flexibility in choosing solvers (e.g., Tsit5, a 5th order explicit Runge-Kutta method, which is often recommended) and offers different backpropagation modes, including "discretize-then-optimize" (unrolling) and "optimize-then-discretize" (backsolving via adjoint methods). The authors adapted Diffrax to seamlessly integrate quaternions and stateful actuators, ensuring compatibility with MJX and other MuJoCo libraries.
- **Memory and Performance:** Diffrax's optimal gradient checkpointing scheme reduces memory requirements from linear to constant with respect to the number of solver steps, exchanging memory for increased runtime (O(n log n)). Adaptive integration aims to reduce overall runtime by taking larger steps where dynamics are less stiff.
- **Collision Detection Smoothing:** The original MJX collision detection routines contain non-differentiable operations due to discrete case distinctions (e.g., for plane-cylinder contacts). DiffMJX addresses this by smoothing these distinctions with standard proxies, specifically using sigmoid-based interpolation to ensure continuous differentiability. This ensures analytical gradients align with central differences.
- **Muscle Actuator Smoothing:** Differentiation errors in muscle-tendon units (e.g., singularities in arcsin functions for muscle wrapping) are addressed using a "double-where" trick to prevent gradient flow at these singular points.

**Contacts From Distance (CFD) with Straight-Through Estimation**

- **Motivation:** To generate informative gradients for non-colliding objects, addressing the "zero gradient" problem.
- **Creating Artificial Contact Forces:** CFD modifies MuJoCo's contact model. Specifically, the impedance function `d(r)` (which determines a constraint's ability to generate force) and the position-level reference acceleration `h(r)` are augmented. For positive signed distances `r > 0` (i.e., when objects are not touching but are close), `d(r)` is extended by an additional polynomial spline, ensuring it remains non-zero. The ReLU function in `h(r)` is replaced with a softplus function for smoothness. This allows the solver to compute "artificial" contact forces `f_CFD` for objects at a distance.
- **Preserving Simulation Realism:** A critical aspect of CFD is that these artificial contact forces are *only* introduced during the gradient computation (backward pass) and do not affect the physical forward simulation. This is achieved using the "straight-through-trick" on the ODE level. In JAX, this is implemented by computing the system's acceleration twice: once without CFD (`d_mjx`) and once with CFD (`d_cfd`). A `grad_replace_fn` then ensures that the forward pass uses the original `d_mjx` (via `stop_gradient(x_mjx)`), while the backward pass computes gradients based on the CFD-modified dynamics (effectively `x_cfd - stop_gradient(x_cfd)`). This means the derivatives are evaluated at the unmodified forward trajectory.
- **Computational Cost:** This approach requires computing the forward pass twice, but the backward pass (gradient computation), which is often the more expensive operation, is only performed once using the CFD-modified dynamics.
- **Compatibility with Integration Modes:** The straight-through-trick can be combined with both "discretize-then-optimize" and "optimize-then-discretize" modes. The authors note that in specific corner cases where the forward ODE is non-stiff (no physical contact) but the CFD-modified adjoint ODE becomes stiff, using "optimize-then-discretize" can prevent gradient oscillations by allowing the adjoint solver to adapt its step size.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings across three main areas: gradient correctness and informativeness, parameter identification, and model predictive control.

**Gradient Correctness and Informativeness**

- **DiffMJX for Hard Contacts:** Experiments with various geometric primitives (box, capsule, cylinder, ellipsoid, sphere) bouncing off a surface demonstrated that standard MJX with default integration parameters produced highly oscillating and inaccurate gradients compared to central differences. DiffMJX, leveraging adaptive integration (specifically the Tsit5 solver), achieved analytical gradients that closely matched central differences. This indicates that adaptive integration effectively mitigates time-discretization errors that cause gradient inaccuracies in stiff penalty-based simulations. DiffMJX significantly reduced loss and gradient errors for a given computational budget compared to fixed-step MJX integrators.
- **Collision Detection Smoothing:** The explicit smoothing of non-differentiable operations within MJX's collision detector (e.g., discrete case distinctions) was crucial for obtaining these accurate analytical gradients across different object primitives. Without these modifications, some primitives still exhibited gradient artifacts.
- **CFD for Pre-Contact Gradients:** A billiard shot example demonstrated the utility of CFD. When a force `F` on a white ball did not cause it to hit a black ball, vanilla MJX yielded zero gradients for the black ball's position with respect to `F`. DiffMJX with CFD, by introducing artificial contact forces from a distance, provided informative, non-zero gradients in this scenario, allowing an optimizer to guide the white ball towards the black ball. This mechanism addresses the problem of vanishing gradients effectively.
- **Integration Mode and CFD:** An analysis using a simplified billiard example (without gravity or table contact) showed that when the forward ODE is non-stiff (no physical contact) but the CFD-modified adjoint ODE is stiff, "discretize-then-optimize" can lead to gradient oscillations because the forward solver takes large steps. In such specific cases, "optimize-then-discretize" allowed the adjoint solver to adapt its step size, thereby eliminating the oscillations and yielding correct gradients.

**Parameter Identification**

- **Contactnets Dataset:** The capabilities of DiffMJX and CFD were evaluated on a real-world system identification task using the Contactnets dataset, which consists of trajectories of a cube tossed onto a wooden table. The objective was to estimate the cube's geometric and contact parameters (side length, mass, solref, solimp, friction coefficients) using gradient-based optimization.
- **Performance:**
 - **Side Length Estimation:** When initialized with estimates far from the true cube side length (e.g., 60mm or 140mm for a 100mm cube), vanilla MJX often stalled or showed limited convergence. The incorporation of CFD significantly improved convergence by providing informative gradients even when the initial simulation did not predict physical contact.
 - **Accuracy:** DiffMJX further enhanced estimation accuracy by dynamically adjusting time steps during collisions, thereby mitigating time discretization errors. After training, DiffMJX predictions closely matched real-world cube tosses, achieving an error of around 5% relative to the ground truth for side length and reducing trajectory error from approximately 0.32 to 0.26 when estimating multiple parameters simultaneously.
 - **First Demonstration:** This work reports the first demonstration of parameter estimation for real-world cube dynamics using an automatically differentiable penalty-based simulator.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC)**

- **Experimental Setup:** Gradient-based MPC was performed with DiffMJX and CFD, optimizing control sequences over a 256-step horizon with Adam. A predictive sampling planner served as a baseline. Experiments used state-of-the-art muscle-tendon models from MyoSuite (MyoHand with 39 muscle-tendon units, and a bionic model with 63 muscle-tendon units and 17 motor control units).
- **Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation (Ball Swap):** The task involved swapping two balls within the MyoHand. Gradient-based MPC reliably solved this task, outperforming sampling-based planners, particularly in the context of overparameterized musculoskeletal systems where first-order methods can better leverage the high dimensionality. CFD was not critically needed here due to frequent contacts driven by gravity. Gradient clipping and selecting the minimal cost rollout were identified as important for stability.
- **Bionic Tennis Task:** This complex task required a bionic arm to deflect a ball with a racket and then catch it with the muscle hand, with only the ball's distance to target as cost. The initial setup ensured the ball would miss both hands.
 - **CFD's Role:** Vanilla gradient-based MPC failed to solve this task due to the initial lack of contact, resulting in zero gradients. Activating CFD allowed the gradient-based MPC to successfully solve the task by providing informative pre-contact gradients to guide the racket and hand.
 - **Comparison to Sampling:** Sampling-based planners performed well initially for the racket deflection (lower-dimensional control) but struggled with the precise, high-dimensional control required for catching and balancing the ball with the MyoHand, leading to sub-optimal results.
- **Cube Reorientation (In-Hand and On-the-Table):**
 - **In-Hand:** Gradient-based MPC solved this, while sampling-based methods failed. Similar to ball swap, CFD's impact was less pronounced due to gravity assisting contacts.
 - **On-the-Table:** Only gradient-based MPC with CFD perfectly solved this task, reorienting the cube and keeping it at the target location. Without CFD or with sampling, the cube was reoriented but failed to maintain the target position, indicating that CFD provided crucial signals for comprehensive multi-finger contact.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that DiffMJX provides accurate gradients for stiff contacts, and CFD offers informative gradients for non-colliding objects, which collectively enable more effective gradient-based optimization for system identification and complex robot control tasks, surpassing the capabilities of vanilla differentiable simulators and, in many cases, sampling-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses fundamental limitations in the use of simulator gradients, which has significant implications for the field of robot learning and control.

- **Enabling Gradient-Based Robot Learning:** By tackling the issues of erroneous gradients from hard contacts and vanishing gradients from non-colliding objects, this work effectively removes two major barriers to the widespread adoption of gradient-based optimization in robotics. This opens avenues for more direct, efficient, and robust learning of robot policies and model parameters.
- **Narrowing the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The ability to obtain accurate gradients from stiff, realistic simulations, combined with the power of gradient-based optimization for parameter identification, facilitates more precise fitting of simulation models to real-world data. This improved fidelity can substantially reduce the "sim-to-real gap," making policies learned in simulation more transferable and effective in physical robots.
- **Accelerated Policy Development:** With accurate and informative simulator gradients, policies for new and complex tasks could potentially be trained in significantly less time (e.g., seconds instead of hours). This acceleration stems from the direct guidance provided by gradients, enabling optimizers to converge faster and explore the policy space more efficiently than purely gradient-free or sampling-based methods.
- **Enhanced Capability for Complex, Contact-Rich Systems:** The proposed methods, DiffMJX and CFD, provide essential tools for handling the complexities of high-dimensional, collision-rich systems, such as musculoskeletal robots and dexterous manipulation tasks. Demonstrating that gradient-based MPC with CFD can outperform sampling-based methods on such challenging scenarios underscores its potential for advancing capabilities in these domains.
- **New Toolbox for Practitioners:** DiffMJX and CFD introduce practical "tuning knobs" within the MuJoCo XLA framework, allowing practitioners to balance gradient correctness and informativeness against computational cost. This provides a flexible and powerful toolbox for researchers and engineers working with differentiable simulations.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a new perspective on obtaining useful gradients in penalty-based differentiable simulators. It guides future research towards mitigating discretization errors further, exploring implicit differentiation for constraint solvers (which could improve efficiency), and investigating hybrid approaches that combine CFD with sampling-based methods to address non-convex optimization landscapes more comprehensively. The findings also highlight the importance of careful collision detection and actuator model design for differentiability.
