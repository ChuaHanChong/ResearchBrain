# Deep Reinforcement Learning Versus Evolution Strategies: A Comparative Survey

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey provides a comprehensive comparison of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Evolution Strategies (ESs) across their fundamental concepts, algorithms, and capabilities. It highlights their distinct strengths and weaknesses concerning parallelism, exploration, adaptation to dynamic environments, and multi-agent learning, while also exploring hybrid approaches and real-world applications.

## Problem

- A lack of comprehensive comparative analysis systematically contrasting Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Evolution Strategies (ESs) across fundamental aspects and developmental directions.
- Researchers and practitioners face challenges in selecting the most appropriate algorithm for specific sequential decision-making problems due to the distinct advantages and disadvantages of DRL and ESs.
- Limited understanding of the complementary strengths and weaknesses between DRL and ESs hinders interdisciplinary innovation and the development of more robust AI systems.

## Method

- Systematically reviews the foundational principles, formal definitions, and core algorithmic families of both ==Deep Reinforcement Learning== and ==Evolution Strategies==.
- Conducts a detailed comparative analysis of their capabilities in ==parallelism==, ==exploration strategies==, adaptation to ==non-Markovian settings== (including ==meta-learning==), and ==multi-agent learning==.
- Explores existing ==hybrid algorithms== that combine DRL and ES elements and surveys their real-world applications across domains like gaming, robotics, and finance to offer practical insights.

## Results

- ES algorithms (e.g., **OpenAI-ES**) demonstrate high parallelizability with minimal communication overhead, achieving competitive performance to DRL in some tasks, while DRL's parallel architectures (e.g., **A3C**, **Ape-X**) are more complex due to parameter/gradient exchange.
- DRL employs diverse advanced exploration techniques (e.g., count-based, information gain, memory-based) effective for sparse rewards, whereas ESs primarily rely on mutation/recombination, augmented by noise injection and structured exploration, and can be combined with novelty search.
- The survey indicates DRL's broader application prevalence and research output across various domains compared to ESs, particularly in gaming and robotics, though both face challenges in real-world deployment and benefit from hybrid approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - DRL generally leverages gradient-based optimization and deep learning for sample efficiency and handling high-dimensional inputs, while ESs offer robust, gradient-free black-box optimization and inherent parallelism with minimal communication overhead.
> - Hybrid algorithms that integrate DRL and ES components can leverage their complementary strengths, often outperforming individual methods by combining DRL's learning efficiency with ES's robust exploration.
> - While DRL demonstrates significantly higher research activity and application dominance, both paradigms share common challenges such as sample efficiency, effective exploration in sparse reward settings, and the simulation-to-reality gap, indicating areas for future synergistic development.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly in sequential decision-making, has seen substantial advancements driven by two distinct paradigms: Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Evolution Strategies (ESs). Both approaches are inspired by natural intelligence, with DRL drawing parallels to animal learning and ESs mimicking biological evolution. Both DRL and ESs have independently achieved significant milestones, including surpassing human-level control in various complex tasks.

Despite their individual successes, each paradigm possesses inherent strengths and weaknesses, making them more or less suitable for different problem setups. The broader research landscape features numerous specialized surveys, such as those on derivative-free reinforcement learning (encompassing ESs), DRL for specific applications like autonomous driving, or challenges in multi-agent DRL systems. However, a comprehensive comparative analysis that systematically contrasts DRL and ESs across fundamental aspects and developmental directions has been less prominent.

This survey addresses this gap by providing a bird's-eye view comparison of DRL and ESs. It situates itself as a foundational resource for researchers and practitioners, aiming to clarify the capabilities and limitations of each approach. By examining their parallelism, exploration strategies, adaptation to dynamic and multi-agent environments, and potential for hybridization, the paper contributes to a more holistic understanding of these powerful techniques. This comparative perspective is crucial for informing algorithm selection, identifying opportunities for cross-pollination, and guiding future research towards the development of more robust and generally intelligent artificial systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this comparative survey stems from the parallel development and significant achievements of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Evolution Strategies (ESs) in complex sequential decision-making problems. Both fields are inspired by the biological phenomena of learning and evolution, which have fundamentally shaped natural intelligence. While DRL, an extension of Reinforcement Learning (RL), mimics how agents adapt to new situations through interaction and reward maximization, ESs are engineering methods inspired by natural selection, iteratively improving solutions by biasing search towards higher-performing individuals.

Despite their proven capabilities, the authors observe that significant open challenges persist in the pursuit of artificial general intelligence (AGI). Researchers and practitioners often face the dilemma of choosing the most appropriate algorithm for a given problem, a decision complicated by the distinct advantages and disadvantages of DRL and ESs. Existing literature often focuses on specific aspects or individual algorithms within DRL or ESs, lacking a comprehensive, integrated comparison of their broader developmental trajectories.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this paper are multi-faceted:

- **To provide fundamental concepts**: Detail the foundational principles, formal definitions, and core algorithmic families underlying DRL and ESs, ensuring a common understanding for comparison.
- **To analyze capabilities and limitations**: Systematically dissect the strengths and weaknesses of DRL and ESs across several critical dimensions of sequential decision-making.
- **To compare key aspects**: Conduct a direct comparison of DRL and ESs regarding:
 - **Scalability and Parallelism**: How effectively each approach can leverage distributed computation to accelerate learning.
 - **Exploration**: The mechanisms and effectiveness of their strategies for discovering optimal or near-optimal solutions in unknown environments, particularly in the face of sparse or deceptive reward signals.
 - **Adaptation to Dynamic Environments (Non-Markovian Settings & Meta-Learning)**: Their ability to operate in environments where the Markov property does not hold (e.g., partial observability) or to quickly adapt to new, unseen tasks (meta-learning).
 - **Multi-Agent Learning**: Their applicability and performance in scenarios involving multiple interacting agents, addressing challenges like non-stationarity and credit assignment.
- **To highlight hybrid algorithms**: Explore and synthesize existing research on hybrid approaches that combine elements of DRL and ESs, aiming to leverage the complementary strengths of both paradigms.
- **To survey real-world applications**: Provide an overview of the literature detailing applications supported by DRL and ESs, offering insights into their practical utility and comparative performance in various industry sectors.
- **To identify open challenges and future directions**: Outline unresolved issues and propose promising avenues for future research and development, contributing to the ongoing quest for more intelligent and adaptable AI systems.

By achieving these objectives, the survey aims to furnish the research community with a structured and insightful resource that facilitates informed decision-making and fosters interdisciplinary innovation between these two prominent AI methodologies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper employs a survey methodology, synthesizing existing literature on Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Evolution Strategies (ESs) to provide a comparative analysis. It does not present new empirical data or develop novel algorithms, but rather systematically reviews and contrasts established concepts, algorithms, and application trends.

The approach is structured into several sections, each building upon the previous one:

- **Fundamentals (Section II)**: The paper begins by establishing a foundational understanding of both DRL and ESs. For DRL, it defines Reinforcement Learning (RL) as an agent-environment interaction framed within a Markov Decision Process (MDP), detailing concepts like state-value functions, action-value functions, policies, and discount factors. It then introduces DRL as the integration of RL with deep neural networks (DNNs) to handle high-dimensional data, outlining value-based, policy-based, actor-critic, and model-based algorithmic families. For ESs, it describes them as population-based black-box optimization algorithms that do not require an MDP formulation or differentiable objective functions. The generic ES process, including initialization, parent selection, reproduction (recombination and mutation), evaluation, and survivor selection, is explained. This section concludes with a "Comparison" subsection summarizing the fundamental differences in objectives, problem setup, and search mechanisms.

- **Fundamental Algorithms (Section III)**: This section delves into specific, representative algorithms for both paradigms.
 - **DRL algorithms** covered include SARSA (on-policy, value-based), Q-learning (off-policy, value-based), REINFORCE (policy-based, stochastic gradient descent), and Deep Q-network (DQN) (combining Q-learning with CNNs for high-dimensional inputs, addressing instability with experience replay and target networks).
 - **ES algorithms** discussed are (1+1)-ES (simplest form with self-adaptation), (μ/ρ+, λ)-ES (population-based with recombination and mutation), Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) (a popular gradient-free optimizer adapting mean, stepsize, and covariance matrix), and Natural Evolution Strategies (NES) and OpenAI-ES (using gradients to update search distributions).
 A comparative table (Table I) highlights key characteristics like action space, memory consumption, limitations, and use of backpropagation.

- **Comparative Analysis of Capabilities (Section IV)**: This is the core comparative section, organized into detailed subsections:
 - **Parallelism (Section IV-A)**: Reviews parallel architectures for DRL (e.g., Gorila, A3C, Batched A2C, Ape-X, IMPALA, SEED) and for ESs (e.g., OpenAI-ES, NS-ES, TRES, PEL). The comparison focuses on communication bandwidth, synchronization, and hardware utilization.
 - **Exploration (Section IV-B)**: Examines exploration strategies in DRL (simple techniques like ε-greedy, UCB, TS; advanced methods like pseudo-count, approximate posterior sampling, information gain, memory-based methods) and ESs (recombination, mutation, noise injection, structured exploration, compact policies, surrogate gradients, adaptive subspaces, hybridization with novelty search).
 - **Non-Markov Settings (Section IV-C)**: Addresses challenges in partially observable environments and meta-reinforcement learning. It covers RNN-based and gradient-based DRL meta-learners (e.g., R2, DRL-meta, SNAIL, MAML, Reptile) and ES-based meta-learning algorithms (e.g., Evolvability ES, ES-MAML, IW-IES).
 - **Multi-Agent Settings (Section IV-D)**: Discusses multi-agent DRL (MADRL) systems, considering cooperative vs. competitive setups, centralized vs. decentralized control, and challenges like non-stationarity and credit assignment. It reviews MADRL modeling approaches (independent learning, fully observable critic, value function decomposition, learning to communicate). For multi-agent ESs, it highlights how ESs intrinsically handle non-stationarity and provides examples in robotics and credit assignment (e.g., PES-VD).
Each subsection on capabilities includes a "Comparison" part summarizing observations.

- **Hybrid Algorithms (Section V)**: This section focuses on approaches that combine DRL and ESs, illustrating how such hybridization leverages their complementary strengths. Examples include CEM-RL, Evolved Policy Gradients (EPG), MO-CMA-ES, and fine-tuned DRL policies.

- **Applications (Section VI)**: A survey of real-world applications is presented across six domains: Gaming, Robotics, Finance, Communications, Energy, and Transportation. The methodology for this section involved querying Google Scholar with specific keywords for DRL and ESs to gauge their adoption. This provides a quantitative and qualitative comparison of their application records.

- **Challenges and Future Research Directions (Section VII)**: The paper concludes by outlining persistent challenges such as sample efficiency, exploration-exploitation dilemma, sparse rewards, and the simulation-to-reality gap, suggesting future research avenues.

Throughout the report, "Comparison" subsections provide concise summaries of the insights gained from each major thematic review, adhering to a neutral and factual tone.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey systematically compares Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Evolution Strategies (ESs) across fundamental concepts, algorithmic characteristics, and advanced learning paradigms. Key findings are categorized as follows:

**Fundamental Concepts and Algorithms:**
- **Objective and Problem Setup**: DRL aims to maximize discounted cumulative rewards in environments typically modeled as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), often with a single agent improving its policy through interaction. ESs, as black-box optimization methods, optimize a fitness function for a population of candidate solutions, without requiring an MDP or differentiable objective.
- **Search Mechanism**: DRL algorithms (e.g., SARSA, Q-Learning, REINFORCE, DQN) primarily rely on gradient-based optimization and backpropagation to update neural network parameters. ESs (e.g., CMA-ES, NES, OpenAI-ES) are gradient-free, updating population parameters (e.g., mean, covariance of a search distribution) based on the fitness of sampled individuals.
- **Data Efficiency**: Value-based DRL algorithms (e.g., DQN with experience replay) can be data-efficient by reusing off-policy data. Policy-based DRL and ESs are generally data-inefficient as they often discard older data or entire populations in each iteration.
- **Computational Requirements**: ESs typically have lower computational requirements per iteration compared to DRL, as they do not involve backpropagation.

**Scalability and Parallelism:**
- Both DRL and ESs benefit significantly from parallel execution.
- **DRL Parallelism**: Distributed DRL architectures (e.g., Gorila, A3C, Ape-X, IMPALA, SEED) involve multiple actors gathering experiences and one or more learners optimizing a global network. Communication often involves exchanging network parameters or gradient vectors, which can demand high bandwidth. Asynchronous updates (A3C) can lead to inconsistencies, while synchronous batching (Batched A2C) increases communication costs.
- **ES Parallelism**: ES algorithms (e.g., OpenAI-ES) are highly amenable to parallelization due to their population-based nature. Workers evaluate individuals independently, and only scalar fitness values need to be exchanged between workers and a master node, resulting in minimal bandwidth requirements. OpenAI-ES showed competitive performance to DRL on Atari games, often with less wall-clock training time but high CPU hour demands.

**Exploration Strategies:**
- Both paradigms address the exploration-exploitation dilemma, with challenges in sparse or deceptive reward environments.
- **DRL Exploration**: Ranges from simple methods like ε-greedy to advanced techniques:
 - *Count-based methods*: Pseudo-count methods (CTS, PixelCNN) extend UCB-like exploration to high-dimensional state spaces.
 - *Approximate posterior sampling*: Bootstrapped DQN and UCB+InfoGain use ensembles of Q-functions to represent uncertainty and guide deep exploration.
 - *Information gain*: VIME and ICM reward agents for discovering novel states or improving their environmental model.
 - *Memory-based*: Episodic curiosity and Go-Explore leverage memory to revisit interesting states, particularly effective for hard exploration problems (e.g., Montezuma's Revenge). Agent57 uses a meta-controller to adapt exploration-exploitation tradeoffs.
- **ES Exploration**: Primarily driven by recombination and mutation, but can suffer from local optima. Enhanced methods include:
 - *Noise-based*: OpenAI-ES samples Gaussian noise for parameter perturbation, augmented with techniques like virtual batch normalization and fitness shaping.
 - *Structured exploration*: Methods employing random orthogonal directions or compact policy architectures (Choromanski et al.) to reduce dimensionality and improve search efficiency.
 - *Surrogate gradients*: Guided ES and ASEBO use correlated gradients or adaptively control subspace dimensionality for more efficient sampling.
 - *Hybrid with directed exploration*: NS-ES and QD-based algorithms combine ESs with novelty search or quality diversity to improve performance in sparse reward settings and avoid local optima.

**Adaptation to Non-Markov Settings (Meta-Reinforcement Learning):**
- **Partial Observability**: DRL typically uses recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to integrate past observations and estimate hidden states in Partially Observable MDPs (POMDPs).
- **Meta-RL**: Both DRL and ESs have adapted for meta-learning, aiming for policies that generalize across task distributions.
 - *DRL Meta-Learners*: Include RNN-based models (R2, DRL-meta, SNAIL) that learn a meta-policy through recurrent hidden states, and gradient-based models (MAML, Reptile) that learn optimal initial parameters for rapid adaptation with a few gradient steps.
 - *ES Meta-Learners*: Algorithms like Evolvability ES, ES-MAML, and IW-IES leverage ESs to address challenges in gradient-based meta-learning (e.g., second derivatives, high variance). They show competitive performance and can facilitate sim-to-real transfer.

**Multi-Agent Learning:**
- **DRL in Multi-Agent Systems (MADRL)**: Introduces challenges like non-stationarity (due to co-learning agents) and combinatorial complexity of state-action spaces.
 - *Approaches*: Independent learning (treats other agents as part of the environment), fully observable critic (MADDPG, where critics have global info during training), value function decomposition (VDN, QMIX, for cooperative credit assignment), and learning to communicate (RIAL, DIAL, CommNet).
- **ES in Multi-Agent Systems**: ESs intrinsically handle non-stationarity because they do not require an MDP formulation. This simplifies their extension to multi-agent domains. Examples include robotic swarm control and adversarial training. PES-VD combines parallel ESs with a Value Decomposition Network for effective multi-agent credit assignment.

**Hybrid DRL and ES Algorithms:**
- Hybrid approaches aim to combine DRL's sample efficiency and deep learning capabilities with ES's robust exploration and convergence.
- Examples include CEM-RL (combining cross-entropy method with DDPG/TD3), Evolved Policy Gradients (EPG, ES optimizing a loss function for DRL policy), MO-CMA-ES (for multi-objective DRL), and fine-tuning DRL policies with CMA-ES. These hybrids often demonstrate superior performance to either method alone.

**Applications:**
- Both DRL and ESs have found applications in Gaming (e.g., Atari, Go, StarCraft II), Robotics (e.g., navigation, manipulation, locomotion, swarm control), Finance (e.g., trading, portfolio optimization), Communications (e.g., network resource allocation, caching), Energy (e.g., smart grids), and Transportation (e.g., traffic control, fleet management).
- **DRL Application Dominance**: Google Scholar searches indicate significantly higher research activity and published papers for DRL across all surveyed domains compared to ESs. DRL excels in scenarios requiring scalable and adaptive behavior with high-dimensional inputs.
- **Simulation vs. Reality**: A common observation is that most high-performing DRL and ES algorithms are benchmarked in simulated environments, raising questions about their efficacy and the sim-to-real gap in real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This comparative survey holds significant implications for the fields of artificial intelligence and machine learning, offering value to both academic research and practical applications.

**For Research and Development:**
- **Informed Algorithm Selection**: The detailed comparison of DRL and ESs, across fundamental concepts, specific algorithms, and critical capabilities (parallelism, exploration, adaptation, multi-agent learning), provides researchers with a comprehensive framework for selecting the most appropriate methodology for new problems. This reduces redundant efforts and guides towards more effective solution designs.
- **Identification of Complementary Strengths**: By clearly articulating the strengths of DRL (e.g., sample efficiency via replay buffers, ability to handle high-dimensional observations with deep learning) and ESs (e.g., gradient-free nature, inherent parallelism, robustness to local optima, suitability for non-differentiable objectives and non-Markovian multi-agent settings), the survey highlights opportunities for synergistic integration.
- **Promotion of Hybrid Approaches**: The dedicated section on hybrid algorithms underscores a promising direction for future research. By demonstrating that combined approaches often outperform individual methods, the paper encourages further exploration into novel DRL-ES architectures that can mitigate individual weaknesses and leverage collective strengths, potentially leading to more powerful and generalizable AI systems.
- **Guidance for Addressing Open Challenges**: The identification and elaboration of open challenges (sample efficiency, exploration-exploitation, sparse rewards, sim-to-real gap) provide a clear roadmap for future research efforts. This focus can stimulate innovation in critical areas that are currently limiting the broad applicability of both DRL and ESs.
- **Fostering Interdisciplinary Insights**: By bridging two distinct paradigms, the survey encourages cross-pollination of ideas and techniques. For instance, DRL researchers might draw inspiration from ES's robust exploration strategies, while ES researchers might explore ways to integrate deep learning for feature extraction or model approximation to enhance sample efficiency.

**For Practical Applications:**
- **Enhanced System Design**: Engineers and practitioners working on real-world problems in domains like robotics, autonomous systems, finance, and telecommunications can use this report to make informed decisions about which algorithms, or combinations thereof, are best suited for their specific operational constraints and performance requirements.
- **Improved Performance and Robustness**: By understanding the trade-offs, practitioners can design systems that are not only high-performing but also robust to uncertainties and dynamic changes in complex environments, particularly in areas like multi-agent coordination or adapting to unseen tasks.
- **Addressing Real-World Constraints**: The discussions on sample efficiency and the sim-to-real gap are directly relevant to real-world deployments where data collection is expensive or risky. Insights into meta-learning and domain adaptation techniques become crucial for deploying AI agents effectively outside of simulated environments.
- **Future-Proofing AI Systems**: The report's forward-looking perspective on open challenges and future research directions helps anticipate and prepare for the next generation of AI capabilities, facilitating the development of more adaptable and intelligent systems.

In essence, this survey serves as a valuable synthesis of current knowledge, clarifying the landscape of DRL and ESs. Its significance lies in its potential to accelerate progress towards more capable, robust, and generally intelligent artificial agents by guiding research towards synergistic integrations and focused efforts on unresolved challenges.
