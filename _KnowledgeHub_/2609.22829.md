# Whole-Body UMI: Transferring UMI Manipulation Skills to Humanoid Whole-Body Manipulation via Real-Time Motion Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> Whole-Body UMI (WB-UMI) transfers handheld-gripper manipulation skills to a humanoid by decoupling task semantics from whole-body coordination through a shared end-effector interface. A diffusion policy predicts end-effector trajectories from wrist images; a task-agnostic flow-matching motion generator trained only on retargeted motion capture turns those trajectories into whole-body references tracked by a generalist controller, reaching 90% success on drawer closing.

## Problem

- End-effector trajectories alone underconstrain humanoid whole-body coordination, since the same hand path admits many postures and foot placements.
- Teleoperation gives paired image plus whole-body demonstrations but is laborious, costly, and hard to scale to diverse environments and tasks.
- Prior robot-free humanoid interfaces bolt body trackers onto the handheld device and still need task-specific whole-body data, hurting convenience and scalability.

## Method

- **WB-UMI**: ==autoregressive DiT== trained with ==masked conditional flow matching== on retargeted motion capture, generating fixed-length whole-body primitives conditioned on history plus end-effector pose and increments.
- Conditioning design pairs frame-aligned end-effector tracking targets with ==sparsely strided look-ahead context==, plus auxiliary state, ==forward-kinematics end-effector==, ==coordination==, and ==foot-skate losses==.
- **Three-layer asynchronous deployment**: 10 Hz diffusion policy, ~1 Hz generator, 50 Hz ==SONIC controller==, with observation-anchored targets, latency trim-and-blend, and measured-state history feedback.

## Results

- On unseen action categories the look-ahead context cuts end-effector position error from **5.77 cm** to **4.25 cm** and improves every reported metric including FID **0.0202**.
- Simulated shelf pick-and-place: end-effector orientation error **4.97 deg** and foot slip **0.96 cm/s** versus **38.89 deg** and **7.05 cm/s** for a directly commanded end-effector controller.
- Real **29-DoF** G1 over **10** trials per task: **90%** drawer closing, **80%** shelf pick-and-place, **30%** ball toss, **40%** loco-pick-and-place; latencies **64 ms** policy, **104 ms** generator.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A shared end-effector interface lets coordination priors be learned from generic motion capture, so task data collection never needs paired whole-body labels.
> - Sparse look-ahead of uncertain future targets conveys intent cheaply and improves generation more than treating all predictions as hard tracking targets.
> - Closing the loop with measured robot state trades slight velocity error for large orientation and reference-tracking gains; dynamic tasks still break on compounding errors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

The drawer-closing task asks a G1 humanoid to reach the handle and fully close the drawer through coordinated reaching and body lowering. A Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) records wrist-camera images and end-effector (EE) trajectories, which are the paths of the robot's hands. Those paths do not specify how the robot should position its torso, feet, or balance.

WB-UMI splits the problem in two. One learned system decides what the hands should do from UMI demonstrations. Another supplies plausible body motion for those hand targets. On the real robot, drawer closing succeeded in 9 of 10 trials. That is one task in a four-task evaluation with ten trials per task, and success was lower on ball toss and locomotion pick-and-place.

Author demo of the drawer task: reaching the handle and fully closing the drawer through coordinated reaching and body lowering. The clip is an illustrative example; the paper's ten-trial evaluation is the source of the success rate.

### 2. Two kinds of learning, one handoff

A diffusion policy (DP) learns task-specific hand plans from native UMI demonstrations. Separately, WB-UMI learns body coordination from retargeted G1 motion-capture data. It derives EE conditions from that data, so it does not require paired UMI images and whole-body trajectories. At use time, WB-UMI takes the DP's EE plan plus recent whole-body history and generates kinematic references. The pretrained SONIC whole-body controller (WBC) then tracks those references on the robot. Task semantics and body coordination meet through the EE interface.

Related systems pair task demonstrations with body data. [[2602.06643|HuMI]] records whole-body tracking data alongside task demonstrations. [[2605.03452|BifrostUMI]] uses VR-collected whole-body keypoints. WB-UMI instead preserves native EE-only UMI task collection. The pipeline still relies on a separate motion-capture prior and a whole-body controller.

The online generator predicts short motion segments from recent body history and aligned EE targets. It then generates the next segment using the previous segment's ending history. It also receives sparse samples of farther-ahead EE targets as look-ahead context. The described implementation uses eight history frames and predicts 32 future frames at 30 Hz.

Training and inference design. Retargeted mocap supplies paired whole-body motion and EE conditions. A DiT model processes masked flow state and EE context to predict velocity. Two generated primitives feed history updates and a resampled motion plan, and WBC execution feeds back into the loop.

### 3. What the tests establish

The central offline test is an EE look-ahead ablation. Both EE-only variants use the same data, backbone, and sampling procedure. The full method also gets sparse samples of the farther-ahead EE plan. The metric is EE position error: the distance between generated EE motion and the input EE trajectory during autoregressive rollout. On unseen semantic action categories, adding look-ahead reduces this error from 5.77 cm to 4.25 cm. The paper also reports lower error on all other listed generation metrics. This result comes from offline generation. It does not show that look-ahead causes real-robot task success.

The real-robot tests used the 29-DoF G1. Success rates were 9/10 for drawer closing, 8/10 for shelf pick-and-place, 3/10 for ball toss, and 4/10 for locomotion pick-and-place (Loco-PnP). Only the proposed hierarchy was tested on the physical robot, so these rates are not a baseline comparison.

The authors associate ball-toss failures with gripper-release latency and with DP errors during fast hand motion. For Loco-PnP, they suggest that execution drift may cause unfamiliar observations and worsening downstream predictions.

### 4. Where the Hand-Off Holds

EE-only demonstrations carry task intent. An independently learned motion prior supplies a body plan that the controller can execute. The results show this handoff can work on a real humanoid for these tested tasks. Drawer closing and shelf transfer gave the strongest reported results, and performance was not equally reliable across all four tasks.
