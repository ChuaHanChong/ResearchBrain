# COSMIC: Concurrent Optimization of Structure, Material, and Integrated Control for robotic systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed COSMIC, a computational framework that concurrently optimizes the structure, material distribution, and control policy for discrete truss-lattice robots. This framework consistently produced robotic designs with improved performance, such as a 66% increase in forward displacement over sequentially designed systems, and adapted morphologies and gaits to diverse tasks and environments.

## Problem

- Conventional robotic design relies on separate or sequential optimization of structure, materials, and control, resulting in suboptimal systems compared to co-evolved natural organisms.
- Achieving comprehensive co-design for dynamic robotic systems is difficult due to complex, high-dimensional design spaces, computationally expensive simulations, and highly non-convex optimization landscapes.
- Existing gradient-based co-design approaches primarily focus on continuum soft robots, struggling with the discrete variables and discontinuities inherent in discrete robotic systems.

## Method

- A unified, differentiable design representation was developed, relaxing discrete topology and material choices into continuous variables suitable for gradient-based optimization of truss-lattice robots.
- A GPU-parallel differentiable physics simulator was created, incorporating a high-fidelity Time of Impact (TOI) scheme to accurately model contact-rich dynamics and enable efficient gradient computation.
- A constrained optimization method concurrently designs structure, material distribution, and neural network control policies, employing Augmented Lagrangian for binarization and volume constraints, with an alternating update scheme.

## Results

- Co-designed robots consistently achieved superior locomotion performance, with one co-designed configuration demonstrating a 66% improvement in forward displacement (1.90 m) compared to a sequentially designed baseline (1.14 m).
- The framework generated diverse and adapted robotic designs, including a robot capable of upward incline locomotion (1.36 m horizontal, 0.35 m vertical displacement) and another that adapted its morphology for slippery ground by increasing contact area.
- Ablation studies revealed material layout as the most impactful design entity, contributing a 781% average increase in displacement, and confirmed that simultaneously optimizing all three entities yielded the highest performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Material layout, particularly actuation distribution, significantly impacts robot performance, demonstrating the largest individual contribution with a 781% average displacement increase in ablation studies.
> - Full co-design of structure, materials, and control results in synergistic performance improvements, with the combined effect exceeding the sum of individual contributions, such as an 87% increase in travel distance when control was co-evolved with morphology.
> - The framework autonomously generates specialized robotic designs and locomotion strategies that are robustly adapted to varying task objectives and environmental conditions, demonstrating emergent morphological adaptations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The design of autonomous robotic systems has traditionally involved the separate or sequential optimization of their constituent components: structure, materials, and control. This approach contrasts with natural biological systems, where these elements co-evolve to produce highly adapted and diverse organisms. The existing paradigm in robotics often leads to suboptimal designs and a limited understanding of how these design entities individually and collectively contribute to system performance. While advancements in soft robotics and responsive materials have expanded the design space, robotic morphologies typically remain narrower than the diversity observed in nature, and autonomous robots often exhibit less adaptivity compared to their natural counterparts.

A growing interest in co-design for robotic systems is driven by two main considerations. First, structure, materials, and control are physically coupled, leading to emergent collective effects in system dynamics that cannot be achieved by optimizing each entity independently. Second, co-design significantly expands the overall design space, increasing the probability of discovering superior optimal solutions that might be inaccessible through separated design processes.

However, the practical implementation of co-design presents several challenges. The design variables for different entities are often of varied types, resulting in large and potentially combinatorial design spaces. Evaluating a robot's dynamic performance, which involves complex phenomena such as contacts, friction, large deformations, and non-linear material behavior, is computationally intensive. Such evaluations often require extensive simulation time steps over long horizons, making exhaustive search methods unfeasible. Furthermore, the inherent physical coupling among design entities contributes to a highly non-convex, and potentially discontinuous, optimization landscape, where minor changes can lead to substantial and unpredictable shifts in performance. Consequently, comprehensive co-design has primarily been demonstrated for quasi-static systems, which are less computationally demanding and feature smoother objective functions.

Recent advancements in computing power, particularly GPU-parallel physics solvers, have significantly accelerated simulation evaluations for dynamic systems. Concurrently, the development of automatic differentiation techniques enables efficient gradient calculation over long temporal horizons, even in the presence of discontinuities, for a wide range of design variables. These technological developments have facilitated the emergence of gradient-based optimization frameworks for co-design, which reduce the number of simulations required compared to heuristic or exhaustive search methods. Many of these contemporary approaches often focus on continuum soft robots, as their continuous structural and material variations offer a more stable optimization landscape.

This work addresses a recognized gap in the co-design landscape: the application of comprehensive gradient-based platforms to discrete robotic systems. The discrete nature of such systems introduces mixed-variable design spaces, sharp discontinuities, and severe non-convexity, which pose greater challenges for gradient-based co-design. Discrete robots, however, have numerous applications, including legged robots and tensegrity structures. Additionally, the paper highlights a continued lack of clear understanding regarding the individual and collective contributions of different design entities to overall system performance. The presented research aims to develop a computational framework that tackles these challenges, providing a method for comprehensive co-design and contributing to a deeper understanding of the interplay between structure, materials, and control in discrete robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research is to advance robotic autonomy toward replicating and potentially surpassing the capabilities observed in natural organisms. The authors identify a fundamental limitation in conventional robotics design, where structure, materials, and control are typically developed in isolation or sequence. This segregated approach is contrasted with the co-evolutionary processes observed in nature, leading to suboptimal robotic designs and an incomplete understanding of how these design elements interact to influence system performance.

The research is specifically motivated by the challenges inherent in achieving comprehensive co-design for dynamic robotic systems. These challenges include:
- The diverse types of design variables (e.g., discrete topology, continuous material properties, neural network parameters) that create a vast and complex design space.
- The computational expense associated with accurately simulating dynamic robot performance, especially in contact-rich and non-linear environments.
- The highly non-convex and potentially discontinuous nature of the optimization problem due to the strong physical coupling between design entities.
- The limited applicability of existing gradient-based co-design frameworks to discrete robotic systems, which present more pronounced difficulties compared to continuum soft robots.
- The lack of systematic methods to quantitatively assess the individual and collective contributions of structure, material distribution, and control policy to a robot's overall performance.

To address these motivations, the study sets forth four key objectives:
1. **Develop a unified, differentiable design representation:** The framework aims to integrate mixed-type spatial topological variables, discrete material selection, and spatiotemporal neural control into a continuous, differentiable design space suitable for discrete truss-lattice robots. This representation is intended to facilitate efficient gradient-based optimization.
2. **Create a GPU-parallel differentiable simulator:** A critical objective is to develop a simulation environment that accurately models the physical interdependencies among design entities and their environment, particularly in contact-rich scenarios. This simulator must efficiently evaluate system dynamics and provide precise gradient information through automatic differentiation.
3. **Propose a constrained optimization method:** The framework seeks to implement a robust optimization approach that concurrently designs structure, materials, and control. This method must incorporate mechanisms to ensure structural integrity and physical feasibility, ultimately generating robotic designs for customized tasks that exceed the performance of those obtained via conventional, separated design strategies.
4. **Extract design insights:** A final objective is to leverage the co-design framework to uncover how individual and collective contributions of structures, materials, and control affect the system-level performance of robots, thereby enhancing fundamental understanding in robotic design.

The immediate application focus for demonstrating these objectives is the forward locomotion of a discrete truss-lattice robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, termed COSMIC, implements a gradient-based co-design approach for dynamic robotic systems. It concurrently optimizes the topology, material distribution, and control policy of a discrete truss-lattice robot by integrating a unified design representation, a differentiable physics simulator, and a constrained optimization scheme.

#### 3.1. Design Representation

The robot is modeled as a lattice structure composed of truss elements (edges) connected at mass nodes. Each edge can exist in one of three discrete states: void, skeleton, or actuator. To enable gradient-based optimization, this discrete choice is relaxed into a continuous three-dimensional vector, *z_ij_* ∈^3, for each edge *E_ij_*. This continuous vector is then projected to a normalized material state vector, *˜z_ij_*, using a scaled softmax function, where a scaling factor β controls the sharpness of the projection towards a one-hot encoding. An explicit binarization constraint is incorporated into the optimization to further drive these variables toward discrete states.

To interface with physical simulation, each material state (void, skeleton, actuator) is associated with specific mechanical properties, including stiffness (*k*), linear density (*ρ*), and maximum actuation strain (*a_max*). These properties are defined in a material property library. The effective property vector for each truss is computed as a weighted sum based on its *˜z_ij_* vector. For instance, void trusses have ultra-low stiffness and density; skeleton trusses provide high stiffness and medium density with no actuation; and actuator trusses are characterized by lower stiffness, higher density, and significant actuation capability. This scheme reflects material trade-offs in real systems. Node masses are determined by lumping the mass of connected trusses, with a baseline mass to prevent singularities and a larger mass for a designated payload node.

Actuation control is governed by a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) neural network. The MLP receives an input vector at each time step, *I(t)*, which includes synchronized clock signals from a Central Pattern Generator (CPG), nodal positional offsets relative to the robot's center, nodal velocity vectors, and a 2D goal position vector. The CPG signals ensure periodic, synchronized actuation. The MLP outputs a raw control signal, *u_ij_(t)* ∈ [-1, 1], for each edge. This signal is then modulated by the edge's assigned actuator material ratio (*˜z_ij,3_*) and maximum actuation strain (*a_max*) to produce the effective target strain, *ϵ_target,ij_(t)*. Only trusses in the actuator state receive a non-zero target strain.

#### 3.2. Design Evaluation

The system's dynamic performance is evaluated using a differentiable simulator. Truss forces are calculated based on a generalized Hooke's law, where the effective stiffness *k_ij_* and target actuation length *l_target,ij_(t)* (derived from *ϵ_target,ij_(t)*) determine the internal forces. These forces are integrated over a simulation time step (*∆t*) to compute nodal impulses. System dynamics are updated using symplectic Euler integration, which includes exponential frictional damping for atmospheric drag and internal energy dissipation, and accounts for gravity.

Crucially, the simulator incorporates a high-fidelity Time of Impact (TOI) scheme to handle ground contact. This scheme prevents interpenetration and minimizes gradient discontinuity. Upon contact, the exact TOI is linearly interpolated, and post-collision velocities are determined based on either an infinite friction model (zero-velocity boundary) or a Coulomb friction model (inelastic normal collision, tangential velocity penalized by dynamic friction coefficient *μ*). The entire simulation procedure forms a forward computational graph, enabling automatic differentiation to backpropagate gradients from the final state nodal displacement to all design variables (topology, materials) and neural network weights (control), which are then used for optimization.

#### 3.3. Design Optimization

The co-design problem is formulated as a constrained optimization aimed at maximizing the forward displacement of a designated payload node, which translates to minimizing the negative displacement *L_disp_ = -x_0(T)*.

Two primary categories of constraints are imposed:
1. **Volume Fraction Constraints:** Limits are placed on the total solid volume fraction (*V*) and the actuator volume fraction (*V_act*). These bounds are progressively relaxed by a small tolerance, *∆_V_*, during optimization. This relaxation allows intermediate material states to explore design possibilities and prioritizes task performance, while also mitigating potential physical instability caused by strict volume enforcement.
2. **Binarization Constraints:** These ensure that the continuous material state vectors, *˜z_ij,k_*, eventually converge to discrete one-hot encodings. A global binarization penalty (*g_bin_*) drives the L2 norm of the material state vector towards one, and a pairwise orthogonal penalty (*g_ortho_*) minimizes overlap between distinct material types. This adaptive approach is chosen over traditional density-based penalization (e.g., SIMP) to avoid severe instability in the highly non-convex landscape of discrete truss robots.

The optimization problem is solved using iterative gradient-based methods. Binarization constraints are handled using the Augmented Lagrangian (AL) method, which allows for occasional constraint violations to facilitate exploration, with adaptively adjusted penalty strengths. The augmented objective function *L(Z, λ, τ)* incorporates *L_disp_* and penalties for constraint violations. Lagrangian multipliers (*λ*) and penalty weights (*τ*) are dynamically updated.

Material design vectors (*Z*) are initialized uniformly and updated using the Method of Moving Asymptotes (MMA) optimizer, which takes the augmented objective and gradients from the differentiable simulator. MLP controller weights (*θ*) are initialized using Xavier Initialization and updated with the Adam optimizer. To balance the co-evolution of morphology and control, *Z* and *θ* are updated alternately at a 3:2 ratio.

To address challenges posed by intermediate material states and ensure structural integrity, an auxiliary "stability pass" is implemented. This pass evaluates a highly sharpened proxy geometry (*z*^*^*), generated using a centered softmax with a much higher β_stab_, to provide a high-fidelity approximation of the final discrete structure. The resulting displacement *L_stab_* serves as an auxiliary objective, with gradients computed using a Straight-Through Estimator (STE) to mitigate vanishing gradients. Stability passes are interleaved with "performance passes" (using the standard β).

In later stages of optimization, an iterative vector attraction phase nudges design vectors towards their dominant discrete material state if a confidence threshold is met, accelerating binarization. Both the confidence threshold and nudge strength are linearly annealed. Finally, near the end of the optimization, a hard discretization "snap" enforces a strictly binarized topology and material layout, which are then frozen. The neural controller parameters (*θ*) are then fine-tuned alone for the remaining iterations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents findings across three main areas: the impact of initial design, the individual and collective contributions of design entities, and the framework's generality to diverse tasks and environments.

#### 4.1. Effect of Design Initialization

The framework's ability to navigate the design space and the benefits of co-design were investigated by comparing optimized designs against a sequential design baseline and across different initializations.
- **Sequential Design Baseline (Figure 6a):** A three-legged robot with a fixed vertical actuation layout, optimized only for control, achieved a final horizontal displacement of 1.14 m. Its fixed structure led to restricted limb strokes and gait instability.
- **Co-Design with Stability Heuristic (Figure 6b):** Initiated with a bias towards void for higher trusses to prevent collapse. The optimization process, driven by Augmented Lagrangian binarization penalties, rapidly transitioned most design vectors to discrete states. Iterative attraction resolved persistent intermediate states. The final design adopted a propulsive leaping gait, primarily using a large back leg for thrust and a smaller front leg for traction. This co-designed robot achieved 1.90 m displacement, representing a 66% improvement over the baseline.
- **Co-Design with Three-Legged Heuristic (Figure 6c):** Initialized to resemble the baseline's three-legged configuration. The co-design process concentrated actuators in the middle leg, enabling sweeping motions and longer strides. It achieved 1.87 m displacement, demonstrating that co-design consistently improves performance even when starting from a similar initial configuration to a sub-optimal baseline.
- **Co-Design with Heuristics-Free Initialization (Figure 6d):** Starting with uniform material ratios, the framework successfully discovered a viable design. This robot adopted a hopping gait, utilizing a front leg for support and allocating compliant actuators near the back leg payload. It achieved 0.98 m displacement, underperforming heuristic-guided designs but demonstrating the framework's robustness in finding valid locomotion strategies without prior guidance. Notably, it autonomously discovered structural features (e.g., disconnected bottom trusses, low center of gravity) similar to the stability-heuristic design.

These results indicate that co-design consistently produces higher-performing locomotion strategies compared to sequential design, even when starting from similar initial configurations. While performance is influenced by initialization, the framework can identify viable solutions without strong prior heuristics.

#### 4.2. Individual and Collective Contribution of Design Entities

An ablation study was conducted by selectively enabling or disabling control, topology, and material layout design, resulting in eight combinatorial trials (Figures 7 and 8).
- **Material Layout (Actuation Layout):** This entity demonstrated the largest individual contribution to performance, increasing average displacement by 781% compared to conditions where it was not designed. This highlights its significant impact and adaptability to varied initial conditions.
- **Topological Design:** While less impactful on its own (116% improvement), topology acted as a multiplier for material design, consistently increasing traversal distance when combined with other enabled entities (e.g., comparing Trial 7e vs. 7g, and 7f vs. 7h).
- **Control Pattern Design:** Surprisingly, control alone showed a small individual contribution and occasionally led to negative performance changes (Trial 7c vs. 7d). This is attributed to limitations imposed by suboptimal structures and material distributions, which restrict mobility and potentially lead to noisy gradients from ground contacts. However, when combined with material layout optimization, control consistently enhanced traversal (Trial 7e vs. 7f, 7g vs. 7h).
- **Collective Contribution:** The most significant finding was the synergistic effect of fully co-designing all three entities. When control was co-evolved alongside topology and material layout (Trial 7h), the travel distance increased by 87% compared to the trial without control optimization (Trial 7g, from 1.02 m to 1.91 m). This indicates that the benefits of joint optimization surpass those achieved by optimizing entities in isolation.

The study observed emergent, topology-dependent material distributions and locomotion strategies. For instance, fully-filled topologies (Trials 7e, 7f) concentrated actuators for traction, while freely evolved topologies (Trials 7g, 7h) developed a convergent central motif of a horizontal actuator for enhanced leg coordination. This emphasizes the strong coupling between material and topology, demonstrating that optimal performance requires their joint design.

#### 4.3. Generality to Other Tasks and Environments

The framework's flexibility was demonstrated across varying objectives, design spaces, and environmental parameters (Figure 9).
- **Upward Incline Locomotion (Figure 9a):** For a goal on a 15-degree incline, the robot evolved a slimmer structure to prioritize a low center of mass, mitigating toppling forces. It adopted an arching motion, combining hopping and crawling strategies, and slightly undercut volume bounds to reduce weight for upward movement, achieving 1.36 m horizontal and 0.35 m vertical displacement.
- **Smaller Design Space (Figure 9b):** When tested on a 5x5 nodal grid (smaller than the standard 6x6), the framework successfully generalized. It rediscovered the leg division strategy (front and rear legs) seen in other designs and employed a front-leg assisted leaping gait, achieving 1.10 m traversal distance.
- **Slippery Ground Contact (Figure 9c):** Under a Coulomb friction model (μ = 2.5), the robot exhibited distinct morphological adaptation. Instead of lifting the payload, it kept the heavy payload in contact with the ground for anchorage against skidding. It also folded its front leg inward to increase contact area, functioning as a friction pad. This represents a beneficial morphological transformation adapted to environmental physics.

These results collectively demonstrate the framework's adaptability and generalizability to different task requirements and environmental conditions, producing tailored robotic designs and effective motion patterns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by proposing a novel computational framework, COSMIC, for the concurrent optimization of robot structure, material distribution, and control policy. The development of a unified, differentiable design representation that seamlessly integrates mixed-variable spatial topology, discrete material selection, and spatiotemporal neural control is a foundational contribution. This approach enables the application of efficient gradient-based optimization methods to the complex challenge of co-designing discrete robotic systems, a domain where such methods have previously faced significant hurdles.

The framework's ability to consistently discover high-performance solutions that surpass those obtained through conventional sequential design processes, even with simple initializations, highlights its practical utility. By expanding the accessible morphological design space, the framework facilitates the generation of diverse and adaptable robotic designs tailored to specific tasks and environmental conditions, as demonstrated by the emergence of varied locomotion strategies (e.g., leaping, sweeping, hopping gaits) and adaptations to inclines or slippery surfaces.

Furthermore, this work provides empirical insights into the individual and collective contributions of different design entities to robotic locomotion. The finding that material layout plays the most critical role, capable of significantly reshaping robot dynamics, offers guidance for future design efforts. While control alone may be bounded by underlying structural limitations, the study conclusively shows that the synergistic interaction among structure, materials, and control yields performance gains exceeding the sum of individual contributions. The observation of convergent structural motifs across designs with similar objectives also suggests the presence of underlying form-function relationships that can be systematically uncovered through this co-design approach.

The potential impact of this framework is broad. It establishes a computational foundation for the autonomous co-design of robotic systems capable of complex autonomous behaviors like reconfiguration and locomotion. By enabling the automated generation of tailored robotic designs, the approach could accelerate the development of specialized robotic material systems. Potential applications extend to diverse fields, including bio-hybrid robots, mechanical computing systems, and more.

While the authors acknowledge limitations such as sensitivity to initialization, reliance on a simplified simulator, and the potential for integrating advanced reinforcement learning strategies, the proposed framework represents a significant step towards creating robots that embody the highly integrated and co-evolved characteristics observed in natural organisms. Future work addressing these limitations can further enhance the fidelity, robustness, and generalizability of this promising co-design paradigm.
