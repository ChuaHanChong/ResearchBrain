# NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT) introduces a supervised learning method that enables large language models to learn from self-generated incorrect answers in math reasoning tasks. This approach achieves competitive performance with reinforcement learning algorithms while offering a more memory-efficient alternative for autonomous model improvement.

## Problem

- Supervised Learning (SL) traditionally struggles with verification-driven training for Large Language Models (LLMs) due to its reliance on positive examples and lack of mechanisms to learn from negative feedback.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been the dominant framework for LLM self-improvement via binary verifiers, perceived as uniquely capable of utilizing mistakes for autonomous growth.
- Existing SL baselines like Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) discard self-generated incorrect answers, preventing LLMs from reflecting on failures and limiting their self-improvement capacity.

## Method

- **NFT** constructs an '==implicit negative policy==' by re-parameterizing it using the target positive policy and the observed correctness rates of the original LLM's generations.
- It optimizes a combined maximum-likelihood objective function that leverages both correct and incorrect self-generated answers to refine the positive policy.
- The method incorporates practical enhancements such as token-level loss calculation, clipping the negative likelihood ratio for stability, and prompt weighting to prioritize more challenging questions during training.

## Results

- NFT achieved an average accuracy of **51.7%** on math benchmarks with **7B** models, matching or slightly outperforming state-of-the-art RL methods like DAPO (**51.2%**).
- On **32B** models, NFT reached **59.2%** accuracy, substantially surpassing the Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) baseline's **52.8%** by effectively incorporating negative data.
- Utilizing prompt weighting schemes that prioritize harder questions, such as ω(q)=(1−r^q)/r^qω(q) = √((1-r_q)/r_q)ω(q)=(1−r^q)/r^q, consistently improved performance, demonstrating the impact of targeted learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - NFT reveals a theoretical equivalence between supervised learning and Group Reward Policy Optimization (GRPO) in strict-on-policy training, suggesting a deeper connection between SL and RL paradigms.
> - Learning from self-generated negative examples is crucial for LLM self-improvement, providing significant performance gains over positive-only learning and fostering exploratory behavior.
> - The ability to leverage negative feedback becomes increasingly important for larger models, where the performance gap between RFT and NFT widens more rapidly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning" addresses a contemporary challenge in the training of Large Language Models (LLMs) for complex reasoning tasks, particularly in mathematics. Recent advancements in LLMs' mathematical abilities have largely been attributed to a paradigm shift from traditional imitation learning, which relies heavily on human-annotated reference answers, to self-improvement through verification-driven training. This new paradigm necessitates only a question dataset and a binary verifier to judge the correctness of self-generated answers, allowing LLMs to autonomously improve their performance without continuous reliance on external teachers. This approach not only alleviates the high cost of data annotation but also removes potential competence ceilings imposed by human-provided supervision.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a prominent framework for this verification-driven training. Algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Reward Policy Optimization (GRPO) are designed to maximize reward signals, which can be directly derived from binary verifier outcomes. In contrast, Supervised Learning (SL) has typically been considered less suitable for this self-reflective learning paradigm. The common understanding is that SL primarily focuses on memorizing positive data and lacks an inherent mechanism to learn from negative feedback or mistakes, which is deemed crucial for autonomous improvement. Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT), an SL baseline, exemplifies this limitation by discarding all self-generated incorrect answers and only fine-tuning on correct ones, thereby preventing the model from reflecting on its failures.

This research challenges the prevailing notion that self-improvement in LLMs, especially from negative feedback, is exclusive to RL. It proposes a supervised learning alternative that aims to incorporate learning from mistakes, thereby bridging the perceived theoretical and practical gap between SL and RL in binary-feedback learning systems. The work seeks to demonstrate that the benefits of verification-driven training can be realized within an SL framework, potentially offering a simpler and more memory-efficient approach compared to complex RL algorithms while maintaining competitive performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT), a supervised learning approach that enables Large Language Models (LLMs) to learn from their self-generated incorrect answers and improve autonomously in math reasoning tasks. This objective is driven by several motivations:

Firstly, the authors aim to **challenge the prevailing notion that verification-driven training is exclusive to Reinforcement Learning (RL)**. While RL algorithms like PPO and GRPO have been central to the recent advancements in LLM math abilities by leveraging binary verifier signals, Supervised Learning (SL) has largely been overlooked for this purpose due to its perceived inability to reflect on mistakes and its reliance on positive reference answers. The research seeks to demonstrate that self-improvement through reflection on failures can be achieved within an SL paradigm.

Secondly, a key motivation is to **overcome the limitations of existing supervised learning baselines, specifically Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT)**. RFT, while effective in some contexts, discards all self-generated negative answers, preventing the LLM from learning from its failures. The authors posit that the ability to reflect on one's own mistakes is critical for achieving general intelligence, representing a shift from mere imitation to self-reflective learning. NFT is designed to directly address this limitation by actively leveraging negative feedback.

Thirdly, the research aims to **enable more efficient and robust policy optimization by utilizing the full spectrum of self-generated data (both positive and negative)**. By constructing an implicit negative policy parameterized by the same positive LLM, NFT allows for direct policy optimization on all LLM generations. This approach avoids the need for external teachers or costly human annotations, aligning with the broader trend of autonomous LLM improvement.

Finally, the authors are motivated to **conduct an in-depth comparison between NFT and leading RL approaches like GRPO and DAPO, not only empirically but also theoretically**. They seek to understand the underlying connections between these seemingly disparate frameworks. A specific motivation is to investigate whether an equivalence exists between SL and RL in strict-on-policy training settings, potentially bridging theoretical gaps and offering insights into the mechanisms of self-improvement in LLMs. This theoretical understanding could lead to simplified or unified approaches for developing powerful reasoning models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT) is developed as an online learning algorithm designed to enable LLMs to learn from both correct and incorrect self-generated answers in verification-driven training scenarios.

**Problem Setup:**
The process begins with a dataset of math questions, a pretrained LLM (denoted as $\pi_{old}$), and a binary verifier that judges the correctness of generated answers. In each iteration, the LLM generates $K$ answers for each question, and these answers are verified. The generated data is then split into two subsets: $D^+$ containing all correct answers and $D^-$ comprising all incorrect ones. The learning target is to optimize the old policy $\pi_{old}$ into a new policy $\pi_{\theta}^+$ that approximates $\pi(a|q, r=1)$, where $r=1$ signifies a correct answer.

**Addressing the Limitations of Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT):**
RFT, a baseline supervised learning approach, addresses the problem by solely fine-tuning on the $D^+$ dataset and discarding $D^-$. While effective in reinforcing correct behavior, RFT prevents any learning from negative feedback. NFT is introduced to overcome this by incorporating negative data into the learning process.

**Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers:**
The core of NFT lies in leveraging negative data to optimize the target positive policy $\pi_{\theta}^+$. The authors formalize a negative policy $\pi^-(a|q) := \pi(a|q, r=0)$. A key observation is a coupling relationship between the positive policy $\pi^+$, the negative policy $\pi^-$, and the original generation policy $\pi_{old}$:
$r_q \pi^+(a|q) + [1 - r_q] \pi^-(a|q) = \pi_{old}(a|q)$
where $r_q$ is the correctness rate of $\pi_{old}$ for question $q$, estimated as the mean of $K$ rewards.

To utilize this coupling, NFT constructs an "implicit negative policy," $\pi_{\theta}^-$, by re-parameterizing it using the target positive policy $\pi_{\theta}^+$ and the observed $r_q$:
$\pi_{\theta}^-(a|q) := \frac{\pi_{old}(a|q) - r_q \pi_{\theta}^+(a|q)}{1 - r_q}$
The method then applies a maximum-likelihood objective to train this implicit negative policy on negative answers ($D^-$). A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) demonstrates that, under ideal conditions, optimizing this objective leads to $\pi_{\theta}^+(a|q) = \pi^+(a|q)$.

The full NFT objective combines training on both positive and negative data. A baseline term, $-\log \pi_{old}(a|q)$, is subtracted from the loss, which does not affect gradients but helps initialize the loss to zero. The final NFT loss function is expressed as:
$L_{NFT}(\theta) = r [-\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}^+(a|q)}{\pi_{old}(a|q)}] + (1-r) [-\log \frac{\pi_{old}(a|q) - r_q \pi_{\theta}^+(a|q)}{1 - r_q}]$
NFT is stated to be memory-efficient, requiring only a single model copy and pre-computation of $\pi_{old}(a|q)$ likelihoods. It is also applicable to continuous reward settings.

**Practical Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
Several practical improvements are incorporated into the final algorithm:
1. **Token-level loss:** To address high variance and numerical instabilities associated with sequence-level likelihoods, the loss is summed over individual token decisions.
2. **Clipping negative likelihood ratio:** The argument of the logarithm in the negative loss must remain positive. To prevent training collapse, a minimum value $\epsilon > 0$ is enforced on the negative likelihood ratio using a `max_v` operator with straight-through gradient. This prevents overly aggressive penalization for mistakes.
3. **Prompt weighting:** Each prompt $q$ is weighted by $\omega(q)$ to prioritize more informative instances, such as hard questions with low correctness rates $r_q$. Common weighting schemes include $\omega(q)=1$, $\omega(q)=1-r_q$, or $\omega(q)=\sqrt{(1-r_q)/r_q}$.

**Understanding the Gap Between NFT and GRPO:**
The paper also provides a theoretical comparison between NFT and GRPO by analyzing their loss gradients. It is shown that with a specific prompt weighting $\omega(q) = \sqrt{(1-r_q)/r_q}$, the "normalized advantage" term characteristic of GRPO is implicitly present in NFT's loss function. Proposition 4.2 demonstrates that NFT and GRPO are gradient-equivalent in strict-on-policy training settings ($R_t^{\theta}(q,a) = 1$), despite their different theoretical derivations. The primary difference arises in off-policy settings, where they employ distinct strategies for clipping model gradients when the learned policy deviates from the old policy.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on 7B and 32B Qwen models using the DAPO-Math-17k dataset, which consists of math questions with integer ground-truth answers. Models are fine-tuned for approximately 5,000 gradient steps. Evaluation is performed on six validation benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023, MATH500, OlympiadBench, and Minerva Math. Baselines include Iterative DPO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO, DAPO, and RFT.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on 7B and 32B Qwen models in math reasoning tasks yielded several key findings regarding NFT's performance, the benefits of negative data, and the impact of specific design choices.

#### 4.1. NFT Performance Evaluation

- **Competitive Performance:** NFT-7B-Zero, a model fine-tuned using NFT, achieved competitive performance across various benchmarks (AIME24, MATH500, OlympiadBench, Minerva Math, etc.) when compared to other zero-style 7B math models in the Qwen series. This suggests that a supervised learning approach can effectively drive verification-driven training in math tasks.
- **Matching/Surpassing RL Baselines:** When benchmarked against state-of-the-art online fine-tuning algorithms using identical training data and infrastructure, NFT demonstrated performance that matched or even surpassed leading Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms such as GRPO and DAPO. On 7B models, NFT achieved an average accuracy of 51.7%, slightly outperforming DAPO's 51.2%. On 32B models, NFT achieved 59.2%, just below DAPO's 59.9% but significantly higher than RFT's 52.8%.
- **Stability and Convergence:** Training curves across multiple runs indicated that NFT exhibited convergence speed and final performance on par with DAPO, suggesting robust training stability.

#### 4.2. Benefits of Negative Data

- **Significant Performance Gain:** NFT consistently outperformed the Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) baseline by a clear margin across both 7B and 32B models. This finding highlights the substantial benefit of incorporating negative feedback into the training process, rather than discarding incorrect generations. For example, on 7B models, NFT improved average accuracy from 48.3% (RFT) to 51.7%.
- **Enhanced Exploration:** Analysis of entropy curves showed a divergence in training dynamics. While RFT tended to reduce policy entropy over time, NFT, similar to RL methods like DAPO, encouraged increasing entropy. This suggests that leveraging negative feedback in NFT promotes a more exploratory behavior, which is generally considered beneficial for robust learning.
- **Increased Importance for Larger Models:** The performance gap between RFT and NFT widened more rapidly over training iterations in 32B experiments compared to 7B. This observation implies that the ability to learn from negative data becomes increasingly crucial as model scale increases, potentially because larger models already possess strong memorization capabilities, making reflection on mistakes a more prominent bottleneck for further improvement.
- **RFT as a Strong Baseline:** Despite being surpassed by NFT and RL methods, RFT contributed a significant portion of the performance gain. In 32B settings, learning from positive data (RFT) accounted for approximately 80% of the total gain achieved by the best-performing model, with negative data contributing the remaining 20%. This indicates that while negative feedback is valuable, learning from positive examples remains a fundamental and highly effective component.

#### 4.3. Ingredients Behind NFT's Effectiveness

- **Prioritizing Harder Questions (Prompt Weighting):** The empirical design choice of assigning higher weights ($\omega(q)$) to difficult questions (those with a low answer correctness rate, $\hat{r}_q$) improved model performance. Specifically, weighting schemes like $\omega(q) = 1-\hat{r}_q$ (aligning with Dr. GRPO) or $\omega(q) = \sqrt{(1-\hat{r}_q)/\hat{r}_q}$ (aligning with GRPO) performed similarly and both outperformed constant weighting ($\omega(q)=1$). This suggests that focusing on challenging examples during training is beneficial.
- **Avoiding Overpenalization of Mistakes:** The clipping value $\epsilon$ for the negative likelihood ratio in NFT's loss function proved crucial. An overly aggressive penalization (i.e., setting $\epsilon$ close to zero, which allows for very high penalties to rising likelihoods of incorrect answers) was found to degrade overall performance. A default setting of $\epsilon = 1.0$ was adopted, suggesting a balance in penalizing mistakes is more effective than extremely harsh penalties.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT) carries significant implications for the field of Large Language Model (LLM) training, particularly in areas requiring complex reasoning such as mathematics.

Firstly, the most significant contribution is its **bridging of supervised learning (SL) and reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms** in the context of verification-driven training. Historically, self-improvement capabilities, especially learning from negative feedback, have been almost exclusively attributed to RL. NFT demonstrates that SL can achieve similar ends, challenging a long-held notion and opening new avenues for algorithm design. This is particularly impactful as SL methods are often simpler to implement and debug compared to the complexities of many RL algorithms.

Secondly, the **unveiling of a theoretical equivalence between NFT and GRPO in strict-on-policy training** is a crucial theoretical finding. This equivalence, despite their entirely different theoretical foundations, suggests a deeper underlying connection between SL and RL. It indicates that certain "advantage normalization" characteristics, previously considered unique to RL algorithms like GRPO, are implicitly present within NFT's supervised loss function. This insight could lead to a more unified understanding of learning dynamics in binary-feedback systems and inspire the development of hybrid or more generalized algorithms.

Thirdly, NFT provides a **memory-efficient and effective method for LLMs to achieve autonomous self-improvement**. By directly leveraging self-generated negative examples through an implicit negative policy, NFT offers a concrete mechanism for LLMs to reflect on their failures without requiring external teachers or costly human annotations. This aligns with the push for more autonomous and data-efficient training paradigms, reducing reliance on resource-intensive human feedback loops.

Fourthly, the empirical results demonstrate that NFT can **match or even surpass the performance of leading RL algorithms like GRPO and DAPO** in math reasoning tasks. This competitive performance, achieved within a supervised learning framework, suggests that the "performance gap" between SL and RL in online training largely stemmed from SL's inability to effectively use negative feedback, rather than an inherent superiority of RL. NFT mitigates this gap, offering a potentially simpler yet powerful alternative.

Finally, the work emphasizes the **critical importance of learning from mistakes for achieving general intelligence**. By demonstrating that actively incorporating negative feedback significantly enhances performance and encourages exploration (as indicated by increased policy entropy), NFT reinforces the idea that true intelligence involves not just reinforcing correct actions but also intelligently adapting from incorrect ones. This could guide future research towards developing more robust and adaptable LLMs that can continuously refine their reasoning abilities.

In summary, NFT represents a notable advance by demonstrating the power of negative feedback within a supervised learning framework, bridging a significant conceptual and practical divide between two major machine learning paradigms, and offering a promising direction for developing more capable and autonomous LLMs.
