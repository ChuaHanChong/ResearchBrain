# SkillX: Automatically Constructing Skill Knowledge Bases for Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> SkillX automatically builds a plug-and-play, three-tier skill knowledge base (planning, functional, atomic skills) distilled from a strong backbone agent's rollouts, then iteratively merges, filters, and expands it via experience-guided exploration. Retrieved through pseudo-plan rewriting and injected once into the system prompt, the library lifts weaker agents by roughly 10 points on AppWorld, BFCL-v3, and tau2-Bench while cutting execution steps.

## Problem

- Self-evolving agents learn in isolation, repeatedly re-extracting near-identical experience from the same tasks, which wastes exploration budget and yields redundant memories.
- Scarce high-quality training data in complex tool environments makes mined insights, workflows, or trajectories generalize poorly to unseen tasks.
- Experience harvested only from an agent's own reflection is capped by that agent's capability frontier, and long-context progressive-disclosure skill formats demand heavy sandboxing and multi-round interaction.

## Method

- **Multi-Level Skills Design** decomposes trajectories into planning skills (subtask ordering and dependencies), functional skills (tool-composition macro-operations with name/document/content fields), and atomic skills (enriched single-tool specs).
- **Iterative Skills Refinement** applies a text-based optimization operator: ==embedding-clustered== **Skills Merge** aggregates similar skills as multiple update directions, then a general filter plus tool-schema filter rejects non-portable or hallucinated skills.
- **Exploratory Skills Expansion** targets under-invoked and failure-prone tools to collect new rollouts, synthesizes fresh tasks from them, and reruns extraction; at inference, a rewritten ==pseudo-plan== drives per-step skill retrieval.

## Results

- Qwen3-32B rises from **53.67** to **63.67** Avg@4 on BFCL-v3 and **53.75** to **66.87** on tau2-Bench Retail; Kimi-K2 gains **46.88** to **56.40** Avg@4 on AppWorld.
- SkillX beats A-Mem, AWM, and ExpeL under both self-extraction and GLM-4.6 distillation, and lifts GPT-4.1 BFCL-v3 Avg@4 from **49.66** to **60.00** with GLM-extracted skills.
- Ablations show refinement helps to iteration 2 (AppWorld **62.35** to **64.29**) then overfits at iteration 3 (**61.46**); experience-guided expansion yields more novel skills and **88.69** Pass@4 versus random exploration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Representation format matters more than extractor strength: strong-model distillation helps only when the experience is encoded in a transferable, itemized hierarchy rather than raw workflows or trajectories.
> - Optimal skill granularity is model-dependent; weak models benefit most from planning skills alone and can over-imitate retrieved functional skills instead of adapting them.
> - Text-based library optimization overfits with scarce seed data, so iteration count needs tuning, and skills tied to tool schemas transfer poorly across different tool ecosystems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents has shown progress in managing long-horizon decision-making tasks, including API calling, web navigation, and interactive assistance. Despite these advancements, many agents still initiate new tasks without leveraging past experiences, relying on direct reasoning or limited task-specific demonstrations. This approach is characterized by high cost, fragility, and a lack of cumulative learning.

Recent research has explored "self-evolving" agents that iteratively reflect on past executions to improve behavior. However, these methods often encounter limitations such as isolated learning, where agents repeatedly rediscover similar behaviors; weak generalization, where learned experiences do not transfer effectively to novel tasks or environments; and a model capability bottleneck, where the quality of extracted experience is constrained by the agent's inherent capabilities.

Previous attempts to represent agent experience have included insights, workflows, or raw trajectories. The authors argue that these representations generally lack strong transferability, efficient retrieval mechanisms, or direct executability. Inspired by existing skill-based designs, which often suffer from demands related to long context windows and complex environment instrumentation, this work positions itself by proposing a novel, hierarchical, and itemized skill representation. SkillX aims to address the aforementioned limitations by creating a plug-and-play skill knowledge base that is reusable across diverse agents and environments, thereby advancing generalizable agent learning. This approach integrates elements of static knowledge base construction with guided exploration, a notable contribution given the challenges of task scarcity in complex agent settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop SkillX, a fully automated framework for constructing a plug-and-play skill knowledge base from agent experience. This framework is designed to enable the reuse of learned competencies across various agents and environments, thereby enhancing their performance and efficiency.

The motivation for SkillX stems from several identified limitations within prevailing self-evolving agent paradigms:

- **Isolated Learning**: Current agents often learn in isolation, repeatedly re-discovering similar behaviors from limited individual experiences. This leads to redundant exploration and inefficient use of computational resources. SkillX aims to mitigate this by creating a centralized, reusable skill library.
- **Weak Generalization of Experience**: In complex environments, high-quality training data for agents can be scarce. Consequently, experiences mined by individual agents frequently exhibit poor transferability to new tasks or different environmental contexts. SkillX addresses this by designing a hierarchical skill representation intended to be concise, composable, and robust to distributional shifts, thereby improving generalization.
- **Model Capability Bottleneck**: When agents rely solely on their own exploration and reflection to harvest experience, the quality and scope of extractable knowledge are inherently limited by the agent's current capabilities. SkillX proposes to use a strong backbone agent for initial skill extraction, allowing weaker base agents to benefit from distilled, higher-quality knowledge.

In response to these challenges, SkillX is motivated to provide a form of experience representation—structured, hierarchical skills—that simultaneously offers strong transferability, efficient retrieval, and direct executability, contrasting with prior experience representations that the authors contend do not fully satisfy these criteria.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SkillX framework operates through a fully automated, iterative pipeline designed to construct and refine a reusable skill knowledge base. The core methodology integrates three synergistic innovations: Multi-Level Skills Design, Iterative Skills Refinement, and Exploratory Skills Expansion. The framework typically utilizes a strong backbone agent for initial experience generation and skill distillation.

**Multi-Level Skills Design**
SkillX structures skills into a three-tiered hierarchy:
- **Planning Skills ($S_{plan}$)**: These skills capture high-level task organization. They are extracted from successful trajectories by compressing them into an ordered set of high-level steps, filtering out non-essential transitions (e.g., exploration, backtracking) and summarizing verbose environment feedback.
- **Functional Skills ($S_{func}$)**: These skills abstract subtasks, functioning as macro-operations grounded in a set of tool actions. Each functional skill is represented by a `name`, a `document` (describing inputs, outputs, and usage notes), and `content` (the tool invocation pattern). They are extracted by prompting the model to align with the objective of each subtask identified in the planning skill.
- **Atomic Skills ($S_{atomic}$)**: These are single tool specifications that extend the original tool schema. They encode execution-oriented usage patterns, typical parameter configurations, constraints, and observed common failure modes. Atomic skills are distilled from invocation patterns within trajectories and unified in representation with functional skills.

**Rollout and Skills Extraction**
Initially, a backbone agent performs multiple rollouts on training tasks to collect trajectories. A skill extractor then processes these trajectories to distill the multi-level skills described above. For planning skills, successful trajectories are compressed. For functional skills, the model extracts subtask-aligned skills guided by the planning skill. For atomic skills, invocation patterns and practical notes are distilled from tool usage within trajectories.

**Iterative Skills Refinement**
To maximize the utility of limited seed training data and continuously improve skill quality, SkillX employs an iterative optimization paradigm:
- **Iterative Construction**: The process begins with an empty skill library ($D^{(0)}$). In each iteration, the agent, augmented with the current library ($D^{(k)}$), performs rollouts on training tasks. Candidate skills are extracted from the resulting trajectories.
- **Skills Merge**: Functionally redundant skills are identified and merged. Semantically similar skills are clustered, and their distinct update directions are aggregated to refine the underlying skill. Overly complex merged skills may be further decomposed.
- **Skills Filter**: A two-stage filtering process enforces skill quality:
 - *General Filter*: Removes skills that are unlikely to be portable or compositional, such as those depending on extraneous Python packages, having idiosyncratic function definitions, or being overly encapsulated.
 - *Tool-specific Filter*: Validates each skill against the environment-provided tool schema, rejecting skills that reference non-existent tools, invalid parameters, or incompatible argument structures.
- **Skills Library Update**: After merging and filtering, the skill library ($D^{(k)}$) is updated by adding new skills, modifying existing ones, or retaining unchanged skills. This process continues until test performance no longer improves.

**Exploratory Skills Expansion**
To broaden skill coverage beyond the initial training distribution, SkillX implements an Experience Guiding Exploration scheme:
- **Guided Exploration**: The agent's past rollout experience (e.g., reliably used tools, high-failure-rate tools, or uninvoked tools) guides exploration, prioritizing under-explored or failure-prone areas.
- **Task Synthesis**: After collecting exploratory trajectories, new tasks are synthesized from these interactions. The skill acquisition and refinement pipeline is then rerun on this expanded data, enabling the skill library to generalize beyond the initial training distribution and discover a more diverse set of skills compared to random exploration.

**SkillX Usage**
During inference for a new task:
- **Planning Skills Retrieval and Pseudo-Plan Rewriting**: High-level planning skills associated with similar tasks are retrieved. An LLM then rewrites a task-specific pseudo-plan, conditioned on the current task and retrieved planning skills. This pseudo-plan serves as an intermediate retrieval query but is not directly injected into the system prompt to prevent hallucination.
- **Functional and Atomic Skills Retrieval**: Each step in the rewritten pseudo-plan acts as a retrieval query for relevant functional and atomic skills. Duplicate skills are removed, and the LLM selectively filters the candidates to retain only the most applicable skills for the current task. These filtered skills are then utilized by the LLM agent.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SkillX was conducted on three benchmarks: AppWorld, BFCL-v3, and $\tau^2$-Bench, all characterized by complex, long-horizon, and user-interactive agent tasks. Metrics included Avg@4 and Pass@4 for AppWorld and BFCL-v3, and Pass@1 for $\tau^2$-Bench. The base models for evaluation were Qwen3-32B, Kimi-K2-Instruct-0905 (K2), and GLM-4.6, with GLM-4.6 serving as the backbone for skill construction. Baselines included No-memory, A-Mem, AWM, and ExpeL. Experiments were run in two settings: a distillation paradigm (GLM-4.6 extracts experience for other models) and a self-evolution paradigm (extraction model aligns with inference model).

**Performance Improvements across Base LLMs**
SkillX demonstrated consistent performance improvements across the evaluated base models. Qwen3-32B, for instance, showed an approximate 10-point gain in success rate across multiple benchmarks. Kimi-K2-Instruct-0905 exhibited notable improvement on AppWorld, although gains were modest on other tool-intensive benchmarks, which the authors attribute to K2's reliance on original tool schemas over additional contextual information. Even for the strong backbone model GLM-4.6, SkillX yielded modest but consistent performance increases.

**Multi-Level Skills Design Effectiveness**
The multi-level skills design proved to be an effective representation of experience, consistently outperforming all baseline methods when the experience extraction model was aligned with the execution model. This was observed even against ExpeL, which utilizes past trajectories as few-shot demonstrations. The decoupling of experience into planning, functional, and atomic skills was identified as a more advantageous form of experience representation.

**Impact of Experience Representation on Transferability**
When experience extracted by a strong agent (GLM-4.6) was applied to weaker models, SkillX's performance surpassed that of AWM and ExpeL. This suggests that while distilling knowledge from a powerful model is beneficial, the *form* of experience representation is a critical factor for effective knowledge transfer across different base models.

**Expansion of Base Model Capability Boundaries**
For weaker models such as K2 and Qwen3-32B, SkillX led to substantial improvements in Pass@4, indicating that experience-based learning, particularly knowledge distillation from stronger models, can expand the functional capability boundaries of these models. Stronger models like GLM-4.6 showed less significant Pass@4 gains, implying their inherent robustness in exploration and planning.

**Analysis of Skill Types and Execution Efficiency**
- **Planning Skills**: Consistently reduced the number of execution steps, particularly for weaker models. However, for stronger models in complex scenarios, pseudo-planning could occasionally be counterproductive.
- **Functional Skills**: Contributed most significantly to overall performance improvements, especially when combined with Atomic Skills.
- **Atomic Skills**: Provided crucial clarifications for key APIs, with their absence leading to substantial performance drops.
- **Model-Specific Optimal Combinations**: Optimal skill combinations varied by model; GLM-4.6 benefited most from all skill types, K2 from functional and atomic skills, and Qwen3-32B achieved its best performance with planning skills alone. This suggests the multi-level design can accommodate diverse model needs.
- **Execution Efficiency**: SkillX was shown to enhance agent execution efficiency by reducing the number of input tokens and execution steps, contributing to overall performance despite not always achieving the absolute minimums for these metrics.

**Iterative Refinement and Skill Expansion Contributions**
- **Iterative Refinement**: Multiple rounds of iterative skill refinement led to further performance improvements on both training and test sets by enhancing skill documentation and content. It also slightly expanded the skill library size, though the authors note a risk of overfitting with limited training data.
- **Skill Expansion**: The experience-guided skill expansion strategy yielded a significantly larger number of novel skills and improved generalization compared to random exploration. This component was found to be crucial in complex environments with scarce training data.

**Evaluation on Stronger Base Models and Ablation Studies**
SkillX demonstrated consistent performance gains on other strong base models like DeepSeek-V3.2 and GPT-4.1, regardless of whether skills were self-extracted or constructed by GLM-4.6. Ablation studies confirmed the contributions of each core component: multi-level skills design provided baseline improvements, with iterative refinement and skill expansion offering additional gains.

**Qualitative Case Studies**
Case studies illustrated how SkillX assists agents in avoiding common failures, such as incorrect API call sequences, missing prerequisite checks, and difficulty handling conversational topic shifts. By leveraging reusable domain knowledge encoded as skills, agents were able to complete complex multi-step tasks that baseline methods failed, often achieving success on the first attempt.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SkillX represents an advancement in generalizable agent learning by transforming isolated, trial-and-error experiences into a structured, reusable skill knowledge base. This design enables the sharing of learned competencies across different agents and environments, addressing critical limitations of current self-evolving paradigms.

The proposed framework has the potential to significantly impact the efficiency of LLM agent training and deployment. By allowing weaker agents to leverage skills distilled by stronger models, SkillX can reduce redundant exploration, improve sample efficiency, and lower the computational and environmental costs associated with developing capable agents. The plug-and-play nature of the skill library promotes modularity and reproducibility, which can facilitate broader adoption and research in areas requiring long-horizon, user-interactive applications.

While the work primarily focuses on tool-using agent environments, its implications extend to improving agent robustness and capability across various complex tasks. The automated refinement and expansion mechanisms inherently provide pathways to mitigate potential risks such as over-reliance on initial skills or the propagation of biases from source agents, encouraging continuous adaptation and improvement of the skill knowledge base.

However, the authors acknowledge certain limitations. The extracted skills are currently tied to specific tool schemas, which may limit their direct reusability across substantially different domains or tool ecosystems. Furthermore, the primary focus remains on tool-using agents, and its applicability to more nuanced user-interactive scenarios, particularly dialogue without explicit function calls, is an area for further exploration. Despite these limitations, SkillX contributes to the development of more intelligent, adaptable, and efficient LLM agents.
