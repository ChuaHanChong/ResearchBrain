# MemoAct: Atkinson-Shiffrin-Inspired Memory-Augmented Visuomotor Policy for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> MemoAct, a visuomotor policy for robotic manipulation, incorporates a hierarchical memory system inspired by the Atkinson-Shiffrin human memory model to manage both precise short-term task state tracking and robust long-horizon retention. The system achieved an average success rate of 96.5% on the new MemoryRTBench benchmark, significantly outperforming existing history-aware and Markovian policies in memory-dependent tasks across simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation policies often adhere to the Markov assumption, making them unable to handle memory-dependent tasks that require historical context due to perceptual aliasing.
- Existing history-aware policies either use fixed-size FIFO memory, losing long-horizon retention, or similarity-based merging, which extends memory but sacrifices fine-grained details for precise task state tracking.
- There is a persistent trade-off between maintaining lossless short-term information and achieving compressed long-term retention in current memory-augmented robotic policies.

## Method

- MemoAct introduces a hierarchical memory system inspired by the Atkinson-Shiffrin model, featuring a lossless Short-Term Memory Bank (STMB) for recent task flow and a compressed Long-Term Memory Bank (LTMB) for distant historical context.
- A specialized memory consolidation module orchestrates the transfer of summarized information from STMB to LTMB, ensuring efficient long-horizon retention while preserving short-term precision.
- A conditional diffusion-based action decoder generates robot actions, guided by an adaptively fused token representing both current sensory input and retrieved historical context.

## Results

- MemoAct achieved an average success rate of 96.5% on the new MemoryRTBench, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline (MVMP at 72%) and Markovian policies (ACT 4.5%, DP 3%).
- It demonstrated superior performance in diverse simulated (49.5% average success on RMBench) and real-world tasks (87.5% average success), consistently surpassing baselines that struggled with memory-dependent challenges.
- The Long Short-Term Memory Consolidation Module (MCM) showed plug-and-play extensibility, boosting a baseline policy (DP3) from 26% to 76.5% success rate on MemoryRTBench, confirming its modular effectiveness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hierarchical memory architecture, mirroring human cognitive processes, effectively resolves the trade-off between precise short-term task state tracking and robust long-horizon retention in visuomotor policies.
> - Explicit temporal modeling and an adaptive gating network are crucial for correctly interpreting sequential dependencies and effectively fusing current sensory perception with relevant historical context.
> - Optimal capacities for both short-term (T_s=6) and long-term (T_k=8) memory banks are essential; excessively large or small capacities can degrade performance by introducing noise or losing critical information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robotic manipulation policies have primarily focused on approaches adhering to the Markov assumption, where future actions are predicted solely based on the current observation. These policies, exemplified by models such as RT-2, OpenVLA, and diffusion-based methods like Diffusion Policy (DP), have demonstrated proficiency in various tasks. However, this paradigm encounters limitations when dealing with memory-dependent tasks, which necessitate the tracking of past states or the retention of long-horizon information due to phenomena like perceptual aliasing. In such scenarios, current observations may be identical across different task stages, making it impossible to infer the correct action without historical context.

To address these limitations, a subfield of research has emerged focusing on history-aware robotic manipulation policies. A common approach involves extending the observation horizon using a fixed-size memory window, often employing a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) mechanism. Algorithms like SAM2Act (SAMP in this paper's baselines) construct a memory bank to track recently completed sub-tasks. While these methods provide short-term historical perception, their primary limitation is the rapid displacement of early observations, leading to a loss of long-horizon retention capability. This hinders their performance in tasks requiring recall of information beyond the memory window, such as an object's initial state.

Another approach, exemplified by MemoryVLA (MVMP in this paper's baselines), attempts to extend long-horizon retention by merging similar adjacent tokens in the memory. This strategy allows for a broader temporal span; however, the merging process can discard fine-grained details essential for precise task state tracking. This trade-off between long-horizon retention and precise state tracking presents a central challenge in developing robust memory-augmented policies. Other methods, such as MTIL, compress the entire history into a fixed-size hidden state, which can also lead to information loss, particularly for distant historical context.

The work presented in this paper, MemoAct, positions itself within this landscape by drawing inspiration from cognitive science, specifically the Atkinson–Shiffrin model of human memory, which proposes distinct memory tiers (sensory, short-term, long-term). By designing a hierarchical memory system that explicitly attempts to balance lossless short-term retention with compressed long-term retention, MemoAct seeks to overcome the limitations observed in existing history-aware policies, which typically prioritize one over the other. The paper also contributes a new benchmark, MemoryRTBench, to specifically evaluate these capabilities, enriching the experimental environment for memory-dependent robotic manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic manipulation policy capable of simultaneously achieving precise task state tracking and robust long-horizon retention, thereby addressing the limitations of existing methods in memory-dependent tasks.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations regarding current robotic policies:
1. **Inadequacy of Markovian Policies:** Conventional robotic manipulation policies, which operate under the Markov assumption (i.e., predicting future actions based only on the current observation), are inherently ill-suited for tasks where historical context is critical. Examples such as the "Sequential Hammer Tap" task illustrate perceptual aliasing, where identical observations correspond to different required actions depending on the sequence of previously executed steps. Markovian policies fail probabilistically in such scenarios.
2. **Limitations of Fixed-Size Observation Windows (FIFO):** A straightforward extension to address memory dependency involves expanding the observation horizon with a fixed-size, FIFO-based memory bank. While this grants some short-term historical perception for tracking completed sub-tasks, it suffers from rapid displacement of older data. Consequently, information beyond the window size becomes inaccessible, leading to a loss of long-horizon retention capabilities. This results in errors like repeating completed sub-tasks or failing to recall initial object states necessary for task completion.
3. **Trade-offs in Similarity-Based Memory Merging:** Approaches like MemoryVLA attempt to extend long-horizon retention by merging similar adjacent tokens. While this strategy can preserve historical information over longer durations, the merging process can lead to the discarding of fine-grained details. These details are often essential for accurate task state tracking and sequential decision-making, causing policies to skip sub-tasks or execute them prematurely.
4. **Inspiration from Human Memory:** Recognizing the dichotomy between existing memory mechanisms, the authors are motivated by cognitive science, specifically the Atkinson–Shiffrin model. This model posits a hierarchical structure of human memory comprising sensory, short-term, and long-term memory. Sensory memory captures immediate, transient perceptions; short-term memory acts as a temporary workspace for recent salient information; and long-term memory serves as a compressed repository for distant experiences. The motivation is to apply this hierarchical principle to robotic policies, hypothesizing that distinct memory tiers, each tailored to specific requirements, could overcome the aforementioned trade-offs.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives are:
- To propose MemoAct, a novel robotic policy inspired by the Atkinson–Shiffrin memory model, capable of explicitly managing different types of historical information.
- To develop a specialized memory update mechanism that coordinates a lossless short-term memory bank for precise task state tracking with a compressed long-term memory bank for robust long-horizon retention.
- To establish a new benchmark, MemoryRTBench, derived from RoboTwin 2.0, specifically designed to evaluate a policy's capabilities in both task state tracking and long-horizon retention.
- To validate MemoAct's efficacy through extensive experiments in simulated and real-world environments, comparing its performance against current Markovian and history-aware baselines, and demonstrating the distinct contributions of its hierarchical memory components.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MemoAct is designed as a hierarchical memory-based visuomotor policy, structured into three primary functional components: a sensory distillation module, a long short-term memory module, and a memory-augmented action decoder. The overall framework utilizes a streaming training paradigm to preserve temporal continuity.

#### 3.1. Sensory Distillation Module (f_sdm)

This module is responsible for extracting essential sensory memory from raw visual and proprioceptive inputs.
- **Visual Encoding:** Given an RGB image $I_t$, a DINOv2 backbone is employed to encode it into a patch-level feature map $F^I_t$. The parameters of DINOv2 are frozen during training to leverage its robust visual representations.
- **Visual Compression:** To mitigate redundancy and reduce memory consumption, a dedicated transformer layer with a single learnable readout token queries the patch-level features, yielding a compressed visual representation $F^R_t$.
- **Proprioceptive Encoding:** The robot's proprioceptive state $S_t$ is projected into a latent embedding $F^S_t$ using a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), ensuring dimensionality alignment with the visual token.
- **Feature Fusion:** A Feature Fusion Module combines these multi-modal inputs. The compressed visual token $F^R_t$ acts as the query, while the projected state features $F^S_t$ serve as both key and value in an attention mechanism. This process generates the final sensory memory $F^O_t$.

#### 3.2. Long Short-Term Memory Module (f_lstm)

This module manages the hierarchical memory banks and processes historical context.
- **Memory Bank Definition:**
 - **Short-Term Memory Bank (STMB):** $M_S \in R^{T_s \times C}$, designed to be lossless with a maximum capacity $T_s = 6$. It tracks recent task flows.
 - **Long-Term Memory Bank (LTMB):** $M_L \in R^{T_k \times C}$, designed for compressed, long-horizon retention with a maximum capacity $T_k = 8$.
- **Memory Consolidation:** A joint consolidation module orchestrates the interaction between STMB and LTMB.
 - **STMB Update:** A newly generated observation embedding is first appended to the STMB.
 - **STMB to LTMB Transfer:** When the STMB reaches its capacity ($T_s$), the oldest $N_{sc} = 3$ tokens are retrieved. These tokens are augmented with learnable Temporal Positional Embeddings (TPE) and processed by a causal transformer encoder alongside a learnable summary token. This summary token distills essential spatio-temporal features. The updated summary token is then transferred to the LTMB, and the original $N_{sc}$ tokens are removed from the STMB.
 - **LTMB Compression:** When the LTMB reaches its capacity ($T_k$), a mechanism different from FIFO is used. Inspired by MemoryVLA, the pairwise similarity between adjacent tokens in the LTMB is computed, and the two most similar entries are merged. This extends the temporal horizon while maintaining storage efficiency.
- **Memory Retrieval and Gate Fusion:**
 - **Temporal Encoding:** The comprehensive memory context $F^M_t$ (combining STMB and LTMB) is augmented with positional embeddings and processed by a temporal transformer encoder to model long-range dependencies.
 - **Contextual Retrieval:** A cross-attention mechanism uses the current sensory memory $F^O_t$ as a query to retrieve relevant historical information from the encoded memory context, yielding the retrieved feature $F^{OR}_t$.
 - **Adaptive Fusion:** A gating network adaptively fuses the retrieved feature $F^{OR}_t$ and the original sensory memory $F^O_t$ to produce the final conditional token $F^C_t$. This token guides action generation.

#### 3.3. Conditional Diffusion-Based Action Decoder (f_ad)

The action generation is framed as a conditional denoising process.
- **Denoising Process:** The action decoder iteratively eliminates noise, sampled from a Gaussian distribution, to reconstruct a clean action trajectory $A_{t:t+m-1}$.
- **Conditioning:** The denoising process is conditioned on the memory-augmented token $F^C_t$. A UNet-based noise predictor $\epsilon_\theta$ is used, and the DDPM scheduler governs the diffusion process.
- **Training Objective:** The model is trained to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted noise and the ground-truth noise.

#### 3.4. Training Protocol

- **Streaming Training:** To preserve temporal continuity and ensure the memory bank is populated sequentially, a streaming training paradigm is adopted. Each training batch is constructed from a single episode, with samples loaded in strict chronological order.
- **Boundary Conditions:** For instances where the prediction horizon exceeds the trajectory length, the sequence is padded by replicating the terminal time step.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Evaluations are conducted in simulated environments (RoboTwin 2.0 with a Cobot Magic mobile manipulator) and real-world settings (Realman RM65-B robotic arm with a TEK CTAG2F90-C gripper).
- **Perception:** Intel RealSense RGB-D cameras are used for visual input.
- **Tasks:**
 - **MemoryRTBench (New Benchmark):** Four simulation tasks (Sequential Hammer Tap (SHT), Block Pick and Rearrange (BPR), Dynamic Assembling – Sequential Trajectory Rearrangement (DA-STR), Dynamic Assembling – Initial Trajectory Retrieval (DA-ITR)) specifically designed to assess task state tracking and long-horizon retention.
 - **RMBench:** Language-independent tasks including "Put Back Block," "Swap T," "Rearrange Blocks," and "Observe and Pick Up."
 - **Real-world Tasks:** Two tasks, "Sequential Block Pick" (SBP) and "Dynamic Sequential Pick" (DSP).
- **Evaluation:** Trials consist of 50 for MemoryRTBench, 100 for RMBench, and 10 for real-world scenarios, with identical initial states for fairness.
- **Metric:** Success rate, defined by the sequential completion of all sub-tasks.
- **Baselines:** ACT and Diffusion Policy (DP) (Markovian policies); MVMP (MemoryVLA memory mechanism) and SAMP (SAM2Act memory mechanism). MVMP and SAMP are implemented using MemoAct's architectural components, differing only in their memory update mechanisms, to ensure a controlled comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Superiority of MemoAct Over Baselines (Q1)

MemoAct consistently demonstrated superior performance across all evaluated simulation and real-world environments.
- **MemoryRTBench:** MemoAct achieved an average success rate of 96.5%, significantly outperforming the strongest baseline, MVMP (72%), and SAMP (58%). Markovian policies ACT and DP yielded very low success rates (4.5% and 3%, respectively), indicating their inability to handle memory-dependent tasks.
- **RMBench:** MemoAct secured an average success rate of 49.5%, surpassing MVMP (28.5%) and SAMP (24.75%). ACT and DP again showed limited performance (5.5% and 4.5%).
- **Real-world Tasks:** MemoAct achieved an average success rate of 8.75/10 (87.5%), exceeding MVMP (6.5/10) and SAMP (4/10). DP recorded 0/10 successes, highlighting the necessity of historical context in real-world scenarios.

Specific observations regarding baselines:
- **Markovian Policies (ACT, DP):** Their consistent low performance confirms that historical context is indispensable for these tasks, as they fail due to perceptual aliasing.
- **SAMP (FIFO-based):** While effective for executing some sequential subtasks, SAMP frequently suffered from termination failures and erroneous loops. Its FIFO mechanism led to poor long-horizon retention, particularly in tasks requiring recall of initial object states (e.g., BPR, DA-ITR, Put Back Block), as older data were rapidly displaced.
- **MVMP (Similarity-based merging):** MVMP showed robust long-horizon retention in tasks like BPR, SBP, and Swap T. However, in tasks demanding strict sequential tracking (e.g., SHT, Rearrange Blocks), it often bypassed immediate subtasks, leading to disordered execution and reduced performance (e.g., 21% in Rearrange Blocks compared to MemoAct's 98%). This suggests that merging distinct temporal features, while extending memory, compromises fine-grained task state tracking.

These results corroborate the paper's hypothesis that MemoAct's hierarchical memory framework effectively reconciles the trade-off between lossless short-term tracking and robust long-horizon retention. A limitation was noted in the "Observe and Pick Up" task (RMBench), where MemoAct achieved only 4% success. This was attributed to the compression of DINOv2 visual tokens into a single global token, potentially sacrificing fine-grained spatial precision needed for multi-object manipulation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies (Q2)

Ablation experiments demonstrated the contributions of individual modules:
- **DINOv2 Backbone:** Replacing DINOv2 with ResNet-18 resulted in an 18% average success rate drop, underscoring the importance of DINOv2 for robust visual representation.
- **Temporal Modeling in Consolidation:** Removing Temporal Positional Embeddings (TPE) or replacing the causal transformer encoder with a simple addition within the long short-term memory consolidation module significantly degraded performance on complex tasks like SHT (dropping to 78% and 72%, respectively). This indicates that explicit temporal modeling is crucial for strict sequential execution.
- **Gating Network:** Replacing the gating network with direct addition reduced performance on SHT and DA-ITR tasks. This supports the necessity of an adaptive feature aggregation mechanism for effectively fusing current sensory perception with retrieved historical context.

#### 4.3. Influence of Memory Capacities (Q3)

The capacities of the long-term ($T_k$) and short-term ($T_s$) memory banks were found to critically influence performance:
- **Long-Term Memory Capacity ($T_k$):**
 - Setting $T_k = 0$ (effectively no long-term memory) severely degraded tasks requiring initial state recall (BPR, DA-STR, DA-ITR).
 - Conversely, excessively expanding $T_k$ to 12 led to a drop in the average success rate to 91%, suggesting that an overabundance of historical data can introduce noise and redundancy, interfering with decision-making.
 - An optimal $T_k = 8$ was identified.
- **Short-Term Memory Capacity ($T_s$):**
 - Reducing $T_s$ (e.g., $T_s = 2$) drastically impaired the SHT task (46% success rate) and caused repetitive or skipped sub-tasks in DA-ITR, confirming its role in accurate state tracking.
 - Increasing $T_s$ beyond the baseline did not yield performance improvements but incurred unnecessary computational overhead.
 - An optimal $T_s = 6$ was identified, with $N_{sc} = 3$ for efficient consolidation.

#### 4.4. Module Extensibility (Q4)

The Long Short-Term Memory Consolidation Module (MCM) of MemoAct demonstrated versatility. Integrating MCM into a representative baseline policy, DP3, led to a substantial performance improvement on MemoryRTBench. The average success rate of DP3 surged from 26% to 76.5% with the addition of MCM, indicating its plug-and-play adaptability and effectiveness in enhancing history-aware capabilities in other policy frameworks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents MemoAct, a robotic visuomotor policy that offers an advancement in addressing memory-dependent manipulation tasks. Its primary significance lies in the introduction of a hierarchical memory architecture inspired by the Atkinson–Shiffrin model of human memory, which distinguishes between short-term and long-term retention mechanisms.

The key contributions and potential impact are:
- **Enhanced Memory Management in Robotics:** MemoAct introduces a principled approach to managing historical information, specifically designed to overcome the inherent limitations of Markovian assumptions and the trade-offs in existing history-aware policies. By synergizing a lossless short-term memory for precise task state tracking and a compressed long-term memory for robust long-horizon retention, it provides a more robust framework for complex, multi-stage manipulation sequences.
- **Superior Performance in Memory-Dependent Tasks:** The empirical results consistently demonstrate MemoAct's superior performance across various simulated and real-world tasks that explicitly require historical context. This suggests that the proposed hierarchical memory model enables robots to effectively navigate perceptual ambiguities and long temporal dependencies that challenge conventional and existing memory-augmented methods.
- **Establishment of a Specialized Benchmark:** The creation of MemoryRTBench is a notable contribution to the robotics research community. This benchmark provides a standardized, rigorous evaluation environment specifically tailored to assess policy capabilities in task state tracking and long-horizon retention, which are critical but often not comprehensively tested aspects of robotic intelligence. This will facilitate more focused research and development in this area.
- **Versatility and Adaptability:** The demonstrated plug-and-play compatibility of MemoAct's memory consolidation module with other baseline policies (e.g., DP3) suggests its potential for broad applicability. This indicates that the core memory mechanisms could serve as a foundational component for enhancing the historical awareness of various existing or future robotic manipulation frameworks.
- **Inspiration from Cognitive Science:** The work highlights the utility of drawing inspiration from cognitive models for engineering robotic systems. By translating human memory principles into a computational architecture, this research opens avenues for further interdisciplinary exploration between cognitive science and artificial intelligence in robotics.
- **Future Research Directions:** The identified limitations, such as the potential loss of fine-grained spatial precision during visual compression, provide clear directions for future research. Exploring adaptive compression mechanisms and methods to distill historical knowledge into permanent, reusable experience for lifelong learning could further enhance robot autonomy and generalization capabilities in increasingly complex and dynamic real-world environments.

Overall, MemoAct contributes to the development of more intelligent and capable robots by equipping them with sophisticated memory mechanisms essential for complex, sequential, and context-dependent manipulation tasks.
