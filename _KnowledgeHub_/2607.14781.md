# Curvature-Constrained and Constant-Speed Distributed Simultaneous Arrival Control for Multi-Robot Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> A distributed control method enables multi-robot systems to achieve simultaneous arrival at target points while adhering to curvature constraints and constant speeds. This approach ensures robust coordination even from challenging initial conditions, achieving precise synchronization with demonstrated positional differences under 0.1m and virtual time differences under 0.5s in real-world quadrotor experiments.

## Problem

- Many existing multi-robot simultaneous arrival control laws do not fully account for realistic physical constraints such as maximum curvature or constant speed for nonholonomic robots.
- Conventional approaches often struggle when robots begin extremely close to their targets, potentially with adverse initial headings, leading to inefficient or infeasible paths.
- Most theoretical results for distributed simultaneous arrival have primarily been validated through simulations, with limited experimental verification on physical robotic platforms.

## Method

- A ==distributed switching control== method is developed, based on a ==maximum consensus protocol==, which integrates ==Dubins path== theory with optimization principles.
- A ==virtual time variable== T(r,heta)T(r, 	heta)T(r,heta) representing the minimal time to reach the target under curvature constraints is introduced, transforming the coordination into a time optimality problem.
- A hybrid control law is employed, using a ==saturated proportional controller== for the curvature-feasible region (D^c) to adjust bearing angle towards a desired virtual time from consensus, and applying an optimal ==bang-bang control== law in the curvature-constrained region (D).

## Results

- Theoretical analysis guarantees simultaneous arrival (Theorem 2) and establishes conditions under which the proposed method achieves the optimal arrival time (Theorem 3).
- Simulations with up to **50 robots** demonstrated successful simultaneous arrival across varied initial conditions and scalability, also showcasing compatibility with an integrated collision avoidance mechanism.
- Real-world experiments using **two** quadrotor UAVs validated the method's robustness, achieving simultaneous arrival with positional differences under **0.1m** and virtual time differences under **0.5s** despite real-world disturbances.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Dubins path length function exhibits strict monotonicity with respect to the bearing angle in the curvature-feasible region, enabling effective consensus-driven control over path length and arrival time.
> - A max-consensus mechanism applied to the virtual time variable effectively coordinates a distributed multi-robot system to achieve synchronized arrival times.
> - The hybrid control strategy, leveraging optimal single-robot maneuvering in complex states and consensus-based adjustments elsewhere, provides robust performance against nonholonomic constraints and challenging initial conditions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of cooperative motion planning for multi-robot systems, specifically the distributed simultaneous arrival problem. This area of research focuses on enabling a group of mobile robots to coordinate their movements to achieve a common objective, often under various physical and environmental constraints.

The simultaneous arrival problem, where multiple robots must reach a pre-specified target location at precisely the same time, is positioned as a more stringent variant of the general multi-robot rendezvous problem. While rendezvous typically requires robots to gather at an unspecified location, simultaneous arrival demands accuracy and temporal consistency at a predetermined point. The paper highlights its relevance to applications such as cooperative target interception, encirclement, disaster relief, and environmental monitoring.

Existing cooperative control approaches for simultaneous arrival are generally categorized into time-to-go estimation guidance laws, leader-following methods, and consensus-based algorithms. Time-to-go estimation methods explicitly regulate arrival time by estimating remaining flight duration. Leader-following strategies coordinate robots relative to a designated leader or reference, often relying on centralized information. Consensus algorithms, in contrast, facilitate global synchronization through local information exchange, offering scalability and robustness, even under dynamic communication conditions.

The authors identify two primary limitations in existing literature concerning the distributed simultaneous arrival problem for nonholonomic mobile robots:
1. **Physical Saturation Constraints:** Many theoretical control laws do not fully account for actuator limitations, such as maximum curvature or steering angle limits, which can hinder their practical implementation on real systems.
2. **Challenging Initial Conditions:** Conventional approaches often struggle when robots begin extremely close to their targets, as limited maneuvering space can lead to infeasible paths or inefficient coordination.
Furthermore, the authors note that many theoretical results in this domain have primarily been validated through simulations, with limited experimental verification on physical robotic platforms.

The paper situates its contribution by leveraging Dubins path theory, which provides geometrically feasible and time-optimal path generation for constant-speed vehicles subject to curvature constraints. By integrating Dubins paths with consensus protocols, the research aims to address the aforementioned challenges, particularly the kinematic constraints and the issue of adverse initial conditions, within a distributed control framework. This integration positions the work as an advancement in distributed control for multi-robot systems operating under realistic physical limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a distributed control method that enables a multi-robot system to achieve simultaneous arrival at designated target points while adhering to specific physical and operational constraints.

The motivation for this work stems from the importance of simultaneous arrival in various cooperative robotic tasks and the inherent complexities involved. Key motivations and objectives include:

- **Addressing Complex Cooperation Tasks:** Cooperative tasks such as encirclement, target interception, disaster relief, and environmental monitoring often require multiple robots to arrive at a target point concurrently. This temporal synchronization is critical for mission success and efficiency.
- **Incorporating Real-World Kinematic Constraints:** Many robotic platforms, particularly fixed-wing Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) and certain Unmanned Ground Vehicles, are nonholonomic and operate under strict curvature constraints (minimum turning radius) and often at constant speeds. Existing simultaneous arrival solutions frequently overlook these physical limitations, making theoretical control laws impractical for real-world deployment. The paper aims to explicitly integrate these constraints into the control design.
- **Ensuring Constant-Speed Operation:** The requirement for robots to maintain constant (though potentially different) speeds further complicates simultaneous arrival, as it removes the flexibility of speed adjustment as a control variable for temporal synchronization. The objective is to achieve simultaneous arrival under this specific constraint.
- **Developing Distributed Control:** To ensure scalability, robustness, and reduce reliance on centralized computational resources, the control law for each robot must be distributed, meaning it depends only on its own state and information exchanged with direct neighbors via a communication network. This avoids single points of failure and enhances adaptability in dynamic environments.
- **Handling "Undesirable" Initial Conditions:** A significant challenge arises when robots start very close to their target locations, potentially with headings directed away from the target. In such scenarios, conventional path planning and time coordination mechanisms can fail, leading to infeasible trajectories. The research aims to design a control strategy that can effectively manage these challenging initial states.
- **Achieving Optimal Arrival Time:** Beyond merely achieving simultaneous arrival, the work aims to investigate conditions under which the proposed method can attain a theoretically optimal arrival time, maximizing efficiency for the slowest robot while synchronizing the others.
- **Validating with Physical Systems:** Recognizing the gap between simulation and real-world implementation, a key objective is to validate the proposed approach not only through extensive simulations but also through experiments on physical robotic platforms (quadrotor UAVs).
- **Minimizing Communication Overhead:** The motivation includes designing a control mechanism that is efficient in terms of communication requirements, transmitting minimal information (a single scalar virtual time variable) between neighboring robots.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between theoretical multi-robot cooperative control and practical implementation by providing a distributed, scalable, and robust simultaneous arrival solution that rigorously accounts for nonholonomic kinematic constraints and challenging initial conditions, while maintaining low communication demands and aiming for optimal performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a distributed switching control method predicated on a maximum consensus protocol, which integrates the geometric properties of Dubins paths with optimization principles.

**A. Virtual Time Variable Introduction:**
The core of the approach is the introduction of a **virtual time variable** $T(r, \phi)$. This variable represents the theoretically minimal time a single robot requires to reach its target under its specific curvature constraints, assuming a constant speed $v$. Since speed is constant, this virtual time is directly proportional to the Dubins path length $L$ (i.e., $L = vT$). This formulation transforms the multi-robot coordination problem into an analysis of time optimality for individual robot trajectories.

- **Optimal Control Law for a Single Robot:** The paper utilizes an optimal control law $\omega^*(r, \phi)$ for a Dubins car model, which dictates the angular velocity based on the robot's relative state (distance $r$ and bearing angle $\phi$) to the target. This law is "bang-bang" in nature, switching between maximum negative angular velocity ($-\bar{\omega}$), zero, and maximum positive angular velocity ($\bar{\omega}$) depending on the robot's state relative to defined regions ($C^\pm, S^\pm, S^0$).
- **Workspace Partitioning:** The robot's state space $(r, \phi)$ is partitioned into two regions based on the minimum turning radius $\rho$:
 - **Curvature-Constrained Region (D):** Defined as $\{(r, \phi) : r \leq 2\rho \sin |\phi|\}$. In this region, optimal Dubins paths are typically "CC" (circular arc - circular arc) or "C" (single circular arc) types, where the robot maneuvers at its maximum allowable curvature.
 - **Curvature-Feasible Region (D^c):** The complement of D. Here, optimal Dubins paths are "CS" (circular arc - straight line) or "S" (straight line) types, allowing for straight segments.
- **Analytical Dubins Path Length Derivation:** To facilitate the calculation of the virtual time variable, the paper provides analytical solutions for the Dubins path length $L(r, \phi)$ in both regions, derived using geometric constructions and the Law of Cosines. These expressions, (6) for $D^c$ and (7) for $D$, account for different bearing angle ranges ($|\phi| < \pi/2$ or $|\phi| \ge \pi/2$).
- **Monotonicity Analysis:** The paper proves in Theorem 1 that within the curvature-feasible region $D^c$, the Dubins path length function $L(r, \phi)$ is strictly increasing with respect to the bearing angle $\phi \in [0, \pi]$. This monotonicity is crucial for control design. However, in the curvature-constrained region $D$, $L(r, \phi)$ exhibits only local monotonicity.

**B. Max-Consensus Control Mechanism:**
To achieve simultaneous arrival, a max-consensus mechanism is employed. Each robot $i$ computes its desired virtual time $T^d_i$ by taking the maximum virtual time among its direct neighbors, including itself: $T^d_i = \max_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} T_j$. The control law aims to adjust the robot's state so that its actual virtual time $T_i$ converges to $T^d_i$.

- **Control Strategy for $T^d_i > T_i$:** When a robot's current virtual time is less than its desired maximum virtual time, its control input $\omega_i$ is designed to increase its path length and thus its virtual arrival time.
 - **Case 1: Robot in Curvature-Feasible Region (ζ_i ∈ D^c):** Leveraging the strict monotonicity of $T_i$ with respect to $\phi_i$ in $D^c$, the inverse function $T_i^{-1}(T^d_i)$ is used to determine a desired bearing angle $\phi^d_i$. If $T^d_i$ is unattainable (i.e., $T_i(\pi) < T^d_i$), $\phi^d_i$ is set to $\pi$. A saturated proportional controller, $\omega_i = \text{Sat}_{\bar{\omega}_i}^{-\bar{\omega}_i}(k_\theta(\theta^d_i - \theta_i))$, is then applied to guide the robot's heading $\theta_i$ towards the desired heading $\theta^d_i = \text{arg}(p_i - p^d_i) - \phi^d_i$.
 - **Case 2: Robot in Curvature-Constrained Region (ζ_i ∈ D):** Due to the local non-monotonicity of $T_i$ in this region, directly controlling to a desired $T^d_i$ through $\phi_i$ is problematic. Instead, the optimal control law $\omega^*(r_i, \phi_i)$ (from the single-robot optimal control) is applied. The intent is to rapidly drive the robot out of this complex region and restore system stability.
- **Overall Hybrid Control Law:** The control law $\omega_i$ is a switching strategy: if $\zeta_i \in D^c$ and $T_i \ne T^d_i$, the saturated proportional control is used; otherwise (i.e., $\zeta_i \in D$ or $T_i = T^d_i$), the optimal control $\omega^*(r_i, \phi_i)$ is applied.

**C. Convergence Analysis:**
The paper presents two key theoretical results for convergence:
- **Theorem 2:** This theorem proves that simultaneous arrival is guaranteed. It states that if one robot reaches its target, all other robots in the system must also have reached their respective targets. This is shown by contradiction, using the property that if one robot arrives while another has not, the consensus mechanism would prevent the arriving robot's bearing angle from reaching zero.
- **Theorem 3:** This theorem establishes conditions under which the proposed method achieves an optimal arrival time. If the robot with the maximum initial virtual time ($T_{i^*}$) satisfies a specific inequality relating its own virtual time to the sum of maximum consensus times of other robots and their minimum turning radii, and with sufficiently high control gain $k_\theta$, then all robots arrive simultaneously at $t^* = T_{i^*}$, which is proven to be the optimal arrival time.

**D. Collision Avoidance (Integration):**
While not a primary focus, the paper briefly describes a simple trigger-based cooperative collision avoidance mechanism that can be integrated. This mechanism activates when robots are sufficiently close, moving towards each other, and a potential future collision is predicted (via ray-circle or trajectory intersection). Upon activation, the involved robots set their angular velocities to their maximum values to maneuver away.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical findings and empirical validations through simulations and real-world experiments.

**A. Theoretical Findings:**

- **Monotonicity of Dubins Path Length (Theorem 1):** The paper formally proves that the Dubins path length function $L(r, \phi)$ is strictly increasing with respect to the bearing angle $\phi$ within the curvature-feasible region ($D^c$) for a robot moving at constant speed. This monotonicity is a foundational element for designing the consensus-driven control in $D^c$.
- **Guarantee of Simultaneous Arrival (Theorem 2):** A significant theoretical result is the proof that if any robot within the connected communication graph reaches its target position, then all other robots in the system must also have simultaneously arrived at their respective targets. This addresses a core objective of the problem by ensuring global synchronization in target arrival.
- **Conditions for Optimal Arrival Time (Theorem 3):** The research establishes conditions under which the proposed control method achieves the theoretically optimal arrival time. Specifically, if the robot with the maximum initial virtual time ($T_{i^*}$) meets a certain condition (related to total consensus time for neighbors and its minimum turning radius) and the control gain ($k_\theta$) is sufficiently high, then all robots will arrive at the optimal time $t^* = T_{i^*}$. This provides a guarantee of efficiency under specified circumstances.

**B. Simulation Results:**

- **First Simulation (N=5 Robots):** Using a ring communication topology, simulations demonstrated successful simultaneous arrival for five robots under various initial conditions. This included different target circle radii, linear velocities, and maximum angular velocities. The results showed that robots achieved consensus on their virtual time variables and converged to their target points at the same final time, irrespective of whether they started close to or far from their targets. The trajectories and time histories of virtual time, distance to target, and control inputs confirmed the method's effectiveness.
- **Second Simulation (N=50 Robots):** To assess scalability, the method was simulated with N=50 robots with randomly assigned initial states, velocities, and angular velocities. The results indicated that the algorithm maintained its effectiveness, with all 50 robots achieving simultaneous arrival at the target point, thereby demonstrating its applicability to larger-scale multi-robot systems.
- **Third Simulation (N=5 Robots with Collision Avoidance):** This simulation showcased the method's extensibility to include collision avoidance. Five robots, starting in a potentially conflicting configuration, successfully achieved simultaneous arrival while maintaining collision-free trajectories. The integration of a trigger-based cooperative collision avoidance mechanism allowed robots to adjust their headings to avoid close encounters, confirming the compatibility of the simultaneous arrival algorithm with safety protocols.

**C. Real-World Experiments:**

- **Two Quadrotor UAVs:** Real-world experiments were conducted using two quadrotor UAVs equipped with PX4 flight controllers, providing empirical validation. Two distinct sets of initial conditions, including varying positions, headings, constant speeds, and maximum angular velocities, were tested.
- **Robustness to Disturbances:** Despite the presence of real-world disturbances such as oscillations in the z-axis and communication delays, the UAVs successfully achieved simultaneous arrival.
- **Quantitative Performance:** The experiments demonstrated that the error in simultaneous arrival could be maintained within a small range, with a final positional difference between robots of less than 0.1m and a virtual time difference of less than 0.5s at the arrival time. The evolution of virtual time, bearing angles, and UAV trajectories provided indirect validation of the theoretical underpinnings (Theorem 2 and 3) in a physical environment.

In summary, the main findings include robust theoretical guarantees for simultaneous arrival and, under certain conditions, optimal arrival time. These theoretical claims are supported by extensive simulations demonstrating scalability and the ability to integrate collision avoidance, as well as by practical experiments validating the method's effectiveness and robustness on real robotic platforms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The presented research offers several significant contributions and has the potential to impact the field of multi-robot cooperative control and its applications.

**A. Addressing Critical Gaps in Multi-Robot Control:**
The work specifically addresses long-standing challenges in distributed simultaneous arrival control:
- **Integration of Kinematic Constraints:** It provides a method that rigorously accounts for nonholonomic constraints, particularly curvature limits and constant speeds, which are characteristic of many practical robotic platforms like fixed-wing UAVs and ground vehicles. This enhances the applicability of simultaneous arrival strategies to real-world systems where such constraints cannot be ignored.
- **Handling Challenging Initial Conditions:** The proposed hybrid control strategy effectively manages "undesirable" initial states where robots start very close to their targets, a scenario that often leads to infeasible paths or control instability in conventional approaches. By strategically using Dubins path properties, it ensures that robots can still achieve coordination.
- **Distributed and Scalable Solution:** The max-consensus protocol enables a distributed control architecture, eliminating the need for a centralized coordinator. This design ensures scalability, allowing the method to be applied to multi-robot systems of various sizes without increasing computational or communication bottlenecks at a central point. The low communication overhead (a single scalar transmission between neighbors) further contributes to its scalability and practical viability.

**B. Theoretical Rigor and Performance Guarantees:**
The paper provides a rigorous mechanistic analysis of the simultaneous arrival problem.
- **Guaranteed Simultaneous Arrival:** Theorem 2 offers a strong theoretical guarantee that if one robot reaches its target, all others will arrive concurrently, which is fundamental for cooperative missions.
- **Potential for Optimal Arrival Time:** Theorem 3 establishes conditions under which the proposed method can achieve a theoretically optimal arrival time, ensuring efficient resource utilization by aligning all robots with the "slowest" possible arrival without unnecessary delays.

**C. Practical Validation:**
A key strength of this work is its comprehensive validation.
- **Extensive Simulations:** Simulations across various scenarios, including different initial conditions, varying robot parameters, and large-scale systems (N=50 robots), demonstrate the method's effectiveness and robustness in diverse settings.
- **Real-World Experimental Verification:** The successful implementation on actual quadrotor UAVs, even in the presence of real-world disturbances like communication delays and environmental oscillations, significantly enhances the credibility and practical relevance of the proposed control strategy. This addresses a common limitation of much theoretical work.
- **Collision Avoidance Integration:** The demonstration of seamless integration with a simple collision avoidance mechanism highlights the method's potential for safe operation in multi-robot environments, which is crucial for deployment.

**D. Potential Impact on Applications:**
The developments in this paper have direct implications for several applications requiring precise multi-robot coordination:
- **Cooperative Target Interception/Encirclement:** For military or surveillance applications involving multiple agents, simultaneous arrival can be critical for overwhelming targets or ensuring complete containment.
- **Disaster Relief and Environmental Monitoring:** Coordinated deployment of robots to a specific location at the same time can optimize search-and-rescue operations or data collection in hazardous environments.
- **Logistics and Manufacturing:** In automated systems, synchronized arrival of multiple transport robots or assembly components can improve efficiency and throughput.
- **Fixed-Wing UAV Operations:** The explicit consideration of curvature and constant-speed constraints makes this method particularly relevant for cooperative missions involving fixed-wing UAVs, which often operate under such kinematic limitations.

In conclusion, this research provides a robust, scalable, and practically validated framework for distributed simultaneous arrival control in multi-robot systems, specifically addressing the complexities introduced by nonholonomic kinematics and challenging operational scenarios. Its theoretical underpinnings and empirical success suggest a significant step towards enabling more sophisticated and reliable multi-robot cooperative tasks in diverse real-world applications.
