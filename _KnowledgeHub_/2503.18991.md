# Inverse Reinforcement Learning with Dynamic Reward Scaling for LLM Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Beijing Electronic Science and Technology Institute, Alibaba Group, and collaborators introduced DR-IRL, an approach that enhances large language model safety by combining category-specific inverse reinforcement learning with dynamic reward scaling. This method achieved top performance in harmlessness benchmarks and maintained utility across various LLMs, demonstrating improved resistance to jailbreak attacks.

## Problem

- Existing LLM safety datasets are imbalanced, overrepresenting common hazards and neglecting 'long-tail' threats, leading to incomplete safety coverage.
- Traditional reward models in RLHF assign static rewards irrespective of task difficulty, hindering optimization efficiency and limiting alignment gains for nuanced safety scenarios.
- Large Language Models (LLMs) are prone to generating harmful content when subjected to malicious queries or jailbreak attacks, requiring more robust intrinsic alignment mechanisms.

## Method

- **DR-IRL** integrates a balanced, category-specific safety dataset (constructed using Chain of Draft refusal responses) with ==Inverse Reinforcement Learning== (IRL) to train specialized 'shadow reward models' for seven distinct harmful categories.
- The method incorporates dynamic reward scaling within a Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework, where a combined hardness coefficient (from data-level hardness and model-level responsiveness) adjusts rewards based on task difficulty.
- The final LLM alignment iteratively updates the policy by optimizing a dynamically scaled advantage function, emphasizing challenging samples and preventing over-optimization on trivial cases.

## Results

- DR-IRL achieved state-of-the-art harmlessness, with StrongReject scores of **0.9361** for Llama-3.1-**8B** and **0.8798** for Qwen-2-**7B**, while maintaining high utility scores on benchmarks like AdvGLUE (**70.71%**) and GSM8k (**88.10%**).
- The approach demonstrated superior robustness against jailbreak attacks, achieving refusal rates of **96.98%** on GCG and **59.00%** on AutoDAN for Llama-3.1-**8B**, outperforming existing baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both data-level and model-level hardness coefficients are critical, with their combined multiplicative effect preventing degradation in harmlessness (StrongReject dropping by 4 percentage points without them) and stabilizing general capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training category-specific shadow reward models via IRL from balanced single-response demonstrations effectively captures fine-grained safety values and reduces reward interference compared to generic models.
> - Dynamically scaling rewards based on both data-level hardness (semantic similarity) and model-level responsiveness (reward gap) significantly improves optimization efficiency and robustness across diverse safety tasks.
> - The multiplicative combination of hardness coefficients acts as an effective 'AND-gate,' focusing learning on samples that are both semantically distinct from expert responses and still uncertain for the current model, leading to more stable and robust alignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in the deployment of large language models (LLMs): ensuring their alignment with human values and safety principles to prevent the generation of harmful content. LLMs are known to produce undesirable responses, particularly in the face of malicious queries or "jailbreak" attacks. Consequently, aligning these models is a critical area of research aimed at enhancing their reliability in real-world applications.

Current LLM alignment techniques broadly fall into two categories:
1. **Reward-based pipelines:** These methods train a reward model on preference pairs (where one response is preferred over another) and subsequently optimize the LLM's policy using reinforcement learning (RL), such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
2. **Reward-free pipelines:** These approaches bypass the explicit training of a reward model and directly fine-tune LLMs on ranked outputs, as seen in methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

Recent observations indicate that well-tuned reward-based pipelines tend to exhibit greater robustness across diverse benchmarks, particularly for challenging tasks. Additionally, research has suggested that learning rewards from single-response "demonstration data" can sometimes outperform methods relying on more costly pairwise preference data, which may not fully capture human values.

This paper builds upon these findings by focusing on the reward-based, demonstration-driven alignment paradigm. It identifies and seeks to overcome two specific limitations within this landscape:
- **Data imbalance:** Existing safety datasets often overrepresent common hazards while neglecting "long-tail" or less frequent threats, leading to incomplete safety coverage.
- **Static reward models:** Traditional reward models assign fixed rewards irrespective of the difficulty of a given task. This static nature can limit optimization efficiency and the potential for performance gains, especially for nuanced or challenging safety scenarios.

By addressing these limitations, the work aims to contribute to more robust, efficient, and comprehensive LLM alignment, specifically for safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed propensity of large language models to generate harmful responses when confronted with specific prompts, including adversarial suffixes or carefully crafted disguises. Ensuring the safe and responsible deployment of LLMs necessitates robust alignment strategies. While various defensive techniques exist, many rely on external intervention, limiting their broad applicability. This underscores the need for intrinsic alignment mechanisms within the LLM itself.

The authors identify two key challenges in current LLM alignment methodologies that motivate their proposed approach:

1. **Imbalanced Safety Datasets:** Current training datasets for LLM safety are often skewed. They tend to oversample frequently encountered harmful categories while failing to adequately represent "long-tail threats" – less common but potentially severe harmful scenarios. This imbalance can lead to models that are robust against common hazards but vulnerable to novel or rare forms of unsafe input. The objective is to develop an alignment method that accounts for and mitigates this data imbalance, providing more comprehensive safety coverage.

2. **Static Reward Models:** Traditional reward models used in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) assign a fixed reward value to a generated response, irrespective of the inherent difficulty of the task or query. This static reward signal can hinder optimization efficiency. If a task is particularly challenging, a fixed reward might not provide sufficient signal for the model to learn effectively. Conversely, for trivial tasks, over-optimization on a fixed high reward might lead to "alignment tax," where safety improvements come at the cost of utility. The objective here is to introduce dynamic reward mechanisms that adapt based on task difficulty, thereby improving optimization efficiency and maximizing achievable alignment gains.

To address these limitations, the paper proposes **DR-IRL (Dynamic Reward-Inverse Reinforcement Learning)**. The core objectives of DR-IRL are:
- To replace costly pairwise preference labels with a balanced dataset of single-response demonstrations.
- To train category-specific reward models using Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) on this balanced safety dataset, ensuring that the reward signals more accurately reflect human safety values across diverse harmful categories.
- To enhance the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework by incorporating dynamic reward scaling. This scaling mechanism adjusts rewards based on task difficulty, which is quantified at two levels:
 - **Data-level hardness:** Assessed by the semantic similarity between generated and demonstration responses using a text encoder.
 - **Model-level responsiveness:** Measured by the reward gap produced by the trained reward models, indicating the model's confidence or uncertainty for a given prompt-response pair.

By achieving these objectives, DR-IRL aims to foster stronger safety alignment in LLMs while concurrently maintaining their usefulness across a range of tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, DR-IRL, integrates inverse reinforcement learning with dynamic reward scaling within a Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) framework. The approach consists of three main stages: dataset construction, shadow reward model training, and LLM alignment with dynamic reward scaling.

#### 3.1. Balanced Safety Dataset Construction

The first step involves creating a balanced safety dataset, denoted as *D*, which covers seven distinct harmful categories: Insult, Unfairness and Discrimination, Crimes and Illegal Activities, Physical Harm, Mental Health, Privacy and Property, and Ethics and Morality. The dataset is constructed using harmful instructions (queries) sampled from existing datasets (Do-Not-Answer and Safety-Prompts). For each harmful instruction, the researchers prompt an LLM (such as LLaMA-3) to generate a "Chain of Draft" (CoD) refusal response. CoD involves concise intermediate reasoning steps leading to a final safe refusal, designed to be more efficient and reliable than traditional Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. This dataset of (harmful instruction, CoD refusal response) pairs serves as demonstration data for subsequent reward model training.

#### 3.2. Shadow Reward Model Training via Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)

Instead of relying on pairwise preferences, the method trains specialized "shadow reward models" using the balanced CoD demonstration dataset. For each of the seven harmful categories, a separate reward model, denoted as *R_j*, is trained. The training leverages a maximum likelihood inverse reinforcement learning (ML-IRL) formulation. This formulation involves a bilevel optimization problem:
- The upper level optimizes the policy (π_θ) to maximize the likelihood of observed demonstrations.
- The lower level optimizes the policy based on the current reward model *r(x, y; θ)*, constrained by a KL-divergence term (βD_KL(π(·|x)∥π_ref(·|x))) with respect to a reference policy (π_ref).
The ML-IRL problem is framed as a minimax optimization problem (Equation 2), which allows reward functions to be learned from single demonstrations, drawing parallels to RLHF without explicit preference labels. The Shadow Reward Learning (SRL) algorithm (Algorithm 2 in supplementary materials) details this process, involving iterative updates of reward model parameters via stochastic gradient descent and policy updates derived from the closed-form solution of the lower-level problem.

#### 3.3. Dynamic Reward Scaling (Hardness Measurement)

To dynamically adjust rewards based on task difficulty during LLM alignment, DR-IRL introduces a combined "hardness coefficient" (α_ji). This coefficient is derived from two components:

- **Data Hardness Measurement (α_D_ji):**
 1. For each harmful instruction *q_ji* from category *j*, the current LLM policy generates a response *eo_ji*. This is paired with the expert CoD response *o_ji*.
 2. Both *o_ji* and *eo_ji* are split into sub-sentences.
 3. An open-source text encoder (e.g., Llama encoder) is used to compute the cosine similarity between the embeddings of sub-sentence pairs.
 4. An overall similarity score (*W_ji*) is calculated for each response pair, representing the average maximal similarity of sub-sentences in the expert response to those in the generated response.
 5. The "difference" (δ_ji = 1 - W_ji) quantifies how dissimilar the responses are.
 6. Data hardness (α_D_ji) is then calculated as σ(δ_ji) / σ(δ_j_bar), where σ is the sigmoid function and δ_j_bar is the mean difference for category *j*. A higher α_D_ji indicates an "easier" sample with higher confidence, assigning greater loss weight.

- **Model Responsiveness Measurement (α_M_j):**
 1. For each sample pair, the "reward gap" (*R_ji*) is calculated using the trained shadow reward model *R_j(q_ji, o_ji) - R_j(q_ji, eo_ji)*.
 2. Outlier reward gaps are filtered out using a mask vector based on the squared distance from the average reward gap for the category.
 3. The filtered average reward gap for category *j* (R_P_θ_j_bar) is computed.
 4. Model responsiveness (α_M_j) is derived as σ(R_P_θ_j_bar) / σ(R_j_bar), where R_j_bar is the overall average reward gap for the category. A larger α_M_j indicates stronger model responsiveness and clear reward gaps.

- **Hardness Combination:** The final combined hardness coefficient (α_ji) is the product of data hardness and model responsiveness (α_ji = α_D_ji * α_M_j). This multiplicative form acts as an AND-gate, emphasizing samples only when they are both semantically distinct from the generated output (data-hard) and still uncertain for the model (model-uncertain), preventing over-optimization on trivial cases and promoting stable learning.

#### 3.4. Dynamic Reward for LLM Alignment

The core LLM alignment is performed using a modified Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), referred to as DR-IRL (Algorithm 3 in supplementary materials). GRPO is a PPO variant that reduces variance by using group-level comparative scores from multiple sampled policy outputs. DR-IRL enhances GRPO by:
1. Using the category-specific shadow reward models (*R_j*) to score responses.
2. Dynamically scaling the advantage function. For each harmful instruction *q*, *G* responses are generated. The advantage (A_j_i) for each response is calculated by multiplying its normalized reward relative to the group mean and standard deviation by the combined hardness coefficient α_j(q) (Equation 12).
3. The LLM policy (π_θ) is then iteratively updated by optimizing an objective function *J_j_DR-IRL(θ)* (Equation 13), which incorporates this dynamically scaled advantage and a KL-divergence regularization term against a reference policy (π_ref). The reference policy is periodically updated to the latest policy in an outer loop.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** For baseline training, UltraFeedback, PKU-SafeRLHF, and JailbreakV-28k were used. For DR-IRL, the custom balanced CoD safety dataset (described above) was employed.
- **Models:** Qwen-2-7B and Llama-3.1-8B (and 3B for ablations) were used as base LLMs.
- **Baselines:** A range of methods were compared, including Base, CoT, SFT, DPO, SACPO, Self-Rewarding, STAIR, and standard GRPO. The paper also introduced an "IRL" baseline, which is GRPO with category-specific shadow reward models but without dynamic scaling.
- **Benchmarks:**
 - **Harmlessness:** StrongReject (goodness score against jailbreak attacks), XsTest (refusal rate for exaggerated safety), WildChat (refusal rate for toxic prompts), and Stereotype (refusal rate for stereotype-related prompts).
 - **Helpfulness/Utility:** GSM8k (mathematical reasoning), AlpacaEval2.0 (HHH, helpfulness/harmlessness/honesty), SimpleQA (truthfulness), and AdvGLUE (general language understanding robustness).
- **Implementation:** Experiments utilized 4-8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80GB memory, PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, and TRL. DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 and FlashAttention-2 were used for memory optimization. Reward models were trained for 3-5 epochs, and DR-IRL fine-tuning for 4-6 epochs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated that DR-IRL consistently outperformed baseline methods across various benchmarks and LLMs, indicating enhanced safety alignment without compromising model utility.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance (Table 1)

- **Harmlessness:** DR-IRL achieved the highest StrongReject scores (0.9361 for Llama-3.1-8B and 0.8798 for Qwen-2-7B) and showed leading performance in XsTest, WildChat, and Stereotype benchmarks across both LLM backbones. For instance, on Llama-3.1-8B, DR-IRL achieved 99.00% on XsTest and 74.21% on WildChat.
- **Helpfulness/Utility:** The method also set new state-of-the-art results on AdvGLUE, GSM8k, and HHH, suggesting that the safety enhancements did not incur a significant "alignment tax" on general task performance. For example, Llama-3.1-8B with DR-IRL achieved 70.71% on AdvGLUE, 88.10% on GSM8k, and 86.16% on HHH.

#### 4.2. Category-wise Refusal Rates (Figure 3)

DR-IRL achieved the highest refusal rate across all seven specific harmful instruction categories (Insult, Unfairness and Discrimination, Crimes and Illegal Activities, Physical Harm, Mental Health, Privacy and Property, Ethics and Morality). This indicates a comprehensive and fine-grained safety alignment capability across diverse threat types.

#### 4.3. Detailed Analysis and Ablation Studies

- **Hardness Ablation Study (Figure 4):**
 - Removing both data-level (α_D) and model-level (α_M) hardness coefficients (the "No Hardness" variant) resulted in a notable degradation of harmlessness, with StrongReject scores dropping by approximately 4 percentage points.
 - Removing only data-level hardness (w/o α_D) primarily affected refusal precision, leading to higher WildChat toxicity and lower XsTest refusal rates.
 - Removing only model-level hardness (w/o α_M) caused greater fluctuations in general-capability metrics like SimpleQA and GSM8k.
 - These results support the conclusion that α_D contributes to fine-grained safety enforcement, while α_M stabilizes overall model usefulness.

- **Cost-Performance Trade-off of Per-Category Reward Models (Table 2):** Training seven per-category reward models incurred a modest 20% increase in compute time (from 100 to 120 GPU-h for LLaMA-3.1-8B) but yielded tangible gains in harmlessness metrics (e.g., +1.79 percentage points on StrongReject, +2.73 pp on WildChat). This suggests that category-specific reward models reduce "reward interference" and sharpen reward signals, justifying the computational overhead.

- **Combination Rule: Product vs. Weighted Sum (Table 3):** The multiplicative combination of data hardness and model responsiveness (α_D * α_M) consistently outperformed additive (weighted-sum) alternatives. This indicates that the product rule, acting as a strict AND-gate, is more effective at identifying and emphasizing samples that are simultaneously difficult in content and uncertain for the model, leading to more stable and robust optimization.

- **Effectiveness of CoD in Data Generation (Table 7):** Replacing Chain-of-Thought (CoT) with Chain-of-Draft (CoD) for generating safety demonstrations preserved or slightly improved accuracy while significantly reducing token usage (by 73-76%) and generation latency (by two-thirds). This validates CoD as a more efficient and effective method for creating high-quality demonstration data.

- **Defense Against Jailbreak Attacks (Table 4):** DR-IRL exhibited superior robustness against known jailbreak attacks, achieving higher refusal rates compared to baselines on GCG, AutoDAN, and DRA. For LLaMA-3.1-8B, DR-IRL obtained refusal rates of 59.00% (AutoDAN), 96.98% (GCG), and 64.92% (DRA), outperforming the Base model and STAIR.

- **Shadow Reward Model Quality (Table 8):** The internally trained shadow reward models achieved an overall pairwise accuracy of 91.1% in correctly ranking safe refusals over model replies across the seven harmful categories. This performance surpassed that of external baselines like OpenAI RM and Anthropic Harmless RM in every category.

- **Impact of Reward Model Size (Figure 5):** DR-IRL's effectiveness scaled to smaller 3B models (Llama-3.1-3B and Qwen-2-3B). It consistently outperformed baselines, improving StrongReject scores by 4-5 percentage points and reducing WildChat toxicity, suggesting that the difficulty-aware shadow reward mechanism is adaptable across different model sizes without extensive re-tuning.

- **Difficulty-Weighted Updates Improve PPO, DPO, and GRPO (Table 5):** The application of the hardness coefficient (α) as a general weighting mechanism was tested across PPO, DPO, and GRPO. In all cases, the difficulty-weighted variants (denoted with "-S" suffix) consistently improved harmlessness metrics without degrading general capabilities, confirming the broad applicability and beneficial impact of dynamic scaling.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces DR-IRL, an alignment approach that addresses key limitations in existing LLM safety methodologies. Its significance lies in its multi-faceted strategy to enhance the harmlessness and reliability of large language models.

- **Enhanced Safety Alignment:** By training category-specific reward models via Inverse Reinforcement Learning on a balanced demonstration dataset, DR-IRL provides a more granular and accurate representation of human safety values. This approach moves beyond generic safety principles to address distinct types of harm, leading to models that are more adept at identifying and refusing harmful instructions across a broader spectrum of threats, including long-tail phenomena often neglected by imbalanced datasets.

- **Improved Optimization Efficiency:** The dynamic reward scaling mechanism, which adjusts rewards based on both data-level hardness and model-level responsiveness, represents a methodological advancement. This adaptive weighting system allows the alignment process to focus computational resources on the most challenging or uncertain cases, preventing over-optimization on trivial examples and ensuring more effective learning for critical safety scenarios. This contributes to a more efficient and stable training regimen, improving the overall safety-utility trade-off.

- **Robustness Against Attacks:** The demonstrated superiority of DR-IRL against various jailbreak attacks indicates an enhanced robustness of the aligned LLMs. This capability is crucial for the secure deployment of LLMs in public-facing applications, where models are frequently targeted by adversarial prompts designed to bypass safety filters.

- **Reproducible Research & Benchmarking:** The construction of a balanced safety dataset using Chain of Draft (CoD) demonstrations and the development of category-specific reward models contribute valuable resources to the research community. These components can serve as reproducible benchmarks for future alignment research, facilitating consistent evaluation and comparison of new methodologies. The CoD method itself offers a more efficient and effective way to generate high-quality training data.

The potential impact of DR-IRL extends to several domains:
- **Safer LLM Applications:** By improving both harmlessness and usefulness, DR-IRL can contribute to the development of more trustworthy LLMs for a wide array of applications in education, healthcare, customer service, and public information dissemination. This can help mitigate risks associated with disinformation, hate speech, and other forms of harmful content.
- **Ethical AI Development:** The methodology fosters a more nuanced approach to AI safety, moving towards models that can exhibit fine-grained safety reasoning. This aligns with broader efforts to ensure responsible and ethical AI development.

**Limitations and Safeguards:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations. The balanced safety corpus, although carefully curated, is partly synthetic, which may mean it could miss subtle cultural cues or very rare adversarial patterns present in real-world interactions. Furthermore, the computational cost associated with training a separate shadow reward model for each harmful category is a practical consideration, especially for scaling to finer taxonomies or rapidly evolving threat landscapes.

To mitigate potential misuse and ensure responsible deployment, the authors outline several safeguards: a layered release strategy involving automatic and manual filtering of prompts and responses, a research-only license for datasets, reward models, and code (requiring a data-use agreement for access), and access to aligned LLMs only via a rate-limited API with toxicity monitoring. Full model weights are to be shared only with accredited researchers after ethics review. These measures aim to balance research advancement with public safety.
