# Min-Max Regret Task Allocation and Planning of Heterogeneous Multi-Robot System in Partially Known Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A planning framework for heterogeneous multi-robot systems, the Extended Planning Decision Tree (E-PDT), enables robust execution of complex temporal-logic tasks in partially known environments by minimizing min-max regret. This approach achieves near-linear scalability for thousands of robots and dynamically adapts missions based on real-time observations, effectively balancing exploration and exploitation without relying on prior probabilities.

## Problem

- Existing multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) methods face computational bottlenecks when handling complex temporal logic tasks in partially known environments.
- The inherent dilemma of balancing exploration for information gathering and exploitation for task progress in uncertain environments, often requiring unavailable prior probability distributions.
- Lack of scalability in advanced planning algorithms, limiting their applicability to large-scale heterogeneous multi-robot systems (HMRS) with numerous robots and task types.

## Method

- Introduces the **Extended Planning Decision Tree (E-PDT)** framework, leveraging ==min-max regret optimization== to minimize the worst-case opportunity cost of incomplete information.
- Proposes ==Region-Binding Atomic Propositions (RbAP)== to formally integrate resource uncertainty into the ==task automaton== structure, allowing for explicit reasoning about unknown resources.
- Develops a regret-based ==Branch-and-Bound (BnB)== pruning strategy within the E-PDT to dynamically prune suboptimal policies, significantly reducing computational complexity for large HMRS.

## Results

- The E-PDT framework demonstrated **near-linear scalability**, coordinating up to **9,000 robots** within seconds, a significant improvement over MILP baselines that failed beyond **6,000 robots**.
- Simulation experiments showed E-PDT effectively balances exploration and exploitation, with its task completion cost outperforming **MILP** in moderately to highly uncertain environments as resource existence probability increased.
- Physical experiments validated the framework's real-world applicability, generating robust contingent policy trees that dynamically adapted robot assignments based on real-time observations and resource discoveries in **milliseconds**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Min-max regret optimization offers a robust planning strategy for partially known environments by accounting for the worst-case cost difference compared to hindsight-optimal decisions.
> - Integrating environmental uncertainty directly into temporal logic specifications via RbAP allows for a formal yet flexible representation of unknown resource locations.
> - A hierarchical search coupled with an admissible regret-based pruning heuristic can achieve near-linear scalability in multi-robot planning, overcoming challenges faced by traditional optimization methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses the problem of task allocation and planning for Heterogeneous Multi-Robot Systems (HMRS) in environments where information is incomplete. This area is critical for applications such as warehouse logistics and disaster response, where robots must perform complex tasks while navigating uncertain conditions.

Current research in multi-robot task allocation often leverages formal methods, particularly Linear Temporal Logic (LTL), to express rich and complex task specifications. However, existing approaches encounter two primary challenges when operating in partially known environments (PKE):
1. **Environmental Uncertainty:** Traditional methods, such as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), frequently rely on prior probabilities of environmental states, which are often unavailable or difficult to obtain in novel or rapidly changing real-world scenarios. Alternatively, purely worst-case analysis can lead to overly conservative and inefficient planning strategies. Some prior work has introduced regret as a metric to balance exploration (information gathering) and exploitation (task execution), but these methods have suffered from high computational complexity, limiting their scalability.
2. **Computational Complexity:** The state space for task allocation and planning problems grows exponentially with the number of robots, tasks, and environmental regions. Numerous efforts have focused on reducing this complexity through techniques like hierarchical search, problem decomposition, sampling-based optimal control, and alternative automaton representations. The Planning Decision Tree (PDT) framework, for instance, has demonstrated scalability for large robot fleets in fully known environments and has been extended for reactive planning in uncertain semantic environments, but it has not proactively addressed risk avoidance during initial planning stages under resource uncertainty.

This paper positions itself by building upon existing theoretical foundations of syntactically co-safe LTL (scLTL) semantics, the PDT framework, and general regret formulations. It aims to bridge the gap by simultaneously addressing the challenges of formal representation of uncertainty, explicit measurement of uncertainty (through regret), and computational complexity for large-scale HMRS in PKE, focusing on proactive risk minimization during the initial planning phase rather than purely reactive replanning. It seeks to provide a robust planning framework that balances exploration and exploitation without relying on prior probabilities, while maintaining scalability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the practical and computational challenges associated with deploying large-scale Heterogeneous Multi-Robot Systems (HMRS) to execute complex, temporal-logic-specified tasks in environments with incomplete information. The authors identify several specific issues:

1. **Computational Bottleneck with Temporal Logic Tasks in PKE:** Existing task allocation methods often struggle to handle both intricate temporal logic constraints and environmental uncertainty concurrently. The combined complexity frequently leads to exponential computational demands, rendering them impractical for large-scale HMRS.
2. **Balancing Exploration and Exploitation:** In partially known environments, robots face an inherent dilemma: whether to prioritize exploiting known resources for immediate task progress or to explore uncertain regions to gather new information that could potentially lead to more efficient task completion in the long term. Traditional approaches either rely on unavailable prior probability distributions (e.g., in MDPs) or adopt overly conservative worst-case analyses that neglect the benefits of exploration.
3. **Lack of Scalability:** Many advanced planning algorithms, while theoretically sound, do not scale efficiently to systems involving thousands of robots or numerous task types. This limits their applicability in real-world scenarios such as large-scale logistics or disaster response.
4. **Inadequate Formal Representation of Uncertainty:** Current temporal logic formalisms often assume a fully known environment or lack explicit mechanisms to represent and reason about resource uncertainty across multiple regions within the automaton structure.

To address these motivations, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a Robust Planning Framework:** The primary goal is to create a planning framework that can simultaneously manage high-level logical constraints (expressed in scLTL) and environmental uncertainty without compromising scalability.
2. **Formulate Min-Max Regret Optimization:** To resolve the exploration-exploitation dilemma, the problem is formulated as a min-max regret optimization. This aims to minimize the maximum possible "regret," defined as the difference between the actual cost incurred by a chosen policy and the cost of the optimal policy that could have been achieved with perfect hindsight (i.e., if the environment were fully known).
3. **Introduce Region-Binding Atomic Proposition (RbAP):** A novel formal construct, RbAP, is proposed to integrate resource uncertainty directly into the automaton structure. This allows atomic propositions to be bound to sets of regions, including both guaranteed and potential locations for resources.
4. **Propose the Extended Planning Decision Tree (E-PDT) with Regret-based Branch-and-Bound (BnB):** The existing PDT architecture is extended to incorporate uncertainty. This involves equipping the E-PDT with a regret-based BnB strategy designed to dynamically prune suboptimal policies, thereby reducing computational complexity and balancing information gathering with task completion.
5. **Achieve Near-Linear Scalability:** A critical objective is to ensure that the proposed framework achieves near-linear scalability with respect to the number of robots and robot types, making it suitable for large-scale HMRS deployments.
6. **Validate Performance and Efficiency:** The framework's feasibility, completeness, robustness, and computational efficiency are to be validated through theoretical analysis and extensive numerical, simulation, and physical experiments, with comparisons against established baselines like MILP.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a robust planning framework, called the Extended Planning Decision Tree (E-PDT), designed for min-max regret optimization in partially known environments (PKE). This framework integrates several key components: a formal definition of PKE, a novel atomic proposition type (RbAP), a regret-based policy evaluation, and a Branch-and-Bound (BnB) pruning strategy within the E-PDT architecture.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The problem addresses task allocation for HMRS operating in a PKE, subject to scLTL temporal constraints, with the objective of collecting resources while minimizing regret.

- **Partially Known Environments (PKE):** The environment is modeled with a known topological structure, where the set of possible resource types for each region is known. Each resource type has at least one "guaranteed" region, while its presence in other "potential" regions is uncertain. Upon a robot's visit, resource presence is unambiguously revealed and remains static. This involves definitions of:
 - **Workspace and Region Belief:** A finite set of regions `R`, and each region's belief `r_b,i = (r_i, pot_i, cer_i)`, indicating potential (`pot_i`) and certain (`cer_i`) resources.
 - **World Belief (`W_b`):** The collection of all region beliefs representing the robot's current knowledge.
 - **Environmental Instantiation (`E`):** A determined instance of the world.
 - **Compatibility:** `E` is compatible with `W_b` if `cer_e,i ⊆ (cer_b,i ∪ pot_b,i)` for all regions.
 - **Partially Known Environment (`E`):** The set of all environmental instantiations compatible with the initial world belief.
- **Heterogeneous Multi-Robot Systems (HMRS):** A fleet `A` of `n_a` robots categorized into `n_t` distinct types `T`. Heterogeneity is defined by resource discovery and carrying capabilities.
- **Regions Binding Atomic Proposition (RbAP):** A 5-tuple `ap = (l_p, m_p, R_p, GR_p, A_p)` that explicitly binds atomic propositions (`l_p`) to a set of regions (`R_p`), a resource type (`m_p`), a guaranteed region (`GR_p`), and the robot set (`A_p`) required for the task. This structure allows integration of uncertainty into the DFA.
- **Policy and Regret:** A policy `π = (ρ, ξ)` consists of a sub-task (`ρ`) derived from DFA decomposition and a choice set (`ξ`) for each proposition, indicating whether to explore or exploit. A plan `τ` is a sequence of region visits for each robot.
 - **Regret:** Defined as `reg_Wb(π) = max_E∈E_Wb [cost(π, E) - min_π'∈Π cost(π', E)]`. It quantifies the opportunity cost of incomplete information, aiming to minimize the worst-case difference between the policy's cost and the hindsight-optimal cost for any compatible environmental instantiation.

**B. Extended Planning Decision Tree (E-PDT) Framework**
The E-PDT framework (Algorithm 1) extends the PDT by incorporating uncertainty handling and regret minimization. An E-PDT node `Nd_i = (Pol, A_s, EP_pre, ET_pre, Cost, B_s, Reg, O)` includes the policy `Pol`, specific robot assignments `A_s`, predicted locations and times, DFA state `B_s`, and new elements for PKE: `Reg` (regret) and `O` (world belief). Node `Cost` is redefined based on observation results.

The core of the framework is the `treeExpansion` function (Algorithm 2), which iteratively expands parent nodes to generate child nodes under environmental uncertainty.

1. **Node Selection:** From the current DFA state, the algorithm greedily selects the transition edge associated with the maximal set of atomic propositions. This heuristic prioritizes concurrency to minimize makespan.
2. **Policy Generation:** For the selected subtask, `generatePolicy` creates hybrid policies by combinatorially assigning either "exploration" or "exploitation" modes to each atomic proposition. This balances information gain with task fulfillment.
3. **Robot Allocation:** A two-level greedy mechanism is employed:
 - **Inter-region Assignment:** Iteratively evaluates unassigned regions and selects the best region-team pairing with the lowest makespan.
 - **Intra-team Composition:** For a single region, it selects a coalition of available robots to satisfy task requirements with minimum makespan.
 - `alloExploit(ap)`: First assigns a robot team to the *guaranteed* region for `ap`, then dispatches surplus robots to *potential* regions for parallel exploration.
 - `alloExplore(ap)`: Allocates robots exclusively to *potential* regions for pure information gathering.
4. **Observation Simulation:** The `geneObs` function projects all possible environmental observations based on exploration actions. Each distinct observation leads to a different child node, with the same policy but varying environmental outcomes.
5. **Automaton State Update:** `modifyAutomatonState` updates the DFA state for each child node based on the simulated observation. For exploration outcomes, it identifies fulfilled RbAPs and selects the maximal transition path.
6. **Regret Computation (Heuristic):** To address the intractability of direct regret calculation, a heuristic `˜reg(π)` (Equation 3) is introduced:
 `˜reg(π) = max_Nd∈Nds(π) [Nd.Cost + H(Nd.O) − opt(Nd.O)]`
 - `Nd.Cost` is the accumulated cost from the root.
 - `H(Nd.O)` is an estimated future cost-to-go, calculated under an "optimistic resource assumption" where all potential regions are assumed to contain the resource.
 - `opt(Nd.O)` is the minimum possible cost from the current state under this same optimistic assumption.
 This heuristic provides a strict lower bound for the true regret, ensuring the admissibility of the BnB strategy.
7. **Regret-Based Branch-and-Bound (BnB) Pruning:** A crucial component for computational efficiency, this strategy (Algorithm 2, `pruneByRegret`) maintains a global upper bound `U_best` on regret.
 - **Pruning Rules (Definition 11):**
 1. Nodes reaching an accepting DFA state are pruned.
 2. Nodes representing states and observations already encountered with smaller regret are pruned.
 3. **Early-Exit Pruning:** For a candidate policy, if any single sequential observation `o_k` yields a regret `˜reg(π, o_k)` that is greater than or equal to `U_best`, the evaluation of that policy is immediately terminated, and all its associated child nodes are pruned. This avoids computing all possible outcomes for suboptimal policies.

**C. Plan Generation**
The `getPlan` function extracts a robust contingent policy tree. This tree represents optimal policies and concrete robot assignments for every reachable belief state. During online execution, real-world observations guide the system along the precomputed branches, dictating subsequent robot assignments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical analyses and empirical validations through numerical, simulation, and physical experiments to support the proposed E-PDT framework.

**A. Theoretical Analysis**
- **Feasibility and Completeness:** The framework guarantees that any optimal plan `τ*` generated by E-PDT is feasible with the scLTL formula (Theorem 1). Furthermore, the algorithm is proven to be complete, meaning it is guaranteed to find a feasible plan if one exists, provided the regret-based pruning rule is disabled (Theorem 2).
- **Admissibility of Regret Heuristic:** The estimated regret `˜reg(π)` (Equation 3) is formally proven to be a valid lower bound of the true regret (Lemma 1). This property ensures the admissibility of the Branch-and-Bound (BnB) strategy, meaning the pruning mechanism does not prematurely discard potentially optimal solutions.
- **Computational Complexity:** The total computational complexity (`C_total`) is analyzed as the product of the number of visited nodes (`N_visited`) and the cost to process a single node (`C_node`). While the worst-case number of visited nodes can be exponential (`O(|Π| · 2^|R_unc|·D_max)` due to uncertainty branching, an inherent aspect of min-max regret problems), the cost to process a single node (`C_node`) is `O(2^|AP_sub| · |AP| · |R_s| · (n_a log n_a + n_t))`. This indicates that the single-node computation scales near-linearly with the number of robots (`n_a`) and linearly with the number of robot types (`n_t`). The BnB pruning strategy is presented as the primary mechanism to mitigate the exponential term in practice.

**B. Numerical Experiments**
Numerical experiments evaluated the framework's components and overall performance:
- **Maximum Transition Edge Strategy:** Comparisons between selecting the maximal transition edge (Method 1) and traversing all transition edges (Methods 2 and 3) showed that Method 1 generally achieved faster computation speeds and the lowest or one of the lowest actual execution costs across various scLTL formulas (Table I). This suggests that prioritizing concurrency by selecting the maximum transition edge allows for more aggressive pruning of the uncertainty space, leading to improved planning efficiency.
- **E-PDT vs. MILP Baseline:** The E-PDT framework was compared against a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) baseline, configured for worst-case planning towards guaranteed regions.
 - **Scalability with Number of Robots (`n_a`):** E-PDT demonstrated near-linear scalability, coordinating up to 9,000 robots within seconds. In contrast, the MILP approach showed a drastic increase in computation time and failed to generate solutions for over 6,000 robots (Table II).
 - **Scalability with Number of Robot Types (`n_t`):** E-PDT exhibited linear scalability, handling up to 100 robot types efficiently. The MILP baseline's computation time significantly increased and eventually failed for higher numbers of robot types (Table III).
 - **Efficiency and Solution Quality:** Despite addressing the theoretically more demanding Min-Max Regret problem (which requires computing hindsight-optimal costs for potential outcomes), E-PDT achieved orders-of-magnitude faster solution times than the MILP baseline, highlighting the practical efficiency gained from its regret-based pruning strategy.

**C. Simulation Experiments**
Simulations in a 60x60 continuous workspace assessed task completion cost under varying environmental complexity and uncertainty for a fleet of 6 robots (3 types, 2 per type) with a specific scLTL formula.
- **Robustness Premium and Crossover Probability:** In scenarios with low resource existence probability (high uncertainty), E-PDT incurred a "Robustness Premium," resulting in a higher task completion cost compared to the MILP baseline. This is attributed to the overhead of exploration. However, as the probability of resource existence (`p`) increased (reducing uncertainty), the benefits of exploration outweighed the risks, causing E-PDT's cost to decrease and eventually outperform the MILP baseline (Figs. 3, 4). The "Crossover Probability," where E-PDT becomes superior, shifted left (to lower `p` values) as environmental complexity (more regions, larger minimum distances) increased, indicating that regret-based planning is more advantageous in complex PKE.
- **Scalability with Fleet Size:** Expanding the fleet size (e.g., to 4 or 6 robots per category) demonstrated that the algorithm effectively utilized additional robots for concurrent exploration and exploitation, enhancing mission efficiency even in uncertain environments (Fig. 6). Robot trajectories illustrated adaptive plan execution based on discoveries, with tasks terminating early upon successful resource identification (Fig. 5).

**D. Physical Experiments**
Two experiments using Wheeltec R550 mobile robots and an OptiTrack system validated the framework's real-world applicability.
- **Experiment 1 (3 robots, 3 resource types, 6 regions):** The algorithm generated a policy tree in 0.0065 seconds. The initial policy dispatched one robot to explore a potential region while others went to guaranteed regions. Upon exploration failure, the plan dynamically redirected the exploring robot to its guaranteed region, successfully completing the mission.
- **Experiment 2 (4 robots, 2 types, 2 resource types, 6 regions):** The policy tree was generated in 0.0078 seconds, with an initial strategy prioritizing exploration for all robots. The plan adapted dynamically; exploration failures led to redirection to guaranteed regions, while a successful resource discovery by one robot immediately halted the search for that resource by others.

These experiments collectively confirm that E-PDT enables robust, adaptive, and efficient task allocation for HMRS in PKE, demonstrating significant advantages in scalability and decision quality compared to traditional methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that contributes to the field of multi-robot system planning by addressing a critical gap in handling environmental uncertainty and computational scalability simultaneously. The significance of this work lies in several aspects:

- **Robust Decision-Making under Uncertainty:** By formulating the problem as a min-max regret optimization and introducing the Region-Binding Atomic Proposition (RbAP), the framework provides a method for robots to make robust decisions in partially known environments without requiring prior probability distributions of resource locations. This proactive approach to minimizing worst-case regret ensures dependable performance even under unfavorable environmental realizations.
- **Balancing Exploration and Exploitation:** The E-PDT framework directly tackles the exploration-exploitation dilemma. It enables HMRS to strategically gather information in uncertain regions while simultaneously progressing towards task objectives based on known information. The simulation results demonstrate that while exploration might incur an initial "robustness premium," it ultimately leads to more efficient task completion in moderately and highly uncertain environments.
- **High Scalability for Large HMRS:** A major contribution is the near-linear scalability of the E-PDT framework with respect to the number of robots and robot types. This is achieved through the integration of a regret-based Branch-and-Bound (BnB) pruning strategy into an extended Planning Decision Tree (PDT) architecture. This scalability significantly surpasses traditional MILP-based approaches, which often become computationally intractable for large fleets.
- **Adaptive and Contingent Planning:** The framework generates a robust contingent policy tree, allowing the robot system to adapt its plan dynamically based on real-time observations of the environment. This is crucial for real-world deployment where environmental conditions can only be revealed through interaction.

The potential impact of this research is substantial, particularly for applications involving large-scale autonomous systems operating in complex and unpredictable environments:

- **Real-World Deployments:** The improved scalability and robust decision-making capabilities could enable the deployment of larger and more capable HMRS in critical applications such as urban search and rescue, hazardous environment monitoring, large-scale automated logistics (e.g., warehouses), and space exploration, where prior knowledge is often incomplete and adaptation is essential.
- **Reduced Operational Costs and Time:** By optimizing the balance between exploration and exploitation, the framework can lead to more efficient resource utilization and reduced mission completion times, offering economic benefits in industrial and service robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The RbAP concept provides a formal basis for integrating various types of semantic environmental uncertainty into temporal logic planning. The regret-based BnB strategy offers a paradigm for efficient pruning in complex decision spaces.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The current framework assumes a known static map topology with uncertain resource locations and is not designed to handle model-external disturbances or fully dynamic changes not enumerated in advance. It also relies on structural assumptions that may be challenging to satisfy in highly unstructured scenarios. Future work is proposed to extend the framework to fully dynamic and unknown environments by integrating reactive synthesis mechanisms and exploring learning methods for modeling complex, unstructured uncertainties.
