# LEAD: Learning Decomposition for Source-free Universal Domain Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> The LEAD framework introduces an orthogonal feature decomposition approach for Source-free Universal Domain Adaptation (SF-UniDA), enabling accurate and efficient identification of target-private unknown data without requiring access to source information. It consistently improves performance across various domain adaptation scenarios, boosting existing methods and significantly reducing computational time for pseudo-labeling compared to clustering-based techniques.

## Problem

- Many existing Source-free Universal Domain Adaptation (SF-UniDA) methods rely on hand-crafted thresholds to distinguish common from private "unknown" data, which are difficult to tune and often sub-optimal.
- Other SF-UniDA approaches utilize time-consuming and unstable iterative clustering strategies for data separation, which are inefficient for large, high-dimensional datasets due to the "curse of dimensionality."
- A core challenge in SF-UniDA is the accurate identification of target data belonging to common categories versus those from categories unknown to the source model, all without access to the original source data.

## Method

- Target features are decomposed into "source-known" and "source-unknown" components by applying ==Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)== to the pre-trained classifier's weight matrix.
- An adaptive, instance-level decision boundary is employed for ==pseudo-labeling==, estimated using a two-component ==Gaussian Mixture Model== on the magnitude of "source-unknown" feature projections and combined with target prototypes and source anchors.
- The model is optimized through a combined loss that includes ==pseudo-label learning==, ==feature decomposition regularization== to enhance the discriminative power of the source-unknown component, and ==feature consensus regularization==.

## Results

- LEAD achieved superior H-scores across OPDA, OSDA, and PDA scenarios on Office-31, Office-Home, VisDA, and DomainNet, notably increasing UMAD's H-score by up to **8.9%** and GLC's by **3.7%** in specific OPDA tasks when integrated.
- The framework demonstrated exceptional efficiency, deriving pseudo-label decision boundaries in 0.29 seconds on DomainNet, compared to 897.65 seconds for clustering-based methods like GLC, representing an improvement of several orders of magnitude.
- Instance-level adaptive decision boundaries improved the H-score from **63.1%** to **75.0%** on Office-Home in OPDA scenarios compared to a global threshold, validating the importance of adaptive techniques.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Target-private "unknown" data tend to project more strongly onto a feature space orthogonal to the features learned from the source model, offering a direct and robust indicator for unknown categories.
> - Adaptive, instance-level decision boundaries are more effective than global thresholds for distinguishing common from private data in SF-UniDA, as they can better account for varying covariate shifts.
> - The proposed orthogonal feature decomposition and adaptive pseudo-labeling strategy can seamlessly integrate with and significantly enhance the performance of existing SF-UniDA methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work operates within the rapidly evolving landscape of Domain Adaptation (DA), a critical area in machine learning addressing the challenge of model generalization across different data distributions. Deep neural networks (DNNs), despite their impressive performance, often struggle when tested on data from a distribution different from their training data (known as covariate shift). Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) aims to mitigate this by transferring knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain.

Traditionally, most UDA methods assumed a "closed-set" scenario, where the label spaces of the source and target domains are identical. However, real-world applications frequently encounter more complex "label shifts," where the target domain may contain classes unknown to the source, or vice-versa. This led to the development of Universal Domain Adaptation (UniDA), which encompasses various label shift scenarios like Partial Domain Adaptation (PDA, target label space is a subset of source), Open-set Domain Adaptation (OSDA, target has private unknown classes), and Open-Partial Domain Adaptation (OPDA, both domains have private classes, and some common classes). UniDA is challenging because it assumes no prior knowledge about the label overlap.

A further practical constraint emerged with data privacy regulations (e.g., GDPR) and logistical challenges, leading to Source-free Domain Adaptation (SFDA), where the original source data is inaccessible during target adaptation. When SFDA is combined with the complexities of UniDA, it creates Source-free Universal Domain Adaptation (SF-UniDA), which is the specific and highly challenging problem addressed by this paper. SF-UniDA is crucial for real-world deployment in areas like autonomous driving or medical imaging, where sensitive source data might not be permitted for transfer or storage.

Existing SF-UniDA methods often face two significant limitations:
- **Reliance on hand-crafted thresholds:** Many approaches use fixed thresholds on prediction confidence or entropy to distinguish common from private "unknown" data. These thresholds are tedious to tune, often sub-optimal, and lack universality across diverse scenarios.
- **Time-consuming and unstable clustering:** Other methods employ iterative clustering strategies (e.g., K-means) to identify target-private data. These can be computationally intensive, especially for large datasets, and prone to instability or poor performance in high-dimensional feature spaces due to the "curse of dimensionality" (as noted in Figure 1a of the paper).

"LEAD: Learning Decomposition" directly confronts these limitations by introducing a novel approach based on orthogonal feature decomposition. While feature decomposition has been explored in DA/DG for separating content and style, its application to explicitly distinguish "source-known" from "source-unknown" components for common/private data separation in SF-UniDA is a unique contribution. This work seeks to provide a more elegant, efficient, and robust solution that overcomes the pitfalls of existing thresholding and clustering-based methods, thereby pushing the boundaries of practical and ethical domain adaptation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the LEAD framework is to develop an effective and efficient method for Source-free Universal Domain Adaptation (SF-UniDA). Specifically, the authors aim to accurately distinguish target data belonging to common categories (shared with the source) from target-private "unknown" data, without requiring access to the original source data or prior knowledge about label space overlap.

This objective is driven by several key motivations:

- **Practicality of SF-UniDA:** The increasing importance of data protection policies, such as GDPR, makes concurrent access to source data during target adaptation increasingly impractical. SF-UniDA, which only uses a pre-trained source model, aligns better with these real-world constraints, making it a crucial area for research.
- **Addressing Limitations of Current SF-UniDA Methods:**
 - **Sub-optimal Hand-crafted Thresholds:** Existing methods often rely on manually defined, global thresholds to separate common and private data. These thresholds are difficult to tune, rarely optimal for all scenarios, and fail to adapt to the varying degrees of covariate shift across different categories or even within a category's samples.
 - **Inefficiency and Instability of Iterative Clustering:** Approaches that use iterative clustering (e.g., GLC) for data separation are computationally intensive, especially for large, high-dimensional datasets. The "curse of dimensionality" can also lead to unstable and imprecise cluster assignments, hindering accurate identification of target-private data, as empirically suggested by the paper (Figure 1a).
- **Leveraging a Novel Insight for Data Separation:** The core motivation for LEAD stems from a hypothesis rarely explored in the literature: even with covariate shifts, target-private data from unknown categories are likely to exhibit a higher proportion of components from the orthogonal complement (source-unknown) space relative to the features learned during source pre-training. Figure 1b provides empirical support for this idea, showing distinct distributions for common and private data in the source-unknown space. This new perspective promises a more principled and robust way to identify target-private data.
- **Developing a More Efficient and Adaptive Solution:** The authors are motivated to create a solution that not only improves accuracy but also significantly reduces the computational overhead associated with iterative clustering, offering a faster and more resource-friendly approach for pseudo-labeling. This involves designing adaptive, instance-level decision boundaries that are more responsive to individual data characteristics rather than relying on a single, global threshold.
- **Creating a Complementary Framework:** The motivation also includes designing a framework that is not necessarily a standalone replacement but can complement and enhance existing SF-UniDA methods, thereby offering broad applicability and boosting their performance.

By focusing on feature decomposition and instance-level adaptive decision boundaries, LEAD aims to overcome the major hurdles in SF-UniDA, offering a more elegant, efficient, and robust solution for recognizing common and unknown data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The LEAD (LEArning Decomposition) framework proposes a novel approach to SF-UniDA by tackling the problem of distinguishing common and private data through orthogonal feature decomposition and instance-level adaptive decision boundaries.

The SF-UniDA setting assumes a pre-trained source model $f^s_\theta = h^s_\theta \circ g^s_\theta$, where $g^s_\theta$ is the feature extractor and $h^s_\theta$ is the classifier. During target adaptation, the classifier $h^t_\theta$ is frozen (initialized from $h^s_\theta$), and only a target-specific feature extractor $g^t_\theta$ is learned, using only unlabeled target data.

The core methodology of LEAD involves three main stages:

#### 3.1. Orthogonal Feature Decomposition

The foundational idea is that features of target-private data, being "unknown" to the source model, should reside more in a space orthogonal to the source-learned features.
- **Defining Spaces:** The authors define two orthogonal feature spaces:
 - **Source-known space ($F_{knw}$):** This space is spanned by the weight vectors of the pre-trained classifier ($W_{cls} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times D}$, where C is the number of source classes and D is the feature dimension).
 - **Source-unknown space ($F_{unk}$):** This is the orthogonal complement of $F_{knw}$.
- **Technical Implementation:** To obtain these orthogonal spaces, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is applied to the classifier weight matrix $W_{cls} = U \Sigma V^T$. The columns of $V$ (orthogonal unitary matrix) corresponding to the first C singular values span $F_{knw}$, while the remaining $D-C$ columns span $F_{unk}$.
- **Feature Projection:** For any normalized target feature $z^t_i = g^t_\theta(x^t_i) / ||g^t_\theta(x^t_i)||_2$, it can be decomposed into two orthogonal components: $z^t_{i,knw}$ (projection onto $F_{knw}$) and $z^t_{i,unk}$ (projection onto $F_{unk}$).
- **Private Data Indicator:** The magnitude of the projection onto the source-unknown space, $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$, is proposed as a direct indicator for target-private data. A higher value suggests the instance is more likely from an unknown category.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Separation for Private Data

A simple threshold on $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$ might not be robust due to covariate shifts. LEAD introduces an adaptive, instance-level decision boundary.
- **GMM for Distribution Estimation:** A two-component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is applied to the empirical distribution of $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$. This helps estimate the expected magnitudes for common ($\mu_{com}$) and private ($\mu_{pri}$) data, where $\mu_{com} < \mu_{pri}$.
- **Collecting Prototypes and Anchors:**
 - **Target Prototypes ($c^t_c$):** For each potential common category $c$, target prototypes are constructed by averaging the features of top-K instances (chosen by highest softmax probabilities) in the target domain. The number K is adaptively determined based on the estimated number of target categories ($\hat{C}_t$) using the Silhouette metric. (Details in Appendix B.1)
 - **Source Anchors ($c^s_c$):** These are directly derived from the classifier weights $W_{cls}$, representing the centroids of source categories. They are crucial for scenarios involving source-private categories.
- **Instance-level Common Score ($\epsilon_{i,c}$):** A novel metric, $\epsilon_{i,c}$, is designed to quantify how "common" a target instance $x^t_i$ is to a category $c$. It considers distances (cosine similarity-based) to both target prototypes ($c^t_c$) and source anchors ($c^s_c$). Different exponential functions are used for target prototype similarity ($\epsilon^t_{i,c}$) and source anchor similarity ($\epsilon^s_{i,c}$), with source anchor proximity being a stronger indicator of commonality. These are combined using a geometric mean: $\epsilon_{i,c} = \sqrt{\epsilon^t_{i,c} \cdot \epsilon^s_{i,c}}$.
- **Adaptive Pseudo-labeling:** The final instance-level decision boundary for each instance $x^t_i$ and category $c$ is defined as $\rho_{i,c} = \mu_c + \epsilon_{i,c} \cdot (\mu_{pri} - \mu_c)$. Here, $\mu_c$ is the expectation of $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$ for category $c$.
 - An instance $x^t_i$ is pseudo-labeled as **"unknown"** if $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2 \ge \rho_{i,c}$.
 - Otherwise, it's pseudo-labeled as the common category with the highest common score ($\text{argmax}(\epsilon_i)$).

#### 3.3. Optimization Objectives

The training process combines three loss functions:
- **Pseudo-label Learning Loss ($L_{ce}$):** A cross-entropy loss is applied using the pseudo-labels generated by the adaptive separation strategy. To account for varying certainty, a Student's t distribution assigns a weight $\tau^t_i$ to each pseudo-label based on the distance between $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$ and its decision boundary $\rho_{i,\kappa}$. For instances identified as private, a uniform distribution is used instead of a C+1 class.
- **Feature Decomposition Regularization Loss ($L_{reg}$):** This loss encourages $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$ to be high for private data and low for common data, relative to $||z^t_{i,knw}||_2$. It promotes the discriminative power of the source-unknown component for private data.
- **Feature Consensus Regularization Loss ($L_{con}$):** This objective enforces consistency among nearest neighbors in the feature space, a common practice in SFDA to improve model stability. (Details in Appendix B.2)
- **Overall Loss:** The total loss is a weighted sum: $L = \lambda \cdot L_{ce} + L_{reg} + L_{con}$, where $\lambda$ is a trade-off hyper-parameter.

#### 3.4. Integration with Existing Methods

A key aspect of LEAD is its complementary nature. It can be integrated into existing SF-UniDA methods (e.g., UMAD, GLC) by combining their respective optimization objectives with LEAD's loss, using a trade-off parameter $\gamma$. ($L_{overall} = \gamma \cdot L_{LEAD} + (1 - \gamma) \cdot L_{baseline}$).

This comprehensive methodology allows LEAD to robustly and efficiently identify target-private data by leveraging the intrinsic structure of feature spaces, adapting to individual instance characteristics, and avoiding the limitations of prior approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of LEAD demonstrates its effectiveness and superiority across various Universal Domain Adaptation (UniDA) scenarios, specifically Open-Partial Domain Adaptation (OPDA), Open-set Domain Adaptation (OSDA), and Partial Domain Adaptation (PDA), and datasets including Office-31, Office-Home, VisDA, and DomainNet.

- **Superior Performance Across UniDA Scenarios:**
 - **OPDA:** LEAD consistently achieves high H-scores, often surpassing or matching state-of-the-art methods, particularly those without clustering. For instance, on the VisDA dataset in OPDA, LEAD achieves an H-score of 76.6%, outperforming many baselines (Table 3).
 - **OSDA:** Similar trends are observed in OSDA, where LEAD delivers competitive H-scores (e.g., 90.3% on Office-31 and 74.2% on VisDA) (Table 4).
 - **PDA:** In PDA scenarios, LEAD also demonstrates strong performance, with overall accuracies of 73.8%, 95.5%, and 75.3% on Office-Home, Office-31, and VisDA, respectively, outperforming even some PDA-tailored methods (Table 5).

- **Significant Improvement to Existing SF-UniDA Methods (Complementarity):** A remarkable finding is LEAD's ability to enhance the performance of other SF-UniDA methods when integrated.
 - **UMAD Improvement:** LEAD notably boosts UMAD. For example, in OPDA on Office-Home, UMAD + LEAD increases the H-score from 70.1% to 78.0% (+7.9%). On VisDA, it improves UMAD's H-score from 58.3% to 67.2% (+8.9%). In OSDA, UMAD + LEAD sees improvements of 1.2%, 2.5%, and 3.4% on Office-Home, Office-31, and VisDA, respectively.
 - **GLC Improvement:** Even for GLC, a strong clustering-based baseline, LEAD offers further gains. In OPDA on VisDA, GLC + LEAD improves the H-score from 73.1% to 76.8% (+3.7%). This demonstrates that LEAD’s decomposition strategy is fundamentally complementary and not just an alternative.

- **Exceptional Efficiency in Decision Boundary Derivation:** LEAD significantly reduces the computational time required to derive pseudo-label decision boundaries compared to clustering-based methods like GLC.
 - On the large-scale DomainNet dataset, LEAD takes only 0.29 seconds, whereas GLC requires 897.65 seconds – an improvement by several orders of magnitude.
 - On smaller datasets like VisDA, LEAD still reduces runtime by over 75%, highlighting its practical advantage (Table 7). This efficiency is critical for deploying SF-UniDA models on large-scale data.

- **Ablation Studies Confirm Component Contributions:** The ablation studies confirm that all proposed components of LEAD (pseudo-label learning $L_{ce}$, feature decomposition regularization $L_{reg}$, and feature consensus regularization $L_{con}$) are effective and complementary to each other, each contributing positively to the overall performance (Table 6).

- **Robustness and Stability:**
 - **Parameter Sensitivity:** LEAD demonstrates stable performance across a range of hyper-parameter settings (e.g., $\lambda$ in Figure 3a, nearest neighbor size $|L^t_i|$ in Figure 8a, and entropy threshold $\omega$ in Figure 8b).
 - **Varying Unknown Classes:** LEAD maintains stable and competitive results even as the number of unknown private classes increases, consistently boosting integrated methods (Figure 3c-d).
 - **Training Stability:** The training curves show that LEAD’s optimization process is generally stable and effective (Figure 3b).

- **Effectiveness of Instance-level Decision Boundary:** The proposed instance-level adaptive decision boundary strategy is demonstrably superior to a vanilla global decision boundary, with a performance gain from 63.1% to 75.0% H-score on Office-Home in OPDA scenarios (Figure 4a). This validates the importance of adaptive thresholds for varying covariate shifts.

- **Superiority of Feature Decomposition Indicator:** Using $||z^t_{i,unk}||_2$ (projection on source-unknown space) as the indicator for private data proves more effective than using entropy, as commonly done in other threshold-based methods. The authors attribute this to entropy's limitation in distinguishing between highly uncertain common samples and genuinely unknown private samples (Figure 4b).

- **Visualizations:** t-SNE visualizations (Figure 7 in Appendix) clearly show that LEAD (and UMAD w/ LEAD) achieves a much clearer separation between common and private data in the feature space compared to source-only or even baseline UMAD models. Confusion matrices (Figure 6 in Appendix) further illustrate LEAD's superior ability to correctly classify common data and avoid misclassifying private data.

In summary, LEAD introduces a novel, efficient, and robust solution for SF-UniDA, addressing critical limitations of prior work. Its demonstrated performance gains, computational efficiency, and complementary nature position it as a significant advancement in the field.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LEAD framework represents a significant advancement in the field of Source-free Universal Domain Adaptation (SF-UniDA), offering a novel and highly practical solution to a challenging problem. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Novel Paradigm for Unknown Class Detection:** LEAD introduces a new, principled approach for identifying target-private unknown data based on orthogonal feature decomposition. By decoupling features into source-known and source-unknown components, it moves beyond heuristic thresholding or computationally heavy clustering, providing a more elegant and theoretically grounded mechanism. This could inspire a new line of research in domain adaptation and open-set recognition that leverages intrinsic feature space properties.

- **Enhanced Practicality for Real-World Applications:**
 - **Data Privacy Compliance:** SF-UniDA is inherently more aligned with strict data privacy regulations (like GDPR) as it does not require access to sensitive source data during adaptation. LEAD's robust performance within this setting makes it highly valuable for real-world deployments in privacy-sensitive sectors such as healthcare (medical imaging), finance, and autonomous driving.
 - **Scalability and Efficiency:** The drastic reduction in runtime for decision boundary derivation, particularly evident on large datasets like DomainNet (orders of magnitude faster than clustering methods), makes LEAD highly scalable. This efficiency is critical for deploying domain adaptation solutions in resource-constrained environments or for applications requiring rapid adaptation to new domains.

- **Generality and Complementarity:** A key strength of LEAD is its design as a complementary framework. Its ability to seamlessly integrate with and significantly boost the performance of existing SF-UniDA methods (e.g., UMAD, GLC) is a major contribution. This means that researchers and practitioners can readily adopt LEAD to enhance their current SF-UniDA pipelines without discarding previous work, accelerating the development and deployment of more robust domain adaptation models.

- **Improved Robustness and Accuracy:**
 - **Adaptive Decision Boundaries:** The instance-level adaptive decision boundaries, tailored by both target prototypes and source anchors, significantly improve the accuracy of distinguishing common from private data compared to static global thresholds. This adaptability ensures better performance across diverse and dynamically shifting target domains.
 - **Reduced Sensitivity to Hyper-parameters:** The empirical results indicate that LEAD is relatively stable across various hyper-parameter settings and varying numbers of unknown classes, suggesting a more robust and less finicky solution in practice.

- **Advancement of the Domain Adaptation Field:** By addressing the core challenges of SF-UniDA (unknown category detection, efficiency, robustness) with an innovative methodology, LEAD pushes the state-of-the-art. It provides a strong benchmark for future research in SF-UniDA and may encourage the development of similar decomposition-based techniques for other complex knowledge transfer scenarios.

In essence, LEAD offers a scientifically sound, practically viable, and broadly applicable solution for a critical problem in modern AI. Its impact extends from improving the performance of domain-adaptive systems to enabling their ethical and efficient deployment in a wide array of real-world applications.
