# Neglected Free Lunch from Post-training: Progress Advantage for LLM Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Wisconsin–Madison and Argonne National Laboratory introduce 'progress advantage,' an annotation-free, implicit process-level reward signal extracted from RL-trained LLM agent policies. This signal, derived from the log-probability ratio between the trained and reference policies, consistently enhanced task success rates by an average of 15.5% for Gemma4-4B and 11.3% for Qwen3.5-9B and achieved superior performance in trajectory success prediction.

## Problem

- Existing outcome reward models (ORMs) provide coarse signals, limiting fine-grained credit assignment over long, multi-turn LLM agent trajectories.
- Developing process reward models (PRMs) for LLM agents is challenging due to long-horizon, irreversible actions in stochastic environments, prohibitive human annotation costs, and poor generalization across tasks.
- Prior implicit PRM formulations are inadequate for the stochastic transitions and external interactions inherent in real-world LLM agent environments.

## Method

- Derived 'progress advantage,' an annotation-free process-level reward signal, from the log-probability ratio between an RL-trained **LLM** policy and its corresponding reference policy.
- Theoretically proved that progress advantage precisely recovers the optimal advantage function in stochastic Markov Decision Processes, extending prior deterministic implicit reward formulations.
- Demonstrated the applicability of this signal to a broad range of RL algorithms, including those utilizing clipping-based surrogate objectives, integrating it into existing LLM post-training pipelines.

## Results

- Consistently improved LLM agent task success rates in best-of-N selection, achieving average gains of **15.5%** for Gemma4-4B and **11.3%** for Qwen3.5-9B over baselines.
- Significantly outperformed all baselines, including pre-trained PRMs and confidence-based methods, in trajectory-level success or failure prediction (uncertainty quantification) across multiple LLM backbones.
- Exhibited promising performance in localizing decisive error steps within multi-agent systems, approaching the accuracy of specifically trained failure attribution methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal advantage function in stochastic MDPs can be directly recovered by contrasting the log-probabilities of the optimal RL-trained policy and its reference policy.
> - Standard RL post-training pipelines for LLM agents implicitly encode valuable process-level signals that can be extracted without requiring additional human annotation or dedicated reward model training.
> - Comparing the behavior and reference policies provides a more refined signal for goal achievement and trajectory quality than using either policy's log-probability in isolation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has increasingly focused on enabling them to operate as autonomous agents, performing complex, multi-turn tasks involving tool use, web navigation, and code execution. Reinforcement Learning (RL) serves as a dominant paradigm for post-training these LLM agents, enhancing their capabilities beyond initial supervised fine-tuning (SFT). A critical challenge in deploying these agents is evaluating the quality of their behavior at a fine-grained, step-level granularity, rather than relying solely on a final outcome.

Existing evaluation methods include outcome reward models (ORMs), which assign a single scalar reward to a generated output. While scalable in terms of annotation, ORMs provide coarse signals that offer limited guidance for credit assignment over long trajectories with hundreds of actions. Process reward models (PRMs) aim to address this by providing step-level supervision, enabling finer-grained trajectory evaluation beneficial for test-time scaling, runtime monitoring, and failure diagnosis. However, PRMs have primarily been explored in deterministic reasoning settings, such as mathematical problem-solving, and their application to LLM agents remains largely underexplored.

Building PRMs for LLM agents presents significant difficulties. Agentic trajectories often involve long horizons and interactions with stateful environments where actions can be irreversible. This complexity precludes traditional Monte Carlo estimation, which relies on backtracking and repeated rollouts. Furthermore, collecting step-level human annotations for such intricate interactions is prohibitively expensive, and domain-specific PRMs often exhibit poor generalization across tasks or environments. This creates a gap where the LLM agents most in need of process-level evaluation are precisely those for which PRM development is least feasible.

Prior implicit PRM approaches have leveraged likelihood-based signals in deterministic reasoning contexts, but these interpretations do not directly extend to the stochastic transitions and external interactions characteristic of agentic environments. This paper positions itself by proposing a method that bypasses the need for explicit process annotations or dedicated PRM training, instead extracting a process-level signal directly from the artifacts of standard RL post-training pipelines. It aims to provide a theoretically grounded, annotation-free, and domain-agnostic approach to step-level scoring for LLM agents in stochastic environments, thereby addressing a significant practical and theoretical challenge in the deployment and monitoring of advanced LLM-based systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective and efficient mechanism for fine-grained, step-level evaluation of LLM agents operating in multi-turn, stochastic environments. This objective is motivated by the inherent difficulties and limitations of current approaches to process reward modeling (PRM) for such agents.

The core motivation stems from the observation that traditional PRM construction for LLM agents is prohibitively difficult. Specifically:
- **Long-horizon interactions:** Agentic tasks often involve extended sequences of actions, making it challenging to attribute success or failure to individual steps.
- **Irreversible actions:** Actions in real-world agent environments (e.g., sending an email, deleting a file) cannot be easily undone, complicating the use of Monte Carlo estimation methods that require repeated rollouts or backtracking.
- **Stochastic environment feedback:** Agent interactions often involve non-deterministic observations (e.g., user responses, tool outputs), which introduce variability that existing deterministic implicit reward formulations do not adequately address.
- **Prohibitive annotation costs:** Collecting human annotations at the step level for complex agent trajectories is extremely expensive and time-consuming.
- **Limited generalization:** Even when trained, domain-specific PRMs frequently exhibit poor generalization across different tasks or environments.

Given these challenges, the authors hypothesize that the Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training process, which LLMs undergo to become agents, implicitly encodes a valuable process-level signal. The key objective is to formally derive and validate this implicit signal, which they term "progress advantage." This signal is intended to be:
- **Annotation-free:** Eliminating the need for manual step-level labeling.
- **Domain-agnostic:** Applicable across various agentic tasks and environments without requiring retraining.
- **Implicitly available:** A byproduct of the standard RL post-training pipeline, computed from existing model checkpoints.
- **Theoretically grounded:** Providing a robust mathematical basis for its application in stochastic environments.

Beyond theoretical derivation, the research aims to demonstrate the practical utility of progress advantage across three distinct inference-time applications:
1. **Test-time scaling (best-of-N selection):** Utilizing progress advantage to score and select optimal trajectories from multiple generated candidates to boost task success rates.
2. **Uncertainty quantification:** Employing progress advantage as a signal to predict the success or failure of an agent's trajectory, thereby enabling runtime monitoring and enhancing reliability.
3. **Failure attribution:** Localizing the specific step or steps where an agent system makes a decisive error within a multi-agent system.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to provide a scalable and practical solution for fine-grained evaluation of LLM agents, reducing development costs and improving the reliability and interpretability of these complex systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology focuses on deriving and validating an implicit, step-level reward signal directly from LLM policies that have undergone Reinforcement Learning (RL) post-training. This approach aims to bypass the need for explicit human annotation or dedicated reward model training.

**Theoretical Foundation:**
1. **Problem Setup:** The work models the operation of stochastic LLM agents in multi-turn interaction settings as a general token-level Markov Decision Process (MDP). This MDP includes a state space (full sequence of tokens), an action space (generated tokens), stochastic state transition dynamics (incorporating external observations like user messages or tool outputs), and a reward function. The objective of RL is to maximize expected cumulative rewards.
2. **KL-Regularized RL:** Modern LLM post-training often involves KL-regularized reward maximization, where the policy is optimized to maximize rewards while staying close to a reference policy ($\pi_{ref}$) through a KL divergence penalty. This objective is expressed as:
 $J(\pi_\theta) = E_{a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s_t)} \left[ \sum_t r(s_t, a_t) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(a_t|s_t)} \right]$
3. **Limitations of Prior Implicit Reward Formulations:** Previous work showed that in *deterministic* MDPs, the reward function $r(s_t, a_t)$ could be implicitly recovered as $\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(a_t|s_t)}$. However, the authors demonstrate that this direct recovery does not hold in *stochastic* MDPs, which characterize LLM agent environments. In stochastic settings, additional value function terms appear that do not cancel out, making the exact reward irrecoverable from policy distributions alone (Remark 1).
4. **Derivation of Progress Advantage:** The core theoretical contribution is Proposition 1, which proves that while the exact reward is elusive, the *optimal advantage function* ($\tilde{A}^*(s, a) = \tilde{Q}^*(s, a) - \tilde{V}^*(s)$) *is* precisely recovered by the log-probability ratio between the optimal RL-trained policy ($\tilde{\pi}^*$) and the reference policy ($\pi_{ref}$):
 $\tilde{A}^*(s, a) = \beta \log \frac{\tilde{\pi}^*(a|s)}{\pi_{ref}(a|s)}$
 This quantity is termed "progress advantage." It measures the relative merit of taking a specific action compared to the average action under the optimal policy at a given state. The proof relies on the Lagrangian solution of the per-state optimization induced by the soft Bellman equation.
5. **Generality to Clipping-Based RL:** Proposition 2 extends the applicability of progress advantage to a broader class of RL algorithms. It shows that clipping-based surrogate objectives (e.g., in PPO-Clip style algorithms) implicitly enforce a KL constraint, meaning policies trained with such methods also operate within a KL trust region and are thus covered by the theoretical framework.

**Practical Implementation:**
1. **Policy Specification:**
 - $\tilde{\pi}^*$: Any policy checkpoint trained via KL-regularized or clipping-based RL objective (e.g., Qwen3.5-9B).
 - $\pi_{ref}$: The corresponding base or intermediate checkpoint used during RL training (e.g., Qwen3.5-9B-Base). The choice of $\pi_{ref}$ is critical; it should not be too similar or too different from $\tilde{\pi}^*$. The paper empirically explores policy merging techniques (WISE, TIES) to craft sharper reference policies.
2. **Progress Advantage Aggregation:** Since progress advantage is a token-level signal, strategies are needed to aggregate it into step-level and trajectory-level scores for different applications. Proposed methods include:
 - Vanilla sub-trajectory sum
 - Per-token average advantage
 - Position-weighted advantage
 - Extreme token advantage (min or max within a sub-trajectory)
 The choice of aggregation method is shown to impact performance and is optimized per task.
3. **Token Probability Representation:** The default uses the direct log-probability ratio. An alternative "progress k-advantage" is explored, which uses a top-k smoothed average of token probabilities to potentially reduce noise and instability, similar to confidence-based methods.

**Empirical Validation:**
1. **Benchmarks:** The study uses five benchmarks representative of agentic workloads: BFCLv4-MT (multi-turn tool calling), WebShop (tool-augmented online shopping), AgentDojo (general task solving), $\tau^2$-bench (conversational agents in customer service), and Who & When (failure attribution in multi-agent systems).
2. **Models:** Four LLM families are evaluated: Gemma4-4B, Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3-14B, and Olmo3-7B, each paired with its corresponding base/intermediate checkpoint.
3. **Baselines:** Comparisons are made against two categories:
 - **Trained Reward Models:** WildReward-8B, ThinkPRM-7B/14B, and AgentPRM (task-specific).
 - **Confidence-based Methods (training-free):** Self-Certainty and DeepConf (Tail and B10).
 Additionally, Claude-Sonnet-4.6 is used as an LLM-as-a-Judge baseline for uncertainty quantification.
4. **Applications and Metrics:**
 - **Test-time Scaling:** Best-of-N (N=8) sampling to select the best trajectory; measured by task success rate.
 - **Uncertainty Quantification:** Predicting trajectory-level success or failure on greedy-decoded trajectories; measured by AUROC.
 - **Failure Attribution:** Detecting the decisive error step in failure trajectories; measured by step-level prediction accuracy.

This comprehensive approach combines theoretical rigor with extensive empirical validation across diverse agentic tasks, models, and applications, aiming to establish progress advantage as a viable and effective process-level evaluation signal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the effectiveness of progress advantage across various applications and experimental settings.

**Test-time Scaling (Best-of-N Sampling):**
- Progress advantage consistently improved task success rates when used for best-of-8 trajectory selection. Across four benchmarks (BFCLv4-MT, WebShop, AgentDojo, $\tau^2$-Airline) and two model families (Gemma4-4B, Qwen3.5-9B), it outperformed confidence-based baselines (Self-Certainty, DeepConf) and, on average, dedicated pre-trained reward models (WildReward-8B, ThinkPRM-7B/14B).
- Specifically, progress advantage yielded average success rate improvements of 15.5% for Gemma4-4B and 11.3% for Qwen3.5-9B compared to the mean of baselines.
- The method demonstrated particular strength in tasks benefiting from exploratory (non-zero temperature) decoding, such as WebShop and $\tau^2$-Airline.
- In a direct comparison on WebShop, progress advantage (without task-specific training) matched or exceeded the performance of AgentPRM-7B, a reward model specifically trained on that downstream task.
- Further fine-tuning the behavior policy with GRPO on a task (WebShop) and then calculating progress advantage based on this refined policy led to additional performance gains, indicating the signal can be further sharpened by task-specific RL.

**Uncertainty Quantification (Trajectory Monitoring):**
- Progress advantage significantly outperformed all baselines, including pre-trained PRMs (WildReward, ThinkPRM) and confidence-based methods (Self-Certainty, DeepConf), in predicting trajectory-level success or failure, as measured by AUROC.
- On the $\tau^2$-bench Airline domain, progress advantage consistently achieved the highest AUROC across all four tested LLM backbones (Gemma4-4B, Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3-14B, Olmo3-7B). It also demonstrated competitive results on the $\tau^2$-bench Retail domain.
- Progress advantage was shown to function effectively as an external scorer. When Gemma4-4B's progress advantage was used to score trajectories generated by other policies (Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3-14B), it yielded higher AUROC values than other baselines, suggesting its potential as a general-purpose, off-the-shelf reward model.
- Qualitative analysis (Figure 3) illustrated that progress advantage effectively assigned positive scores to correct tool-calling actions and relevant domain constraints, unlike pure policy log probability, which sometimes assigned low scores to correct but less frequent token sequences.

**Failure Attribution:**
- Progress advantage exhibited promising performance in localizing the decisive error step in multi-agent systems using the Who & When benchmark.
- Its step-level prediction accuracy approached that of AgenTracer, a method specifically trained for this failure attribution task, particularly on the "Hand-Crafted" split.

**Additional Analyses:**
- **Importance of Policy Contrasting:** Comparing the log probabilities of the behavior and reference policies (as in progress advantage) was more effective than using either log probability in isolation for uncertainty quantification. This highlights that the "difference" between the policies provides a more refined signal for goal achievement.
- **Aggregation Strategy Impact:** The choice of token-level and step-level aggregation strategies for progress advantage influenced its effectiveness, with optimal combinations varying by application and model. For example, (MEAN, MIN) aggregation (mean token advantage, minimum step advantage) performed well for best-of-N selection, while (MAX, MEAN) (maximum token advantage, mean step advantage) was effective for UQ on the Airline domain.
- **Progress k-Advantage:** The top-k smoothed variant (progress k-advantage) generally performed comparably to or slightly less effectively than the vanilla progress advantage in TTS and UQ but occasionally surpassed it in some failure attribution scenarios, suggesting potential benefits of probability smoothing for precise step-level credit assignment.
- **Reference Policy Specification:** Varying the reference policy through linear interpolation (merging the base and final checkpoints) affected performance. Robust merging variants, such as TIES, consistently induced better results for specific merge coefficients, supporting the hypothesis that an optimally crafted reference policy can sharpen the progress advantage signal.
- **Evolution of Progress Advantage:** Visualization showed that progress advantage effectively separated successful from failed trajectory groups from early steps in the Airline domain, while in the Retail domain, the separation became more pronounced towards the end of the trajectory.

In summary, the main findings indicate that progress advantage provides a robust, annotation-free, and empirically effective signal for process-level evaluation in LLM agents, consistently outperforming several baselines across diverse applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of large language models and reinforcement learning, particularly concerning the evaluation and monitoring of LLM agents. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

**Theoretical Advancements:**
- **Implicit Reward in Stochastic MDPs:** The work provides a theoretical foundation for deriving an implicit process-level signal in complex, stochastic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), which are characteristic of real-world LLM agent environments. This extends previous implicit reward formulations that were largely confined to deterministic settings.
- **Progress Advantage as Optimal Advantage Function:** By formally proving that the log-probability ratio between an RL-trained policy and its reference policy directly recovers the optimal advantage function, the research establishes a robust theoretical grounding for this signal. This provides a principled mechanism for evaluating action quality in context.
- **Generality Across RL Algorithms:** Demonstrating that this formulation applies to a broad class of RL algorithms, including those with explicit KL penalties and those with clipping-based surrogate objectives, enhances its practical applicability across diverse LLM post-training pipelines.

**Practical Utility and Efficiency:**
- **Annotation-Free Evaluation:** The most immediate impact is the elimination of the need for expensive and time-consuming human annotation for process-level rewards. This drastically reduces the overhead associated with developing and deploying PRMs for LLM agents.
- **Training-Free and Domain-Agnostic:** Progress advantage does not require dedicated reward model training or task-specific fine-tuning. It is extracted as a byproduct of existing RL post-training, making it a "free lunch" in terms of additional resource expenditure. Its domain-agnostic nature allows for transferability across different agentic tasks and environments.
- **Improved Agent Performance and Reliability:** The empirical results demonstrate that progress advantage can consistently improve agent performance in critical inference-time applications:
 - **Test-time Scaling:** By enabling effective best-of-N selection, it can boost task success rates, making LLM agents more capable.
 - **Uncertainty Quantification:** Providing a robust signal for predicting trajectory success or failure allows for better runtime monitoring, safety interventions, and more trustworthy agent deployments.
 - **Failure Attribution:** Pinpointing error steps aids in debugging, diagnosis, and iterative improvement of complex multi-agent systems.

**Paradigm Shift in Agent Monitoring:**
- This work encourages a new paradigm for agent evaluation, shifting from external, labor-intensive reward model training to leveraging intrinsic signals already encoded within the agent's own learning process. This can foster the development of more autonomous and self-aware agentic systems.
- By offering an off-the-shelf solution for process-level evaluation, progress advantage could accelerate the development and deployment of LLM agents across various industries, from customer service to complex scientific research.

**Future Research Directions:**
- The findings open avenues for further research into refining the selection and crafting of reference policies, potentially through advanced model merging techniques, to further sharpen the progress advantage signal.
- The modality-agnostic nature of the theoretical formulation suggests its potential extension to multi-modal agents, vision-language-action (VLA) models, and embodied agents, offering fine-grained monitoring capabilities beyond text-only interactions.
- The work also contributes to the broader discussion on self-improving intelligent systems, where internal progress signals can inform future policy iterations, and highlights the value of transparent, open-source AI development for shared community resources.

In conclusion, "Progress Advantage" offers a theoretically sound and practically effective method for fine-grained evaluation of LLM agents. Its ability to extract valuable process-level signals without additional training or annotation presents a substantial impact on the scalability, reliability, and accessibility of advanced AI agent systems.
