# SPIRAL: Self-Play on Zero-Sum Games Incentivizes Reasoning via Multi-Agent Multi-Turn Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPIRAL framework trains Large Language Models (LLMs) to acquire transferable reasoning skills by engaging in self-play on two-player zero-sum language games. This approach automates curriculum generation and leads to substantial improvements, such as an 8.6% increase in mathematical reasoning and 8.4% in general reasoning for a Qwen3-4B model.

## Problem

- Existing Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods for LLM reasoning suffer from a 'scalability bottleneck,' relying heavily on human-curated problem-answer pairs, domain-specific reward engineering, and expert supervision.
- Prior applications of self-play to language models were limited to offline updates, simple word games, or single-turn tasks, failing to leverage the full potential of multi-turn competitive dynamics for extended strategic reasoning.
- The challenge of developing an autonomous and scalable method to enhance generalizable reasoning capabilities in LLMs without continuous human intervention.

## Method

- The SPIRAL framework implements a fully ==online, multi-turn, multi-agent reinforcement learning== system for LLMs using two-player zero-sum language games.
- It frames learning as a turn-level ==Markov Decision Process==, where each action is a complete, multi-token response, and both players share a single, role-conditioned policy.
- A key technical innovation, ==Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE)==, uses separate, exponentially moving average baselines for each game and player role to stabilize training and reduce variance in multi-agent REINFORCE.

## Results

- SPIRAL, trained exclusively on Kuhn Poker, improved Qwen3-4B-Base's mathematical reasoning by **8.6%** and general reasoning by **8.4%**, demonstrating transferability of skills from games to standard reasoning benchmarks.
- Self-play significantly outperformed training against fixed opponents (which caused 'thinking collapse' with random opponents or exploitation with strong ones); Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE) was essential to prevent this collapse and ensure stable policy gradient norms.
- Multi-game training (TicTacToe + Kuhn Poker + Simple Negotiation) achieved superior average performance on out-of-distribution games (**44.9%** vs. **27.1-34.4%** for single-game specialists) and improved even a stronger DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B model by **2.0 percentage points**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Competitive pressure within self-play on zero-sum games forces Large Language Models (LLMs) to develop genuine, transferable reasoning abilities rather than memorization or exploitation of static strategies.
> - Self-play's automatic, adaptive curriculum consistently outperforms fixed-opponent training, which leads to overfitting and poor generalization in LLMs.
> - Different types of zero-sum games cultivate specialized reasoning skills in LLMs, and training across multiple games yields synergistic benefits, enhancing performance on novel, composite reasoning challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in large language model (LLM) reasoning, exemplified by models such as OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1, have demonstrated the capacity of reinforcement learning (RL) to enhance Chain-of-Thought reasoning. This enhancement is often achieved through the application of outcome-based rewards, enabling LLMs to develop generalizable reasoning strategies for complex problems where supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has shown limited efficacy.

However, a fundamental challenge in current RL-based approaches for LLM reasoning is their dependence on human-curated data, meticulously engineered reward functions, and expert supervision. Each new reasoning domain typically necessitates the involvement of human experts to define evaluation metrics, prepare training problems, and validate reasoning traces. This manual process restricts the scalability and diversity of reasoning challenges that LLMs can learn from, potentially impeding progress towards more generalized artificial intelligence.

Self-play, particularly within competitive game environments, presents an alternative by generating training data without direct human supervision. Historical successes in artificial intelligence, ranging from TD-Gammon to AlphaGo and OpenAI Five, have leveraged self-play to achieve high-level performance in complex games. Despite these achievements, the application of self-play in games specifically to enhance language model reasoning has remained largely underexplored. Previous attempts in this area have often been confined to simple word games with offline updates, employed LoRA adaptations, or focused on single-turn tasks. These prior efforts have not fully capitalized on the potential of multi-turn competitive dynamics for developing extended strategic reasoning capabilities in LLMs. The present work addresses this gap by introducing a framework designed to leverage multi-turn, zero-sum language games for autonomous reasoning development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate SPIRAL, a self-play framework that enables language models to acquire sophisticated and transferable reasoning capabilities. This framework aims to achieve this without relying on human-curated problem-answer pairs or domain-specific reward engineering, thereby addressing key scalability and generalizability limitations of existing approaches.

The motivation behind SPIRAL is multifaceted:

- **Addressing Scalability Bottlenecks in RL-based Reasoning**: Current state-of-the-art LLM reasoning models that utilize RL often depend on extensive human-curated datasets and carefully designed reward functions. This manual intervention is resource-intensive and limits the scale and diversity of reasoning tasks that models can learn. The motivation is to automate the data generation and feedback process to allow for continuous, unsupervised learning.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: While SFT is effective for many language tasks, it often shows limited progress in developing generalizable reasoning strategies for complex problems. The paper posits that game-based learning can elicit more robust and transferable reasoning abilities than imitation learning from expert demonstrations.
- **Enhancing Beyond Fixed-Opponent Training**: Training against fixed opponents can lead models to overfit to static strategies, preventing the development of truly generalized reasoning. The motivation is to create an adaptive curriculum where the opponent continuously improves alongside the learning agent, fostering more robust and adaptive strategies.
- **Leveraging Multi-Turn, Zero-Sum Game Dynamics**: Zero-sum games inherently provide a strong signal for learning through win/loss outcomes and force players to develop robust strategies to overcome an adaptive opponent. The multi-turn nature of these games also mirrors the sequential decision-making and planning required in complex reasoning problems, motivating their use as a training ground.
- **Addressing Technical Challenges of Online Multi-Agent RL for LLMs**: Implementing multi-turn, multi-agent reinforcement learning for LLMs at scale presents significant computational demands due to autoregressive generation and the high variance often encountered in multi-agent learning environments. A key motivation is to develop a robust system and algorithmic innovations to stabilize this complex training process. Specifically, ensuring stable gradient updates in a shared-parameter, zero-sum setting is identified as a critical challenge requiring a dedicated solution.
- **Demonstrating Transferability of Reasoning Skills**: A core motivation is to demonstrate that reasoning patterns developed through competitive self-play in abstract games can transfer effectively to out-of-domain academic reasoning benchmarks, thereby showing that games can serve as a fundamental training scaffold for general intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPIRAL operates as a self-play framework designed for language models to develop reasoning capabilities by engaging in multi-turn, zero-sum games against continuously improving versions of themselves. This approach aims to eliminate the need for human supervision in training data generation and reward engineering.

**Framework Formulation:**
SPIRAL utilizes turn-based, two-player zero-sum Markov games drawn from a collection G = {G1, G2..., Gn}. In this formulation, states (s ∈ S) represent complete game contexts (e.g., board configurations, conversation history), and actions (a ∈ A) are complete multi-token responses generated by the LLM. The transition function (Ti) dictates state evolution after a full turn. A crucial aspect is the zero-sum property, where the sum of rewards for player 0 and player 1 (r0 + r1 = 0) is always zero, establishing competitive dynamics. Rewards are sparse, given only at game termination (R0(τ) = ρi(sT) and R1(τ) = -ρi(sT)), which incentivizes robust strategy development. The multi-turn structure requires models to maintain context, plan sequentially, and adapt strategies, mirroring real-world complex reasoning problems.

**Self-Play Mechanism:**
Instead of training separate policies for each player, SPIRAL employs a single shared policy (πθ) with parameters θ. Role conditioning, facilitated through system prompts, allows the shared policy to learn distinct strategies for each player position. In each turn, the active player generates a complete response (y(p)t ∼ πθ(·|st, p, Gi)), which includes both reasoning (e.g., "[thinking]...[/thinking]") and the executable action. The action is then extracted to update the game state. This shared-parameter approach promotes efficient resource utilization and naturally creates an automatic curriculum: as the model improves in one role, it simultaneously faces a stronger opponent in the other, driving continuous learning.

**Reinforcement Learning Objective:**
The objective is to optimize the shared policy using Monte Carlo policy gradient methods, specifically REINFORCE. The gradient formulation accounts for actions taken by both players across their respective turns in a trajectory. However, the inherent high variance of Monte Carlo returns, particularly in non-stationary multi-agent self-play environments, poses a challenge to stable training.

**Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE):**
To address the training instability caused by high variance and the opposing objectives in zero-sum self-play, SPIRAL introduces Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE). RAE maintains separate baselines (bG,p) for each game (G) and player role (p ∈ {0, 1}). These baselines estimate the expected return for a specific role in a specific game and are updated using an exponential moving average. The advantage (AG,p(τ) = Rp(τ) - bG,p) is then computed by centering the actual return around this role-specific expectation. This approach aims to reduce variance more effectively than a global baseline, accounting for game asymmetries (e.g., first-move advantage). By isolating the genuine learning signal, RAE prevents gradients from being dominated by inherent positional advantages, which is critical for preventing what the authors term "thinking collapse"—a phenomenon where models cease generating reasoning traces.

**Implementation Details:**
SPIRAL is implemented as a fully online, multi-agent, multi-turn RL system for fine-tuning LLMs.
- **Architecture**: It utilizes a distributed actor-learner architecture, building upon the Oat framework.
- **Actors**: Parallel actors are responsible for executing the self-play loop and collecting game trajectories. vLLM is used for efficient model inference during this phase.
- **Game Environments**: TextArena is employed to simulate the language games. The training involves three distinct games: TicTacToe (for spatial reasoning), Kuhn Poker (for probabilistic reasoning), and Simple Negotiation (for strategic optimization).
- **Learner**: A centralized learner processes the collected multi-turn, multi-game self-play experiences. It applies policy gradient methods, incorporating the RAE for stable updates.
- **Models**: The framework was applied to various LLM architectures, including Qwen3-4B/8B-Base, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Octothinker-8B-Base.
- **Training Configuration**: Experiments were conducted over 400 steps, with 128 samples per step, using 8 H100 GPUs. The Adam optimizer was used with a learning rate of 1 × 10−6 and a sampling temperature of 1.0.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted with SPIRAL yielded several key findings regarding its ability to develop and transfer reasoning capabilities in large language models.

**Transferable Reasoning Capabilities:**
Multi-game SPIRAL training (involving TicTacToe, Kuhn Poker, and Simple Negotiation) demonstrated substantial improvements in reasoning performance across a suite of 8 academic benchmarks. For instance, the Qwen3-4B-Base model showed an absolute improvement of 10.5%, increasing its average performance from 34.0% to 44.5%. Qwen3-8B-Base improved by 10.1%, from 39.5% to 49.6%. The approach also generalized across different model families, including base models (Qwen3-4B/8B-Base, Octothinker-8B-Base) and instruction-tuned models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B), all showing consistent performance gains.

**Superiority over Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
SPIRAL consistently outperformed supervised fine-tuning (SFT) approaches trained on 25,000 expert game trajectories across all 8 reasoning benchmarks. This suggests that the self-play dynamic enables the discovery of more effective and generalizable reasoning strategies than those learned through imitation of expert demonstrations. Further experiments with SFT on 52,000 trajectories also did not match SPIRAL's performance, indicating that the benefits stem from the reinforcement learning dynamic rather than simply dataset size.

**Emergence and Transfer of Reasoning Patterns:**
Analysis of chain-of-thought traces, conducted using GPT-4.1 for classification, revealed that distinct cognitive patterns emerged during gameplay and subsequently transferred to mathematical reasoning tasks. Three patterns identified were:
- **Case-by-Case Analysis**: Showed near-perfect transfer, with its frequency in games (72%) closely matching its presence in math solutions (71%).
- **Pattern Recognition**: Exhibited amplification during transfer, increasing from 35% in games to 45% in math.
- **Expected Value Calculation**: Transferred more selectively, from 78% in games to 28% in math.
These findings indicate that competitive gameplay encourages the development of structured reasoning approaches applicable across different domains.

**Adaptive Curriculum vs. Static Opponents:**
Self-play training, which continuously adapts the opponent's strategy, proved critical. Training against fixed opponents (e.g., Random, Mistral-Small-3, Gemini-2.0-Flash-Lite) resulted in models either failing to learn effectively or plateauing after exploiting static strategies. Self-play, by contrast, maintained a balanced win rate (50-52%) against its evolving self, demonstrating continuous improvement in reasoning.

**Complementary Skill Development from Different Games:**
Each game fostered distinct cognitive abilities: TicTacToe primarily developed spatial reasoning, Kuhn Poker emphasized probabilistic thinking, and Simple Negotiation cultivated strategic optimization. These specialized skills transferred effectively to out-of-distribution games requiring similar cognitive processes. Multi-game training synergistically combined these skills, leading to superior average performance (59.5% win rate against Gemini-2.0-Flash) compared to single-game specialists (best single-game specialist at 52.9%).

**Importance of Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE):**
The RAE mechanism was demonstrated to be essential for stable self-play training. Without RAE, models suffered from "thinking collapse" within approximately 100 policy iterations, characterized by a sharp decline in the generation of reasoning traces (from ~2,000 to near-zero characters) and a corresponding drop in general reasoning performance (from 44% to 40%). RAE maintained stable response lengths and consistent performance improvements, indicating its role in stabilizing gradient updates and preventing policy degradation.

**Generalization to Increased Complexity:**
SPIRAL models showed better generalizability to higher-complexity variants of the training games (e.g., 5x5 Tic-Tac-Toe, 5-Card Kuhn Poker) compared to SFT models, suggesting improved adaptability to novel, more challenging environments.

**Integration into Mid-Training Stages:**
Experiments indicated that SPIRAL can serve as a robust performance enhancer when integrated into different training stages. Both "SPIRAL → RLVR" and "RLVR → SPIRAL" pipelines for Qwen3-4B-Base outperformed a standalone "Base → RLVR" baseline, with "RLVR → SPIRAL" yielding the highest average performance. This suggests SPIRAL can mitigate the "alignment tax" often observed in standard fine-tuning processes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPIRAL framework introduces a method for autonomous reasoning development in large language models, offering implications for the future of AI training and capabilities.

**Autonomous Reasoning Development:** The most significant contribution of SPIRAL is its demonstration that sophisticated reasoning capabilities can be developed in LLMs without reliance on human-curated datasets, domain-specific reward engineering, or expert supervision. By utilizing self-play on zero-sum games, the framework generates its own automatic curriculum and feedback, pushing towards more autonomous and less human-dependent AI development paradigms. This could accelerate research into general artificial intelligence by removing key bottlenecks related to data acquisition and annotation.

**Scalability and Efficiency:** By eliminating the need for human-engineered reward functions and problem-answer pairs, SPIRAL addresses a critical scalability bottleneck in current RL-based LLM reasoning methods. The framework's ability to generate unlimited, diverse training data through intrinsic game dynamics means that reasoning development can potentially scale to a much broader range of complex tasks and environments than currently feasible.

**Generalizability of Reasoning Skills:** The research provides empirical evidence that reasoning skills acquired in abstract, competitive game environments can transfer broadly to out-of-domain academic reasoning benchmarks, including mathematics and general knowledge tasks. This suggests that games can serve as a fundamental "training scaffold" for developing generalizable cognitive patterns, rather than merely domain-specific game-playing tactics. The identification and quantification of specific reasoning patterns (e.g., case-by-case analysis, pattern recognition, expected value calculation) further illuminate the mechanisms of this transfer.

**Improved Training Stability for Multi-Agent RL with LLMs:** The introduction of Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE) offers a methodological advancement for stabilizing multi-agent reinforcement learning in LLM contexts. By preventing phenomena like "thinking collapse," RAE enhances the robustness and reliability of self-play training, making the approach more practical for real-world application and further research.

**Foundation for Future Research:** SPIRAL opens several avenues for future research. This includes exploring the applicability of self-play to cooperative games, environments with partial observability, and the design of games specifically engineered to target particular reasoning weaknesses. A deeper understanding of game-skill mappings could enable more principled environment design for targeted autonomous reasoning development. The framework's demonstrated effectiveness even in advanced, instruction-tuned models suggests its potential as a universal "reasoning booster" that can be integrated into various stages of LLM development.
