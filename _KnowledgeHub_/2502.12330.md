# X-IL: Exploring the Design Space of Imitation Learning Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The X-IL framework offers a modular, open-source platform for systematically exploring imitation learning policy designs, integrating diverse backbones and multi-modal inputs. The research demonstrates that xLSTM and Mamba backbones, combined with multi-modal fusion, achieve higher success rates on robot learning benchmarks compared to Transformer-based policies.

## Problem

- The expanding design space of imitation learning policies, encompassing various feature encodings, architectural designs, and policy representations, lacks a systematic evaluation framework.
- Existing modular imitation learning libraries often do not integrate recent advancements like state-space models (e.g., Mamba, xLSTM), multi-modal inputs, or diffusion-based policies comprehensively.
- A clear understanding of how different policy configurations impact performance in imitation learning remains largely unexplored, hindering optimal design choices.

## Method

- Introduction of **X-IL**, a modular, open-source framework supporting multi-modal inputs (RGB, point clouds, language) and interchangeable components for observation representations, backbones (Transformer, Mamba, xLSTM), architectures, and policy representations (==Behavior Cloning==, Diffusion, Flow Matching).
- Systematic exploration of the imitation learning design space through extensive experimentation on the LIBERO and RoboCasa robot learning benchmarks.
- Integration of Adaptive LayerNorm (AdaLN) conditioning within X-Blocks to enhance performance by conditioning input tokens on context information.

## Results

- xLSTM-based policies achieved a **92.3%** success rate on LIBERO with **100%** demonstration data and **53.6%** on RoboCasa using RGB inputs, surpassing BC-Transformer's **40.0%**.
- Multi-modal fusion (Point Cloud + RGB) increased the xLSTM model's success rate on RoboCasa to **60.9%**, an improvement over RGB-only (**53.6%**) and Point Cloud-only (**32.8%**) inputs.
- BESO and Rectified Flows showed robust performance with varying inference steps, while DDPM was less effective with a single inference step in action generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mamba and xLSTM backbones consistently achieve better or comparable performance than Transformer-based policies across both visual and point cloud inputs on tested benchmarks.
> - Multi-modal fusion of RGB and point cloud inputs generally improves performance, indicating the benefit of integrating complementary sensory information.
> - Encoder-Decoder architectures, particularly with AdaLN conditioning, typically outperform Decoder-only models for action generation, and domain-specific or fine-tuned encoders are critical for visual tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL) has been established as a paradigm for enabling agents to acquire complex behaviors by mimicking demonstrations, circumventing the need for explicit reward engineering. The field of machine learning, however, is characterized by rapid advancements, continuously introducing novel techniques across various domains. This progress has led to an expanding and increasingly complex design space for IL policies, encompassing choices related to feature encoding, architectural designs, and policy representations. A challenge in the current research landscape is the difficulty in systematically evaluating the potential impact of these diverse and emerging techniques on imitation learning performance.

Previous IL methods have predominantly relied on either state-based or image-based environmental representations. More recent developments in multi-modal imitation learning have integrated natural language for goal specification and 3D representations, such as point clouds, to capture richer spatial information. Concurrently, sequence models have gained prominence in IL for modeling non-Markovian human behaviors. Early applications involved RNNs, but Transformer-based architectures have become widespread due to their scalability. More recently, State-Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba and improved RNNs like xLSTM have emerged, exhibiting efficiency and representation learning capabilities, though their application within imitation learning remains comparatively less explored.

While several open-source libraries offer algorithm-specific implementations for IL, few provide a modular design that facilitates the systematic exploration of multiple algorithms and architectures. Existing modular libraries like Robomimic and Imitation lack integration of recent diffusion-based IL approaches. CleanDiffuser addresses this for diffusion models but is limited to low-dimensional or 2D inputs. This research contributes to the broader landscape by introducing a framework that expands upon existing modularity, incorporating multi-modal inputs (2D images, point clouds, language) and state-of-the-art sequence models (Mamba, xLSTM) within a comprehensive and interchangeable design. The work aims to systematically explore this expanding design space and provide insights into the efficacy of diverse policy configurations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that the rapid evolution of machine learning techniques has created an expansive and largely unexplored design space for imitation learning policies. Developers and researchers are faced with numerous decisions, including the selection of feature encoding mechanisms, architectural paradigms, and policy representations. The absence of a systematic framework for exploring these combinations hinders the ability to identify optimal configurations and understand the underlying performance characteristics of various design choices.

In response to this, the key objectives of this work are articulated through the following contributions:

1. **To introduce X-IL:** The development of X-IL is central to this research. It is designed as an accessible, open-source framework characterized by a highly modular structure. This modularity is intended to enable seamless swapping of key policy components, including backbones (e.g., Transformer, Mamba, xLSTM) and policy optimization techniques (e.g., Score-matching, Flow-matching). The framework is designed to support multi-modal inputs, flexible encoders, diverse architectures, and state-of-the-art policy representations, thereby providing a unified platform for experimentation.
2. **To systematically explore the design space of IL policies:** Utilizing the X-IL framework, a principal objective is to conduct a systematic exploration of the vast design space. This exploration aims to identify and evaluate novel policy configurations. The ultimate goal is to discover configurations that demonstrate performance improvements over existing methods on contemporary robot learning benchmarks.
3. **To derive valuable insights:** Through comprehensive experimentation, a key objective is to generate empirical data that provides valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of various design choices. This includes understanding the comparative performance of different sequence models as backbones, evaluating the impact of multi-modal input fusion, and assessing the efficacy of different architectural approaches. These insights are intended to inform and guide future research directions in imitation learning.
4. **To serve as a practical reference and foundation:** The study intends to serve as a practical reference for practitioners engaged in imitation learning development and as a foundational resource for guiding future academic research. By consolidating and evaluating a range of modern techniques, the work aims to streamline the experimentation process and foster further advancements in the field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces X-IL, an open-source framework designed for the systematic exploration of imitation learning policy designs. The framework adheres to principles of modularity, ease-of-use, and the incorporation of novel techniques. It decomposes the imitation learning pipeline into four key interchangeable modules: observation representations, backbones, architectures, and policy representations.

#### 3.1. Observation Representations

X-IL supports three primary types of observation representations:
- **RGB Inputs:** Used for visual imitation learning, capturing texture and semantic information. The framework integrates feature extractors such as ResNet, FiLM-ResNet, and pre-trained CLIP. Each image is encoded as a single token for backbone processing.
- **Point Cloud:** Provides 3D spatial structures and geometric information. Furthest Point Sampling (FPS) is used for downsampling. Encoders include a lightweight MLP with Max Pooling for computational efficiency and an attention-based encoder with a class token for enhanced feature extraction.
- **Language:** Offers a high-level, abstract means to describe tasks, enhancing generalization. Pre-trained CLIP is utilized to convert textual information into dense embeddings, which are then fused with visual and point cloud features to create a multi-modal representation.

#### 3.2. Backbones (X-Block)

The backbone is defined as the core computational unit responsible for modeling sequential information. X-IL introduces the X-Block, inspired by the DiT-Block, with an X-Layer as its central component for processing temporal information. The framework supports three backbone options:
- **Transformer:** An attention-based model known for handling non-Markovian behavior.
- **Mamba:** A structured state-space model (SSM) designed for improved efficiency and linear computational complexity, potentially outperforming Transformers on smaller datasets.
- **xLSTM:** A variant of LSTM aimed at enhancing long-term dependency modeling while maintaining computational efficiency.
Adaptive LayerNorm (AdaLN) conditioning is integrated within the X-Block to condition input tokens on context information, such as time embeddings in diffusion models or representation features, which is indicated to enhance performance.

#### 3.3. Architectures

X-IL supports two architectural paradigms for processing inputs and generating actions:
- **Decoder-Only Models:** These models stack multiple X-Blocks, jointly processing both observations and actions within the decoder, which then outputs action tokens for policy training.
- **Encoder-Decoder Models:** This architecture employs a two-stage approach. An encoder first processes multi-modal inputs into a latent representation. A decoder then generates actions conditioned on this structured embedding. For Mamba and xLSTM, which lack built-in cross-attention mechanisms for variable-length sequences, AdaLN conditioning is utilized to integrate encoded representations into the decoding process.

#### 3.4. Policy Representations

Beyond basic behavior cloning, the framework integrates state-of-the-art policy representations:
- **Behavior Cloning (BC):** Assumes a Gaussian distribution for policy representation and minimizes the mean squared error between predicted and ground-truth actions.
- **Diffusion-Based Policies:**
 - **Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM):** Captures the score function field for iterative action optimization.
 - **BESO (Behavior Equilibrium with Score-based Optimization):** A continuous-time diffusion framework allowing for variable diffusion steps and diverse sampling techniques. X-IL supports both DDPM-style and continuous-time BESO-style policies.
- **Flow-Based Policies:**
 - **Flow Matching (Rectified Flows/Stochastic Interpolants):** Continuous-time normalizing flows trained via flow matching, supported within the framework.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on two robot learning benchmarks:
- **LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023):** A benchmark designed to evaluate knowledge transfer in lifelong robot learning, comprising four task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long). Models were evaluated using 20% and 100% of available demonstrations (10 and 50 trajectories, respectively), trained for 100 epochs, and evaluated with 50 rollouts per sub-task over 3 seeds. ResNet-18 was used for image processing.
- **RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024):** A large-scale simulation framework featuring diverse everyday tasks with extensive intra-task variations (scenes, objects, initial positions), requiring strong generalization. Five specific tasks were evaluated with 50 human demonstrations, trained for 200 epochs, and rolled out for 50 episodes over 3 seeds. FiLM-ResNet18 was used for image encoding, and an attention-based encoder for point cloud inputs.

Model sizes for Transformer, Mamba, and xLSTM backbones were matched for fair comparison. Diffusion and flow matching policies used 4 sampling steps in main experiments. Several existing IL models were used as baselines, including BC-Transformer, Diffusion Policy, Octo, OpenVLA, MDT, MaIL, ATM, EnerVerse, and 3D Diffusion Policy (DP3).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using the X-IL framework on the LIBERO and RoboCasa benchmarks yielded several findings concerning the design space of imitation learning policies.

#### 4.1. Evaluation on Visual Inputs (RGB)

- **LIBERO Benchmark:** X-IL policy configurations demonstrated competitive performance, frequently exceeding that of publicly available baseline models. Specifically, xLSTM-based policies achieved an average success rate of 74.5% with 20% of the demonstration data and 92.3% with 100% of the data, indicating its potential in data-efficient and full-data scenarios. Mamba-based policies also performed comparably to or better than Transformer-based policies in several categories.
- **RoboCasa Benchmark:** In the more challenging RoboCasa environment, which features dynamic backgrounds and object variations, xLSTM-based X-BESO achieved an average success rate of 53.6% when using RGB inputs, surpassing the 40.0% of the BC-Transformer baseline. The trend observed in LIBERO, where Mamba and xLSTM backbones generally outperformed Transformer-based backbones, was consistent in RoboCasa.

#### 4.2. Evaluation on Point Cloud Inputs

- **RoboCasa Benchmark:** X-BESO using point cloud inputs showed superior results compared to the 3D Diffusion Policy baseline. However, a notable observation was that point cloud inputs did not consistently outperform RGB-based inputs (e.g., xLSTM with RGB achieved 53.6%, while with point cloud it achieved 32.8%). This was attributed to the complexity of RoboCasa’s scenarios, where point clouds captured from diverse sources can lead to information loss during sampling, particularly for tasks involving small objects. The Furthest Point Sampling method, by distributing points evenly, may not always preserve task-relevant fine-grained details.

#### 4.3. Multi-modal Fusion (Point Cloud + RGB)

- **RoboCasa Benchmark:** Combining Point Cloud and RGB inputs resulted in performance enhancements. The xLSTM-based model, for instance, achieved a 60.9% success rate with this fusion, an increase compared to using RGB alone (53.6%) or Point Cloud alone (32.8%). This suggests a benefit from integrating complementary modalities.

#### 4.4. Comparison on Architectures

- Experiments comparing Decoder-only and AdaLN-conditioned Encoder-Decoder architectures on select tasks from LIBERO and RoboCasa indicated that the Encoder-Decoder design generally achieved superior performance. This was attributed to its ability to process observations and actions separately, offering more flexibility and scalability.

#### 4.5. Comparison of Diffusion Models Across Varying Inference Steps

- Evaluation of DDPM, BESO, and RF with Decoder-only xLSTM on the TurnOnStove task in RoboCasa demonstrated that DDPM performed less effectively with a single inference step. BESO and RF, however, showed robust performance and improved with an increased number of inference steps. The inference times for BESO and RF were similar, with the speed advantage of flow matching being less pronounced in this context due to the low action dimension.

#### 4.6. Comparison on Different Encoders

- **RGB Encoders:** On RoboCasa, frozen CLIP encoders performed less effectively compared to fine-tuned FiLM-ResNet18 and FiLM-ResNet34. This suggested that a significant domain gap between CLIP's pre-training data and robot manipulation datasets can limit its effectiveness, highlighting the importance of domain adaptation or task-specific fine-tuning for visual representations in imitation learning.
- **Point Cloud Encoders:** The attention-based point cloud encoder generally exhibited better performance than the Max-Pooling encoder, indicating its capability to capture geometric structures more effectively.

#### 4.7. General Observations

The study summarized several general observations:
- Mamba and xLSTM-based policies consistently outperformed Transformer-based policies, given similar model sizes and configurations, across both RGB and point cloud inputs on both benchmarks.
- AdaLN conditioning was identified as an effective method for constructing Encoder-Decoder architectures, improving performance by injecting observation representations into the action decoder.
- Point cloud representations do not inherently outperform RGB-based representations, particularly in complex tasks where standard sampling methods may lead to information loss.
- While simple concatenation of point cloud and RGB inputs improved performance, further exploration of more structured multi-modal fusion mechanisms is warranted.
- Robot-specific trained encoders or domain adaptation were found to be crucial, with general-purpose pre-trained models like CLIP performing poorly on manipulation tasks due to a lack of task-specific features.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several points of significance and potential impact within the field of imitation learning and robotics.

Firstly, the introduction of **X-IL as a modular and open-source framework** addresses a fundamental challenge in the rapidly advancing field of machine learning: the difficulty in systematically evaluating the multitude of emerging techniques for imitation learning. By providing a unified platform with interchangeable components for observation representations, backbones, architectures, and policy representations, X-IL streamlines the experimentation process. This accessibility can accelerate research by reducing the overhead associated with implementing and comparing diverse methods.

Secondly, the **systematic exploration of the design space** undertaken by this study has led to the discovery of novel policy configurations. The empirical demonstration that xLSTM and Mamba-based policies can outperform Transformer-based policies in various scenarios, and that multi-modal fusion significantly enhances performance, represents a step forward in the capabilities of imitation learning agents. Achieving competitive results on challenging benchmarks like LIBERO and RoboCasa indicates the potential for these new configurations to improve the robustness and generalization of learned robotic behaviors.

Thirdly, the **detailed findings and general observations** provide valuable insights for both practitioners and researchers. The identification of Mamba and xLSTM as viable alternatives to Transformers, the effectiveness of AdaLN-conditioned Encoder-Decoder architectures, the nuances of point cloud representations, the benefits and challenges of multi-modal fusion, and the necessity for robot-specific or domain-adapted encoders, all contribute to a deeper understanding of policy design. These insights can inform more efficient and effective development strategies, guiding design choices rather than relying on trial-and-error.

Finally, the work serves as a **practical reference and foundation for future research**. By cataloging and comparing various state-of-the-art components, X-IL provides a robust base for developing scalable and generalizable policies. The identified areas requiring further exploration, such as more structured multi-modal fusion mechanisms, task-aware point cloud sampling, and robotics-specific pre-training for encoders, clearly delineate directions for future investigations. This can contribute to the development of more capable and versatile robots, particularly in complex, dynamic, and real-world environments that demand robust perception and interaction capabilities.
