# ACID: Action Consistency via Inverse Dynamics for Planning with World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The ACID framework enhances decision-time planning with action-conditioned world models by explicitly verifying the 'realizability' of predicted intermediate transitions using an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM). This approach integrates an action consistency cost into the planning objective, leading to improved success rates and reduced total planning compute across diverse robotic manipulation and navigation tasks.

## Problem

- Current decision-time planning with world models evaluates predicted trajectories based only on the final state's proximity to the goal, overlooking the physical realizability of intermediate steps.
- This oversight creates a "realization gap" where a world model might predict a visually plausible path that, when executed, deviates substantially from the prediction.
- Prior methods focused on improving world models themselves, often requiring high training costs or specific model formulations, without directly modifying the planning cost to assess step-by-step realizability.

## Method

- **ACID** introduces ==cycle action consistency== by employing an ==Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM)== (a ==flow-matching== action decoder) to recover the action from a predicted state transition and compare it to the conditioned action.
- Integrated this ==per-step consistency residual==, aggregated over the planning horizon, into an ==augmented planning cost== that combines it with the standard goal cost within ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== and the ==Cross-Entropy Method (CEM)==.
- Developed a ==scale-invariant adaptive weight== to dynamically balance the influence of the action consistency cost relative to the goal cost across different world models, tasks, and CEM iterations.

## Results

- ACID consistently improved planning performance across **four** action-conditioned world models and **six** diverse tasks, yielding higher success rates in manipulation (e.g., **+14.0%** on Reacher for PLDM) and reduced error metrics in navigation (e.g., **-2.3% ATE** for NWM).
- The framework demonstrated robustness to key hyperparameters, matching or exceeding the performance of full-budget baselines with significantly fewer CEM samples (e.g., **30 samples** often equaled **300 samples** for baselines).
- Despite adding a per-step overhead (**8.9%** to **39.4%** of world model latency), ACID frequently achieved target quality with less total planning compute, effectively reducing the required planning steps (e.g., **~0.7x** for DINO-WM on Granular).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An Inverse Dynamics Model can be effectively repurposed as a decision-time verifier within the planning loop, providing a real-time assessment of the physical plausibility of predicted trajectory steps.
> - By augmenting the planning cost with an action consistency term, the planner is guided to select action sequences that are not only goal-reaching but also maintain step-by-step physical realizability in the environment.
> - Adaptive weighting of cost components is crucial for robust performance, allowing the framework to generalize across diverse tasks and world models without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

World models have emerged as a significant component in embodied decision-making, functioning as learned simulators that capture environmental dynamics. Within this domain, decision-time planning, often implemented through test-time optimization, has gained traction as a paradigm for generating control policies without requiring additional policy learning. A common instantiation of this approach involves search-based planning with an action-conditioned world model, where candidate action sequences are simulated through the model, and the sequence minimizing a predefined planning cost is executed. Model Predictive Control (MPC) combined with the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) is a widely adopted framework for this type of decision-time planning.

A recognized limitation within this paradigm is that current decision-time planning methods rely on predicted trajectories without explicitly verifying their "realizability" in the actual environment. The standard planning cost typically evaluates a candidate action sequence solely based on the proximity of its predicted terminal state to the goal state. This approach overlooks the fidelity of intermediate transitions; a trajectory might appear to reach the goal within the world model, but the corresponding actions might not produce the same outcome in the physical environment, leading to a "realization gap." This discrepancy can result in the planner committing to action sequences that cannot be successfully reproduced.

Prior efforts to address this realization gap have largely focused on enhancing the world models themselves. These approaches include scaling up world models with large video-generative backbones or introducing mechanisms to enforce stronger action conditioning during training. However, these methods often entail substantial training costs or necessitate specific probabilistic formulations of the world model, which can be problematic for deterministic latent state transition models like those in the JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) lineage. Furthermore, these improvements to the world model do not fundamentally alter the planning cost, which continues to score trajectories primarily by their final state proximity, without a direct mechanism to assess the realizability of intermediate steps. The presented work aims to provide a complementary, decision-time mechanism that evaluates action fidelity directly within the planning cost, allowing it to be integrated with existing and future world model advancements without requiring modifications to the world model itself. This approach differentiates itself from conventional uses of Inverse Dynamics Models (IDMs), which have typically served as action decoders for state-only planners, auxiliary training tasks for representation learning, or pseudo-labelers for action-free videos, none of which directly influence the planner's selection of action sequences at decision time.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to improve decision-time planning with action-conditioned world models by explicitly addressing the realizability of predicted intermediate transitions. The authors identify a significant limitation in the standard planning paradigm: the planning cost, typically defined by the distance between the predicted final state and the goal state, does not assess whether the intermediate steps of a predicted trajectory are physically realizable by the conditioned actions in the environment. This oversight means that a world model might predict a visually plausible path to a goal that, when executed, deviates substantially from the prediction due to non-realizable transitions, leading to planning failures.

The motivation stems from the observation that while a world model might generate a convincing future state sequence, the environment rollout might not follow it. The planning cost currently lacks a mechanism to discern a genuinely achievable trajectory from one that merely appears successful within the model's internal simulation. This leads to the core question posed by the authors: without retraining or altering the world model, how can the planning cost evaluate if a trajectory actually reaches the goal via realizable steps, rather than merely appearing to do so?

To address this, the paper proposes leveraging a property specific to embodied control: a pair of consecutive observations in a physical environment strongly constrains the action that occurred between them. This property suggests that an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) can be used to verify the consistency of predicted transitions.

The key objectives are:
1. **Introduce Cycle Action Consistency:** Define a per-step measure where the action inferred backward from a predicted transition ($\hat{z}_t, \hat{z}_{t+1}$) by an IDM should match the action ($a_t$) that was conditioned on. This measure quantifies the "realizability" of each predicted step.
2. **Integrate into Planning Cost:** Incorporate this per-step residual, aggregated over the planning horizon, into the existing planning cost. This augmented cost would then prefer action sequences whose predicted trajectories not only reach a goal-like final state but also consist of step-by-step realizable transitions.
3. **Develop a Decision-Time Framework:** Propose ACID (Action Consistency via Inverse Dynamics) as a framework that repurposes an IDM as a decision-time verifier within the planning loop, rather than its conventional offline roles.
4. **Ensure Robustness and Efficiency:** Design the integration with a scale-invariant adaptive weight to manage the relative influence of the consistency cost across different world models, tasks, and CEM iterations. The aim is to achieve consistent performance gains, even with reduced computational budgets, without requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning per task.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to enhance the reliability and efficiency of decision-time planning with action-conditioned world models across diverse control tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, ACID, augments the standard decision-time planning framework with a mechanism to verify the realizability of predicted trajectories. The core components and their integration are detailed as follows:

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Action-Conditioned World Models and Decision-Time Planning

- **World Model:** The work primarily uses action-conditioned world models, particularly JEPA-style latent predictors. These models consist of an encoder $E_\theta$ that maps a raw image observation $o_t$ to a latent state $z_t = E_\theta(o_t)$, and a world model $F_\theta$ that predicts the next latent state $\hat{z}_{t+1}$ from $z_t$ and an action $a_t$, i.e., $\hat{z}_{t+1} = F_\theta(z_t, a_t)$. These models are trained offline on trajectories without reward signals. The same formulation is extended to pixel-space models by treating $E_\theta$ as an identity function.
- **Decision-Time Planning:** The planning is instantiated as Model Predictive Control (MPC) with the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) for optimizing action sequences. Given a current observation $o_0$ and a goal observation $o_g$, both are encoded to latent states $z_0$ and $z_g$. For a candidate action sequence $a_{0:H-1}$ over a planning horizon $H$, the world model autoregressively predicts a latent trajectory $\hat{z}_{1:H}$.
- **Goal Cost:** The standard planning cost, $c_g(a_{0:H-1}) = ||\hat{z}_H - z_g||_2^2$, measures the squared L2 distance between the predicted terminal latent state and the goal latent state. CEM iteratively samples action sequences, evaluates this cost, and updates a Gaussian distribution based on the top-K elite candidates to find an optimal sequence.

#### 3.2. Cycle Action Consistency via Inverse Dynamics

- **Core Principle:** ACID introduces "cycle action consistency." This principle dictates that if a predicted transition $\hat{z}_{t+1} = F_\theta(\hat{z}_t, a_t)$ is truly indicative of the action $a_t$, then an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) $G_\phi$, trained to infer actions from state transitions, should recover $a_t$ when given the predicted transition $(\hat{z}_t, \hat{z}_{t+1})$.
- **Consistency Residual:** At each step of the predicted trajectory, a per-step consistency residual is calculated: $||a_t - G_\phi(\hat{z}_t, \hat{z}_{t+1})||_2^2$. This residual quantifies the discrepancy between the conditioned action and the action inferred by the IDM from the predicted transition. A low residual indicates a realizable transition, while a high residual suggests the world model's prediction might be drifting from what the action actually produces.
- **Action Consistency Cost:** This per-step residual is aggregated over the planning horizon to form the action consistency cost: $c_a(a_{0:H-1}) = \frac{1}{H} \sum_{t=0}^{H-1} ||a_t - G_\phi(\hat{z}_t, \hat{z}_{t+1})||_2^2$. This cost is computed using the same predicted trajectory already generated for evaluating $c_g$, minimizing additional world model rollouts.
- **IDM Implementation:** The IDM, $G_\phi$, is implemented as a flow matching-based action decoder. It uses a compact prefix-suffix transformer, taking two consecutive latents $z_t$ and $z_{t+1}$ as input and inferring the action $a_t$ that generated the transition. The IDM is trained separately for each task using offline datasets and the frozen encoder latents of the corresponding world model. Inference is designed to be lightweight, typically requiring a single Euler step, adding minimal overhead.

#### 3.3. Planning with Cycle Action Consistency

- **Augmented Planning Cost:** The proposed augmented planning cost combines the standard goal cost $c_g$ with the new action consistency cost $c_a$: $c(a_{0:H-1}) = c_g(a_{0:H-1}) + w_a \cdot c_a(a_{0:H-1})$. This augmented cost is then used within the CEM optimization loop. It encourages the planner to select action sequences that both lead to a goal-like final state and maintain step-by-step realizability.
- **Scale-Invariant Adaptive Weight:** To manage the relative influence of $c_g$ and $c_a$, an adaptive weight $w_a$ is introduced. It addresses the observation that the absolute and relative magnitudes of $c_g$ and $c_a$ can vary significantly across different world models, tasks, and CEM iterations. A fixed weight would generalize poorly. The adaptive weight is calculated at every CEM iteration as: $w_a = \lambda \cdot \frac{\sigma_g}{\sigma_a}$, where $\sigma_g$ and $\sigma_a$ are the standard deviations of the goal cost and action consistency cost, respectively, across the current batch of $N$ candidate action sequences. This formulation equalizes the spread of the consistency cost relative to the goal cost, scaled by a user-tuned parameter $\lambda$. This ensures that both costs contribute comparably to the ranking of candidates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate ACID's performance across various world models and tasks, its robustness to hyperparameters, and its computational efficiency.

#### 4.1. Generality Across World Models and Dynamics (Q1)

ACID consistently improved planning performance across four action-conditioned world models and six diverse tasks:

- **Rigid/Articulated Manipulation (Cube, Reacher, PushT):** For JEPA-style latent predictors Le-WM and PLDM, ACID resulted in increased success rates. For instance, Le-WM showed improvements of +4.0% on Cube, +12.0% on Reacher, and +4.0% on PushT. PLDM demonstrated similar gains, with +10.0% on Cube, +14.0% on Reacher, and +4.0% on PushT.
- **Deformable Object Manipulation (Rope, Granular):** With DINO-WM, ACID achieved substantial reductions in Chamfer distance, indicating closer adherence to target configurations. Chamfer distance decreased by 0.82 units on Rope and 0.19 units on Granular.
- **Visual Navigation:** When integrated with NWM trained with CompACT, ACID led to improvements in navigation trajectory quality, with ATE (Absolute Trajectory Error) reducing by 2.3% and RPE (Relative Pose Error, translational component) reducing by 1.5%.

These consistent gains are attributed to ACID's mechanism of filtering out non-realizable candidate trajectories by verifying each step against its conditioned action via the inverse dynamics model. Qualitative results further illustrated this, showing that baseline plans often resulted in predicted goal-reaching trajectories that diverged significantly from actual environment rollouts, whereas ACID's plans produced environment rollouts that closely tracked the predicted trajectories.

#### 4.2. Hyperparameter Robustness (Q2)

ACID exhibited robustness to key hyperparameters:

- **CEM Planning Budget:** Across a range of CEM samples (30 to 300), ACID consistently outperformed the baseline. Notably, ACID operating at the smallest budget (30 samples) often matched or exceeded the performance of the full-budget baseline (300 samples) for both Le-WM and PLDM on tasks like Reacher, indicating a significant reduction in required samples for comparable accuracy.
- **Consistency Weight ($\lambda$):** Sweeping $\lambda$ over nearly two orders of magnitude (0.005 to 0.1) demonstrated that a broad range of values yielded consistent performance gains without requiring fine-tuning per task. This robustness is attributed to the scale-invariant adaptive weight.
- **Adaptive vs. Constant Weight:** Empirical analysis confirmed that using a fixed consistency weight ($w_a$) performed inconsistently across tasks, with the optimal constant varying. The proposed adaptive weight, $w_a = \lambda \cdot (\sigma_g / \sigma_a)$, consistently provided larger and more robust total improvements across all tasks.

#### 4.3. Net Efficiency (Q3)

Despite the additional computation introduced by the inverse dynamics verifier, ACID often achieved target quality with less total planning compute:

- **Per-Step Overhead:** The IDM verifier added a latency ranging from 8.9% (PLDM) to 39.4% (DINO-WM) of the world model's forward latency per CEM iteration.
- **Reduced Total Compute:** Due to its ability to improve planning effectiveness, ACID frequently reached baseline quality with significantly fewer CEM samples or planning steps. For instance, on the Granular and Rope manipulation tasks, ACID achieved the baseline's final Chamfer distance plateau in fewer planning steps, leading to a net reduction in compute (e.g., approximately 0.7x for DINO-WM on Granular) despite the per-step overhead.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ACID framework introduces a method to enhance the reliability and efficiency of decision-time planning with action-conditioned world models, addressing a core limitation in current approaches.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

- **Directly Addresses Realizability Gap:** The primary significance lies in explicitly verifying the realizability of intermediate transitions within a predicted trajectory. By incorporating "cycle action consistency" into the planning cost, ACID moves beyond simply evaluating the terminal state, ensuring that the entire path to the goal is plausible given the conditioned actions. This can lead to more robust and trustworthy control policies in embodied AI.
- **Generality and Modularity:** ACID demonstrates consistent performance improvements across a diverse set of action-conditioned world models (latent predictors, video generative models) and tasks (rigid, deformable, articulated manipulation, visual navigation). Crucially, the method intervenes only on the planning cost and leaves the world model untouched. This modularity means ACID can be seamlessly integrated with existing and future advancements in world model architectures without requiring retraining or specific model formulations, enhancing its broad applicability.
- **Improved Efficiency:** Despite introducing a per-step overhead for the inverse dynamics verifier, ACID often achieves comparable or superior performance with substantially less total planning compute. This is primarily due to its ability to filter out non-realizable trajectories earlier, leading to faster convergence to effective action sequences or achieving target quality with fewer planning iterations. Such computational efficiency is valuable for real-world robotic applications where planning resources can be limited.
- **Novel Role for Inverse Dynamics Models:** The work repurposes the Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) from its conventional roles (e.g., action decoding after a trajectory is chosen, or as an auxiliary training objective) into a real-time, decision-time verifier within the planning loop. This new application of IDMs adds a critical feedback mechanism during the planning process.
- **Adaptive Cost Weighting:** The introduction of a scale-invariant adaptive weight for balancing the goal cost and action consistency cost contributes to the robustness of the framework. This mechanism ensures consistent influence of the consistency term across varying world models, tasks, and planning stages, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**

The authors acknowledge certain limitations. ACID's effectiveness relies on the assumption that a pair of consecutive observations strongly identifies the action between them. Its performance might degrade under conditions of partial observability or when exogenous interventions disrupt the expected dynamics. Furthermore, the method requires a one-time training phase for the inverse dynamics verifier. However, this training reuses existing offline trajectories without needing additional environment interaction, and the trained verifier remains valid even if the world model is subsequently changed. Future work could explore extending ACID to scenarios with higher partial observability or integrating it with even more advanced action-conditioned world models to leverage combined improvements.
