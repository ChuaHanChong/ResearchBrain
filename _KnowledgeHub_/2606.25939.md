# DeformGen: Dynamics-Based Topology Augmentation for Deformable Manipulation Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> DeformGen augments deformable-object manipulation demonstrations by applying randomized localized forces and forward-simulating physics from a valid state, so synthesized configurations stay on the physically plausible manifold, then warping a single source trajectory onto each new state through a KNN inverse-distance deformation field that adapts both gripper position and orientation. One demo per task yields success rates of 37-59% versus 1-3% unaugmented.

## Problem

- Rigid-style demonstration augmentation assumes SE(3) equivariance, so 6-DoF pose perturbation cannot reach the high-dimensional shape and topology variation that deformable objects actually occupy.
- Naive geometric or per-particle perturbations leave the physically plausible manifold, producing broken connectivity or dynamically inadmissible shapes that post-hoc simulation repair cannot reliably restore.
- Material points move non-equivariantly, so rigid trajectory transfer misaligns the grasp with local geometry; prior deformable-aware transfer still samples states rigidly, limiting coverage.

## Method

- Dynamic Topological Transformation: randomized spatially localized forces applied to a known valid state, forward-simulated then stabilized, so augmented configurations never leave the plausible subspace and need no repair.
- Deformation-Field Warping: per-particle displacements lifted by K-nearest-neighbor inverse-distance weighting into a continuous spatial field, offsetting each end-effector waypoint with a time-decayed factor back toward the source path.
- Orientation adaptation fits a least-squares local Jacobian over the KNN neighborhood, projects it onto SO(3) via SVD, and SLERPs gripper rotation; small K for grasp, all points for manipulation.

## Results

- Across rope routing, toy packing and cloth folding, the full method averaged 59.0% (ACT), 56.5% (SmolVLA), 56.7% (pi0) versus 1.3-2.5% for the single unaugmented demo.
- Isolating state augmentation, it beat the rigid-state SoftMimicGen reimplementation on 3 of 4 architectures (pi0 56.67% vs 27.83%); field warping beat local rigid transfer on 3 of 4.
- Scaling synthetic demos from 100 to 750 lifted ACT 19.50% to 61.50% and SmolVLA 36.83% to 63.17%; policies also succeeded on states where trajectory synthesis itself failed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulators preserve plausibility going forward from a valid state but cannot restore it after an invalid edit, so sample by pushing physics, not by editing coordinates.
> - Broader state coverage and deformation-aware trajectory transfer contribute independently and stack, but the gain size swings by architecture; diffusion policy benefited least.
> - Topological diversity costs little on rigid-only test states, yet cloth stays near or below 30% success, so contact-heavy multi-fold tasks remain unsolved.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot manipulation has advanced significantly due to the integration of imitation learning and visuomotor policy learning, enabling robots to perform various tasks across different environments. A crucial factor in this progress has been the availability of large-scale and diverse demonstration data. However, the collection of such data is often expensive, time-consuming, and challenging to scale. To address this bottleneck, data augmentation techniques have emerged, aiming to generate multiple training trajectories from a limited number of human demonstrations.

For rigid objects, existing augmentation methods typically rely on the equivariant assumption, where the Euclidean distance between material points remains invariant under motion or contact forces. This allows for simple Euclidean transformations (SE(3) perturbations) of an object's pose, maintaining a valid relative pose between the end-effector and the object.

However, this conventional augmentation paradigm is not directly applicable to deformable object manipulation. The inherent properties of deformable objects—such as their high-dimensional state space with dynamic, physics-induced constraints and the non-equivariant nature of material point movement under deformation—render rigid transformations ineffective. Naive geometric perturbations of deformable objects frequently result in physically implausible shapes or structural discontinuities. Furthermore, directly applying rigid transformations to trajectories for deformable objects can lead to misaligned grasp poses and an inability to compensate for local deformations, thus failing to capture task semantics.

Prior research in deformable object manipulation has explored various approaches, including model-based planning using physical simulators (mass-spring, FEM, particle-based models) and learning visuomotor policies directly from demonstrations or interactions. More recently, large-scale vision-language-action (VLA) models have shown potential for generalization but typically require even larger datasets, exacerbating the data scarcity problem. Some attempts, such as SoftMimicGen, have tried to mitigate the trajectory-transfer challenge for deformable objects but still face limitations in generating diverse and plausible state-space variations.

This work, DeformGen, addresses the identified gaps by proposing a framework that specifically tackles the challenges of state-space diversity and trajectory transfer for deformable objects. It aims to provide a method for cost-efficient data acquisition that is tailored to the unique characteristics of deformable manipulation, thereby enhancing the generalization and robustness of learned policies beyond what rigid-style augmentation or existing deformable-specific methods can achieve.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for DeformGen is to overcome the data acquisition bottleneck in deformable object manipulation by developing an effective data augmentation framework. The authors identify two core challenges that limit the applicability of existing rigid-object augmentation methods to deformable objects:

1. **State-Space Challenge:**
 - **High Degrees of Freedom:** Unlike rigid bodies, which can be adequately described by a 6-DoF pose, deformable objects exhibit complex, high-dimensional shape and topological variations. Rigid transformations alone are insufficient to generate the diverse and valid state distributions necessary for robust deformable manipulation.
 - **Dynamic Constraint:** For rigid and articulated objects, valid perturbations can often be directly constructed in pose or joint space using kinematic constraints. In contrast, deformable objects are subject to internal dynamic constraints, meaning their deformation depends on the interaction of internal particles. Consequently, arbitrary geometric perturbations often lead to physically implausible shapes and discontinuous structural changes that do not conform to real-world physics. The physically plausible states form a constrained manifold within the high-dimensional particle state space, and naive perturbations frequently cause the object to leave this manifold.

2. **Trajectory-Transfer Challenge:**
 - **Non-Equivariance:** The fundamental equivariant assumption—that relative positions and orientations are preserved—does not hold for deformable objects because material points do not move rigidly together. Directly applying a global rigid isometry to transfer trajectories to topologically varied deformable objects creates two problems:
 - **Grasp Misalignment:** The end-effector's grasp pose can become misaligned with the object's local geometry, preventing successful grasping.
 - **Lack of Compensation for Local Deformation:** A rigid-style trajectory transfer can only translate and rotate the entire trajectory, failing to capture or compensate for localized object deformations that are critical for successful manipulation.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of DeformGen are:

- To develop a dynamics-based augmentation framework that generates *topological diversity* for deformable objects.
- To synthesize *physically plausible deformable states* that remain within the valid manifold, thereby expanding the training data beyond rigid pose variations.
- To enable *deformation-aware transfer of manipulation trajectories* that consistently adapt the end-effector's behavior to the deformed geometry.
- To jointly augment both the state distribution and its associated manipulation behavior, leading to policies that generalize better to unseen and diverse deformable configurations.

The authors formulate three specific contributions:
1. Identifying physically valid deformable state synthesis as the critical missing component beyond rigid-style augmentation.
2. Proposing a dynamics-consistent pipeline for generating topology-coherent deformable assets and corresponding manipulation trajectories.
3. Providing empirical evidence that the synthetic demonstrations generated by DeformGen significantly improve policy learning compared to baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DeformGen is designed as a dynamics-based topology data augmentation framework comprising two main components: state augmentation and trajectory augmentation. It aims to synthesize a substantial volume of valid manipulation data for various initial object states from a sparse set of demonstrations.

#### 3.1. State Augmentation (Dynamic Topological Transformation)

The objective of state augmentation is to generate diverse, physically plausible object configurations for a given task. The authors define the configuration space $S = R^{3N}$ for an object discretized into $N$ particles, with $S_{real} \subset S$ representing physically plausible configurations. A core working assumption is that a well-calibrated physics simulator can approximately preserve physical plausibility when evolving from a valid state, but cannot reliably restore it from an invalid one.

DeformGen's approach, termed "Dynamic Topological Transformation," generates augmented states by applying localized external forces to a known valid initial state ($s_0 \in S_{real}$) and then forward-simulating the object's dynamics using a physics simulator ($\Phi_{sim}$):
$s_{aug} = \Phi_{sim}(s_0, f, \Delta t)$, where $f$ is a localized force field.
This method ensures that the augmented states remain within $S_{real}$ because they evolve through the simulator’s own dynamics, which inherently enforces physical constraints (e.g., collision handling, material properties). This avoids the problem of needing post-hoc repair from invalid states. Localized forces can induce various non-rigid deformations like bending, twisting, and draping, thereby expanding the reachable set beyond low-dimensional rigid transformations.

This approach is contrasted with three alternative strategies that fall short:
- **Global rigid transformation:** Preserves plausibility but offers only 6-DoF variations, lacking shape or topological diversity.
- **Per-particle independent perturbation:** Can reach diverse states but typically breaks inter-particle connectivity, leading to implausible configurations that subsequent stabilization often fails to recover reliably.
- **Kinematic topological transformation:** Preserves connectivity but generates states that may violate internal dynamic or material constraints, leading to dynamically inadmissible configurations.

In implementation, localized physical disturbances are realized by commanding the robot's gripper to execute randomized Cartesian perturbations while in contact with the object, transmitting forces through contact dynamics. These perturbations are followed by a stabilization phase where the object reaches quasi-static equilibrium. Task-specific parameters for step counts, translation magnitudes, and rotation probabilities are used.

#### 3.2. Trajectory Augmentation (Deformation-Field Warping)

The objective of trajectory augmentation is to synthesize valid manipulation trajectories for the newly generated object configurations. Unlike rigid trajectory transfer methods that assume uniform object transformation, DeformGen uses "Deformation-Field Warping" to adapt trajectories to local deformations.

- **Position Warping:** For a source object state ($p_{orig}$) and a deformed state ($p_{def}$), per-particle displacements $\delta_i = p_{def,i} - p_{orig,i}$ are calculated. For each end-effector position $x_t$ in the source trajectory, its $K$ nearest neighbors from $p_{orig}$ are identified. These displacements are then aggregated using inverse-distance weighting to compute a local displacement vector $d(x_t)$. The warped position $x_{warp,t}$ is obtained by applying this displacement to $x_t$, modulated by a time-dependent decay function $\alpha_t = decay(t)$, which allows for stronger local adaptation initially and gradual reversion to the original path.
 $x_{warp,t} = x_t + \alpha_t \cdot d(x_t)$

- **Orientation Adaptation:** To adapt the end-effector's orientation, local relative coordinates are constructed within the KNN neighborhood of $x_t$ for both original ($\ell_{orig}$) and deformed ($\ell_{def}$) points. A local Jacobian matrix $J_t$ is estimated via least squares fitting to map the original local vectors to the deformed ones. This $J_t$ is then used to derive an induced rotation $R'_t$, which is projected onto the SO(3) manifold via SVD. The final warped orientation $R_{warp,t}$ is computed using Spherical Linear Interpolation (SLERP) between the original orientation $R_t$ and $R'_t$, also modulated by $\alpha_t$.
 For practical considerations, different values of $K$ are used for warping the grasp pose (small $K$ for local correlation) versus the manipulation phase (large $K$ or all points $N$ for global deformation). In the experimental setup, rotations are constrained to the Z-axis due to the tabletop manipulation scenario.

#### 3.3. Policy Training

To evaluate DeformGen, policies are trained using imitation learning on the augmented dataset and evaluated within a simulation environment (Real2Sim-Eval with PhysTwin). The process involves:
1. Collecting one teleoperation demonstration per task.
2. Synthesizing over 1200 distinct object states per task using the state augmentation method.
3. Synthesizing corresponding manipulation trajectories for these augmented states using Deformation-Field Warping (and a rigid-transfer ablation).
4. Executing these augmented trajectories in simulation to verify their success and record RGB images and actions.
5. Splitting successful episodes into training and held-out test sets.
6. Training four different policy architectures (ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA, π0) on the augmented data.
7. Evaluating the trained policies on held-out object states, including configurations where trajectory synthesis itself failed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on three deformable manipulation tasks (rope routing, toy packing, and cloth folding) using the Real2Sim-Eval benchmark with PhysTwin for high-fidelity physics and rendering. Policies were trained using ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), SmolVLA, and π0 architectures.

#### 4.1. Overall Policy Performance

Table 2 presents the policy evaluation success rates across the four training regimes:
- **1 Src.:** Training on a single source demonstration.
- **SoftMimicGen\* (SMG\*):** Rigid state augmentation with deformation-field warping.
- **DeformGen\* (DG\*):** Topological state augmentation with local rigid trajectory transfer.
- **DeformGen (DG):** Full DeformGen, combining topological state augmentation with deformation-field warping.

DeformGen (DG) achieved the highest average success rate across three out of the four policy architectures (ACT, SmolVLA, π0), demonstrating an improvement over training with a single demonstration and baseline augmentation methods. For instance, with the ACT architecture, DG achieved an average success rate of 59.00%, compared to 1.33% for 1 Src., 48.17% for SMG*, and 46.83% for DG*.

#### 4.2. Contributions of State and Trajectory Augmentation

The comparative analysis revealed insights into the individual contributions of DeformGen's components:

- **Topological State Diversity (SMG\* vs. DG):** Comparing SMG\* (rigid state augmentation + deformation-field warping) with DG (topological state augmentation + deformation-field warping) showed that DG generally achieved higher average success rates. This suggests that generating a broader coverage of physically plausible deformable object configurations through dynamics-based topological state augmentation is important for policy generalization, surpassing the diversity offered by rigid state perturbations.
- **Deformation-Field Warping (DG\* vs. DG):** Comparing DG\* (topological state augmentation + local rigid trajectory transfer) with DG (topological state augmentation + deformation-field warping) indicated that DG often improved over DG\*. This implies that deformation-aware trajectory warping, which adapts the manipulation path via a continuous deformation field, provides complementary benefits to policy learning compared to simpler local rigid transformations.

Qualitative examples (Figure 6, 7) illustrate that policies trained with DeformGen successfully completed tasks across diverse deformable configurations, exhibiting improved grasp alignment and more complete manipulation compared to comparison methods.

#### 4.3. State Coverage Analysis

A quantitative analysis of the augmented states (Figure 8) decomposed each state into a rigid SE(3) component and a non-rigid residual using Procrustes alignment.
- States generated by rigid augmentation clustered near the source configuration with negligible non-rigid residuals, confirming their limited diversity.
- States generated by DeformGen, in contrast, spread broadly in the state space and exhibited large non-rigid residuals. This empirically verified that DeformGen achieves genuine topological diversity by producing physically plausible yet distinct shape deformations, such as varying rope curvatures, stuffed toy compressions, and cloth folds.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Generalization to Rigid-Only Scenarios (Table 3):** When evaluated on test states involving only rigid transformations, SMG\* (trained with rigid state augmentation) generally achieved the highest success rates for ACT, DP, and SmolVLA, as expected. However, DeformGen (DG) remained competitive and even performed best for the π0 architecture. This suggests that training on topologically diverse data does not substantially compromise performance on simpler rigid scenarios.
- **Impact of Synthetic Data Quantity (Table 4):** Experiments showed that average policy performance generally improved with an increased quantity of synthetic data. For ACT and SmolVLA, average success rates increased monotonically as the number of synthetic demonstrations rose from 100 to 750, indicating that dynamics-based augmentation benefits from larger datasets.
- **Generalization to Synthesis Failure Cases (Table 5):** Policies trained with DeformGen augmentation achieved non-trivial success rates on "hard samples"—object states for which the trajectory synthesis method itself failed to produce a valid demonstration. This suggests that the policies learned generalizable manipulation strategies rather than merely memorizing individual augmented demonstrations, enabling a degree of extrapolation to unseen configurations.

#### 4.5. Failure Analysis

Common failure modes (Figure 9) included imprecise grasping of highly deformed configurations, where the visual appearance significantly deviated from the training data, and premature release of objects due to unstable contact under large deformations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DeformGen addresses a recognized bottleneck in robot learning: the high cost and difficulty of acquiring diverse demonstration data, particularly for deformable objects. By proposing a principled, dynamics-based augmentation framework, this work provides a method for generating physically plausible deformable states and associated manipulation trajectories more efficiently.

The primary significance of this work lies in its demonstrated ability to improve policy learning and generalization for deformable manipulation tasks in simulation. The findings suggest that effective augmentation for deformable objects requires a joint approach that considers both dynamics-consistent state synthesis and deformation-aware trajectory transfer, moving beyond the limitations of rigid-style augmentation.

Potential positive impacts include:
- **Reduced Data Collection Costs:** Automating the generation of diverse training data can significantly lower the resource requirements for developing robot skills involving soft materials.
- **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Generalization:** Policies trained with DeformGen demonstrated improved success rates on novel, unseen deformable configurations, which is crucial for real-world deployment where object states can vary significantly.
- **Expanded Applications:** More accessible and scalable robot learning for deformable objects could accelerate advancements in various domains, such as:
 - **Household assistance:** Robots capable of handling laundry, preparing food (e.g., dough, soft vegetables), or arranging flexible items.
 - **Manufacturing:** Automation of tasks involving flexible components, textiles, or soft packaging.
 - **Healthcare:** Development of more dexterous surgical robots or assistive devices for handling delicate tissues.

While the work is currently validated in high-fidelity simulation, it lays a foundation for future sim-to-real transfer. The identified limitations, such as reliance on single-arm manipulation, limited task diversity, and the sim-to-real gap, provide clear directions for future research to broaden the framework's applicability and real-world impact. The acknowledgment of trajectory synthesis failures and the promising generalization to these "hard samples" also points to the learning of more fundamental manipulation strategies. This work could therefore contribute to making robot learning for complex deformable tasks more practical and scalable.
