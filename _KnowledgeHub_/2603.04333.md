# What Does Flow Matching Bring To TD Learning?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University explain the empirical success of flow-matching critics in Temporal Difference (TD) learning, attributing their benefits to Test-Time Recovery and Plastic Feature Learning rather than distributional modeling. This approach significantly enhances performance and stability in high update-to-data online reinforcement learning, yielding up to 2x higher final performance and 5x better sample efficiency.

## Problem

- The underlying mechanisms driving the observed empirical gains and robustness of flow-matching critics in RL remained largely unexplained.
- Prevailing hypotheses attributed flow-matching success to implicit modeling of return distributions, similar to distributional RL, requiring rigorous testing.
- Standard TD learning algorithms suffer from pathologies like plasticity loss and sensitivity to non-stationary targets, which existing methods often address with explicit regularization.

## Method

- The ==`floq` architecture==, which uses ==flow matching== solely for expected Q-value parameterization, was utilized to disentangle its benefits from distributional RL.
- A rigorous combination of theoretical analysis and empirical validation was applied to formalize and test two proposed mechanisms: ==Test-Time Recovery (TTR)== and ==Plastic Feature Learning==.
- Controlled experiments, including injecting staleness, adding noise, and freezing features, were designed to validate these mechanisms and compare flow-matching critics against monolithic baselines.

## Results

- Flow-matching critics demonstrate approximately **2x** higher final performance and **5x** better sample efficiency in high update-to-data online RL on OGBench tasks compared to monolithic baselines.
- They exhibit strong Test-Time Recovery, maintaining comparable performance even when **25-50%** of initial integration steps use stale parameters, and show significantly higher robustness to noisy TD targets.
- The learned features in flow-matching critics display improved plasticity, allowing adaptation to non-stationary TD targets through feature reweighting, while monolithic critics suffer severe performance collapse when features are frozen.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The success of flow-matching critics is not due to their ability to model return distributions; an expected-value flow-matching critic performs comparably to or better than distributional variants.
> - Test-Time Recovery (TTR), enabled by iterative integration, allows flow-matching critics to correct imperfect intermediate estimates and dampen errors, with error effects decaying polynomially with integration steps.
> - Dense velocity supervision during training induces Plastic Feature Learning, allowing flow-matching critics to adapt to non-stationary TD targets by reweighting existing features rather than modifying them directly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the field of Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically focusing on value function estimation using Temporal Difference (TD) learning. Recent advancements have introduced flow matching, a technique rooted in generative modeling, into RL for Q-value estimation. These "flow-matching critics" have demonstrated empirical performance gains and improved robustness compared to traditional "monolithic" critics, which estimate Q-values in a single forward pass. However, the underlying mechanisms responsible for these observed benefits have remained largely unexamined.

A prevailing hypothesis in prior work has been that flow-matching critics succeed due to their ability to implicitly model return distributions, akin to distributional RL approaches such as C51 or IQN. Several recent studies applying flow matching to value learning have adopted this distributional perspective. This paper challenges that hypothesis, providing evidence that the gains do not primarily stem from distributional modeling.

The research also connects to a broader body of work addressing pathologies in TD learning. These pathologies include value overestimation, growth in parameter norms, and rapid loss of representational plasticity, often attributed to bootstrapping and target non-stationarity. Existing stabilization techniques involve architectural modifications (e.g., layer normalization), feature normalization, or alternative objectives. This paper proposes that flow matching offers an intrinsic mechanism for stabilizing TD learning by improving plasticity and robustness, without requiring additional, explicit regularization techniques.

Furthermore, the work differentiates itself from other analyses of flow matching outside of RL by focusing on the context of non-stationary TD targets. It also contrasts with studies on iterative computation in policies that operate on static datasets, emphasizing the unique challenges posed by the evolving nature of TD learning targets. By clarifying the mechanisms of flow-matching critics, this paper aims to contribute a fundamental understanding that can guide future developments in value-based RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to elucidate the mechanisms through which flow-matching Q-functions enhance Temporal Difference (TD) learning. While empirical gains from flow-matching critics have been demonstrated, the specific reasons for their effectiveness have been unclear. The paper aims to answer fundamental questions, such as how iterative integration interacts with TD bootstrapping and why a flow-matching approach, even when targeting only expected values, can improve performance.

The motivation stems from two main hypotheses, which the authors propose as explanations for the success of flow-matching critics, in contrast to the commonly held belief that distributional RL is the driving factor:

1. **Challenging the Distributional RL Hypothesis:** Previous works often implicitly or explicitly linked flow matching in RL to distributional learning, suggesting that modeling the full return distribution was key. The authors explicitly test this by comparing flow-matching critics trained with expected-value backups (which do not learn the return distribution) against those trained with distributional backups. Their motivation is to determine if explicitly modeling return distributions is necessary for the observed performance gains or if simpler expected-value targets suffice.
2. **Proposing Test-Time Recovery (TTR):** The authors hypothesize that the iterative integration process used by flow-matching critics, coupled with dense velocity supervision during training, enables a mechanism called "test-time recovery." This means that the critic can iteratively refine its Q-value prediction, allowing later integration steps to correct or dampen errors that may have arisen in earlier estimates. This corrective ability is posited to be absent in monolithic critics, which compute values in a single pass.
3. **Proposing Plastic Feature Learning:** The research also posits that supervising the velocity field at multiple interpolant values along the integration trajectory induces more plastic feature learning within the network. In the context of non-stationary TD targets, monolithic critics often struggle with plasticity loss, having to constantly overwrite previously learned features to adapt to changing targets. Flow-matching critics, by contrast, are hypothesized to accommodate these shifts through the integration process itself, allowing their underlying features to remain more stable and adaptable to future targets without discarding prior knowledge or overfitting to individual targets.

By formalizing and empirically validating these proposed mechanisms (Test-Time Recovery and Plastic Feature Learning), the paper seeks to provide a foundational understanding of flow matching's role in TD learning, thereby guiding the development of more robust and efficient RL algorithms. The investigation predominantly uses "floq," a flow-matching critic that targets expected values, to isolate and analyze these non-distributional benefits.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on analyzing the mechanisms of flow-matching critics, particularly "floq" (Agrawalla et al.,), which uses flow matching to represent an expected Q-function without explicitly modeling return distributions. This is compared against traditional "monolithic" critics and, in specific cases, a distributional flow-matching variant.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries and Critic Architectures

- **Standard RL Setup:** The work operates within the framework of value-based RL, training policies in Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) using an offline dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(s, a, r, s')\}$.
- **Flow-Matching Critics (General):** These critics parameterize a time-dependent velocity field $v_{\theta}(z, t | s, a)$ that defines an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). Starting from an initial noise sample $z_0 \sim p_0(z)$ at $t=0$, numerical integration of this ODE (yielding $\psi(t, z|s, a)$) produces a value sample at $t=1$.
- **Distributional Flow-Matching Critics:** As implemented by prior works (e.g., Chen et al.), these critics construct a distributional TD target $Z(s, a; z') = r(s, a) + \gamma \psi_{\bar{\theta}}(1, z' | s', a')$ by pushing noise $z'$ through the target flow. The loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{dist}}(\theta)$ aligns $v_{\theta}(z(t), t | s, a)$ with the target velocity $\bar{s}_{z,z'}(s,a) = Z(s, a; z') - z$, where $z(t)$ is an interpolant between $z$ and $Z(s, a; z')$.
- **floq (Expected-Value Flow-Matching Critic):** This variant uses flow matching purely as a parameterization for an expected Q-function. It estimates an expected-value TD target $y(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^m \psi_{\bar{\theta}}(1, z'_j | s', a')$ by averaging over multiple noise samples from the target flow. The loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{floq}}(\theta)$ regresses $v_{\theta}(z(t), t | s, a)$ to $(y(s, a) - z)$, where $z(t)$ interpolates between $z$ and $y(s, a)$.

#### 3.2. Experimental Design to Test Hypotheses

- **Is Distributional RL Crucial?**
 - **Method:** Compare `floq` (expected-value) with a modified `floq` using the distributional backup objective from Equation 2. Both variants use identical velocity field architectures and hyperparameters.
 - **Evaluation:** Conducted on four OGBench tasks, measuring expected Q-value, variance of learned Q-value distribution, and policy performance.
- **Flow Matching Enables Test-Time Recovery (TTR):**
 - **Theoretical Formalization:** Introduce Definition 5.1 for Test-Time Recovery (perturbations in velocity evaluations are dampened along the trajectory) and Definition 5.2 for the $c$-conic condition (velocity field's derivative with respect to $z$ is negative and scales with $1/(1-t)$). Theorem D.4 establishes polynomial decay of perturbations under these conditions.
 - **Experiment: Injecting Staleness:** After training, the integration process is split. The first $\kappa\%$ steps use a stale snapshot of the velocity field, while subsequent steps use current parameters. Performance is compared against a baseline with no staleness, and against monolithic critics where early layers are frozen.
 - **Experiment: Robustness to Noisy TD Targets:** Random i.i.d. zero-mean noise is added to the TD velocity targets (for `floq`) or TD targets (for monolithic critics) during training. Performance degradation is evaluated as noise magnitude increases.
- **Flow Matching Learns Plastic Features:**
 - **Theoretical Analysis in a Linear Setting:** A simplified linear model is used to compare a linear monolithic critic (parameterized by a single weight vector) against a linear Euler flow-matching critic (decomposing into slice-wise weights and gain parameters). Theorem 6.1 demonstrates that flow-matching critics can adapt by reweighting existing features through gain dynamics, while monolithic critics must modify their feature directions.
 - **Experiment: Measuring Properties of Learned Features:** The $\ell_2$-norm of features in the penultimate and last hidden layers is measured for `floq` and monolithic critics under three training objectives: TD learning, SARSA, and supervised regression to Monte Carlo (MC) returns.
 - **Experiment: Measuring Feature Plasticity by Freezing Features:** During offline training, all layers except the final two are frozen at an intermediate point. The recovery or collapse of performance is observed for `floq`, monolithic critics (including ResNet and transformer architectures), and a `floq` variant with only a single integration step (train at zero only).
 - **Experiment: Intermediate Velocity Supervision is Crucial:** A `floq` variant is implemented where each integration step is supervised to predict the *absolute TD target value* itself, rather than the *velocity*. Its performance is compared to standard `floq` and monolithic critics.
- **Application: High-UTD Online RL with Prior Data:**
 - **Method:** Integrate flow-matching critics into the RLPD (Reinforcement Learning from Prior Data) framework. This involved adapting RLPD to use flow policies with a behavior cloning (BC) regularizer and standard replay buffer initialization.
 - **Evaluation:** Compare `floq` against a monolithic FQL baseline across a range of Update-to-Data (UTD) ratios on OGBench environments, assessing final performance, sample efficiency, and learning stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several key findings across its experimental and theoretical analyses, addressing the mechanisms underlying the efficacy of flow-matching critics in TD learning.

#### 4.1. Distributional RL is Not Crucial for Success

- **Empirical Observation:** When comparing `floq` (expected-value flow matching) with a distributional flow-matching variant, both models learned similar expected Q-values. However, the distributional variant yielded Q-value distributions with significantly higher variance, more closely reflecting the expected variation in returns for OGBench tasks.
- **Performance:** Despite modeling return distributions more accurately, the distributional flow-matching variant did not offer performance benefits and frequently performed worse than expected-value `floq`. This finding, combined with previous results showing `floq` outperforming strong distributional RL baselines, indicates that the success of flow-matching critics is not primarily attributable to their capacity for distributional modeling.

#### 4.2. Flow Matching Enables Test-Time Recovery (TTR)

- **Theoretical Basis:** The paper formalized TTR by showing that if the learned velocity field satisfies a $c$-conic condition (where its derivative with respect to the interpolant is negative and scales with $1/(1-t)$), then perturbations or errors introduced at earlier integration steps are progressively dampened. The terminal error decreases polynomially with the number of integration steps ($O(K^{-c})$ for $K$ steps). This contraction arises from the dense supervision of the velocity field across multiple interpolant values and time steps.
- **Injecting Staleness Experiment:** Flow-matching critics demonstrated resilience to staleness. On 2 out of 3 tasks, using stale velocity parameters for the initial 25% or 50% of integration steps in the critic resulted in policies with success rates comparable to or even slightly higher than using fully updated parameters. This contrasts with monolithic critics, which experienced significant performance degradation when early layers were frozen, indicating the absence of an analogous recovery mechanism.
- **Robustness to Noisy TD Targets:** Flow-matching critics exhibited greater robustness to i.i.d. noise injected into TD targets during training. Their performance degradation was more graceful, maintaining higher success rates compared to monolithic critics as noise magnitude increased. This suggests that the iterative integration dynamics can attenuate the effects of noisy supervision.

#### 4.3. Flow Matching Learns Plastic Features

- **Theoretical Insight (Linear Setting):** In a simplified linear model, the analysis demonstrated that flow-matching critics can adapt to non-stationary targets by reweighting contributions from existing features through their gain parameters (which evolve during the integration process), even if the feature directions themselves remain fixed. In contrast, monolithic critics and ensembles of monolithic critics must explicitly modify their underlying feature weights to track changes in targets. This "feature reweighting" mechanism is a direct consequence of dense velocity supervision.
- **Feature Norms:** Empirical measurements showed that `floq` critics trained with TD learning learned significantly lower $\ell_2$-norm features in their penultimate hidden layers compared to monolithic critics. This effect was notably absent or less pronounced when training with SARSA or Monte Carlo (MC) regression, indicating that this plasticity-promoting behavior is specific to the non-stationarity inherent in TD targets. The lower feature norms suggest that `floq` defers the task of modeling the Q-value scale to later layers and the integration process, rather than encoding it within early features.
- **Freezing Features Experiment:** Freezing the early layers of monolithic critics (including those with ResNet and transformer architectures) at an intermediate training point led to a sharp collapse in performance, demonstrating a loss of plasticity and inability to adapt to future TD targets. Conversely, flow-matching critics largely recovered to competitive performance levels, indicating that their learned features remained suitable for representing subsequent value functions. A `floq` variant with only a single integration step was more stable than monolithic critics but less stable than full `floq`, underscoring the importance of the full integration process for feature plasticity.
- **Intermediate Velocity Supervision is Crucial:** A crucial finding was that supervising the integration steps to predict *absolute TD target values* (rather than velocities) eliminated the benefits of flow matching. This variant performed comparably to monolithic critics, effectively collapsing to an ensemble of monolithic predictors. This highlights that dense *velocity* supervision, which enables the iterative correction mechanism, is essential for both test-time recovery and plastic feature learning.

#### 4.4. Application to High-UTD Online RL

- **Performance and Stability:** When integrated into an adapted RLPD framework, `floq` consistently achieved substantially stronger performance in high Update-to-Data (UTD) online RL settings. It exhibited roughly 2x higher final returns and a 5x improvement in sample efficiency compared to monolithic FQL baselines on various hard OGBench tasks.
- **Learning Dynamics:** Flow-matching critics demonstrated greater stability during learning, even at very high UTD values where monolithic critics often showed unstable dynamics or failed to make meaningful progress.

In summary, the research concludes that the effectiveness of flow-matching critics in TD learning is not due to distributional modeling but rather to the combined effects of test-time recovery and plastic feature learning, both enabled by dense velocity supervision over an iterative integration process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a fundamental reinterpretation of the success of flow-matching critics in reinforcement learning, moving beyond the initial hypothesis of distributional modeling. The identification of **test-time recovery** and **plastic feature representations** as the primary drivers of performance offers a novel understanding of how iterative computation, coupled with dense velocity supervision, fundamentally alters both inference-time behavior and representation learning in value-based RL.

**Key Significance:**
- **Mechanistic Understanding:** The paper provides a clear, formalized explanation for empirical gains previously observed in flow-matching critics. This understanding moves beyond black-box performance improvements to shed light on the underlying learning dynamics.
- **Mitigation of TD Learning Pathologies:** By demonstrating how flow matching enhances robustness to noise, mitigates the effects of target non-stationarity, and preserves feature plasticity, the work offers a new approach to addressing long-standing challenges in TD learning. This is achieved intrinsically through the architecture and training objective, without relying on auxiliary objectives or explicit regularization commonly employed in other stabilization techniques.
- **Improved Algorithm Design:** The insights can inform the design of more stable and sample-efficient RL algorithms. The strong empirical performance of flow-matching critics in high Update-to-Data (UTD) online RL settings, a context often plagued by plasticity loss and instability, underscores their practical utility. A 2x improvement in final performance and a 5x improvement in sample efficiency are notable achievements in challenging environments.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement in Value-Based RL:** The findings could lead to the development of a new generation of value function architectures that explicitly leverage iterative computation and dense supervision for enhanced stability and representational capacity. This might involve exploring further alternative parameterizations of velocity fields or structured gain mechanisms beyond what was studied.
- **Cross-Domain Generalization:** The identified principles—linking iterative computation, dense supervision, plasticity, and non-stationarity—may extend beyond TD learning. They could be relevant to other machine learning domains where models must continuously adapt to evolving targets, such as time-series prediction, online learning, or adaptive control systems.
- **Connections to Cognitive Architectures and LLMs:** The paper draws an analogy between the integration steps in flow matching and the "reasoning steps" or "chain-of-thought" processes in large language models (LLMs). This connection suggests a broader principle where increased test-time computation is beneficial when aligned with training objectives that induce local corrective signals (like dense velocity supervision in flow matching or RL training for productive reasoning in LLMs). This "bi-level optimization" perspective—where weight updates are a slow outer loop for representation adaptation and iterative computation is a fast inner loop for prediction adaptation—could inspire new architectural designs and training paradigms for both critics and LLMs, particularly concerning how to optimally allocate variable computation during inference.

Overall, this research not only advances the understanding of current state-of-the-art RL methods but also opens new avenues for theoretical exploration and practical applications by highlighting a deep connection between iterative computation and robust, plastic learning in dynamic environments.
