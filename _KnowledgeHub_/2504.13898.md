# Social Human Robot Embodied Conversation (SHREC) Dataset: Benchmarking Foundational Models' Social Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Purdue University, and Carnegie Mellon University developed the Social Human Robot Embodied Conversation (SHREC) dataset, a large-scale, annotated collection of real-world human-robot interactions. This dataset serves as a benchmark for evaluating foundational models' social reasoning, revealing that state-of-the-art language and vision-language models perform substantially below human levels across eight HRI-specific social reasoning tasks, particularly in nuanced error detection and correction.

## Problem

- Existing social intelligence datasets primarily focus on human-human interactions, failing to capture the distinct social reasoning challenges and failures unique to human-robot interaction (HRI).
- A lack of large-scale, comprehensively annotated datasets of real-world HRI limited the ability to benchmark and advance social reasoning capabilities in embodied AI agents.
- Foundational models struggled to recognize subtle social deficits and complexities that manifest specifically during robot interactions.

## Method

- Developed the Social Human Robot Embodied Conversation (**SHREC**) dataset, featuring over ==58 hours of real-world human-Jibo robot interaction videos==.
- Incorporated ==10,353 human annotations== detailing robot social errors and competencies, along with underlying social attributes, rationales, and corrective actions.
- Designed ==eight benchmark tasks== to evaluate foundational models across critical social reasoning dimensions, including error detection, attribute identification, interaction flow, and rationale/correction generation.

## Results

- State-of-the-art models achieved limited accuracy in detecting social errors and competencies, with `gemini-1.5-flash` (L+V) reaching **0.32** accuracy for 3-way classification, significantly below human performance (e.g., **0.77** accuracy for binary error detection).
- Language-only models like `gpt-4o` variants showed stronger performance (**0.66**-**0.69** accuracy) in tasks requiring understanding interaction flow (pre- and post-conditions), although still lagging human baselines (**0.77**-**0.87** accuracy).
- Tasks requiring rationale generation (**0.55** accuracy for `gpt-4o-fewshot` L+V) and corrective action suggestions (**0.48** accuracy for `gpt-4o-fewshot` L+V) proved most challenging for models, closely mirroring human difficulty (**0.63** and **0.54** accuracy respectively).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current state-of-the-art foundational models exhibit a substantial performance gap compared to human social reasoning abilities in HRI contexts.
> - Visual input, in its current implementation for vision-language models, does not consistently improve social reasoning performance over language-only models, and can sometimes lead to degradation.
> - Few-shot prompting helps foundational models align with normative social decision boundaries, suggesting a path for improving their social reasoning outputs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of socially intelligent artificial intelligence (AI) agents, particularly physically embodied social robots, is an active area of research. A significant challenge in this field involves endowing AI models with robust social reasoning capabilities to facilitate natural and effective human-robot interactions (HRI). Prior research in social intelligence has predominantly relied on datasets derived from human-human interactions, such as MELD (for emotion and sentiment in dialogue) and CMU-MOSEI (for multimodal opinion, sentiment, and emotion). Datasets like SocialIQA and Social-IQ have focused on commonsense reasoning about social norms, intentions, and emotional responses in human-to-human scenarios, with SOCIAL GENOME extending this to multimodal models for grounded social reasoning traces.

While these resources have advanced the understanding of human social dynamics, they often do not fully capture the unique complexities and subtle failures that arise when humans interact with robots. Embodied AI agents frequently possess varying or incomplete socio-cognitive skills, such as emotion understanding, belief tracking, or conversational coordination, leading to interaction deficits that are distinct from human-human communication failures.

In parallel, frameworks for annotating social errors in HRI have been proposed, categorizing robot behaviors that violate social norms or degrade perceived social competence. These efforts have provided conceptual taxonomies for understanding robot social failures and competencies. However, a large-scale, comprehensively annotated dataset of real-world human-robot interaction episodes, specifically designed to benchmark foundational models on these HRI-specific social reasoning challenges, has been largely absent.

This work addresses this gap by introducing the Social Human Robot Embodied Conversation (SHREC) Dataset. SHREC aims to provide a benchmark for evaluating and advancing the social reasoning capabilities of foundational models within the context of real-world, embodied social robot interactions. By focusing on errors and competencies unique to HRI, this dataset extends the scope of social intelligence research beyond human-human dynamics, offering a resource for diagnosing and improving the social acumen of AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the social reasoning capabilities of physically embodied AI agents, specifically tabletop social robots, within real-world, interactive conversational settings. To achieve this, the work focuses on the detailed understanding and modeling of both desirable social behaviors (social competencies) and problematic social behaviors (social errors, defined as norm violations or interaction failures) as they manifest during natural human-robot interactions.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing the Gap in Existing Datasets:** Current datasets for social intelligence predominantly feature human-human interactions. These datasets do not adequately represent the distinct challenges faced by robots, which may lack the inherent socio-cognitive skills of humans, such as nuanced emotion understanding, belief tracking, or proficient conversational coordination. Consequently, existing resources do not capture the subtle, socially situated failures that characterize real-world HRI.
- **Benchmarking Foundational Models:** There is a recognized need to systematically evaluate the capacity of modern foundational models (e.g., Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs)) to perform social reasoning in HRI contexts. Current models reportedly struggle to recognize the subtle social deficits unique to robot interactions.
- **Developing a Real-World HRI Benchmark:** The research aims to contribute one of the largest publicly available datasets of real-world human-social robot interaction videos, accompanied by extensive human annotations. This addresses the scarcity of such data, which is crucial for advancing research in embodied social reasoning.
- **Defining Comprehensive Social Reasoning Tasks:** The project seeks to establish a set of benchmark tasks that cover critical dimensions of social reasoning. These dimensions include the detection of social errors and competencies, the identification of underlying social attributes (e.g., emotion, engagement, conversational mechanics), the comprehension of interaction flow, and the ability to provide rationales for observed behaviors and suggest corrective actions. These tasks are designed not only to assess models but also to probe fundamental representation learning challenges related to multimodal alignment, reasoning about interaction trajectories, and reward learning for human social preferences.
- **Guiding AI Development:** By benchmarking state-of-the-art models against human performance, the work intends to highlight substantial performance gaps. These findings are expected to underscore the complexity of social reasoning for AI and provide specific directions for the development of more socially intelligent AI agents, as well as for the creation of reward models and evaluators that are better aligned with human social intelligence.

In essence, the research is motivated by the objective of moving beyond theoretical understanding of social intelligence to practical, data-driven advancement of socially capable embodied AI agents that can navigate the nuances of real-world human interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the creation and deployment of the Social Human Robot Embodied Conversation (SHREC) Dataset, followed by the definition and evaluation of eight benchmark tasks using state-of-the-art foundational models.

#### 3.1. SHREC Dataset Construction

- **Data Source:** The dataset comprises 403 interaction videos, totaling over 3,500 minutes (approximately 58 hours) of natural conversational interactions between humans and the Jibo tabletop social robot. This makes it one of the largest real-world human-social robot interaction benchmark datasets.
- **Underlying Studies:** The data was sourced from three prior, IRB-approved human-robot interaction studies: Empathic++ (ChatGPT-powered empathic companion), Wellness-Dorm (socially assistive robot as a positive psychology coach for college students), and Wellness-Home (positive psychology robots deployed in participants’ homes). All studies utilized the Jibo social robot, which possesses capabilities for speech-based interaction, expressive gaze, and attention behaviors, but has limitations such as immobility and lack of object manipulation.
- **Privacy and Anonymization:** To enable public access while preserving participant privacy, the dataset is released under gated access, following institutional IRB procedures. Personally identifiable information (PII) in transcripts is filtered. For video anonymization, the FRESCO framework, a zero-shot video-to-video diffusion technique, was used to perform stylized face transfer. This method was selected for its spatial-temporal consistency, which ensures that critical social signals (e.g., gaze, affect) are maintained while individual identities are protected. A comparative experiment showed no significant degradation in VLM performance on FRESCO-anonymized videos compared to raw or other anonymized variants.
- **Temporal Resolution:** Video frames are sampled at 1 Hz for the primary release, aligning with common VLM processing rates. An extended addendum with 15 Hz videos is planned for fine-grained temporal analysis. A private hold-out set of approximately 40 videos is maintained for future benchmarking.

#### 3.2. Human Annotations

The dataset includes 10,353 human annotations, focusing on the robot’s social errors and competencies.

- **Annotation Process:** Three trained undergraduate research assistants watched interaction videos and identified segments exhibiting either a social error or a social competency. They then categorized the underlying social attribute, provided a rationale, and, for errors, suggested alternative correct actions.
- **Definitions:**
 - **Social Competence:** Defined as behaviors where the agent successfully conducts social interactions by identifying social-emotional cues, processing them, and expressing an expected response.
 - **Social Error:** Defined as behaviors where the robot deviates from user expectations, violating social norms and degrading perceived social competence.
 - **None:** A category for segments that are socially neutral, ambiguous, or weakly valenced.
- **Social Attributes:** Annotators identified which of seven specific social attributes were related to the observed error or competency: Emotions, Engagement, Conversational Mechanics, Knowledge State, User Intention, Social Context and Relationships, and Social Norms and Routines.
- **Annotator Consistency:** Annotators were provided with clear definitions and guidelines, met regularly to discuss ambiguous scenarios, and cross-validated each other’s work. This resulted in 85.1% overlapping annotations, with an overall agreement of 91.3% (Cohen’s κ = 0.7638, Krippendorff’s α = 0.7639) among overlapping samples. Approximately 8.7% of annotations were marked as persistent disagreements, reflecting inherent subjectivity in social interpretation.
- **Dataset Statistics:** The annotations are split between 56% competence and 43% error labels. The majority of annotations (76.7%) were attributed to verbal cues rather than non-verbal cues. Conversational mechanics, intention, and engagement were the most frequently identified social attributes.

#### 3.3. Benchmark Tasks and Experiments

Eight benchmark tasks were designed to measure foundational models' social reasoning capabilities, grouped into four research questions (RQs):

- **RQ1: Automatic Evaluation of Social Interactions:**
 - **(1) Error and Competence Detection:** Models classify segments as Social Competence, Social Error, or None.
 - **(2) Error Detection:** A binary classification task to identify Social Error or No Social Error.
- **RQ2: Identification of Explanatory Factors (Social Attributes):**
 - **(3) Social Attribute Identification:** Given an error/competency, models identify one or more of the seven social attributes it relates to.
 - **(4) Multiple Social Attribute Presence:** Models determine if a segment involves more than one social attribute (True/False).
- **RQ3: Understanding Interaction Flow (If-Then Reasoning):**
 - **(5) Pre-Condition:** Given an agent’s utterance, models select the plausible preceding user behavior from multiple choices.
 - **(6) Post-Condition:** Given a user’s utterance, models select the plausible subsequent agent behavior from multiple choices.
- **RQ4: Rationale and Correction Reasoning:**
 - **(7) Rationale:** Given a social error, models select the correct explanatory reason from multiple choices.
 - **(8) Correction:** Given a social error, models select the most appropriate alternative correct action from multiple choices.

#### 3.4. Model Evaluation

- **Models:** 18 state-of-the-art LLMs and VLMs were benchmarked, including variants of ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama, Llava, InternVL2, DeepSeek, MiniCPM, and Paligemma.
- **Input Modalities:** Language-only (L) models received interaction transcripts. Language and Vision (L+V) models received transcripts and 15 uniformly sampled video frames (or raw video for video-based models).
- **Output Processing:** Model outputs, often free-form strings, were coerced into discrete answer spaces (e.g., multiple-choice options, Boolean) using Pydantic for objective correctness evaluation.
- **Metrics:** Accuracy was used for all tasks. Macro-F1 was additionally reported for multi-class/multi-label tasks. Partial Match (PM) was reported for attribute identification to account for scenarios where at least one correct label is predicted.
- **Human Comparison:** Human annotators performed the same tasks under identical prompt constraints as the models, providing a baseline for human-level performance. Models were evaluated on three separately sampled subsets per task, with mean performance and standard deviation reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 18 state-of-the-art language and vision-language models on the SHREC benchmark tasks revealed a consistent and substantial performance gap between current AI capabilities and human-level social reasoning. No single model demonstrated high performance across all social reasoning tasks.

- **RQ1: Automatic Evaluation of Social Interactions**
 - In the **Error/Competence/None Detection** task (a 3-way classification), the best-performing model was `gemini-1.5-flash` (L+V), achieving an accuracy of 0.32 and an F1 score of 0.28. This performance indicates a significant struggle for models to distinguish between socially competent, erroneous, or neutral behaviors.
 - For the binary **Error Detection** task, `gemini-1.5-flash` again showed the highest performance, with an accuracy of 0.55 and an F1 score of 0.52. This suggests that models are more effective at identifying the presence of an error than differentiating among all three social categories, but still operate at a modest level. The human performance for this task was 0.77 accuracy and 0.71 F1, highlighting a considerable gap.

- **RQ2: Identification of Explanatory Factors (Social Attributes)**
 - For **Social Attribute Identification**, most models achieved relatively high *partial accuracy* scores, with `gpt-4o-fewshot` (L+V) exceeding 0.80. This indicates models often correctly identify at least one relevant social attribute within a segment.
 - However, models demonstrated limitations in predicting the *full and correct set* of attributes, as evidenced by low overall accuracy and modest F1 scores. This suggests difficulty in handling the complex, multi-label nature of co-occurring social attributes.
 - In the **Multiple Social Attribute Presence** task, models generally performed at or below 0.5 accuracy, with `InternVL2-8B` (L+V) showing the highest F1 score. This indicates a persistent challenge for models to recognize instances where multiple social attributes contribute to an interaction segment. Human performance was notably higher (0.67 F1 for attribute identification, 0.76 Acc for multi-attribute presence).

- **RQ3: Understanding Interaction Flow (If-Then Reasoning)**
 - Language-only models, particularly variants of `gpt-4o`, performed comparatively well on the **Pre-Condition** (0.66 accuracy) and **Post-Condition** (0.69 accuracy) tasks. This is attributed to their training objectives, which often involve predicting discourse coherence and temporal continuity.
 - Despite this, model performance still lagged behind human capabilities (0.77 accuracy for Pre-Condition and 0.87 accuracy for Post-Condition).
 - A notable observation was a significant performance gap between nascent open-source vision-language models and text-only LMs, suggesting that the addition of visual input, in its current implementation, does not consistently enhance reasoning about interactional contingencies, and in some cases, might degrade original text-only reasoning abilities.

- **RQ4: Rationale and Correction Reasoning**
 - These tasks were among the most challenging for both models and human annotators.
 - For the **Rationale** task, `gpt-4o-fewshot` (L+V) achieved the best performance with an accuracy of 0.55.
 - Similarly, for the **Correction** task, `gpt-4o-fewshot` (L+V) performed best, with an accuracy of 0.48.
 - The relatively better performance with few-shot prompting suggests that providing explicit examples can help align model outputs with the dataset's normative decision boundaries and social error taxonomy. Human performance for these tasks was 0.63 for Rationale and 0.54 for Correction, indicating that these are challenging even for humans.

In summary, the results highlight that current foundational models possess rudimentary social reasoning abilities but exhibit significant limitations in tasks requiring nuanced understanding of HRI social errors, the co-occurrence of social attributes, and the ability to provide accurate rationales and corrections. The gap between model and human performance across the benchmark tasks underscores the difficulty of embodied social reasoning and the necessity for further advancements in this field.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Social Human Robot Embodied Conversation (SHREC) Dataset and its accompanying benchmark tasks represent a significant contribution to the field of socially intelligent AI and human-robot interaction.

**Scientific Significance:**

- **Novel Resource for HRI Research:** SHREC addresses a critical gap by providing a large-scale, richly annotated dataset of real-world human-social robot interactions. Unlike prior datasets focusing on human-human dynamics, SHREC specifically captures the unique socio-cognitive limitations and challenges encountered by embodied AI agents, offering a more relevant benchmark for HRI development.
- **Comprehensive Social Reasoning Benchmark:** The eight distinct benchmark tasks systematically probe various facets of social reasoning, including error/competency detection, attribute identification, interaction flow prediction, and rationale/correction generation. This structured evaluation framework enables researchers to diagnose specific strengths and weaknesses of foundational models in HRI contexts.
- **Diagnostic Tool for Foundational Models:** The observed substantial performance gap between state-of-the-art LLMs/VLMs and human capabilities on SHREC tasks provides concrete evidence of current models' limitations in embodied social reasoning. This diagnostic capability can guide future research by pinpointing areas requiring concentrated effort, such as improving multimodal grounding, developing more sophisticated counterfactual action modeling, and achieving explicit alignment with human social preferences.
- **Advancing Reward Model Development:** The fine-grained annotations of social errors, competencies, rationales, and corrective actions offer a valuable resource for training and evaluating reward models. These reward models are crucial for aligning embodied AI agents with human social preferences, a key challenge in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) for social robotics.

**Potential Societal Impact:**

- **Improved Human-Robot Collaboration and Trust:** By fostering the development of robots that can better understand and respond to social cues, SHREC can lead to more natural, effective, and trustworthy human-robot interactions. This is particularly relevant for applications in assistive robotics, healthcare (e.g., eldercare, mental wellness coaching), education, and companionship, where socially intelligent robots can enhance user experience and well-being.
- **Enhanced Robot Safety and Adaptability:** The ability of robots to detect and understand social errors, coupled with the capacity to infer corrective actions, can contribute to safer and more adaptable robot behaviors. Robots that can recognize and mitigate their social missteps are likely to be more robust and accepted in human environments.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations that also present avenues for future research:

- **Subjectivity and Normative Judgments:** Social error/competence labels are inherently subjective, reflecting human normative judgments that can vary across cultures, contexts, and communication styles. Models trained on SHREC may learn these specific decision boundaries rather than universal social rules.
- **Generalization to Diverse Embodiments and Contexts:** The dataset primarily features a tabletop social robot (Jibo) from specific long-term studies. Generalization to other robot embodiments (e.g., mobile manipulators), languages, and interaction contexts remains an open question.
- **Temporal Granularity:** The 1 Hz frame sampling rate for video input might under-represent fine-grained temporal cues (e.g., micro-pauses, gaze shifts) crucial for conversational mechanics.
- **Task Ambiguities:** The formulation of certain tasks, such as "pre-condition" and "post-condition," might contain inherent ambiguities due to the nature of conversational adjacency and the presence of multiple plausible responses. The construction of distractor items also presents challenges in ensuring consistent task difficulty.
- **Focus on Human Behavior:** The majority of videos in SHREC tend to focus on the human participant, which limits the dataset's ability to support systematic evaluation of the robot's full-body non-verbal behaviors or control policies. Future data collection could address this by incorporating robot-centric or wide-angle views.
- **Unexplored Biases:** While anonymization efforts address privacy, the dataset's U.S.-centric and somewhat limited demographic representation (e.g., in age or neurodiversity) means potential demographic biases in model outputs or social interpretations remain largely unexplored.
- **Task Decomposition:** While SHREC provides a broad benchmark, future work could focus on task formulations for individual social attributes, as the dataset contains sufficient instances for construct-specific recognition.

**Ethical Considerations:**

The authors address the ethical implications, noting that the source studies adhered to approved IRB protocols regarding participant consent and informing users about the robot's non-sentient nature and limitations. They highlight the risk of over-reliance on socially adept AI, potential ethical blurring due to mimicry of human empathy, and the possibility of reinforcing normative biases if not carefully managed. The environmental impact of computational requirements, estimated at approximately 77 kg CO2e, is also acknowledged. These considerations underscore the ongoing need for ethical safeguards, human oversight, and inclusive design practices as socially intelligent AI advances.
