# Large Discovery Models: Empirically-grounded Model-Based Open-Ended Search

> [!summary] Summary
> Large Discovery Model (LDM) couples an LLM candidate generator with a continually-updated Gaussian-process surrogate that predicts reward and epistemic uncertainty; a KL-regularized acquisition-tilted policy reweights LLM proposals toward high-value candidates, cycling exploitation, exploration, and frontier-expanding discovery. Across neural-network training-program search, antibody design, and molecular optimization, LDM achieves 2.4x greater validation-BPB reduction, 18.2% lower binding energy, and over 60% relative hypervolume gains versus LLM-only or Bayesian-optimization baselines.

## Problem

- LLM likelihoods and self-assessments are unreliable proxies for externally measured scientific objectives, especially for novel candidates outside the observed data distribution.
- Bayesian optimization grounds selection in empirical observations but depends on a fixed representation and search operators, so it fails to reach candidates outside its current search support.
- Autonomous LLM research agents generate hypotheses and code but show limited diversity, weaker feasibility, and cannot reliably evaluate their own proposed designs.

## Method

- ==Acquisition-tilted search policy==: a ==KL-regularized variational objective== yields a closed-form ==exponentially-tilted distribution== reweighting the LLM's proposal reservoir by predicted reward plus ==epistemic uncertainty==.
- ==Gaussian-process surrogate== on a dynamic support: fit over an LLM-defined active search domain each round, using ==UCB==, ==EI==, or ==EHVI== acquisition with top-b or ==Gumbel-top-b== inference-time sampling.
- **LDM-TTS** discovery fine-tuning: acquisition-reweighted candidate traces, optionally paired with a rationale, distill the tilted search policy into model weights via a weighted supervised objective.

## Results

- On AutoResearch, LDM cuts validation BPB by **0.0727** from a shared **1.0069** start versus the LLM-only baseline's **0.0301**, a **2.4x** larger reduction, and a B200 run reaches **0.9023** val_bpb.
- On antibody CDRH3 design across five antigens, policy-mode LDM matches AntBO (**-110.8** vs **-110.1** on 1ADQ_A) and beats LLM-only reflection by **18.2%** mean binding energy after 200 evaluations.
- On KRAS G12D molecular design, LDM (Direct-Softmax) reaches hypervolume **27.325** versus Pure-LLM **16.826** and MOBO **16.751**, a **62.4%** and **63.1%** relative gain respectively.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Extra test-time compute only helps when guided by a calibrated, uncertainty-aware acquisition signal; raw LLM likelihood or posterior-mean-only selection plateaus early.
> - Discovery, expanding the search frontier, is mechanistically distinct from exploration and exploitation; without it, search stays confined to a fixed support regardless of budget.
> - Distilling acquisition-guided search traces into model weights transfers to held-out targets and even entirely unseen task types, indicating learned research strategy rather than memorization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a significant challenge in scientific discovery: optimizing complex objectives over vast, structured, and often open-ended hypothesis spaces (e.g., molecules, proteins, computer programs) where evaluations are expensive and feedback is limited.

The authors position their work within the context of recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) and their evolution from open-ended generation to reasoning. LLMs have demonstrated capabilities in tasks like mathematics, coding, and game playing, particularly when combined with cheap verifiers that provide reliable and repeatable feedback (e.g., AlphaZero-like methods, Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thoughts). These "reasoning LLMs" leverage inference-time computation to generate, compare, and refine solutions.

However, the paper identifies limitations of current LLM-only approaches in scientific discovery. LLMs provide expressive priors over hypothesis spaces, but their internal likelihoods and self-assessments are often unreliable proxies for external, empirically measured objectives, especially for novel candidates outside the observed data distribution. They lack calibrated epistemic uncertainty and struggle with costly, noisy, and sparse feedback. Autonomous LLM agents, while capable of generating hypotheses and executing experiments, exhibit limitations in diversity, feasibility, novelty assessment, and methodological reasoning, failing to provide the empirically grounded value signal required for true discovery.

Complementarily, traditional Bayesian optimization (BO) methods excel at learning probabilistic surrogates of unknown objectives from limited observations and allocating experimental budgets efficiently. BO uses acquisition functions to balance exploitation (of promising designs) and exploration (of uncertain ones). However, BO typically requires a predefined representation of the design space and struggles to propose or reach candidates in highly structured, combinatorial, or open-ended spaces where the search support is not fully specified. Optimizing the acquisition function itself can be challenging in such complex domains.

This work introduces the Large Discovery Model (LDM) to bridge the gap between the generative power of LLMs and the empirical grounding and uncertainty quantification of BO. It proposes a framework that couples an LLM's ability to generate and refine structured candidates (expanding the search frontier) with a Bayesian non-parametric surrogate model's ability to predict performance and quantify uncertainty (guiding experimental resource allocation). The LDM seeks to combine the strengths of both paradigms to enable effective search and discovery in open-ended scientific domains under expensive black-box evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a general-purpose scientific discovery engine that can effectively search and design in open-ended hypothesis spaces, particularly when empirical evaluations are costly and feedback is limited. This is motivated by the identified shortcomings of existing LLM-only and Bayesian optimization (BO) approaches when applied to complex scientific problems.

The key motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Addressing Limitations of LLM-only Approaches:**
 - **Unreliable Self-Assessment:** LLMs are proficient at generating plausible designs (e.g., molecules, code, protein sequences) based on their training data. However, their internal likelihoods or "self-assessments" do not reliably correlate with external, empirically measured scientific properties. For instance, a high-likelihood molecule might not have a strong binding affinity.
 - **Lack of Calibrated Uncertainty:** LLMs do not inherently provide calibrated estimates of epistemic uncertainty, which is crucial for distinguishing between candidates that are genuinely good (low uncertainty, high predicted value) and those that are merely plausible but untested (high uncertainty, unknown true value). This makes it difficult to prioritize experiments, especially for novel candidates outside the observed data distribution.
 - **Inefficient Resource Allocation:** Without reliable value and uncertainty signals, LLMs cannot efficiently allocate expensive experimental resources. They may propose many valid candidates but cannot reliably determine which ones are most promising or informative to evaluate.

2. **Overcoming Constraints of Bayesian Optimization (BO):**
 - **Fixed Design Space:** Traditional BO typically operates over a predefined, fixed design space. In many scientific contexts, the "design space" is vast, combinatorial, and not fully enumerated in advance (e.g., the space of all possible valid molecules or computer programs).
 - **Difficulty in Candidate Generation:** While BO excels at learning from observations, it requires an external mechanism to propose or reach candidate designs. Optimizing the acquisition function in a complex, structured, or combinatorial space can be computationally challenging or infeasible without a sophisticated proposal mechanism. BO might efficiently rank candidates *within* its current representation but fail to discover valuable candidates *outside* its existing search support.

3. **Formulating Scientific Discovery as Sequential Inverse Design:**
 - The paper frames scientific discovery as a sequential process where an agent repeatedly proposes hypotheses/designs, selects a limited number for costly evaluation, observes outcomes, and updates its beliefs to guide subsequent search. A "discovery" occurs when this process identifies a previously unknown and non-trivial design supported by empirical evidence.
 - This formulation highlights the need for a system that can not only efficiently explore a *known* space but also *expand* the search frontier into previously unrepresented or unreachable regions (termed "discovery" distinct from "exploration" within existing support).

4. **Integrating Generative Power with Empirical Grounding:**
 - The core motivation is to combine the strengths of LLMs (structured prior, ability to generate novel candidates, expand the search frontier) with the strengths of BO (empirical grounding, uncertainty quantification, efficient budget allocation).
 - This integration aims to move beyond LLM-only "reasoning" (which relies on cheap verifiers) to "discovery" in settings with expensive, black-box evaluations.

5. **Developing an Uncertainty-Aware Value Signal:**
 - A key objective is to use an empirically grounded value signal, derived from a probabilistic surrogate model, to guide both candidate generation and selection. This signal, an "acquisition value," is designed to balance predicted performance with epistemic uncertainty, directing computational resources for refinement and experimental budget for evaluation.

By addressing these motivations, the LDM aims to serve as a general-purpose discovery engine capable of operating across diverse scientific domains and advancing the state of autonomous scientific research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Large Discovery Model (LDM) is introduced as an empirically grounded recurrent architecture that couples a generative model (LLM) with a Bayesian non-parametric reward surrogate model. It operates on a recurrent loop of generation, evaluation, belief updating, and search-frontier expansion.

The LDM framework is built upon three interacting components:

1. **A Generative Foundation Model (LLM):**
 - The LLM provides a structured prior over the design space, enabling the generation and refinement of plausible candidates (e.g., code, sequences, molecular structures).
 - It defines the *proposal distribution* `p_θ,α(x | C_t)`, where `θ` are model parameters and `α` represents inference configuration (e.g., prompting, sampling temperature, reasoning strategy).
 - This component is responsible for "discovery" by expanding and reshaping the set of reachable candidates, including previously unrepresented designs.

2. **A Probabilistic Surrogate Model:**
 - A Gaussian Process (GP) is employed as the surrogate model, learning a probabilistic representation `p(R | D_t)` of the unknown reward function `R` from the cumulative dataset `D_t` of observed experiments.
 - The GP provides a *predictive mean* `μ_t(x)` (expected reward) and *predictive uncertainty* `σ_t(x)` (epistemic uncertainty) for any candidate `x`.
 - This component provides the "empirical grounding" that LLMs alone lack, quantifying calibrated uncertainty.

3. **A Model-Based Acquisition Function:**
 - An acquisition function `a_t(x)` is constructed from the GP's predictive distribution, assigning a scalar value to each candidate. Common choices include Expected Improvement (EI) or Upper Confidence Bound (UCB).
 - The acquisition function explicitly balances exploitation (pursuing high-reward candidates) and exploration (evaluating uncertain but potentially informative candidates), mediating the "exploitation–exploration trade-off."
 - Crucially, this acquisition value is not merely a post-generation filter but an empirically grounded signal that guides the allocation of both computational (inference-time) and experimental (costly evaluation) resources.

**Acquisition-Tilted Search Policy:**
The core of LDM's decision-making is an acquisition-tilted search policy `π_t`, derived from a KL-regularized objective (Equation 2). This policy maximizes the expected acquisition value while staying close to the LLM's generative prior. The optimal policy `π_t(x)` takes an exponential form: `π_t(x) ∝ p_θ,α(x | C_t) exp{η a_t(x)}`, where `η` controls the strength of acquisition-based tilting. This formulation connects the LLM's structured prior with the empirically grounded acquisition value.

**Acquisition-Guided Inference-Time Search:**
Since exact sampling from `π_t` is often intractable, LDM approximates it via inference-time search:
- The LLM generates a finite pool of `N` candidate designs from its proposal distribution.
- The acquisition function `a_t(x)` scores these candidates.
- A selection mechanism chooses `b` candidates for evaluation. This can be deterministic (e.g., top-`b` candidates by acquisition score) or stochastic (e.g., softmax reweighting with Gumbel-top-`b` sampling for diversity).
- The candidate pool size `N` acts as a knob for test-time compute, allowing trade-offs between inference cost and search quality.

**Recurrent Learning Loop (Algorithm 1):**
The LDM operates in an iterative loop:
1. **Update Active Decision Domain:** The LLM's context `C_t` (including prior observations) shapes the active search domain `A_t`.
2. **Fit GP Surrogate:** A GP is fitted over `A_t` using `D_t` to obtain `μ_t` and `σ_t`.
3. **Construct Acquisition Function:** `a_t` is computed (e.g., UCB = `μ_t(x) + √β_t σ_t(x)`).
4. **Draw Candidates:** A batch `B_t` of candidates is drawn from the acquisition-tilted policy (Algorithm 2).
5. **Observe Rewards:** `B_t` is evaluated by an external black-box function, yielding noisy rewards `r_t,i`.
6. **Update Data and Context:** `D_t` and `C_t` are updated with new observations. The LLM may also append reflection feedback to `C_t` if stagnation is detected.

**Consolidating Acquisition-Tilted Inference via Model Fine-tuning (LDM-TTS):**
To amortize the cost of repeated inference-time search and imbue the LLM with a "discovery intuition," LDM includes a slow learning loop:
- **Acquisition-Reweighted Data Collection:** During high-budget test-time search, search states, candidate pools, and their acquisition values are recorded. High-value actions (according to the tilted policy) are retained as training examples.
- **Reasoning Augmentation:** Optionally, a concise rationale (`z_t,i`) interpreting the decision (e.g., exploit, explore, discover) is generated alongside the action. This transforms numerical acquisition values into semantic justifications.
- **Supervised Fine-tuning:** The LLM's parameters (`θ`) are fine-tuned using a weighted autoregressive objective (Equation 11) on these `(context, rationale, action)` or `(context, action)` pairs. This distills the acquisition-guided search policy directly into the generative model, enabling faster, more efficient proposals in subsequent tasks.

**Practical Implementations:**
LDM supports direct generation of designs or indirect parameterization (LLM proposes search region, external sampler generates candidates). It can handle constrained optimization, multi-objective tasks (using Expected Hypervolume Improvement - EHVI), and batch selection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper evaluates LDM across three distinct scientific design domains: neural-network training program search (AutoResearch), antibody CDRH3 sequence design, and multi-objective small-molecule optimization.

#### 4.1. Performance on Case Studies

- **AutoResearch (Neural-Network Training Programs):**
 - LDM achieved a **2.4× greater reduction** in validation Bits Per Byte (BPB) compared to an LLM-only reflection baseline, starting from a common point. LDM reduced BPB by 0.0727, while the baseline reduced it by 0.0301. Lower BPB is better.
 - A matched-hardware leaderboard run on B200 reached a validation BPB of 0.902291 in 59 runs, outperforming forge (0.9264) and overmind (0.9274).
 - The improvements were attributed to "physics-grounded discoveries" in program mechanisms, such as n-gram hash memory, QK-normalization, narrower model dimensions, windowed attention, and optimiser choices, demonstrating LDM's ability to drive significant programmatic changes. The optimization trajectory showed initial rapid improvement, a plateau, and then a second improvement phase after the search space was revised, illustrating the "discovery" operation.

- **Antibody CDRH3 Sequence Design:**
 - LDM achieved an **18.2% lower mean binding energy** than an LLM-only reflection after 200 evaluation steps (lower binding energy is better).
 - LDM variants using a "policy-based" generation strategy (LLM parameterizes search region) consistently outperformed "direct generation" variants (LLM generates sequences token-by-token).
 - LDM's performance was competitive with AntBO, a specialized BO method for antibody design, especially with policy-based generation. For instance, on 1NSN S antigen, LDM achieved -104.7 ± 1.0 binding energy, compared to AntBO's -104.6 ± 2.6 and Pure LLM's -87.7 ± 2.6.
 - A representative trajectory showed initial LLM sampling hitting a ceiling, followed by LDM triggering discovery steps to expand sequence families and overcome local plateaus.

- **Multi-objective Small-Molecule Optimization (KRAS G12D):**
 - LDM improved the hypervolume of the Pareto front by **62.4% over LLM-only reflection** and **63.1% over classical Bayesian optimization**.
 - The "Direct-Softmax" LDM variant (LLM generates SMILES candidates directly) outperformed "ReaSyn-Softmax" (LLM proposes seeds, ReaSyn generates analogues), suggesting that general-purpose LLM priors enable wider scaffold exploration.
 - The optimization trajectory demonstrated distinct stages: initial scaffold identification, substituent screening, objective bifurcation, cross-family feature recombination, and fine-tuning, revealing LDM's ability to uncover unrelated molecular backbones.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Test-time Search Scaling and Acquisition Functions:**
 - Increasing the **test-time search budget** (number of candidates proposed/scored by LLM and BO before evaluation) consistently improved performance across domains, but only when candidates were selected by a **calibrated, uncertainty-aware acquisition function**.
 - **Discovery mechanism** (dynamic feature set/search space expansion) was crucial; without it, search converged prematurely at a worse plateau.
 - **Acquisition functions** that balance exploitation and exploration (EI, UCB) consistently outperformed purely exploitative posterior mean-only search. UCB performed best in AutoResearch due to its explicit allocation to under-modelled program families.
 - The pure LLM loop (no BO, no acquisition) could perform multi-stage research but plateaued at a significantly worse performance compared to LDM, confirming the necessity of the empirically grounded value signal.

- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:**
 - For molecular design, both sufficient LLM exploration (higher LLM sampling temperature `T_LLM`) and effective acquisition guidance (higher acquisition temperature `η`) were necessary for optimal performance, with `η` being a dominant driver for Pareto-front expansion.
 - For antibody design, the effect of `T_LLM` and `η` was weaker, possibly because the open-source LLM provided weak sequence-level priors, making policy-based generation and structured search-space parameterization more impactful than reweighting directly generated sequences.

#### 4.3. Learning Discovery Experience with Fine-Tuning (LDM-TTS)

- **General Improvement:** Fine-tuning the LLM proposer with acquisition-weighted data (LDM-TTS) consistently improved proposal quality and overall performance across all three domains within the online LDM loop.
- **Reasoning Augmentation:** Including surrogate-grounded rationales (`z_t,i`) during fine-tuning (reasoning-augmented fine-tuning) generally led to further performance gains, suggesting that explicit traces of research progress facilitate escaping local optima and exploring broader spaces.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization:**
 - **Case-level OOD:** A model fine-tuned on a subset of antibody antigens transferred effectively to unseen antigens, matching or exceeding in-distribution performance.
 - **Task-level OOD:** A model fine-tuned only on AutoResearch and small-molecule tasks, and never exposed to antibody data, still matched the in-distribution model on four of five antibody targets. This indicates that the distilled acquisition policy learns a general search strategy rather than memorizing task-specific designs. The one exception (1H0D C) highlighted that *de novo* domain-specific motif design might still require in-domain knowledge, while acquisition-guided selection generalizes.
- **Value Distillation:** The fine-tuning process successfully distilled the acquisition-guided decision logic into the LLM's weights, enabling the proposer to develop an "intrinsic acquisition strategy" and an "intuition" for scientific search, even when explicit GP values are hidden during inference.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Large Discovery Model (LDM) represents a significant advancement in the field of autonomous scientific discovery, offering a principled framework that integrates the generative capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) with the empirical grounding and uncertainty quantification of Bayesian Optimization (BO). Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

1. **Bridging the LLM-BO Gap for Open-Ended Discovery:** LDM directly addresses the complementary limitations of LLMs (unreliable self-assessment, lack of calibrated uncertainty) and BO (struggle with vast, unstructured search spaces). By tightly coupling these two paradigms, LDM enables effective search in genuinely open-ended scientific domains where hypotheses are not fully specified in advance and evaluations are costly. This is a crucial step towards automating scientific research in complex, real-world settings.

2. **Introduction of the Exploitation-Exploration-Discovery Triad:** The framework formally extends the classical exploration-exploitation dilemma to include "discovery," defined as the expansion of the search frontier into previously unrepresented or unknown regions of the hypothesis space. This conceptual clarity, supported by the regret analysis, provides a more comprehensive model for scientific progress where novel mechanisms or structures can be uncovered. The recurrent observation of plateau-and-revision patterns across diverse domains validates this tripartite approach.

3. **Empirically Grounded and Uncertainty-Aware Search:** LDM's use of a probabilistic surrogate (Gaussian Process) ensures that candidate selection is empirically grounded and explicitly accounts for epistemic uncertainty. This provides a robust mechanism for allocating limited experimental resources to candidates that are either highly promising or highly informative, a critical capability when evaluations are expensive, noisy, or sparse.

4. **Generality Across Diverse Scientific Domains:** The successful evaluation across highly heterogeneous domains—neural-network training programs (code), antibody sequences (biology), and small molecules (chemistry)—demonstrates LDM's general-purpose nature. This suggests that LDM could serve as a versatile "discovery engine" applicable to a wide array of scientific and engineering design problems, reducing the need for domain-specific algorithms.

5. **Effective Use of Inference-Time Computation:** LDM leverages inference-time computation not just for reasoning (as in traditional LLM reasoning systems) but for *acquisition-guided search*. This means that additional computational budget spent on generating and scoring candidates before an expensive experiment directly translates into more efficient and effective scientific discovery.

6. **Transferable Discovery Policy through Fine-Tuning:** The LDM-TTS (Test-Time Search) fine-tuning mechanism enables the distillation of the acquisition-guided search policy into the LLM's weights. This makes the LLM itself a "value holder" rather than just a "value reader," endowing it with an intrinsic "discovery intuition." The demonstrated out-of-distribution generalization across targets and tasks indicates that this distilled policy is a transferable meta-skill for research management, rather than mere memorization of solutions.

7. **Theoretical Foundation:** The regret analysis provides a theoretical understanding of LDM's performance, explicitly decomposing regret into a discovery gap, BO optimization error, and sampling shortfall. This robust theoretical underpinning contributes to the confidence in LDM's algorithmic design.

The potential impact of LDM is substantial. By providing a more autonomous and efficient method for navigating complex design spaces under resource constraints, LDM could accelerate the pace of scientific discovery in fields like drug design, materials science, protein engineering, and automated machine learning. It offers a paradigm for AI systems to not just solve problems with known solutions, but to actively participate in the creation of new scientific knowledge. Future work aiming to address scalability, robustness, and the incorporation of external knowledge could further expand LDM's impact.
