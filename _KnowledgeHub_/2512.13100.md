# OXE-AugE: A Large-Scale Robot Augmentation of OXE for Scaling Cross-Embodiment Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC Berkeley developed OXE-AugE, a large-scale augmented dataset created using the AugE-Toolkit pipeline, to address data imbalance in robot learning datasets by generating physically accurate demonstrations for diverse robot embodiments. Policies fine-tuned on OXE-AugE achieved average success rate improvements of 24-45% on unseen robot-gripper configurations in real-world experiments, significantly enhancing robustness and cross-embodiment generalization.

## Problem

- Large-scale robot datasets like Open X-Embodiment (OXE) suffer from severe data imbalance, with a few robot types dominating the majority of trajectories.
- This imbalance limits the ability of generalist robot policies to effectively generalize across diverse robot embodiments without costly data collection or fine-tuning for each new platform.
- Existing robot data augmentation methods often lack kinematic validity, physical accuracy, or scalability to many target robots.

## Method

- Developed AugE-Toolkit, an open-source, scalable pipeline that fuses learned and simulation-based masks and utilizes automatic base position tuning for physically accurate robot augmentation.
- Created **OXE-AugE**, a large-scale dataset by augmenting 16 popular OXE datasets with up to 9 new robot embodiments, tripling the original data volume to over 4.4 million trajectories.
- Employed physics-based rendering within MuJoCo Playground for augmented robot replay, ensuring physically accurate poses and visual realism over artifact-prone generative models.

## Results

- Simulation studies showed N-robot augmentation consistently improved robustness on the source robot and achieved generalization performance on unseen robots that rivaled or surpassed transfer to explicitly augmented target robots.
- Real-world experiments demonstrated that OpenVLA and Pi0 foundation models fine-tuned on OXE-AugE achieved average success rate improvements of **24%** and **45%**, respectively, on unseen robot-gripper configurations.
- Policies fine-tuned on OXE-AugE achieved high average success rates (75-**82%**) on a novel 'Robotiq++' embodiment, outperforming baseline training and demonstrating superior performance compared to diffusion-based augmentation (which led to a 27-**30%** drop in success).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scaling the number and diversity of robot augmentations significantly improves policy robustness against visual perturbations on the source robot, fostering learning of task-relevant spatial geometry.
> - Diverse robot augmentation facilitates the learning of embodiment-agnostic visual representations and spatial reasoning, enabling effective zero-shot generalization to previously unseen robot embodiments.
> - Physically accurate, simulation-based augmentation is crucial for generating effective robot training data, outperforming diffusion-based methods that introduce visual artifacts and geometric inconsistencies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the forefront of the quest to develop **generalist robot policies** capable of controlling a wide variety of robot embodiments across diverse tasks and environments. Recent years have seen significant progress in robot learning driven by large and diverse datasets, leading to the emergence of "foundation models" or "generalist policies" like RT-1, RT-2, VIMA, Octo, OpenVLA, and Pi0. These models aim to transfer knowledge learned from broad experience to new scenarios, objects, and robot platforms.

A fundamental challenge in this landscape is the **prohibitive cost and time involved in collecting real-world robot data** for every new hardware platform or task. This cost is amplified by the increasing diversity of robotic hardware, with new robots constantly introduced, often leading to poor transferability of policies trained on one platform to another. The ability to achieve **cross-embodiment generalization**—transferring policies across different robot arm and gripper combinations—is thus a critical goal for scalable and practical robot learning.

The **Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset**, released in 2023, represented a significant step towards addressing this by aggregating demonstrations from over 60 real-world robot datasets. OXE has become a cornerstone for training many generalist policies. However, as highlighted by the authors, OXE suffers from a severe **data imbalance**: over 85% of its real trajectories come from just four robot types, while many others are sparsely represented. This imbalance risks overfitting to specific robot–scene combinations and often necessitates costly fine-tuning for generalist policies when deployed on new or underrepresented robots.

To mitigate data scarcity and imbalance, researchers have explored various **data augmentation techniques**. These include domain randomization in simulation, leveraging human videos, and employing image or video generation models. Specifically, the concept of "robot embodiment augmentation," where demonstrations from one robot are transformed into synthetic versions for another, has emerged as a promising direction, building on methods like cross-painting. Prior works such as Mirage and RoVi-Aug explored test-time adaptation or one-to-one transfer between specific source and target robots.

This paper distinguishes itself by moving beyond pairwise transfer to **scalable, large-scale robot augmentation** as a systematic data pipeline. It critically examines the effects of *scaling* the number and diversity of augmented embodiments, addressing questions of robustness, transfer, and generalization in a more comprehensive manner than previous work. By open-sourcing both the augmentation toolkit (AugE-Toolkit) and the resulting augmented dataset (OXE-AugE), the authors contribute directly to the infrastructure required for the next generation of generalist robot learning research. The work also contributes to the ongoing discussion on **scaling laws in robot learning**, investigating how performance scales not just with raw data volume, but specifically with *augmented* data diversity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the limitations of existing large-scale robot datasets, particularly the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset, despite its significant contributions. While OXE has been instrumental in training generalist robot policies, its severe imbalance—with a few robot types dominating the majority of the data—poses a significant risk of overfitting. This imbalance hinders the ability of trained policies to truly generalize across diverse robot embodiments without requiring extensive and costly re-collection of demonstrations or fine-tuning for each new platform. The authors explicitly state that "re-collecting demonstrations and retraining for each new hardware platform are prohibitively costly."

Given this context, the key objectives of this work are multifaceted:

1. **Develop a Scalable and High-Quality Robot Augmentation Pipeline:** The primary objective is to create an improved, open-source augmentation toolkit, named **AugE-Toolkit**, that can transform existing robot demonstration data into synthetic versions for numerous different robot embodiments. This toolkit aims to overcome the limitations of previous augmentation methods, specifically by ensuring physical accuracy and kinematic validity while being visually realistic and scalable to many target robots without requiring extensive manual calibration or retraining for each new embodiment.

2. **Create a Large-Scale, Diverse Robot Augmentation Dataset:** Building upon the developed toolkit, a crucial objective is to construct **OXE-AugE**, a substantial open-source dataset that augments a selection of popular OXE datasets with a wide array of new robot embodiments. The goal is to significantly increase the overall data volume and, more importantly, the diversity of robot embodiments, providing a richer resource for training generalist policies.

3. **Systematically Study the Impact of Scaling Robot Augmentation:** The paper aims to conduct a rigorous investigation into how increasing the number and diversity of robot augmentations influences various aspects of policy learning. Specifically, the authors seek to answer three fundamental questions:
 - Can robot augmentation improve policy robustness on the *original* source robot under visual perturbations (e.g., changes in lighting or occlusions)?
 - Does increasing the number of augmented robot embodiments improve policy performance on *augmented* robots (i.e., known target robots)?
 - Do policies trained on a diverse set of augmented robots generalize effectively to *previously unseen* robot embodiments?

4. **Validate the Practical Utility with State-of-the-Art Generalist Policies:** A practical objective is to demonstrate that state-of-the-art generalist policies, such as OpenVLA and Pi0, can tangibly benefit from fine-tuning on the OXE-AugE dataset in real-world physical experiments. This validation is critical to prove the effectiveness and real-world applicability of the proposed augmentation strategy.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to democratize and scale robot learning by reducing the reliance on costly, bespoke data collection for every new robot, instead leveraging and intelligently expanding existing datasets to foster more robust and transferable robot skills.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a combined approach of developing a novel augmentation pipeline, creating a large-scale dataset, and conducting systematic experimental validation in both simulated and real-world settings.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work operates within the standard imitation learning paradigm, where a dataset of successful trajectories from a "source" robot (comprising RGB observations, gripper 6D poses, and actions) is transformed into synthetic trajectories for various "target" robot embodiments. The core assumption is that grippers across robots are functionally similar (e.g., 2- or 3-jaw), allowing for shared task strategies, and that robot coordinate frames are known for alignment. The goal is to train a policy on a combined dataset of real and augmented data to improve robustness, transfer, and generalization to augmented and unseen robots.

#### 3.2. AugE-Toolkit: Scalable Robot Augmentation Pipeline

The cornerstone of this methodology is the **AugE-Toolkit**, an improved robot augmentation pipeline that builds upon the "cross-painting" framework. Cross-painting involves three stages for each image in a trajectory: (i) source-robot segmentation, (ii) background inpainting, and (iii) augmented-robot replay and compositing. AugE-Toolkit addresses key limitations of prior learning-based (lack of kinematic guarantees, poor scalability) and simulation-based (requires accurate calibration) methods through three novel components:

1. **Fusion of Simulation and Learned Masks:** To achieve accurate robot segmentation without relying on perfect camera calibration, AugE-Toolkit combines the strengths of both learned and simulation-based masks. A SAM2 model is fine-tuned on a small labeled subset of OXE datasets to generate learned masks that are visually accurate but may have minor segmentation errors. Simultaneously, geometrically precise simulation masks are generated. These two mask types are then aligned (via translation to maximize IoU), pruned (removing learned mask pixels far from the aligned simulation boundary), and fused (union and morphological closing) to produce highly accurate, calibrated-error-resilient masks.

2. **Automatic Base Position Tuning:** To ensure that target robot embodiments, which vary significantly in kinematic reach and scale, can faithfully replay the source trajectories, the toolkit automatically adjusts the base position of each target robot in simulation. This iterative process samples offsets along (x, y, z) axes, computes tracking error, and refines the position until all end-effector poses are reachable within a small error tolerance (e.g., 1 cm). Trajectories that remain unreachable are discarded, maintaining motion fidelity across diverse robot sizes.

3. **Scalable Multi-Robot Deployment:** Unlike generative models that can introduce visual artifacts and temporal incoherence, AugE-Toolkit utilizes physics-based rendering within the MuJoCo Playground for augmented robot replay. This guarantees physically accurate poses and realism. The pipeline is designed for "embarrassingly parallel" execution, allowing concurrent augmentation across multiple target robots. This leads to high throughput, with a 50-frame clip augmented for one robot in approximately 25 seconds, translating to 75 clips per minute with 32-way parallelism. Adding new robots simply requires registering their URDF models, without necessitating retraining or calibration.

#### 3.3. OXE-AugE Dataset Creation

Using the AugE-Toolkit, the authors created **OXE-AugE**, a large-scale open-source dataset. They selected 16 popular datasets from the original OXE collection, which collectively form 60% of the widely used Octo pretraining mixture. These source datasets, originally from robots like Franka, UR5, xArm, WidowX, Google Robot, and Jaco, were augmented with up to 9 different robot embodiments (including Panda, Sawyer, Kinova Gen3, KUKA iiwa). The resulting dataset comprises over 4.4 million trajectories, more than triple the size of the original OXE, substantially enhancing embodiment diversity.

#### 3.4. Experimental Validation

The evaluation protocol involved two main phases:

1. **Simulation Study:**
 - **Environment:** Five Robosuite tasks (Lift, Stack, Can, Two Piece Assembly, Square).
 - **Source Data:** 200 demonstrations per task collected on a Franka robot.
 - **Augmented Robots:** UR5e, Kinova Gen3, Sawyer, and Jaco (with a 3-jaw gripper).
 - **Policy Training:** Diffusion Policy using RoboMimic, trained from scratch.
 - **Data Regimes:** Policies were trained under four data configurations: no augmentation, 1 robot augmentation (data only), 1 robot augmentation + source data, and multi-robot (N-robot) augmentation + source data.
 - **Evaluation Conditions:** Assessed performance under three conditions: (1) **Robustness** (on the source robot under visual perturbations like lighting shifts and occlusions), (2) **Transfer** (on robots used for augmentation), and (3) **Generalization** (on robots not used for augmentation).

2. **Physical Experiments:**
 - **Generalist Policies:** Two state-of-the-art foundation models, OpenVLA and Pi0, were fine-tuned.
 - **Training Data:** Original Bridge dataset, and versions fine-tuned on "2 augmented robots" (Franka, UR5e augmentations of OXE-AugE) and "N augmented robots" (all augmentations of OXE-AugE).
 - **Evaluation Tasks:** Four manipulation tasks from the Bridge dataset, including one novel task, performed on a real Franka robot.
 - **Testing Embodiments:** A Franka arm with its default parallel-jaw gripper ("Franka," representing an augmented robot) and a Franka arm with a custom modified Robotiq gripper ("Robotiq++," simulating an unseen embodiment).
 - **Baseline Comparison:** A comparison was made against a diffusion-based augmentation pipeline (RoVi-Aug) to highlight the importance of physical accuracy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The systematic studies conducted in both simulation and real-world environments yielded compelling results that validate the efficacy of large-scale robot augmentation using AugE-Toolkit and OXE-AugE.

#### 4.1. Simulation Study Findings

The simulation study provided quantitative insights into how scaling robot augmentation impacts various aspects of policy performance across five Robosuite tasks.

- **Robustness to Visual Perturbations (Figure 3):** Policies trained with robot augmentation demonstrated significantly enhanced robustness to visual perturbations on the *source* robot. While policies trained solely on the original Franka data degraded severely under lighting shifts and occlusions, training with both 1 and N augmented robots substantially improved success rates. Notably, N-robot augmentation consistently outperformed 1-robot augmentation, suggesting that increased diversity helps policies learn task-relevant spatial geometry between gripper and object, rather than relying on incidental visual features like arm shape or lighting.

- **Transfer to Augmented Robots (Figure 4, orange lines):**
 - Training only on data augmented for a single target robot ("1× Aug (Target)") achieved success rates between 26–65%.
 - Combining the source data with 1× augmented data ("1× Aug (Target) + Source") significantly improved performance by 9–19% compared to augmentation alone, indicating the value of leveraging both real and diverse synthetic experiences.
 - Training on the source data combined with *all* N augmented robots ("N× Aug (incl. Target) + Source") yielded the best overall performance in 60% of the cases, with slight gains over "1× Aug (Target) + Source," suggesting that broad augmentation is beneficial even for robots explicitly included in the augmented set.

- **Generalization to Unseen Robots (Figure 4, blue lines):**
 - Policies trained without any robot augmentation ("0× Aug + Source") performed poorly on unseen robots, as expected.
 - While 1× robot augmentation (training on source + one non-target augmented robot) showed moderate generalization, training with a diverse set of (N-1) augmented robots (excluding the hold-out target) achieved *substantially higher success* across all four unseen target robots.
 - In many instances, the generalization performance of "(N-1)× Aug (not incl. Target) + Source" policies *rivaled* or even surpassed the transfer performance of "1× Aug (Target)" policies that were trained directly on the augmented target robot. This is a strong indicator that diverse robot augmentation fosters embodiment-agnostic visual representations and spatial reasoning, enabling effective zero-shot generalization to novel robot platforms.

#### 4.2. Physical Experiments Findings

Real-world experiments validated the practical utility of OXE-AugE for enhancing state-of-the-art generalist policies.

- **Improved Cross-Embodiment Performance (Figure 5):** Both OpenVLA-OFT and Pi0 policies, when fine-tuned on the original Bridge dataset alone, showed relatively low success rates, particularly on the Franka robot, due to domain shifts from the original WidowX gripper. Fine-tuning with OXE-AugE data significantly boosted performance.
 - "2 augmented robots" (fine-tuning on Franka and UR5e augmentations) improved success across all tasks.
 - **N× augmentation yielded the highest overall success, demonstrating average improvements of 24% for OpenVLA-OFT and 45% for Pi0** compared to no augmentation.
 - On the novel "Robotiq++" embodiment (simulating an unseen gripper), fine-tuned policies achieved high average success rates of 75% (OpenVLA-OFT) and 82% (Pi0), indicating strong generalization capabilities to previously unseen robot-gripper configurations, even for a novel task.

- **Superiority over Diffusion-Based Augmentation (Figure 6):** A critical comparison with the diffusion-based RoVi-Aug pipeline revealed that simulator-based augmentation (AugE-Toolkit) is crucial for robot learning. Fine-tuning policies on data generated by RoVi-Aug led to a **27–30% drop in policy success**. Qualitative examples showed that diffusion-based augmentations often suffer from visual artifacts, misaligned gripper poses, and geometric inconsistencies, highlighting that physically accurate simulation is paramount for generating effective robot training data.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that increasing the diversity of robot augmentations leads to significant improvements in policy robustness, transferability to augmented robots, and, most notably, generalization to entirely unseen robot embodiments. This effect is observed with both single-task imitation learning and complex foundation models, validating the approach across different scales of robot learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "OXE-AugE: A Large-Scale Robot Augmentation of OXE for Scaling Cross-Embodiment Policy Learning" offers several significant contributions and has the potential for a substantial impact on the field of robot learning:

#### 5.1. Addressing a Core Challenge in Generalist Robotics

The most significant impact of this work lies in directly tackling the critical challenge of **cross-embodiment generalization** for generalist robot policies. The current reliance on large, diverse datasets (like OXE) is hampered by data imbalance and the high cost of collecting new data for every robot. OXE-AugE provides a principled and scalable solution to expand data diversity without incurring new real-world collection costs, moving closer to the vision of a single policy controlling any robot.

#### 5.2. Enabling Scalable and High-Quality Data Augmentation

The **AugE-Toolkit** represents a notable methodological advance. By intelligently fusing learned and simulation-based masks, and by incorporating automatic base position tuning, it overcomes common hurdles like imperfect camera calibration and diverse robot kinematics. The toolkit's emphasis on physically accurate simulation over generative models ensures **high-quality, kinematically valid, and temporally coherent augmented data**, which is shown to be crucial for robust policy learning, especially when compared to diffusion-based alternatives. Its open-source nature and parallel deployment capabilities make it a practical and accessible tool for the wider robotics community to scale their own datasets.

#### 5.3. Quantifiable Improvements in Policy Performance

The research provides strong empirical evidence, from both extensive simulation studies and real-world physical experiments, that scaling robot augmentation leads to **quantifiable and significant performance gains**. The observed improvements of **24-45% in success rates** for state-of-the-art foundation models like OpenVLA and Pi0 on unseen robot-gripper combinations are particularly impactful. These results demonstrate that explicit embodiment augmentation is not merely a supplementary technique but a powerful strategy that complements large-pooled data training.

#### 5.4. Fostering Embodiment-Agnostic Learning

A key insight from the findings is that diverse robot augmentation appears to facilitate the learning of **embodiment-agnostic visual representations and spatial reasoning**. By varying the robot's appearance while preserving the task and scene, policies are encouraged to focus on task-relevant geometry (e.g., gripper-object relationship) rather than incidental visual cues. This fundamentally shifts how policies perceive and interact with the world, leading to more robust performance against visual perturbations and superior generalization to entirely novel robot platforms.

#### 5.5. A Valuable Open-Source Resource

The release of **OXE-AugE**, a dataset containing over 4.4 million trajectories across 9 robot embodiments, is a substantial open-source contribution. This expanded dataset provides a richer training resource for researchers developing and evaluating generalist robot policies, accelerating progress in the field by making diverse, high-quality augmented data readily available.

#### 5.6. Guiding Future Research Directions

The paper not only provides solutions but also clearly outlines limitations and promising avenues for future research. Suggestions such as incorporating 3D geometry and physics-aware augmentation, extending to dynamic or bimanual settings, and integrating complementary augmentations (viewpoint, background, object variations) will undoubtedly guide the next generation of work in robust and generalizable robot learning. This forward-looking perspective enhances the paper's long-term impact by identifying crucial unsolved problems.

In conclusion, OXE-AugE and the underlying AugE-Toolkit represent a significant stride towards enabling truly generalist robots. By providing a scalable, high-quality method for diversifying robot data, this work empowers current and future robot learning models to achieve greater robustness, transferability, and generalization, ultimately reducing the barriers to deploying intelligent robots in the real world.
