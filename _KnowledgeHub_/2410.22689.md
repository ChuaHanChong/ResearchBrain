# Multi-Task Interactive Robot Fleet Learning with Visual World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> SIRIUS-FLEET presents a multi-task interactive robot fleet learning framework that integrates a continuously updated multi-task policy with an adaptive runtime monitoring mechanism, underpinned by a visual world model. It reduces human workload by adapting anomaly prediction thresholds while maintaining over 95% combined policy performance in simulation and real-world multi-task settings.

## Problem

- Multi-task robot policies struggle with generalization and robustness in diverse, unstructured real-world environments.
- Deploying robot fleets typically requires high human workload for continuous supervision and intervention, making large-scale operations impractical.
- Existing runtime monitoring methods for robots are often limited to single-task scenarios and lack adaptability for evolving multi-task performance.

## Method

- An iterative learning-deployment cycle where human interventions during robot operation refine the multi-task policy over time.
- A runtime monitoring system powered by a ==visual world model== that proactively predicts future states to detect potential failures and out-of-distribution (OOD) situations.
- Adaptive anomaly prediction thresholds that dynamically adjust based on the robot's autonomy and performance, reducing the need for human intervention as the robot improves.

## Results

- Autonomous policy success rates improved by **13%** in simulation (RoboCasa) and **45%** in real-world (Mutex) over three deployment rounds.
- Achieved over **95%** Combined Policy Performance (overall system success with intervention) in both simulation and real-world multi-task settings.
- Demonstrated improved Return of Human Effort (ROHE) over time, indicating a reduction in human workload for supervision compared to baselines like MoMaRT and PATO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual world models can effectively serve as a backbone for proactive and generalizable runtime anomaly prediction in multi-task robot settings.
> - Dynamically adjusting anomaly prediction thresholds based on robot performance allows for a significant reduction in human intervention over time.
> - Iteratively incorporating human interventions and deployment data enables continuous improvement of multi-task robot policies and their ability to generalize.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

SIRIUS-FLEET sits at the intersection of several rapidly advancing and critical areas in artificial intelligence and robotics: multi-task robot learning, human-in-the-loop (HITL) learning, runtime monitoring, and the emerging field of robot fleet learning with world models.

**Multi-Task Robot Learning and Generalist Robots:** Recent years have seen a significant push towards developing "generalist robots" capable of performing a wide array of tasks across diverse environments, moving beyond single-task, brittle solutions. This trend is fueled by innovations in large-scale datasets (e.g., RT-X models, Bridge Data, Droid), advanced policy architectures (e.g., Robotics Transformers like RT-1 and RT-2, Diffusion Policy, Octo), and new benchmarks (RoboCasa, Mutex). SIRIUS-FLEET contributes to this landscape by developing a framework that enables the *continuous improvement and robust deployment* of such multi-task policies in real-world, unstructured settings, addressing a key limitation of many existing generalist models that are often deployed as static, one-off implementations.

**Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) and Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL):** While generalist robots show promise, their deployment in variable real-world scenarios often encounters challenges with generalization and robustness, necessitating human supervision. Interactive imitation learning (IIL) and human-in-the-loop methods have emerged to address this by allowing robots to learn from human interventions and feedback during deployment. However, a major bottleneck remains the high human workload required for continuous supervision. SIRIUS-FLEET directly addresses this by building upon IIL principles, specifically interactive fleet learning (IFL) approaches, but with a strong emphasis on **reducing human burden** through intelligent monitoring.

**Runtime Monitoring and Anomaly Detection:** To mitigate the need for constant human oversight, runtime monitoring techniques have been developed to automatically detect anomalies and potential failures, requesting human intervention only when necessary. This includes methods like out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and failure prediction. Prior work in this area has largely focused on single-task settings or relied on fixed detection thresholds. SIRIUS-FLEET innovates by extending runtime monitoring to **multi-task scenarios** and introducing **adaptive decision boundaries**, which dynamically adjust based on the robot's evolving performance and task-specific characteristics.

**World Models in Robotics:** A significant trend in modern AI is the development of "world models" – neural networks capable of predicting future states or outcomes from current observations and actions. Inspired by recent successes in video generation and simulation (e.g., Video Generation Models as World Simulators, Genie, Pandora, Gaia-1), SIRIUS-FLEET leverages a visual world model as a core component. This application is novel in its explicit use of world models for *proactive anomaly prediction* in a multi-task robot fleet context, allowing the system to anticipate and preempt failures before they fully manifest.

**Robot Fleet Learning:** The increased scale of robot deployments (e.g., Google's QT-Opt, ALOHA, AutoRT) has propelled interest in robot fleet learning, which tackles challenges like resource allocation, decentralized learning, and system management. SIRIUS-FLEET directly addresses two critical challenges in this domain: **supervising large robot fleets with minimal human oversight** and **enabling continuous multi-task policy improvement** using real-world deployment data. By combining runtime monitoring with iterative policy updates, SIRIUS-FLEET pushes the frontier of making robot fleet deployment more practical and scalable.

In essence, SIRIUS-FLEET synthesizes these elements to bridge the gap between impressive robot learning capabilities in controlled environments and the practical demands of reliable, long-term deployment in diverse, unstructured real-world settings, with a focus on scalable human collaboration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of SIRIUS-FLEET is to enable the robust and scalable deployment of robot fleets capable of performing diverse tasks across various real-world environments, significantly reducing the human workload traditionally associated with robot supervision.

The core motivations stem from recognized limitations in current robot learning paradigms:

- **Generalization and Robustness Challenges:** Despite advancements in multi-task robot learning and generalist policies, robots frequently struggle with generalizing to novel situations and maintaining robust performance when faced with the inherent variability and uncertainty of real-world environments. This undermines the safety and reliability of robot systems and restricts their practical applicability.
- **High Human Workload in Supervision:** Traditional interactive imitation learning (IIL) methods often necessitate continuous human supervision and intervention to ensure trustworthy robot operation. This high human workload renders large-scale, long-term deployment of robot fleets impractical and economically unfeasible.
- **Lack of Continuous Policy Improvement during Deployment:** Many advanced robot policies are developed as static, "one-off" models, meaning they do not learn or adapt from their real-world experiences post-deployment. To achieve true autonomy and handle evolving environments, robots need mechanisms for continuous learning and refinement based on deployment data.
- **Inefficient Runtime Monitoring for Multi-Task Settings:** Existing runtime monitoring approaches (e.g., OOD detection, failure prediction) have largely been confined to single-task settings. Scaling these to supervise multiple diverse tasks simultaneously in a fleet setting presents significant challenges regarding efficiency, generalization, and adaptability.

To address these challenges, SIRIUS-FLEET sets forth two specific, interconnected objectives:

1. **Enable Continuous Policy Update:** To ensure that multi-task policies can continually improve and adapt throughout long-term deployment. This involves mechanisms for incorporating feedback and data collected during live operation to refine the robot's skills and generalize knowledge across tasks.
2. **Efficient Multi-Task Runtime Monitoring:** To build a runtime monitoring mechanism that can supervise multiple tasks simultaneously and effectively, while minimizing the need for constant human attention. This mechanism needs to be capable of identifying potential issues *preemptively* and adapting its intervention criteria as the robot's autonomy evolves.

The motivation for employing a visual world model as the backbone for runtime monitoring is crucial: it allows the system to **predict the outcomes of future actions** and **preempt potential failures** before they occur. This predictive capability, coupled with the ability to learn shared representations across tasks, is vital for building generalizable and efficient anomaly predictors that can effectively supervise a diverse multi-task robot fleet. The introduction of **adaptive anomaly prediction thresholds** is further motivated by the need for the monitoring system to intelligently adjust its intervention criteria, reducing human requests as the robot's performance improves, thus ensuring a gradually diminishing human workload.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SIRIUS-FLEET proposes a multi-task interactive robot fleet learning framework that integrates a continually updated multi-task policy with an adaptive runtime monitoring mechanism, all underpinned by a visual world model. The methodology follows an iterative learning and deployment cycle involving humans.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Iterative Deployment

The problem is framed as operating N robots in N independent Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) that share state, action, and transition functions, allowing for a unified multi-task policy. The learning process is iterative:
1. **Initial Policy Bootstrap:** Each robot starts with a policy ($\pi_0$) learned via behavioral cloning from initial human demonstrations ($D_0$).
2. **Deployment Rounds:** In each round `i`, robots execute tasks using policy $\pi_i$ with runtime monitoring enabled by anomaly predictors $E_i$.
3. **Human Intervention:** If $E_i$ predicts an anomaly, a human is signaled and can intervene (using $\pi_H$) to correct the robot's actions.
4. **Data Collection and Update:** Trajectories (including human interventions) are collected into a buffer $D'$, which then updates the main dataset $D_{i+1} = D_i \cup D'$. This updated dataset is used to train the next-round policy $\pi_{i+1}$ and anomaly predictors $E_{i+1}$.

#### 3.2. Runtime Monitoring Mechanism

The core of SIRIUS-FLEET's efficiency is its runtime monitoring, designed for generalization, task adaptability, and failure preemption across diverse multi-task scenarios.

##### 3.2.1. Training the Visual World Model (VWM)

- **Purpose:** To simulate task progress, predict future action outcomes, and serve as a backbone for anomaly prediction.
- **Architecture:** An autoregressive visual world model $W = (E_\gamma, D_\lambda, T_\psi)$ composed of:
 - **Encoder ($E_\gamma$):** UNet-based, encodes image observation $x_t$ into latent embedding $z_t$.
 - **Decoder ($D_\lambda$):** UNet-based, reconstructs $z_t$ back to image $\hat{x}_t$, minimizing L2 reconstruction loss.
 - **Conditional Next State Prediction Model ($T_\psi$):** cVAE with Transformer architecture. It takes a history of T embeddings $(z_{t-T}..., z_t)$ and autoregressively predicts L future latent embeddings $(\hat{z}_{t+1}..., \hat{z}_{t+L})$.
- **Training:** $E_\gamma, D_\lambda, T_\psi$ are jointly trained on diverse robot trajectory frames. The use of a stochastic latent space (cVAE) is chosen to support multiple future samplings, improving prediction quality.
- **Role in Monitoring:** Once trained, the VWM is *frozen* and provides generalized latent representations and future predictions, which are then used by downstream anomaly predictors.

##### 3.2.2. Building the Downstream Anomaly Predictors

Built on the *frozen* VWM embeddings, task-specific anomaly predictors identify two types of anomalies:

- **Failure Prediction:**
 - **Mechanism:** A small, computationally efficient failure classifier $F_\sigma$ is trained for each task.
 - **Training Data:** Labels are derived from human interventions in the previous deployment round: trajectory segments before human intervention are labeled as "failure."
 - **Objective:** To predict failures similar to those previously identified by human operators.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Prediction:**
 - **Mechanism:** Uses k-means clustering on VWM-generated image embeddings. PCA is applied to reduce embedding dimensions.
 - **Detection:** A state is identified as OOD if its L2 distance $d$ to the nearest cluster centroid exceeds a task-specific threshold $\alpha_g$.
 - **Objective:** To capture scenarios where the robot is in novel or unfamiliar states.
- **Adaptive Decision Boundaries (Key Innovation):**
 - **Concept:** Anomaly prediction thresholds dynamically adjust to the robot's improving performance, reducing human intervention over time.
 - **Failure Prediction:** Finetuned using recent deployment data and updated human-perceived risk.
 - **OOD Prediction:** The threshold $\alpha_g$ is determined by $d_{\theta_g}$, the distance of the top $\theta_g$ percentile of validation latent embeddings to their nearest centroids. Crucially, $\theta_g$ itself is adapted based on the human intervention ratio $p_H$ (autonomous rollout ratio) using a pre-fitted exponential decay function ($\theta_g = a + be^{cp_H}$). This ensures that as the robot becomes more autonomous, the OOD boundary expands, requiring fewer interventions.

#### 3.3. Multi-Task Policy Training

- **Architecture:** A Transformer-based multi-task policy, similar to RoboMimic/RoboCasa, which takes image observations, robot proprioceptive data, and language task goals as input.
- **Output:** Robot actions are outputted using a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) head.
- **Continuous Improvement:** The policy is continuously fine-tuned with data from previous rounds using weighted sampling, enabling knowledge sharing and generalization across tasks.

#### 3.4. SIRIUS-FLEET in Operation

During runtime, the VWM predicts future embeddings over multiple steps ($L$ steps, $N$ scenarios). These predicted future embeddings are then fed into the anomaly predictors. For failure prediction, an average failure score is computed. For OOD prediction, the average distance of future states to their nearest centroids is calculated and compared against the adaptive OOD threshold. If an anomaly is detected, human intervention is requested, and the resulting interaction data is used for subsequent model updates. This complete pipeline ensures iterative improvement and efficient human-robot collaboration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SIRIUS-FLEET was conducted on two large-scale multi-task benchmarks: RoboCasa in simulation (12 tasks) and Mutex in the real world (10 tasks), utilizing a Franka Emika Panda arm. The framework was assessed over three rounds of iterative deployment, with human supervision progressively reduced after the first round.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics

- **Autonomous Performance:** Measures the policy's success rate without any human intervention.
- **Combined Policy Performance (CPP):** Represents the overall system success rate under runtime monitoring, reflecting effective human-robot collaboration.
- **Return of Human Effort (ROHE):** Quantifies the efficiency of human intervention by comparing policy success rate against intervention effort, aiming for maximum performance with minimum human workload.

#### 4.2. System Performance Over Time (Figure 6)

- **Autonomous Policy Improvement:** SIRIUS-FLEET consistently improved its autonomous policy performance across deployment rounds. In simulation (RoboCasa), the success rate increased by 13%, and in the real world (Mutex), it saw a significant 45% increase. This demonstrates the effectiveness of continuous policy fine-tuning using collected deployment data.
- **High Combined Policy Performance:** The system maintained a remarkably high Combined Policy Performance, exceeding 95% in both simulation and real-world environments. This indicates that the runtime monitoring mechanism, combined with timely human intervention, successfully prevented failures and ensured a very high overall task success rate.
- **Improved Return of Human Effort (ROHE):** ROHE consistently improved over time in both environments. This crucial finding confirms that as the robot's autonomy improves, the adaptive anomaly predictors lead to fewer predicted anomalies and, consequently, a reduction in the required human intervention, thereby lowering human workload.

#### 4.3. Baseline Comparison (Figure 7)

SIRIUS-FLEET's multi-task runtime monitoring was compared against three prominent baselines: MoMaRT, PATO, and ThriftyDAgger, evaluated on five diverse tasks in RoboCasa. Critically, these baselines were originally designed for single-task settings and often rely on fixed detection thresholds.
- **Superiority in CPP and ROHE:** SIRIUS-FLEET consistently outperformed all baselines in both Combined Policy Performance and Return of Human Effort.
- **Impact of Adaptive Thresholds:** The paper attributes SIRIUS-FLEET's superior performance to its adaptive anomaly prediction thresholds, which effectively handle varying task distributions and evolving robot performance. In contrast, the fixed thresholds used by baselines often led to either excessive interventions (high human workload) or decreased performance (missed anomalies).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Appendix)

- **Multi-Task World Model vs. Single-Task World Model (Table 1):** The multi-task visual world model demonstrated higher accuracy in future latent state prediction (lower MSE loss) compared to single-task world models. This validates the design choice of a shared, generalizable world model backbone.
- **Failure Prediction vs. OOD Prediction (Table 2):** Combining both OOD and failure prediction in SIRIUS-FLEET yielded superior human intervention overlap accuracy compared to using either component alone. This confirms that these two types of anomaly predictors complement each other effectively, identifying both known failure modes and novel, unfamiliar scenarios.
- **Multi-Task Policy vs. Single-Task Policy (Figure 8):** The multi-task policy, trained on all tasks simultaneously, generally achieved better success rates than single-task policies trained individually for each task. This finding supports the widely observed benefit of multi-task training in fostering better generalization in robot policies.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Analysis (Appendix Figures 9-14)

Qualitative assessments demonstrated that SIRIUS-FLEET's anomaly predictions align more closely with human judgments regarding intervention timings and failure modes, compared to baselines. Baselines like MoMaRT often produced false positives due to pixel changes, while PATO exhibited either false negatives or high oscillations. ThriftyDAgger's risk Q-function, being data-intensive, struggled with generalization and accurately reflecting local failure modes.

In summary, the results conclusively show that SIRIUS-FLEET is an effective framework that successfully improves multi-task policy performance, maintains high overall system success, and significantly reduces human workload over time, outperforming existing methods through its innovative use of a visual world model and adaptive anomaly prediction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SIRIUS-FLEET presents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and deployment, with the potential to profoundly impact the practical viability of autonomous robot fleets. Its significance lies in addressing several critical bottlenecks that hinder the widespread adoption of AI-enabled robots in complex, unstructured real-world environments.

#### 5.1. Key Contributions (as stated by the authors)

1. **A comprehensive framework for multi-task interactive robot fleet learning:** SIRIUS-FLEET provides a complete pipeline where a multi-task robot policy continuously improves during deployment through strategic runtime monitoring and human interaction. This moves beyond static policies to embrace lifelong learning in real-world scenarios.
2. **Novel runtime monitoring mechanism:** The framework introduces a sophisticated runtime monitoring system powered by a visual world model backbone, featuring task-adaptive anomaly prediction thresholds. This is a critical innovation that allows the system to intelligently adjust its intervention sensitivity based on the robot's improving capabilities, significantly optimizing human-robot collaboration.
3. **Empirical validation of high performance:** The demonstration of over 95% average success rates in both diverse simulation (RoboCasa) and real-world (Mutex) multi-task settings provides compelling evidence of SIRIUS-FLEET's effectiveness and practical utility. This level of robustness in varied environments is a crucial step towards reliable robot deployment.

#### 5.2. Broader Impact and Potential Implications

- **Enabling Scalable Robot Deployment:** By drastically reducing the need for continuous human supervision while maintaining high task success rates, SIRIUS-FLEET makes the deployment of large robot fleets economically and logistically feasible. This is crucial for applications in logistics, manufacturing, elderly care, and smart homes, where large numbers of robots might operate simultaneously across diverse tasks.
- **Increased Trust and Reliability:** The proactive anomaly prediction and human-in-the-loop correction mechanism enhances the safety and trustworthiness of robot systems. By preempting potential failures, the framework prevents catastrophic errors and fosters greater human confidence in autonomous agents.
- **Accelerated Policy Improvement:** The continuous learning loop, where deployment data and human interventions are used to fine-tune both the policy and the anomaly predictors, ensures that the robot fleet becomes more capable and autonomous over time. This adaptive learning capability is vital for robots operating in dynamic and evolving environments.
- **Leveraging World Models for Robustness:** The innovative application of visual world models for predictive anomaly detection is a significant methodological contribution. It highlights the power of internal models to anticipate future outcomes, moving beyond reactive control and towards more intelligent, proactive robotic behavior.
- **Reduced Development and Maintenance Costs:** By enabling robots to learn "on the job" and adapt to new scenarios, SIRIUS-FLEET could reduce the upfront data collection and engineering effort required for new tasks or environments. This translates to lower development and maintenance costs for robotic solutions.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by the authors)

- **Focus on Quasi-Static Manipulation:** The current framework is best suited for tasks where anomalies can be easily corrected via teleoperation. Extending SIRIUS-FLEET to highly dynamic tasks, where intervention windows are much shorter and consequences of error are higher, remains a challenge.
- **Limited Human Operator Diversity:** The experiments involved a small group of five human operators. Future work should conduct large-scale human studies to understand the impact of diverse human interventions on monitoring and policy training.
- **Single Embodiment:** The evaluations were conducted on a single robot platform (Franka Emika Panda arm). Generalizing SIRIUS-FLEET to cross-embodiment fleet learning (i.e., deploying the same framework across different robot platforms) would further enhance its applicability.

Despite these limitations, SIRIUS-FLEET represents a substantial step forward in building truly generalist and reliable robot fleets. Its unique combination of multi-task learning, visual world models, and adaptive human-in-the-loop monitoring paves the way for more autonomous, efficient, and practical robotic deployments in the real world.
