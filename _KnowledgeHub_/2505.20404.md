# Co-Design of Soft Gripper with Neural Physics

> [!summary] Summary
> A co-design framework optimizes soft gripper stiffness distribution and grasping poses using a neural physics surrogate model trained on high-fidelity simulation data, yielding an approximately three orders of magnitude speedup in optimization and demonstrating improved grasping performance for diverse objects in both simulated and real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Designing soft grippers and determining optimal grasping poses for diverse objects is challenging due to the need for specific stiffness distributions and robust load-bearing capabilities.
- Traditional co-design approaches are computationally expensive, sensitive to initialization, and often specialize on individual objects, limiting their generalizability.
- High-fidelity physical simulations, essential for evaluating soft robot designs, incur significant computational costs, hindering efficient iterative optimization.

## Method

- A data-driven ==co-design== framework jointly optimizes soft gripper stiffness distribution and grasping poses using a ==gradient-based== approach.
- A ==neural physics== model is trained on high-fidelity simulation data to serve as a fast, differentiable surrogate for physical dynamics, enabling efficient optimization.
- The approach utilizes a monolithic, 3D-printable soft finger with flexure joints and a novel ==uniform-pressure tendon routing== method to achieve controlled, conforming deformation.

## Results

- The neural physics-based optimization achieved an approximately **three orders of magnitude** speedup compared to direct differentiable simulation, with the surrogate model converging within an hour on a single GPU.
- Co-designed grippers demonstrated consistently higher grasp success rates in simulation, achieving **92.1%** for light in-domain objects compared to **79.0%** for soft and **60.5%** for semi-rigid baselines.
- Hardware experiments confirmed the optimized grippers outperformed rigid and super-soft baselines, particularly excelling with heavier and irregular objects, demonstrating successful sim-to-real transfer of design and performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Neural physics models can provide computationally efficient and differentiable surrogates, accelerating soft gripper co-design optimization by approximately three orders of magnitude compared to direct simulation.
> - Jointly optimizing gripper stiffness and grasping poses across a diverse set of objects fosters more generalized designs and improves overall convergence by mitigating local minima.
> - Effective soft gripper designs often feature non-uniform stiffness distributions, such as a relatively rigid tip, which enhance load-bearing and grasping stability for varied objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation heavily relies on both sophisticated control systems and effective end-effector designs. Soft grippers represent a significant advancement in this domain due to their inherent compliance and adaptability, which enable safer human-robot interaction and more robust grasping of objects with diverse geometries compared to rigid grippers. However, the design of soft grippers and the determination of optimal grasping poses present considerable challenges. An overly compliant gripper may lack the load-bearing capacity required for practical manipulation tasks, highlighting the necessity of appropriate stiffness distribution.

This research addresses the intricate challenge of co-design, which involves the joint optimization of a robot's physical morphology (design) and its operational control policy (e.g., grasping pose). Previous work in co-adaptation has explored parallel updates of design and control, sequential optimization, and bi-level optimization frameworks. These approaches, however, frequently encounter hurdles such as high-dimensional, nonlinear search spaces, significant computational costs, sensitivity to initialization, and a tendency to specialize on specific objects or tasks, thereby limiting their broader applicability. Differentiable simulations offer a pathway for co-design by embedding both morphology and controller parameters within a unified framework, but they demand smooth, tractable gradients and can be sensitive to initial conditions. Sampling-based methods, including Bayesian optimization, evolutionary strategies, and reinforcement learning-driven co-evolution, offer broader exploration but often incur substantial computational expense. Generative design and Graph Neural Network (GNN)-based simulators also contribute to computational design but can be computationally intensive.

This paper positions its contribution as a data-driven co-design framework that seeks to overcome these limitations by utilizing a neural physics model trained in simulation. By generating a fast, differentiable surrogate for physical simulation, the authors aim to enable efficient gradient-based optimization of soft gripper stiffness distribution and grasping poses. The work specifically focuses on a monolithic, 3D-printable soft finger with flexure joints and a novel uniform-pressure tendon routing method, contributing to the advancements in soft robot fabrication and stiffness design, which often still rely on heuristic parameters. Thus, this research aims to provide a more efficient and generalizable approach to co-design in soft robotics, bridging the gap between high-fidelity simulation and practical hardware realization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged interdependence between the physical design of a robotic end-effector and its operational control policy, particularly in the context of soft grippers. While soft grippers offer advantages in adaptability and safety, their effective design and the determination of optimal grasping poses for varied tasks remain complex. A gripper that is excessively compliant may not reliably perform manipulation tasks requiring load-bearing capabilities. The authors hypothesize that jointly optimizing gripper design and control policies can yield superior task performance and potentially unlock novel robot morphologies.

However, existing co-design approaches face significant challenges:
- **High-Dimensional and Nonlinear Search Space**: The combined design and control parameter space is vast and complex.
- **Different Timescales**: Design changes are typically static (manufacturing time), while control updates are dynamic (real-time operation), complicating integrated optimization.
- **Computational Cost**: High-fidelity physical simulations, often required for evaluating designs, are computationally expensive.
- **Specialization**: Many prior methods tend to specialize on individual objects or tasks, limiting their generalization to diverse scenarios.
- **Gradient Sensitivity**: Differentiable simulations, while promising, can be sensitive to initialization and require smooth gradients that are not always available for complex contact dynamics.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of this research are:
1. **Develop a Co-Design Framework**: To create a data-driven framework that jointly optimizes the spatial stiffness distribution of a soft gripper and its grasping pose.
2. **Leverage Neural Physics**: To train a neural network surrogate model on high-fidelity simulation data to approximate forward physics, thereby providing a fast and differentiable evaluator for the optimization process. This surrogate is intended to facilitate efficient gradient-based design iterations.
3. **Introduce a Specific Soft Finger Design**: To propose and implement a monolithic, 3D-printable soft finger utilizing flexure joints.
4. **Formulate a Uniform-Pressure Tendon Model**: To derive and apply a uniform-pressure tendon routing method for the flexure-based soft finger, aiming to enhance deformation behavior and object conformity during grasping.
5. **Demonstrate Improved Performance**: To validate that the co-designed grippers, resulting from this framework, exhibit superior success rates in grasping tasks compared to baseline rigid and overly compliant designs, through both simulation and hardware experiments.
6. **Ensure Adaptability and Efficiency**: To ensure the framework maintains adaptability across diverse objects and behaviors while being computationally efficient, thus overcoming limitations of previous co-design methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a data-driven co-design framework that integrates simulation, neural network surrogates, and optimization to develop optimized soft grippers.

#### 3.1. Soft Finger Design and Tendon Model

The gripper finger is designed as a monolithic, 3D-printable structure featuring flexure joints—thin beams connecting thicker segment blocks. Actuation is tendon-driven, allowing motors to be located away from the gripper, contributing to a lightweight design.

- **Tendon Model in Simulation**: A soft finger simulator is developed in Nvidia Warp using the Finite Element Method (FEM). Tendons are routed through a custom tetrahedral mesh. To simplify and accelerate simulation, an inextensible tendon is approximated by applying uniform tension force as concentrated point forces at the mesh's surface waypoints (Equation 1). This "point-force" approximation preserves mechanical effects while maintaining simulation speed and stability.
- **Uniform Pressure Tendon Waypoint Distribution**: To prevent undesirable finger-tip curling and ensure the full finger conforms to object surfaces, a uniform pressure model is adopted. This is achieved by distributing tendon waypoints such that the bending moment along the finger is uniform and constant. The height of the tendon waypoint \(h_i\) relative to its flexure joint follows a quadratic relationship with its distance from the base \(l_i\), specifically \(h_i = H (1 - \frac{l_i}{L})^2\), where H is the base height and L is the total finger length (Equation 3). The finger blocks are cut into a trapezoid shape to minimize torque transfer from the object to the tendon beyond its intended path.

#### 3.2. System Architecture and Optimization (Figure 3)

The framework consists of three main stages: data generation, neural physics training, and co-optimization.

- **Data Generation and Training**:
 - **Objects and Parameters**: Data is generated using objects from the YCB dataset. For each object, various block stiffness values (Young's modulus from 0.7 MPa to 24 MPa) and initial gripper poses are sampled. Two fingers comprise 22 blocks, leading to a high-dimensional design space.
 - **Pose Augmentation**: Initial candidate poses are generated using Anygrasp. To increase diversity and ensure validity for the specific gripper, these poses are augmented through an optimization process using a rigid counterpart of the soft gripper in simulation. This involves optimizing an 8-dimensional pose parameter \([t_x, t_y, t_z, r_r, r_p, r_y, s_1, s_2]\) by minimizing a loss function \(L_{init}\) (Equation 4) that penalizes penetration while encouraging proximity to the object and ground, based on the Signed Distance Function (SDF).
 - **Forward Simulation**: Each sampled setup undergoes a fixed set of motions: tendons tighten with a constant force, and fingers lift a fixed distance.
 - **Data Collection**: The simulation records the object's ending body force, pose displacement, and ground collision status.
 - **Neural Network Surrogate (Neural Physics)**: A neural network is trained to approximate this forward physics mapping. Inputs include partial point clouds (processed by a 3-layer PointNet for feature extraction), object center of mass, density, and the 22D stiffness vector. The PointNet output is fed into a 5-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). The network minimizes the L1 loss between its predictions and the simulation's output (body force, pose, collision). This surrogate provides a fast, differentiable, and smooth approximation of the physical simulation.

- **Co-design of Stiffness and Pose (Algorithm 1)**:
 - The neural physics model is embedded in an end-to-end optimization loop to jointly optimize gripper stiffness and grasping pose.
 - **Objective Function**: The optimization loss \(L_{opt}\) (Equation 5) combines terms to maximize object body force (load-bearing), minimize pose displacement (stability), and specifically penalize downward components of force and displacement, as well as ground collision (indicating a dropped object).
 - **Optimization Algorithm**: An iterative gradient-descent approach is used. For each object, a set of candidate poses is maintained. In each iteration, the neural surrogate evaluates the loss for all pose-stiffness pairs. The top B (B=5) lowest losses for each object are considered. The total loss is the sum of these B losses across all objects. Gradient descent is then applied to update the stiffness parameters \(k\). The process continues until the top-B losses for each object converge, at which point each object's candidate pool is reduced to its single best pose, and optimization continues until overall stabilization. This strategy allows for joint optimization across a set of objects.

#### 3.3. Hardware Realization

Optimized designs from simulation are realized using 3D printing with NinjaFlex Cheetah (TPU 95A). Different stiffnesses are achieved by varying structural parameters:
- **Flexure Blocks**: Wall loops and shell layers are adjusted.
- **Segment Blocks**: Infill percentage and infill printing directions are modified.
Young's modulus for these printed variations is empirically measured using 3-point bending tests for flexure blocks and indentation tests for segment blocks. The simulated stiffness values are linearly scaled to these empirical moduli for physical implementation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world hardware experiments, demonstrating the efficacy of the co-design framework.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Efficiency**: Data generation for 80,000 valid data points took approximately one day on four 3090 GPUs. Training the neural network surrogate converged within one hour on a single 3090 GPU. The high fidelity of the surrogate was confirmed, with an 80k data model achieving a loss of 0.1411 and a corresponding success rate of 79.0%, outperforming a 20k data model with a loss of 0.1919 and a success rate of 69.6%.
- **Performance of Jointly Optimized Gripper**:
 - Table 1 illustrates that the jointly optimized gripper consistently achieved higher grasp success rates compared to baseline soft and semi-rigid grippers, across both in-domain and out-of-domain objects, and for both light (2 kg/m³) and heavy (8 kg/m³) object categories.
 - For light in-domain objects, the jointly optimized gripper achieved a 92.1% success rate with pose sampling, significantly surpassing the soft (79.0%) and semi-rigid (60.5%) grippers.
 - For heavy in-domain objects, it achieved 89.5% with pose sampling, compared to soft (65.8%) and semi-rigid (39.5%).
 - Out-of-domain objects showed similar trends, although with generally lower overall success rates, indicating some drop in generalization for significantly different object geometries and sizes.
 - The use of pose sampling, where the best pose is selected from candidates using the neural network surrogate, consistently improved success rates for all gripper types.
- **Optimization Efficiency and Gradient Properties**:
 - The neural physics-based optimization demonstrated a speedup of approximately three orders of magnitude compared to direct differentiable simulation (Figure 4d).
 - The optimization process exhibited smoother gradients and smaller gradient norms, contributing to more stable convergence.
 - Joint optimization, by considering multiple objects simultaneously, helped mitigate the risk of individual objects getting trapped in local minima, leading to better overall convergence and a lower average loss across the dataset (Figure 4a-c).
 - An observation from the optimized gripper designs was the prevalence of at least one relatively rigid tip, functionally resembling the nails of primates (Figure 3). Optimized log(E) values (Table 4) indicated asymmetric stiffness distributions.

#### 4.2. Hardware Realization

- Experimental characterization of 3D-printed flexure and segment blocks (Figure 5b, c) showed that varying structural parameters (wall loops, shell layers, infill percentage, and direction) effectively enabled control over the Young's modulus, providing a practical method to map simulated stiffness distributions to physical hardware.

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiments

- **Setup**: Experiments utilized a 7-DoF Franka arm with two Intel RealSense D435 cameras for object perception. Anygrasp generated candidate grasp poses, and the neural network surrogate selected the optimal pose for a given design. Tendon actuation was controlled by a Dynamixel motor.
- **Performance Comparison**: Tested on 10 objects with diverse geometries and masses (Table 2), including additional soft/deformable objects (Table 5).
 - **Rigid Grippers**: Performed better with heavier objects due to inherent stiffness but struggled with irregular geometries due to limited contact surfaces, leading to grasp failures.
 - **Super-Soft Grippers**: Exhibited superior adaptability to irregular contours but showed significantly decreased success rates when handling heavier objects (mass > 100g) due to insufficient load-bearing capacity.
 - **Optimized Grippers**: Notably outperformed both rigid and super-soft grippers, particularly excelling with heavier objects and those with irregular geometries. For objects amenable to deformation-based grasping, the optimized gripper achieved higher success rates by conforming effectively to object contours. For example, for a 121g object, the optimized gripper achieved 9/10 success rate, compared to 5/10 for rigid and 1/10 for soft. For a 41g irregular object, it achieved 5/10, compared to 0/10 for rigid and 6/10 for soft.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer**: For overlapping objects tested in both simulation and real-world (Table 3b), the performance was comparable, indicating effective transfer of optimized designs from simulation to hardware (e.g., Pear: Sim 8/10, Real 7/10; Mustard Bottle: Sim 10/10, Real 9/10).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the co-design of soft robotic grippers by introducing a novel, data-driven framework. The development of a uniform-pressure tendon routing model for flexure-based soft fingers addresses a critical aspect of soft gripper design, enabling more effective object conformity and grasping.

The core contribution lies in the integration of a neural physics surrogate model, trained on high-fidelity simulation data, within an end-to-end optimization loop. This approach offers several notable impacts:
- **Computational Efficiency**: The neural surrogate significantly accelerates the design iteration process, achieving approximately three orders of magnitude speedup compared to direct differentiable simulation. This allows for practical gradient-based optimization in a domain traditionally constrained by the high computational cost of physical simulations.
- **Enhanced Performance and Generalization**: The co-designed grippers demonstrate superior performance in terms of load-bearing capacity, grasp stability, and adaptability across a diverse range of objects with varying geometries and masses. This improvement is observed in both simulated and real-world experiments, indicating the framework's capability to yield robust and generalizable designs. Joint optimization, in particular, contributes to finding designs that perform well across a dataset of objects, mitigating specialization.
- **Bridging Simulation and Reality**: The successful sim-to-real transfer of optimized stiffness distributions and grasping performance highlights the fidelity of the neural physics model and the practicality of the hardware realization methodology using 3D printing with variable structural parameters.
- **Design Insights**: The optimization process provides insights into effective soft gripper morphologies, such as the observed utility of relatively rigid tips and asymmetric stiffness distributions, which could inform future end-effector designs.
- **Accelerated Development Cycle**: By streamlining the design and optimization process, the framework has the potential to significantly accelerate the manufacturing and iteration cycle for soft robotic systems. This can lead to faster innovation and deployment of soft robots in various applications requiring sensitive and adaptable manipulation.

Looking forward, the surrogate-driven pipeline has the potential for broader applicability, including richer parameterizations (e.g., co-optimizing shape or topology, though currently limited to stiffness distribution over fixed geometry) and longer-horizon manipulation tasks. This research contributes to a pathway for discovering new, more generalizable, and high-performing soft robot designs, ultimately impacting fields such as manufacturing, assistive robotics, and exploration where robust and adaptable manipulation is crucial.
