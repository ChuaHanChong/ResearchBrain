# DynaMimicGen: A Data Generation Framework for Robot Learning of Dynamic Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> DynaMimicGen (D-MG) introduces a data generation framework for robot learning of dynamic tasks, producing large-scale, diverse datasets from as few as one or two human demonstrations. It leverages Dynamic Movement Primitives and continuous object pose monitoring to synthesize adaptable Cartesian trajectories, leading to improved policy success rates up to 97.33% in simulation and 96.0% in real-world for tasks like Lift, while significantly reducing human data collection effort.

## Problem

- Learning from Demonstration (LfD) and Imitation Learning (IL) for robots demand large, diverse datasets for effective generalization.
- Collecting extensive human demonstrations is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and often impractical for complex, dynamic real-world scenarios.
- Existing synthetic data generation methods for IL primarily assume quasi-static environments, limiting their applicability to tasks where object positions or orientations change during execution.

## Method

- **DynaMimicGen** (D-MG) parses minimal human demonstrations into ==object-centric subtask segments==.
- It employs ==Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs)== to learn and generalize each subtask's trajectory from a single selected demonstration segment.
- The framework continuously monitors ==object poses== during trajectory execution, adapting DMP goals in real-time to new scene configurations and introducing ==structured environmental perturbations== to generate dynamic, diverse data.

## Results

- D-MG achieved comparable or superior data generation rates (DGRs) using **1-2** human demonstrations compared to a baseline method requiring **10**, significantly reducing human supervision.
- Datasets generated with D-MG led to substantial improvements in downstream policy performance, with Diffusion Policy reaching **97.33%** success in the 'Square' task and Behavior Cloning achieving **96.0%** in the 'Lift' task on a real robot.
- Ablation studies confirmed that dynamic scene perturbations are critical for robust policy learning, with image-based agent performance dropping significantly (e.g., from **83.33%** to **45.33%** for DP in Stack D0) when these dynamic variations were disabled.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scalable robot data generation from minimal human input (1-2 demonstrations) is achievable by leveraging DMPs to generalize object-centric subtask trajectories.
> - Integrating dynamic environmental perturbations during data generation is crucial for creating robust and generalizable policies, especially for image-based observations in evolving scenes.
> - Continuous monitoring and adaptation of robot trajectories based on real-time object poses enables effective data synthesis for dynamic tasks, a capability largely absent in prior imitation learning data generation frameworks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning, particularly through Learning from Demonstration (LfD) and Imitation Learning (IL), seeks to enable robots to acquire new skills from human examples. This approach aims to make robot programming more accessible and intuitive. However, a significant challenge in traditional IL is the requirement for large, diverse datasets of human demonstrations to ensure policies generalize effectively across different environments, object instances, and robot platforms. Collecting such datasets is often time-consuming, labor-intensive, and impractical, especially for complex real-world scenarios involving dynamic elements.

Prior efforts to address this data scarcity have focused on scalable synthetic data generation. Methods like MimicGen and DexMimicGen segment human trajectories into object-centric subtasks and replay them with transformations to augment data. Similarly, DemoGen and DreMa reconfigure static scenes. However, a common limitation of these approaches is their assumption of quasi-static environments, where object poses are fixed or only observed at the beginning of a subtask. This restricts their applicability to dynamic task settings where object positions or orientations can change during task execution.

DynaMimicGen (D-MG) positions itself by addressing this limitation, proposing a framework for generating large-scale, diverse robotic datasets that explicitly supports dynamic and evolving environments. It builds upon Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs), which have a long history in imitation learning for encoding and generalizing motions to new start and goal configurations. While DMPs are known for their adaptability, their use for synthesizing large-scale manipulation datasets, especially in dynamic contexts, has not been thoroughly explored in prior work. D-MG aims to bridge the gap between minimal human supervision, DMP-based trajectory generalization, and data generation for deep learning policies capable of operating in dynamic, unstructured settings. It aligns with the broader trend in large-scale imitation learning that emphasizes data diversity and volume as critical for generalization, as demonstrated by initiatives like RoboNet, RT-1, and Open X-Embodiment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind DynaMimicGen (D-MG) stems from the recognition that learning robust robot manipulation policies typically demands extensive and diverse datasets. The collection of these datasets through human demonstrations presents several practical impediments: it is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and often infeasible for dynamic or continuously changing environments. Existing synthetic data generation methods for imitation learning typically assume static scenes, which limits their applicability to real-world scenarios characterized by unpredictable environmental changes.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of the D-MG framework are:

1. **Scalable Data Generation from Minimal Input:** To develop a framework capable of generating large-scale robot manipulation datasets from a minimal number of human demonstrations, ideally as few as one or two. This aims to substantially reduce the human effort and time required for data collection, thereby making robot learning more efficient and accessible. The motivation is to overcome the data scarcity problem that hinders the generalization capabilities of imitation learning policies.

2. **Support for Dynamic Task Settings:** To enable data generation in environments where object positions, robot states, or scene geometry can change dynamically during task execution. This objective directly addresses a limitation of prior work, which largely assumes quasi-static conditions. The motivation is to synthesize datasets that allow trained policies to generalize effectively during deployment in real-world scenarios, where disturbances and variability are inherent.

3. **Generation of High-Quality Data for Imitation Learning Agents:** To produce smooth, realistic, and task-consistent Cartesian trajectories that are suitable for training proficient imitation learning agents. The generated data should enable these agents to achieve strong performance across diverse scene configurations and under unpredictable environment changes, even for tasks involving long-horizon and contact-rich interactions. The motivation is to enhance the robustness and generalization capabilities of learned policies.

To achieve these objectives, D-MG is designed to segment initial human demonstrations into object-centric subtasks and then leverage Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) to adapt and generalize these behaviors. A critical aspect is the continuous monitoring of object poses during trajectory synthesis, allowing for real-time adaptation to changing goal configurations and the introduction of structured environmental perturbations to increase dataset diversity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DynaMimicGen (D-MG) is a data generation framework designed to synthesize large-scale robotic manipulation datasets from minimal human demonstrations, particularly for dynamic environments. The methodology is structured around several sequential steps, underpinned by specific assumptions.

**Problem Formulation and Assumptions:**
The system frames each robot manipulation task as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) and aims to generate a dataset suitable for learning a policy. The data generation process assumes:
1. **Absolute End-Effector Pose-Action Space:** The robot's action space (A) is defined by absolute end-effector pose commands and a binary gripper open/close command. This is crucial for training DMPs, which require full trajectory information for consistent generalization.
2. **Known Sequence of Object-Centric Subtasks:** Tasks are assumed to consist of a predefined, ordered sequence of object-centric subtasks, where each subtask's manipulation is relative to a single object's coordinate frame (e.g., opening a drawer, grasping a mug). This simplifies task planning and segmentation.
3. **Observable Object Poses at Each Time-step:** The pose of the relevant object for a subtask must be observable at every time step during data collection. This enables D-MG to continuously adapt trajectories to dynamic changes in the environment.

**DynaMimicGen System Pipeline (Figure 1):**
The D-MG pipeline initiates by processing a minimal set of human demonstrations (N ≥ 1) for a given task.

1. **Parsing Human Demonstrations into Object-Centric Segments (Section 4.1):**
 - Each human demonstration trajectory (τ) is first decomposed into contiguous segments (τ₁, τ₂..., τ_M), where each segment corresponds to a specific object-centric subtask.
 - The task is defined as a sequence of subtasks S₁(o_S₁), S₂(o_S₂)..., S_M(o_S_M), with each subtask referencing a particular object. For instance, "placing a mug in a drawer" can be broken down into opening the drawer (relative to drawer), grasping the mug (relative to mug), placing the mug (relative to drawer), and closing the drawer (relative to drawer).
 - Subtask boundaries are detected using either automated metrics (e.g., robot and object state signals in simulation) or manual annotation for real-world scenarios.

2. **Generating a DMP for Each Sub-Task (Section 4.2):**
 - Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) are employed to represent and generalize robot trajectories. A DMP models motion as a second-order dynamical system with a nonlinear forcing term, allowing reproduction of complex trajectories while maintaining stability and adapting to new start and goal configurations.
 - The core DMP formulation is given by: `¨y(t) = α_y (β_y (g − y(t)) − ˙y(t)) + f (x)`, where `y(t)` is the state, `g` is the goal, and `f(x)` is a forcing term determined by Gaussian basis functions and learned weights (ωᵢ).
 - The weights (ωᵢ) for each DMP are learned from a *single* selected demonstration segment using Locally Weighted Regression (LWR). This involves inverting the DMP equation to compute the demonstrated forcing term (`f_d(t)`) and then minimizing a locally weighted quadratic error function.

3. **Adapting the Source Sub-task Segment (Section 4.3):**
 - Each sub-task segment, interpreted as a sequence of target end-effector poses (T_W^k), is adapted to a new scene.
 - The new start pose for the DMP is the robot's current configuration in the new scene.
 - To determine the goal pose, the end-effector's target pose relative to the manipulated object's frame in the original demonstration (T_obj^target) is first computed. This object-relative target pose is then transformed into the world frame of the *new* scene using the *current* object pose in that environment (T_W^obj). This yields the updated target end-effector pose for the DMP in the new scene.

4. **Executing the Updated Segment and Dynamic Dataset Generation (Section 4.4, 5.2):**
 - D-MG continuously monitors object positions throughout trajectory execution.
 - At each timestep, the framework samples the updated target pose (derived from the adapted DMP), pairs it with the corresponding gripper action from the original demonstration, and issues control commands via an end-effector controller.
 - This process repeats for all task segments. Only successful rollouts (trajectories that complete the task) are retained for the generated dataset (D_gen).
 - To introduce diversity beyond initial object pose variations, D-MG incorporates structured environmental perturbations during trajectory generation. This involves dynamically perturbing the positions of target objects (e.g., randomized displacements during a pick subtask) while the robot is executing the motion. This forces the data-generating policy to adjust mid-execution, simulating real-world uncertainties.

**Imitation Learning Algorithms:**
The generated datasets are used to train imitation learning policies. The paper evaluates two primary algorithms:
- **Diffusion Policy (DP):** Trained on a randomly sampled subset of 200 trajectories.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) with an RNN-based policy:** Trained on the full set of 1000 generated trajectories.
Policies are evaluated by comparing their performance against MimicGen (MG). For fair comparison, MG-generated delta trajectories are reprocessed into absolute coordinates to align with D-MG's absolute trajectory requirement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to evaluate D-MG's versatility in data generation, its performance against baselines in quasi-static and dynamic settings, and the efficacy of the generated data for training imitation learning agents in both simulation and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. Data Generation Performances: Data Generation Rates in Quasi-Static Task Settings

D-MG was compared to MimicGen (MG) in terms of Data Generation Rate (DGR), which is the success rate of the data generation process.
- **Reduced Supervision:** D-MG achieved comparable or superior DGRs using only one (D-MG1) or two (D-MG2) human demonstrations, whereas MG required ten (MG10). This indicates a significant reduction in human supervision.
- **Task Performance:**
 - **Stack:** D-MG1/2 maintained DGRs around 95% across D0 and D1 distributions, comparable to MG10 (94.3% D0, 90.0% D1).
 - **Square (Contact-Rich):** D-MG1 initially lagged MG10 (53.9% vs. 73.7% for D0), but D-MG2, with an additional demonstration and orientation-based selection, surpassed MG10 (81.4% vs. 73.7% for D0, 82.8% vs. 48.9% for D1, 63.2% vs. 31.8% for D2). This highlights the benefit of a second demonstration for complex tasks.
 - **MugCleanup (Long-Horizon):** D-MG1/2 achieved substantially higher DGRs than MG10 (e.g., 83.7%/84.0% vs. 29.5% for D0; 65.0%/64.7% vs. 17.0% for D1).
- **Conclusion:** D-MG demonstrated the ability to scale high-quality demonstration synthesis from sparse expert demonstrations, outperforming MG, particularly in tasks requiring trajectory adaptability and temporal coherence.

#### 4.1.2. Data Generation Rates in Dynamic Task Settings

D-MG's ability to generate successful demonstrations under dynamic perturbations was assessed, with no direct comparison to MG possible as MG does not support dynamic feedback.
- **Performance Reduction:** Introducing dynamic perturbations generally led to a reduction in DGRs compared to static environments (e.g., Stack D0: 95.0% static vs. 68.7% dynamic for D-MG1).
- **Resilience:** Despite the drop, D-MG consistently generated successful demonstrations across diverse tasks and perturbation regimes. DGRs remained robust across D0, D1, and D2 distributions, indicating the method's ability to maintain performance even with increased scene variability.
- **Impact of Task Complexity:** Performance drops were more pronounced in tasks requiring fine-grained control under non-stationary conditions, such as the contact-rich Square task.
- **Conclusion:** D-MG demonstrated resilience in dynamic settings, a capability not addressed by existing imitation-based data generation frameworks, suggesting its practicality for real-world deployment.

#### 4.2. Imitation Learning Training Performances

Agents (Diffusion Policy - DP, Behavior Cloning - BC) were trained on D-MG generated datasets (1000 trajectories) and compared against those trained on MG datasets (1000 trajectories from 10 demos) and original source demonstrations (1-2 demos).

#### 4.2.1. Agents Performance (Image-based observations)

- **Significant Policy Improvement:** Datasets generated with D-MG led to substantial improvements in downstream policy performance compared to training solely on source demonstrations. For instance, in Stack, source performance was ~1.33%, which increased to 83.33% (DP) and 76.00% (BC) on D0 with D-MG data.
- **Superiority over MimicGen:** D-MG consistently produced more effective policies across all tasks and data distributions, achieving higher and more stable success rates than MG.
 - **Stack:** D-MG policies outperformed MG, e.g., DP D0: 83.33% vs. 74.00%; BC D0: 76.00% vs. 72.67%.
 - **Square:** D-MG showed notable improvements, e.g., DP D0: 86.67% vs. 75.33%; BC D0: 97.33% vs. 88.67%.
 - **MugCleanup:** D-MG policies were significantly better, e.g., DP D0: 90.00% vs. 61.33%; BC D0: 79.33% vs. 65.33%.
- **Conclusion:** D-MG's dynamic data generation strategy, which integrates controlled perturbations and adaptive trajectory refinements, produced more diverse and informative demonstrations, leading to more robust and generalizable policies with significantly less human supervision.

#### 4.2.2. Ablation Study (Impact of Dynamic Scene Perturbations)

The study investigated the effect of disabling dynamic scene variations during D-MG data generation.
- **Reduced Performance without Dynamic Perturbations:** Datasets generated by D-MG without dynamic scene variations yielded markedly lower success rates across all tasks and data distributions for image-based agents.
 - **Stack:** D-MG (no dynamic) D0: DP 45.33% vs. MG 74.00%; BC 32.00% vs. MG 72.67%.
 - **Square:** D-MG (no dynamic) D0: DP 76.67% vs. MG 75.33%; BC 94.00% vs. MG 88.67%. (Note: For Square D0, D-MG without dynamics was comparable to MG or slightly higher in BC, but significantly lower in D1 and D2).
 - **MugCleanup:** D-MG (no dynamic) D0: DP 81.33% vs. MG 61.33%; BC 78.00% vs. MG 65.33%.
- **Role of Variability:** This confirmed that the dynamic perturbations introduced by D-MG are critical for diversifying training trajectories and enabling robust policy learning, especially for image-based observations where policies must learn rich visual representations.
- **Low-Dimensional (Low-Dim) Results (Appendix E):** With low-dimensional state observations, the performance gains of D-MG over MG were still present in many cases, but the relative magnitude was smaller. Low-dim agents, operating on structured features, are inherently less dependent on the variability introduced by dynamic data generation. However, D-MG still generally achieved superior success rates, especially with Diffusion Policy. For Behavior Cloning, D-MG's advantage was more pronounced when dynamic perturbations were enabled, with MG sometimes achieving higher performance when D-MG's dynamic variations were disabled, indicating D-MG's benefits rely on these perturbations.

#### 4.3. Experimental Validation (Real-World Robot)

D-MG was validated on a Franka Emika Panda robot for Lift, Stack, and Cleanup tasks.
- **High Generation Success Rates:** D-MG achieved high trajectory generation success rates (Lift: 94.0%, Stack: 88.0%, Cleanup: 96.0%) in real-world dynamic scenes where a human operator perturbed object positions. These rates were comparable to simulation.
- **Policy Transfer:** Behavior Cloning (BC) agents trained on D-MG-generated datasets showed promising success rates on the physical robot (Lift: 86.0%, Stack: 32.0%, Cleanup: 26.0%). The lower policy success rates, compared to generation rates, were attributed to limited dataset size (50 trajectories per task variant) in real-world settings. A follow-up experiment showed training with 200 trajectories for the Lift task increased success to 96.0%.
- **Sub-task Analysis (Appendix F.6):** Detailed sub-task success rates highlighted performance drops in complex manipulation phases (e.g., placing the cube in Stack, grasping and placing in Cleanup), often due to vision system uncertainties, timing issues, or object being moved outside training space.
- **Conclusion:** D-MG demonstrated practical effectiveness for semi-automatic dataset generation in dynamic real-world conditions, providing a foundation for scalable, hardware-agnostic robotic learning from minimal human supervision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DynaMimicGen (D-MG) represents a notable contribution to the field of robot learning by addressing several critical limitations of existing imitation learning and data generation methodologies. Its primary significance lies in enabling the scalable generation of diverse, high-quality robot manipulation datasets from minimal human input, while uniquely supporting dynamic task environments.

The potential impacts of D-MG include:

1. **Reduced Data Collection Burden:** D-MG significantly lowers the barrier to entry for robot learning by demonstrating that proficient manipulation policies can be trained from as few as one or two human demonstrations, compared to the ten or more typically required by prior methods like MimicGen. This drastically reduces the time, labor, and expertise needed for data collection, making advanced robot skills more accessible to a wider range of users and applications.

2. **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Generalization in Dynamic Settings:** A key innovation of D-MG is its capability to generate data in dynamic environments, where object poses or scene configurations change during task execution. By leveraging Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) and continuously monitoring the environment to adapt trajectories in real-time, D-MG synthesizes datasets that expose learning agents to a broader range of conditions than static methods. This leads to policies that exhibit stronger robustness to environmental perturbations, sensor noise, and unexpected interactions, thereby improving their generalization capabilities beyond the specific conditions seen during initial demonstrations or even static synthetic data generation. This is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured, real-world scenarios.

3. **Improved Downstream Policy Learning:** The research demonstrates that agents trained on D-MG generated datasets consistently achieve higher success rates and greater stability compared to those trained on data from alternative methods. The ablation study further reinforces that the structured variability introduced by D-MG's dynamic scene perturbations is a critical factor for enabling robust and generalizable imitation learning, particularly for visuomotor policies that rely on high-dimensional visual inputs. This suggests that D-MG's approach creates more informative and policy-relevant training data.

4. **Practical Applicability in Real-World Robotics:** Experimental validation on a physical robotic platform (Franka Emika Panda) for dynamically varying tasks confirms D-MG's practical utility beyond simulation. While preliminary, these real-world results demonstrate the framework's potential to accelerate data-driven robotic learning pipelines in physical environments, providing a scalable and hardware-agnostic tool for acquiring complex manipulation skills with reduced human involvement. The framework's compatibility with existing sim-to-real techniques also suggests a clear path towards broader real-world deployment.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite its contributions, D-MG has several identified limitations:
- It assumes a known and fixed sequence of object-centric subtasks, limiting its flexibility for highly unstructured tasks.
- It relies on accurate object pose estimates at every time-step during data generation, which can be challenging in real-world scenarios with occlusions or sensor noise.
- Currently, it supports only one reference object per subtask and is restricted to single-arm manipulation, which limits its application to multi-object or bimanual tasks.
- While robust, DMPs have fixed lengths, meaning they may not recover if dynamic perturbations occur too late in a subtask, leading to failure.
- Real-world validation has been limited to a few representative tasks.

Future work aims to extend D-MG to more complex dynamic scenarios, including obstacle-rich and multi-object environments, by integrating obstacle-awareness and physics-informed motion planning. Coupling D-MG with vision-language or foundation models is also suggested to enhance its generalization across tasks, embodiments, and environments, paving the way for more autonomous data generation systems.
