# Vision-Guided Outdoor Flight and Obstacle Evasion via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A vision-guided reinforcement learning policy enables quadcopters to perform autonomous outdoor flight and obstacle evasion, achieving zero-shot transfer from simulation to real-world platforms, including a commercial drone 10 times heavier than its training counterpart. The system directly uses high-level velocity commands, ensuring compatibility with off-the-shelf drone APIs and relying solely on onboard stereo-vision depth and VIO.

## Problem

- Current autonomous quadcopters struggle with robust navigation in complex, unknown, and GNSS-denied outdoor environments.
- Traditional modular navigation systems face compounding errors and high computational overhead, limiting real-time deployment.
- Existing learning-based policies often produce low-level controls highly coupled to specific drone dynamics, hindering zero-shot transfer and direct use with commercial drone APIs.

## Method

- A two-stage hybrid training approach combining initial privileged learning with pre-computed optimal trajectories and subsequent curriculum-based ==reinforcement learning==.
- A modular neural network architecture that integrates a pre-trained autoencoder for robust depth perception with an LSTM for end-to-end planning and control.
- The policy generates high-level velocity commands (horizontal, vertical, yaw angular) designed for direct compatibility with commercial drone APIs.

## Results

- Achieved high success rates in simulation (up to **96.7%** at **4.0** m/s) in diverse, randomly generated obstacle courses.
- Demonstrated successful zero-shot transfer to a real DJI Matrice 300 RTK (**6.3** kg, ~**10x** heavier than simulated drone) in outdoor environments, navigating obstacles and contending with crosswinds up to 14 kph.
- Outperformed baseline methods in robustness to out-of-distribution drone dynamics, maintaining smoother trajectories and higher success rates across varying drone scales.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining privileged learning with curriculum-based fine-tuning provides an effective balance for efficient bootstrapping and robust generalization in complex environments.
> - Reward shaping, including penalties for acceleration and jerk, is critical for promoting smooth, mechanically sound trajectories that transfer well to real-world dynamics.
> - Comprehensive domain randomization on state, depth images, actions, and drone dynamics effectively mitigates the sim-to-real gap, enabling zero-shot transfer to significantly different hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous aerial robotics, particularly involving quadcopters, has seen significant advancements, yet substantial challenges remain, especially in complex, unknown, and Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) or telemetry-denied environments. Quadcopters possess inherent omnidirectional maneuverability, which offers impressive traversal capabilities. However, their widespread application in scenarios such as sub-canopy forest flight, underground mapping, war zones, or industrial inspection is often hindered by the necessity for continuous human pilot control or the limitations of conventional autonomous navigation systems.

Traditional approaches to autonomous navigation typically decompose the task into distinct modules: planning, perception, and control. While these methods have demonstrated success in structured or less dynamic environments, they often encounter difficulties in unknown, obstacle-rich, or dynamic settings. A primary limitation is the potential for compounding errors originating from imperfect state estimation, system latency, and sensor noise across these integrated modules. Furthermore, the computational overhead associated with tasks like map fusion can make traditional methods less efficient for real-time, onboard deployment compared to end-to-end learning-based systems.

In recent years, research has increasingly focused on learning-based methods, particularly end-to-end sensorimotor policies that directly map raw sensor inputs to control actions. These approaches aim to overcome the limitations of modular systems by learning complex, robust behaviors. Within this paradigm, reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning are prominent techniques. Deep RL methods train policies in simulation using physics and graphics engines, while imitation learning often involves distilling an expert policy (generated from privileged information or traditional planners) into a student policy.

A specific challenge within this landscape is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation often struggle to perform robustly in real-world conditions due to discrepancies in dynamics, sensor noise characteristics, and environmental complexities. Various strategies, including domain randomization, have been developed to mitigate this gap. Additionally, many existing learning-based approaches, particularly those using imitation learning, tend to generate policies that are highly tuned to the specific flight dynamics of the drone used in training, often outputting low-level body rates or local trajectories. This specificity limits their direct deployability across different off-the-shelf commercial drone platforms, which typically offer higher-level velocity command APIs.

This research contributes to the existing landscape by addressing the critical need for a vision-guided autonomous navigation policy that is robust, generalizes well from simulation to real-world, and is directly compatible with the velocity command interfaces of diverse commercial drones. The work differentiates itself by using a combination of reinforcement learning and privileged learning to train a policy that achieves zero-shot transfer to drone platforms with significantly different characteristics (e.g., weight) than those encountered during training, without requiring drone-specific tuning. This focus on generalizability and direct deployability on commercial hardware positions the work as an effort to bridge the gap between advanced learning-based autonomy and practical, widespread application.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current quadcopter autonomy in complex and challenging environments. While quadcopters possess inherent agility and maneuverability, their deployment in scenarios lacking reliable GNSS signals, high-bandwidth telemetry, or requiring navigation through unknown, obstacle-rich terrains is often constrained. Existing solutions often necessitate continuous human pilot intervention, which is impractical for extended missions or hazardous zones, or rely on traditional modular systems prone to compounding errors from perception, planning, and control subsystems. Furthermore, many contemporary learning-based autonomous flight systems are designed to output low-level control signals (e.g., body rates or specific trajectories) that are highly coupled to a particular drone's flight dynamics. This coupling prevents direct, zero-shot transfer to off-the-shelf commercial drones, which typically expose only higher-level velocity command APIs. Consequently, a significant barrier exists to the widespread application of advanced autonomous flight capabilities on readily available drone platforms.

Against this backdrop, the research establishes several key objectives:

1. **Develop a Robust Sensorimotor Policy:** To create a neural network policy capable of autonomously navigating a quadcopter through unknown, obstacle-rich outdoor environments to a specified goal point.
2. **Vision-Based Navigation:** To enable autonomous navigation relying solely on onboard sensors, specifically stereo-vision depth data and visual-inertial odometry (VIO), thereby eliminating dependence on external infrastructure like GNSS.
3. **Direct Deployability on Commercial Drones:** To design a policy that outputs high-level velocity commands (horizontal velocity, vertical velocity, yaw angular velocity) that can be directly interfaced with the built-in APIs of off-the-shelf commercial drones, such as those from DJI or AR Parrot, without requiring drone-specific tuning or a model predictive controller.
4. **Achieve Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To ensure the policy trained entirely in simulation can transfer effectively and without re-training to real-world outdoor environments and drone platforms exhibiting dynamics and characteristics substantially different from those in the simulation. This includes generalizing to drones with significant weight disparities (e.g., 10 times heavier) and contending with real-world factors like sensor noise, background clutter, and crosswinds.
5. **Modular Network Architecture:** To employ a modular network design comprising a pre-trained autoencoder for robust depth image perception and an LSTM network for integrated planning and control.
6. **Hybrid Training Paradigm:** To leverage a two-stage training process combining reinforcement learning with privileged learning (using optimal trajectories as a supervisory signal) for initial policy bootstrapping, followed by fine-tuning in a curriculum-based environment to enhance generalization and robustness.
7. **Effective Sim-to-Real Bridging Techniques:** To implement specific techniques, including domain randomization and reward shaping, to explicitly address and mitigate the sim-to-real gap, thereby fostering policy robustness to noise, domain shifts, and variations in system dynamics.

These objectives collectively aim to advance the practicality and accessibility of autonomous drone flight in challenging real-world applications by developing a generalizable, vision-guided system that can operate on readily available hardware.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves a two-stage process for training a neural network policy that enables a drone to navigate obstacles and reach a target using onboard stereo-vision depth and state estimates. The approach is designed for direct deployment onto commercial drones via velocity command APIs.

**A. Model Architecture**

1. **State and Action Space:**
 - **State Vector ($\vec{s}_t$):** Includes relative goal position ($g_x - p_{t,x}$, $g_y - p_{t,y}$, $g_z - p_{t,z}$), current roll ($\phi_t$), pitch ($\psi_t$), and yaw rates ($\omega_t$), difference between heading to goal and current heading ($\Delta\psi$), and a flattened 192x108 depth image ($z_t$).
 - **Action Vector ($\vec{a}_t$):** Consists of horizontal velocity ($v_{t,x}$), vertical velocity ($v_{t,z}$), and yaw angular velocity ($a_{t,\omega}$). These commands are designed for compatibility with off-the-shelf drone APIs.
2. **AutoEncoder Pretraining and Feature Extraction:**
 - A denoising autoencoder is pre-trained using the DIML dataset, which contains depth images from various indoor and outdoor settings. Gaussian noise is injected into training inputs, scaled by per-pixel depth confidence metrics, to enhance robustness to real-world stereo depth measurement noise.
 - The encoder component acts as the perception head, mapping high-dimensional depth images (R$^{192 \times 108}$) to a low-dimensional latent space (R$^{128}$). This pre-training reduces the number of parameters for the main policy and allows for separate tuning of the perception module.
 - For feature extraction, the first seven state values (goal distance and attitude rates) are processed through two fully-connected layers. This output is then concatenated with the depth image encoding and passed through two more fully-connected layers, yielding a R$^{256}$ feature vector for the actor-critic module.
3. **Actor-Critic Networks and Policy Optimizer:**
 - Both actor and critic networks receive the R$^{256}$ feature vector. Each network passes this vector through an LSTM layer with 64-dimensional hidden and memory units.
 - The LSTM outputs are then fed into two fully-connected layers (actor: 128, 32 neurons; critic: 128, 64 neurons) to produce the action ($\pi(s_t)$) and Q-value ($Q_\pi(s_t, a_t)$).
 - Action outputs are normalized to [-1, 1] and scaled by maximum horizontal, vertical, and angular speeds.
 - Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO2) is used to optimize the actor-critic and state mixing networks during training.
4. **Reward Function:**
 - A continuous reward function ($R(s_{t+1}, s_t, a_t)$) is defined by summing empirically weighted terms (Table I) that incentivize desired states or actions. Key terms include: survival, decreasing distance to goal, minimizing heading and Z-position errors, penalizing high yaw rate magnitude, encouraging vertical velocity towards goal, rewarding velocity towards goal, penalizing acceleration and yaw jerk (for smoothness), and penalizing proximity to obstacles.
 - An additional "Trajectory Proximity" term is used only during privileged learning, rewarding the drone for staying near optimal trajectories.
 - Four terminal rewards are defined: reaching the goal (within 1.0m), going out of bounds, crashing (within 1.25m of an obstacle), and timeout (exceeding 40 seconds).

**B. Simulation and Environments**

1. **Simulation Setup:**
 - The open-source Flightmare platform is utilized, featuring a physics engine for quadrotor dynamics and a Unity-based graphics engine for rendering.
 - The simulation loop runs at 12 Hz ($\Delta t = 0.085$ s), where the drone state is used to render a depth image, fed into the RL policy, which outputs velocity commands. Flightmare's low-level controller translates these into rotor outputs, updating the drone's state.
 - Simulated dynamics are based on a 0.752 kg Kingfisher drone. Training employs an environment with 150 independently simulated drones for efficient rollout collection.
2. **Environment Generation:**
 - **Privileged Learning Environment:** Simple, consisting of randomly placed pillars with randomized start and end locations.
 - **Curriculum Environment:** More complex, divided into three regions of increasing difficulty:
 - Region 1: Grid of pillar clusters with varying start/end radii.
 - Region 2: Variable-length panels at random angles.
 - Region 3: Variable-length walls in rectangular sub-regions.

**C. Training Stages**

1. **Optimal Trajectory Planning:**
 - For the privileged learning stage, the environment mesh is converted into a 3D point cloud (0.15m resolution).
 - A hierarchical motion planner is applied to generate dynamically feasible optimal trajectories for given start/goal pairs, optimizing for time-to-goal and squared jerk.
2. **Privileged Learning with Trajectories:**
 - The policy is initially trained in the simple pillar environment. The "Trajectory Proximity" reward term provides a supervisory signal, guiding the drone to stay near the pre-computed optimal trajectories. This acts as scaffolding, aiding convergence by providing intermediate rewards for desired planning behavior. The goal is not exact emulation but guidance.
3. **Fine-tuning in Curriculum Environment:**
 - After initial training, the policy undergoes further fine-tuning in the more complex curriculum environment.
 - Training progresses by gradually introducing start/goal pairs from harder regions (2 and 3) as training advances from the easiest region (1).
 - Crucially, the "Trajectory Proximity" reward is *removed* during fine-tuning. This prevents the policy from being penalized for exploring multi-modal optimal paths in complex environments and enhances generalization rather than memorization of specific trajectories.

**D. Bridging Sim2Real**

1. **Reward Shaping:**
 - To foster smoother trajectories, which are crucial for real-world stability, reduced mechanical stress, and better generalization across different drone dynamics, the reward function includes penalties for yaw rate magnitude, acceleration, and jerk.
 - To mitigate low-frequency oscillations (wavy motion), rewards are given for the drone being angled towards the goal and for its trajectory matching the smooth optimal trajectory (during privileged learning).
2. **Domain Randomization:**
 - **State Noise:** Small normal noise (±2%) is applied to positional state measurements.
 - **Depth Image Noise:** A 5x5 Gaussian Blur with a randomized $\sigma$ in [0.1, 0.7] is applied to depth input images, approximating real-world outdoor noise characteristics (long range, variable lighting, edge variations).
 - **Action Noise:** Normal noise (±5%) is applied to simulated drone movement at each time step, simulating control inaccuracies and environmental factors like wind.
 - **Dynamics Randomization:** Flight dynamics parameters (mass, moments of inertia) are varied uniformly by ±10% to simulate a range of drone characteristics.
 - **System Delays:** A lag factor is introduced to approximate system delays like forward pass processing time and ROS node communication latency.

This comprehensive methodology aims to create a robust, generalizable, and practically deployable vision-guided autonomous flight policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, evaluating the policy's performance, robustness, and ability to transfer from simulation to reality.

**A. Simulation Evaluation**

- **Performance Across Speeds:** The policy was evaluated over 1000 runs in a randomly generated obstacle course, similar to the curriculum environment, at various maximum speeds (1.0 m/s to 7.0 m/s).
 - Success rates remained high and relatively constant from 1.0 m/s (99.2%) to 4.0 m/s (96.7%).
 - At higher speeds, performance degraded: 5.0 m/s (94.3% success), 6.0 m/s (87.8% success), and 7.0 m/s (78.0% success). This reduction was primarily attributed to increased crashes and out-of-bounds occurrences.
 - The decline at higher speeds suggests a limitation in the policy's ability to easily backtrack or navigate highly dense or dead-end areas, potentially indicating a need for enhanced memory architecture.

**B. Simulation Ablations**

- **Reward Terms:** An ablation study investigated the impact of specific reward terms related to trajectory characteristics (angular velocity magnitude, acceleration, jerk penalties).
 - Removing the angular velocity magnitude penalty led to large, high-frequency oscillations in commanded angular velocity.
 - Removing the acceleration penalty resulted in high-amplitude, low-frequency oscillations.
 - Removing the jerk penalty caused low-amplitude, high-frequency oscillations.
 - Quantitatively, removing these reward terms consistently increased the average jerk over the drone's trajectory. This demonstrates that these penalties effectively incentivize a smoother, mechanically sound flight profile, which is critical for robust generalization to varying real-world dynamics.
- **Training Stages:** An ablation study compared the proposed hybrid training approach (privileged learning followed by curriculum learning) against policies trained with only privileged learning or only curriculum learning.
 - The hybrid method achieved the highest stable success rate and the lowest times-to-goal, indicating its effectiveness.
 - The privileged learning policy learned quickly initially but its performance plateaued and decreased in more complex curriculum scenarios, suggesting that optimal trajectories become a less effective reward signal as complexity increases.
 - The curriculum learning policy took longer to learn but eventually approached the hybrid method's success rate, albeit with longer times-to-goal. This suggests that privileged learning provides an effective bootstrap, while curriculum learning is crucial for robust generalization, especially in complex environments where global optimal trajectories are multi-modal.

**C. Simulation Comparison (vs. MAVRL)**

- The proposed policy was compared against MAVRL, a baseline with a similar architecture, focusing on generalization across drone scale (increased weight and thrust proportionally) and obstacle density.
 - **Robustness to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Dynamics:** The proposed policy demonstrated significantly greater robustness to OOD drone dynamics. MAVRL's performance degraded rapidly as drone scale increased, while the proposed method maintained its success rate even with significant deviations from training dynamics.
 - **Obstacle Density:** MAVRL initially showed comparable or higher success rates in denser scenarios, which was attributed to its training in more cluttered environments. However, this advantage diminished rapidly with increasing drone scale.
 - **Trajectory Smoothness:** The proposed policy consistently generated relatively low-jerk trajectories across the tested range of drone scales, whereas MAVRL's commands became jerkier as drone dynamics became more OOD. This smoothness is hypothesized to be a key factor in the proposed policy's superior robustness, allowing low-level controllers to track commands more accurately across different drone characteristics. The differences are attributed to MAVRL pre-training its LSTM, while the proposed method trains its LSTM entirely within the RL loop, leveraging domain randomization and reward shaping.

**D. Real-World Experiments**

- **Flight System:** Tests were conducted on a DJI Matrice 300 RTK (6.3 kg, 0.81 m span), equipped with a Zed2i stereo camera and IMU for depth/VIO, and a Jetson AGX Xavier for onboard computation. The full pipeline operated at 12 Hz.
- **Environments:** Three obstacle environments (wall, triangle, inverted triangle) were constructed with varying obstacle placements and start/goal scenarios (left, center, right). Additionally, modified environments ("Tri-Mod," "Inv Tri-Mod") tested adaptation to concealed obstacles and reduced clearances. Experiments were conducted in both an open field and a courtyard with more clutter, uneven terrain, and environmental factors.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap Mitigation:** The system successfully transferred despite significant domain shifts:
 - **Drone Dynamics:** Policy trained on a 0.7kg simulated drone (±10% variation) generalized to a 6.3kg DJI M300 (approximately 10 times the weight).
 - **Environmental Scenery:** Successfully navigated environments substantially different from training, including sloped terrain, trees, sculptures, light poles, and buildings.
 - **Environmental Factors:** Contended with crosswinds up to 14 kph and non-ideal lighting conditions, which introduced additional sensor noise.
 - **VIO Inaccuracies:** Demonstrated performance despite the Zed2i VIO exhibiting drift up to 3m.
- **VIO Positioning Trials (Table III):**
 - Achieved a 100% success rate across 18 trials (6 unique environment/scenario combinations, 2 trials each).
 - The average actual measured distance to the goal (using a tape measure) was 2.27m. This relatively large discrepancy was attributed to the VIO device's positioning inaccuracy, as the drone's VIO *believed* it was within 1.0m of the goal in all successful runs.
 - Average speed was 2.58 m/s, indicating the policy aimed for high-speed flight given the 3.0 m/s maximum.
- **GNSS Positioning Trials (Table IV):**
 - To decouple VIO inaccuracy, 6 trials (3 environments, 2 trials each) were conducted using GNSS for positioning.
 - Achieved a 100% success rate.
 - The average actual measured distance to the goal was 0.81m, which is 2.8 times smaller than in the VIO trials. This confirms the policy's ability to accurately reach the goal when provided with a more precise positioning reference.
- **Navigation Adaptability (Figure 8):** In modified environments, the drone demonstrated quick adaptation to concealed obstacles and successfully avoided passages with insufficient clearance.

Overall, the findings indicate that the proposed hybrid training method, combined with robust reward shaping and domain randomization, enables a vision-guided RL policy to achieve successful zero-shot transfer to real-world outdoor environments and significantly different drone platforms, while providing directly usable velocity commands.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of autonomous aerial robotics, particularly in advancing the practicality and generalizability of vision-guided navigation systems. The primary significance lies in the successful demonstration of a reinforcement learning-based policy that achieves zero-shot transfer from simulation to real-world outdoor environments and a drone platform with substantially different dynamics.

Key aspects of its significance and potential impact include:

1. **Enhanced Generalizability and Zero-Shot Transfer:** The work demonstrates that a policy trained in simulation can be directly deployed on a real-world drone platform, such as a DJI M300 (which is approximately 10 times heavier than the simulated drone), without requiring specific tuning or re-training for the new hardware. This addresses a critical challenge in robotics, moving towards more universally applicable learning-based controllers.
2. **Direct Compatibility with Commercial Drone APIs:** By outputting high-level velocity commands, the policy can directly interface with the built-in APIs of off-the-shelf commercial drones. This simplifies deployment and significantly reduces the barrier for integrating advanced autonomous capabilities into existing drone ecosystems, avoiding the need for complex, drone-specific low-level controllers or model predictive controllers.
3. **Robustness to Real-World Complexities:** The policy demonstrates resilience against various real-world environmental factors, including sensor noise (due to non-ideal lighting), background clutter, crosswinds (up to 14 kph), and inaccuracies in onboard VIO positioning (drifting up to 3m). The combination of domain randomization and reward shaping proved effective in bridging these sim-to-real gaps.
4. **Effective Hybrid Training Paradigm:** The research validates the utility of a two-stage training approach: initial privileged learning with optimal trajectories for bootstrapping and guided exploration, followed by fine-tuning in a curriculum environment without trajectory-specific rewards to foster generalization. This strategy effectively manages the trade-off between efficient learning and robust performance in complex, multi-modal environments.
5. **Impact on Practical Applications:** The developed system has the potential to expand the operational envelope of quadcopters in numerous challenging scenarios:
 - **GNSS-denied environments:** Enabling autonomous navigation in areas where satellite signals are unavailable or unreliable, such as dense urban canyons, underground mines, or heavily forested areas (sub-canopy flight).
 - **Industrial Inspection:** Facilitating autonomous inspection of complex structures, power lines, or infrastructure where GPS might be obstructed or hazardous for human pilots.
 - **Search and Rescue:** Deploying drones for reconnaissance in disaster zones or unfamiliar terrains where real-time human control is difficult or dangerous.
 - **Mapping and Exploration:** Improving the efficiency and safety of mapping unknown or hazardous environments.
 - **Logistics and Delivery:** Enhancing the autonomy of drone delivery services in complex urban landscapes.

In conclusion, this research presents a significant step towards developing truly generalizable and deployable autonomous aerial systems. By demonstrating robust, vision-guided navigation on an out-of-distribution physical platform using standard velocity commands, it lays groundwork for broader adoption of learning-based control in practical robotic applications.
