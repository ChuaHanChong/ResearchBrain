# World Action Models Enable Continual Imitation Learning with Recurrent Generative Replays

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of North Carolina at Charlotte developed Recurrent Generative Replay (REGEN), a framework enabling World Action Models (WAMs) to continually learn new robot manipulation tasks without storing original human demonstrations. This approach mitigates catastrophic forgetting, reducing Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) by over 50% in simulation and approximately 40% in real-world settings compared to naive sequential fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Robot policies, including World Action Models (WAMs), suffer from catastrophic forgetting when sequentially fine-tuned on new tasks, leading to a loss of previously acquired skills.
- Existing continual learning methods like experience replay require storing original human demonstrations, which becomes impractical for large, proprietary pre-trained robot foundation models.
- There is a need for a continual imitation learning framework that allows robots to adapt to new tasks while retaining old skills without relying on storage of prior task data.

## Method

- The **REGEN** framework leverages the World Action Model's (WAM's) inherent generative capabilities to synthesize ==pseudo-demonstrations== for previously learned tasks.
- For prior tasks, the WAM generates trajectories by initializing with real observations from the current task, then recursively conditioning on its own previously generated observations and actions.
- The WAM is fine-tuned using a behavioral cloning loss on a combined dataset of current task demonstrations and the synthesized pseudo-demonstrations, effectively rehearsing old skills while learning new ones.

## Results

- REGEN reduced Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) by over **50%** on the LIBERO benchmark suites and approximately **40%** in real-world robot manipulation tasks, significantly mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
- The framework maintained strong Forward Transfer (FWT) and achieved substantially higher Area Under the Curve (AUC) scores compared to non-replay baselines, indicating effective acquisition of new skills while preserving old ones.
- Analysis showed that increasing pseudo-replay quantity improved backward transfer, and a goal-reward-based termination criterion for generation yielded higher fidelity pseudo-trajectories (PSNR **20.3** vs. **18.4** for fixed horizon).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - World Action Models can serve as an "implicit memory" for continual learning by generating synthetic replay data for past tasks, eliminating the need to store actual human demonstrations.
> - Generative replay helps preserve the underlying structure of learned skills by limiting the drift of action latent representations, as evidenced by significantly lower L2 centroid distances compared to naive fine-tuning.
> - The fidelity of the WAM's generated visual observations and the consistency between its predicted states and executable actions are critical factors limiting the overall effectiveness of generative replay.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressively moving towards the development of general-purpose robot policies, often leveraging large-scale pre-trained models. A significant challenge within this domain is "catastrophic forgetting," where a model fine-tuned on new tasks loses its ability to perform previously learned tasks. This phenomenon is a central problem in continual learning research.

Current strategies for mitigating catastrophic forgetting in robot policies, including conventional visuomotor policies and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, largely rely on "experience replay." Experience replay involves storing a subset of demonstrations from previous tasks and replaying them alongside data from new tasks during adaptation. While effective, this approach faces increasing practical limitations. The emergence of robot foundation models, trained on extensive proprietary datasets, often precludes public release of the original demonstration data. This makes the traditional experience replay paradigm, which assumes access to ground-truth prior task demonstrations, increasingly impractical for modern robot learning systems.

World Action Models (WAMs) have recently emerged as a paradigm for robot imitation learning. Unlike conventional policies that primarily predict actions, WAMs are generative models that unify perception, action prediction, and future visual observation generation. They model joint actions and future visual observations, effectively internalizing environment dynamics and causality. This generative capability of WAMs positions them uniquely to address the challenge of continual learning without storing real demonstrations. By being able to synthesize future visual observations conditioned on language instructions and current states, WAMs inherently possess a mechanism to "imagine" past task executions. This work leverages this specific generative capability to construct a novel continual imitation learning framework, thereby contributing to the development of more practical and data-efficient lifelong learning robots in contexts where prior task data cannot be retained.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a continual imitation learning framework that addresses catastrophic forgetting in World Action Models (WAMs) without requiring the storage of original human demonstrations from previously learned tasks.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key observations and challenges in current robot learning:

- **Catastrophic Forgetting in WAMs:** As WAMs become more general-purpose and are continually adapted to new tasks, they are susceptible to catastrophic forgetting, a common issue in sequential fine-tuning of neural networks. The authors pose the question of whether WAMs' inherent generative capabilities, which make them powerful for prediction and control, can also serve as a mechanism to retain previously acquired skills.
- **Impracticality of Stored Demonstrations:** Existing continual learning strategies, particularly experience replay, predominantly rely on storing actual demonstrations from prior tasks. However, the contemporary trend in robot learning involves large-scale pre-trained robot foundation models built on extensive, often proprietary datasets that are not publicly released. This makes the storage and replay of ground-truth demonstrations from past tasks an increasingly impractical assumption for real-world deployments and continual adaptation scenarios.
- **Leveraging WAMs' Generative Nature:** WAMs uniquely model both actions and future visual observations. This joint modeling provides a generative interface that can synthesize entire trajectories for previously learned tasks, conditioned solely on language instructions and an initial observation. This property offers a potential pathway to overcome the reliance on stored human demonstrations. The research aims to explore and formalize this potential, proposing that the WAM itself can act as a "native generative replay mechanism."

Therefore, the core motivation is to enable WAMs to continually learn new tasks while retaining old skills, by having them "hallucinate" or synthesize their own pseudo-demonstrations of past tasks. A secondary, but related, objective is to analyze the limitations of this generative replay process, specifically identifying factors that might limit the quality and effectiveness of synthesized trajectories. This analysis aims to inform future research directions for improving WAMs themselves, thereby enhancing their utility for continual learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes Recurrent Generative Replay (REGEN), a continual imitation learning framework for World Action Models (WAMs) that utilizes the WAM's generative capabilities to synthesize pseudo-demonstrations for previously learned tasks. This approach eliminates the need to store actual human demonstrations from prior tasks.

The methodology can be broken down into the following components:

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem involves continually adapting a pre-trained WAM policy, $\pi_\theta^0$, trained on an initial set of tasks $T_{prev} = \{T_1..., T_M\}$, to a sequence of new tasks $T_k$. The objective is to achieve adaptation to $T_k$ while preserving performance on all tasks in $T_{prev}$. A critical constraint is that for previous tasks $T_i \in T_{prev}$, only the task-level language instruction $\ell_i$ is available; the action-observation trajectories are not retained. For the current task $T_k$, both the instruction $\ell_k$ and expert demonstrations $D_k$ are accessible. This setup explicitly targets the scenario where traditional replay with real data is infeasible.

#### 3.2. Recurrent Generative Replay (REGEN) Framework

REGEN leverages the WAM's joint action and future observation modeling to generate pseudo-demonstrations for previous tasks. The process involves:

- **Initialization:** For a given previous task $T_i$ and its instruction $\ell_i$, the WAM rollout is initiated using real observations from the current task's demonstration dataset $D_k$. Specifically, the first action chunk (of horizon $H$) is conditioned on real observations $o_t$ for $0 \le t < H$. This provides a concrete visual context to start the generation process.
- **Recurrent Generation:** After the initial $H$ steps, the WAM recursively conditions on its *own previously generated observations* ($\tilde{o}_t$) to produce subsequent action chunks ($\tilde{a}_{t-H:t}$) and future observations ($\tilde{o}_t$). This mechanism allows for the complete synthesis of a trajectory for a previous task without any further real environmental input. The model also predicts a reward value $\tilde{r}_t$, indicating task progress.
- **Termination:** Pseudo-trajectory generation concludes either upon reaching a maximum horizon $T_{max}$ or when the WAM's predicted goal reward consistently signals task completion. The latter is defined as $\tilde{r}_t$ exceeding 0.99 for three consecutive steps, with at least one prediction reaching 1.0 within that interval. This goal-reward-based termination criterion aims to generate more realistic and task-relevant trajectories by stopping before significant visual degradation occurs.
- **Pseudo-Trajectory Construction:** The generated input observations $o_{in,t}$ are paired with the first action $\tilde{a}_t$ from the predicted action chunk to form a pseudo-trajectory $\tilde{\tau}_i$. All generated pseudo-trajectories for previous tasks form the replay set $R_k$.
- **Training Objective:** The WAM policy is fine-tuned using a behavioral cloning loss on a combined training set $D_k^+ = D_k \cup R_k$. This objective guides the policy to acquire the new task $T_k$ while simultaneously rehearsing the pseudo-demonstrations of previous tasks $T_i$, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Base WAM:** Cosmos-Policy is used as the underlying WAM, initialized from Cosmos-Predict2-2B.
- **Input:** The WAM takes third-person RGB images, wrist-camera observations, the robot's proprioceptive state, and a natural language instruction.
- **Output:** It jointly predicts an action chunk of horizon $H=16$, future visual observations, and a reward value indicating task completion.
- **Training:** The base policy is trained for 10,000 iterations. Each continual learning stage involves fine-tuning for 2,000 iterations.
- **Replay Data:** For each previous task, 10 pseudo-trajectories are synthesized.
- **Metrics:** Standard continual learning metrics are employed: Forward Transfer (FWT) for new task acquisition, Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) for quantifying forgetting of previous tasks, and Area Under the Curve (AUC) for overall performance. All metrics are based on task success rates.

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

- **Simulation Environment:** Experiments are conducted on the LIBERO benchmark, utilizing three task suites: LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, and LIBERO-Goal. Each suite comprises 10 tasks, with 50 human-teleoperated demonstrations per task. A continual learning setup is used, with six tasks for base pretraining and four tasks introduced sequentially.
- **Real-World Environment:** Experiments are performed on an xArm7 robotic manipulator involving three pick-and-place tasks introduced sequentially. 50 teleoperated demonstrations are collected per task. Evaluation is based on 10 randomized trials.
- **Baselines:** REGEN is compared against Sequential Fine-Tuning (Seq-FT), Sequential LoRA (Seq-LoRA), Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), PackNet (regularization/architecture-based methods), and Experience Replay (ER, as an upper-bound reference that uses real demonstrations). Additionally, Rollouts-as-Replay (RAR) is included to isolate the effect of model-generated versus simulator-rendered replay.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the efficacy of REGEN in mitigating catastrophic forgetting while maintaining forward transfer in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

- **Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation:** REGEN consistently reduces Negative Backward Transfer (NBT) by over 50% relative to naive sequential fine-tuning (Seq-FT) across LIBERO benchmark suites. For instance, on LIBERO-Object, NBT decreases from 82.6 (Seq-FT) to 26.1 (REGEN). In the real-world setting, NBT is reduced from 96.3 (Seq-FT) to 60.5 (REGEN), representing approximately 40% less forgetting. This indicates that pseudo-replays effectively preserve previously learned behaviors.
- **Forward Transfer Preservation:** REGEN maintains strong Forward Transfer (FWT), demonstrating its ability to learn new tasks without significant degradation in acquiring new skills. In the real-world experiments, REGEN even shows improved FWT (from 50 to 80), which the authors attribute to the regularizing effect of pseudo-trajectories in a low-data regime.
- **Overall Performance (AUC):** REGEN achieves substantially higher Area Under the Curve (AUC) scores compared to Seq-FT and other non-replay baselines. For example, on LIBERO-Object, AUC increases from 24.9 (Seq-FT) to 65.5 (REGEN). While REGEN does not fully match the performance of Experience Replay (ER), which uses privileged access to real demonstrations, it significantly closes the gap without requiring such access.
- **Comparison to VLA Models:** WAMs with REGEN demonstrate stronger retention than pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g., $\pi_{0.5}$) that also suffer from catastrophic forgetting, despite VLAs being trained on larger datasets. This suggests that the joint action-observation generative modeling unique to WAMs, when leveraged by REGEN, provides a more suitable foundation for lifelong robot learning without additional data collection.
- **Action Representation Drift:** Analysis of action latent representations shows that REGEN limits the drift of these representations from the base policy more effectively than Seq-FT. The mean L2 centroid distance for REGEN is 0.12, significantly lower than Seq-FT's 0.3, aligning closer to ER's 0.04. This suggests that REGEN helps preserve the underlying structure of learned skills.
- **Visualization of Predicted Actions:** Qualitatively, action trajectories generated by REGEN for previously learned tasks closely match ground-truth demonstrations, indicating retention of manipulation skills. In contrast, Seq-FT often produces erratic and incorrect trajectories.
- **Effect of Replay Quantity:** Increasing the number of pseudo-replays from 5 to 10 per task on LIBERO-Object leads to a further reduction in NBT (from 33 to 31) while maintaining FWT and AUC, suggesting that greater diversity in generated replay improves backward transfer.
- **Termination Criterion for Pseudo-Trajectory Generation:** Using a goal-reward-based termination criterion yields higher fidelity pseudo-trajectories (PSNR 20.3) compared to a fixed-horizon approach (PSNR 18.4 for H=200). This is attributed to avoiding long-horizon rollouts where visual degradation can accumulate.

**Limitations Identified:**

- **Visual Fidelity Degradation:** A monotonic decline in the PSNR of synthesized trajectories is observed across successive continual learning stages. This degradation, characterized by visual artifacts and blurriness, correlates with increased NBT, implying that lower-fidelity pseudo-trajectories provide progressively weaker supervisory signals. The comparison with Rollouts-as-Replay (RAR), which uses simulator-rendered replay, further highlights that generative fidelity is a key limiting factor, as RAR outperforms REGEN in NBT.
- **Mismatch Between Predicted States and Actions:** The WAM exhibits a discrepancy between its imagined success rate (based on generated observations) and its grounded success rate (based on executing generated actions). For example, at Stage 1, imagined success is 83%, while grounded success is only 42%. This indicates that the WAM can generate visually plausible successful outcomes without necessarily generating physically sufficient actions to achieve them. This decoupling is hypothesized to stem from degradation in generated observations, weakening the alignment between visual predictions and action dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Recurrent Generative Replay (REGEN), a novel framework that significantly advances continual imitation learning for robotics, particularly by addressing the growing challenge of data access for robot foundation models.

The primary significance of REGEN lies in its ability to enable continual robot learning without requiring access to or storage of actual human demonstrations from prior tasks. This is a critical development given the increasing prevalence of large, proprietary datasets used to train modern robot foundation models, which often cannot be publicly shared or continuously stored on memory-constrained robotic systems. By leveraging the WAM's inherent generative capabilities to synthesize pseudo-demonstrations, REGEN provides an elegant solution to the data retention problem, establishing the WAM itself as an "implicit memory."

The demonstrated effectiveness of REGEN in both simulation and real-world manipulation tasks underscores its practical viability. The framework's ability to substantially mitigate catastrophic forgetting—reducing NBT by over 50% relative to sequential fine-tuning—while preserving forward transfer, brings continual learning closer to real-world deployment scenarios. Furthermore, its performance approaching that of privileged experience replay methods (which require real data) highlights its potential as a robust alternative.

The comparative analysis with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models also indicates that WAMs, when coupled with generative replay like REGEN, offer a more suitable foundation for lifelong robot learning due to their unique capacity for joint action-observation generation. This suggests a potential shift in how future adaptable robotic systems might be designed.

Moreover, the detailed analysis of REGEN's limitations—specifically the degradation in visual fidelity of generated observations and the inconsistency between predicted states and actions—is a valuable contribution. By explicitly identifying these bottlenecks, the research provides clear, actionable directions for future advancements in WAM architecture and training. Improving generative fidelity and ensuring stronger alignment between imagined outcomes and executable actions are crucial areas that, if addressed, could further close the performance gap between synthetic and real experience replay, ultimately leading to more robust and reliable continually learning robots.

In summary, REGEN represents a step towards more autonomous, adaptable, and data-efficient robotic systems, paving the way for robots that can acquire new skills over extended periods without being hindered by data privacy concerns or memory limitations.
