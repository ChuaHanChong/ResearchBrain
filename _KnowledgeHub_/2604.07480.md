# Active Reward Machine Inference From Raw State Trajectories

> [!summary] Summary
> This research develops a framework to jointly infer reward machine models and their associated labeling functions directly from raw state trajectories and depth-limited history policies, without requiring prior knowledge of rewards or high-level labels. An active learning algorithm is introduced to efficiently query an optimal history policy, which demonstrably recovers ground-truth task structures in multi-stage grid world tasks with significantly reduced computational and memory costs.

## Problem

- Manually designing Reward Machines (RMs), including their finite-state structure, transition logic, and a labeling function, is labor-intensive and prone to errors for complex, multi-stage tasks.
- Existing RM learning methods typically rely on strong prior assumptions, such as observable rewards, known atomic propositions (labels), or the existence of a predefined labeling function.
- Learning RMs from exhaustive histories of state trajectories becomes computationally prohibitive due to the exponential growth of possible trajectories with their length.

## Method

- A ==Boolean Satisfiability (SAT)== problem is formulated to jointly infer the ==RM transition function== and the ==labeling function==, encoding these unknowns as binary variables.
- The SAT problem incorporates constraints derived from the functionality of these functions, an anchoring step for uniqueness, and crucial ==negative examples== which identify distinct RM nodes based on differences in optimal history policies.
- An ==active extension algorithm== iteratively prunes the space of candidate labeled RM models by intelligently selecting and querying high-quality trajectory pairs that are most informative for distinguishing between hypotheses.

## Results

- The framework successfully recovered the ground-truth reward machine models and their labeling functions for multi-stage 'pick_n_drop' and 'patrolABCD' grid world tasks, yielding structurally equivalent solutions.
- The active extension algorithm converged to the ground-truth solution set in **100%** of trials for 'pick_n_drop' (by depth 12) and **96.6%** for 'patrolABCD' (by depth 13) with specified sampling parameters.
- Compared to exhaustive baselines, the active method reduced memory usage by **two orders of magnitude** (e.g., from **24.76 GB** to **0.147 GB** for 'patrolABCD') and achieved an approximate **2x** speedup in total runtime (e.g., from ~7185s to ~3544s).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A theoretical 'sufficient depth' (l∗=∣S∣umax2l^* = |S|u_{max}}^2l∗=∣S∣umax2) for the history policy exists, ensuring that if a labeled RM is identifiable at this depth, it is identifiable at any greater depth.
> - Differences in optimal actions derived from state trajectory histories can provide sufficient signal to infer latent reward machine nodes and the corresponding labeling function, even without direct observation of rewards or labels.
> - An active learning approach, which strategically queries informative trajectory pairs, can drastically reduce the computational and memory requirements for RM inference compared to exhaustive methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) and optimal control frequently encounters multi-stage tasks in robotics, where objectives require sequential and interdependent subtasks. Reward Machines (RMs) provide a formal framework to represent these temporally extended objectives by encoding task progress as a finite-state automaton. This automaton transitions based on high-level events or propositions observed during execution, thereby structuring the reward function and guiding agent behavior in complex environments.

Despite the utility of RMs, their practical application is often limited by the need for human experts to manually define the underlying automaton, including its states, transitions, and, crucially, a labeling function. This labeling function maps low-level raw states of an environment to the high-level atomic propositions that trigger RM transitions. Manual specification is labor-intensive, error-prone, and can hinder the deployment of autonomous systems in novel or dynamic environments.

Existing literature on learning reward machines broadly falls into several categories:
- **Supervised learning approaches:** These methods typically assume access to ground-truth labels for states or rewards, or knowledge of the RM nodes, to infer a consistent automaton.
- **Active learning and oracle-based methods:** Some works utilize techniques like L* learning, which query membership and conjectures to infer automata.
- **Interactive learning with RL:** Other approaches combine automata synthesis with reinforcement learning in interactive settings, where the agent explores and learns simultaneously.
- **Learning from demonstrations:** While some studies infer RMs from demonstrations, they are often restricted to single-stage tasks or primarily use RMs for reward shaping rather than complex temporal logic. More advanced works learn RMs for multi-staged tasks from demonstrations but still rely on a predefined labeling function.

A significant bottleneck across many of these existing methods is their reliance on a predefined labeling function. Recent research has begun to address the ambiguity and noise in labeling functions, but these efforts still assume the *existence* of such a function, albeit a noisy one.

This paper distinguishes itself by addressing the fundamental challenge of learning *both* the reward machine model and the associated labeling function directly from raw state trajectories, without any prior access to observed rewards, labels, or even the internal nodes of the reward machine. This information-scarce regime represents a more challenging and realistic scenario for autonomous system deployment, where symbolic knowledge or predefined event detectors are often unavailable. By eliminating the reliance on such prior knowledge, this work aims to enable robots to autonomously discover the latent logical structure of tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the problem of learning a reward machine (RM) and its associated labeling function directly from raw state and policy information. This is performed under a highly information-scarce regime, where there is no direct observation of rewards, atomic propositions (labels), or the internal nodes of the reward machine.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical challenges in existing approaches to multi-stage task learning in robotics:

1. **Complexity of Manual Specification:** Manually designing reward machines, including their finite-state structure, transition logic, and a mapping from low-level states to high-level propositions (the labeling function), is a labor-intensive and error-prone process. For complex, real-world robotic tasks, this manual specification can lead to incomplete or incorrect task representations, potentially resulting in undesirable robot behaviors.
2. **Reliance on Prior Symbolic Knowledge:** A significant limitation of much of the prior work on RM learning is the assumption that either the rewards are observable, the labels (atomic propositions) are known, or at least a noisy version of the labeling function exists. This dependence on pre-defined symbolic knowledge or event detectors restricts the autonomy and applicability of these methods, particularly in environments where such information is not readily available. The paper aims to overcome this by learning both the labeling function and the RM from scratch, using only raw state trajectories and the optimal behavior induced by the RM.
3. **Computational Scalability:** Learning from exhaustive histories of state trajectories, especially for multi-stage tasks, can be computationally prohibitive. The number of possible trajectories grows exponentially with their length, leading to significant memory and processing demands for standard inference algorithms. This motivates the need for more efficient learning strategies.

To address these motivations, the research formally poses two specific sub-problems:

- **(P1) Identifiability of a Labeled RM:** Given an MDP model, an upper bound on the number of nodes in the true reward machine, and a depth-l\* restriction of the *true* history policy (derived from an optimal product policy), can a labeled reward machine be learned that is policy-equivalent to the underlying true one? This question seeks to establish the fundamental conditions under which such a learning problem is solvable.
- **(P2) Minimal Labeled RM Recovery:** If a sufficient depth l\* exists (as per P1), how can a *minimal* policy-equivalent labeled reward machine be found? Minimality, in this context, refers to a reward machine with the fewest number of nodes, which often corresponds to a more parsimonious and interpretable task representation.

By tackling these objectives, the paper aims to develop a framework that enables robots to autonomously infer the underlying structure of multi-stage tasks, making them more adaptable and less reliant on explicit human programming for complex behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper extends a two-step framework for reward machine (RM) inference, previously applied when labels are known, to the more challenging scenario where both the RM transition function and the labeling function are unknown. The focus is on jointly inferring the RM transition function ($\delta_u$) and the labeling function ($L$), as the recovery of the reward output function ($\delta_r$) has been addressed in prior work.

#### 3.1. Learning a Labeled Reward Machine (SAT Formulation)

The core of the approach is a Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) problem formulation. The unknown quantities, $\delta_u$ (RM transition function) and $L$ (labeling function), are encoded using binary variables:
- $b_{jpi}$: A binary variable that is 1 if $\delta_u(i, p) = j$, indicating a transition from RM node $i$ to node $j$ upon observing atomic proposition $p$.
- $L_{pk}$: A binary variable that is 1 if $L(k) = p$, indicating that MDP state $k$ is mapped to atomic proposition $p$.

The SAT problem, termed Problem 1, incorporates several constraints:
- **Functionality of $\delta_u$ and $L$ (Constraints 7a, 7b):** These ensure that $\delta_u$ and $L$ are well-defined functions, meaning each RM node and atomic proposition pair maps to exactly one next RM node, and each MDP state maps to exactly one atomic proposition.
- **Anchoring (Constraint 7c):** This constraint sets $L(1)=1$ for the initial MDP state and atomic proposition. This is a normalization step to remove solutions that are equivalent up to permutation of node or label names, ensuring a unique representation among structurally identical solutions.
- **Compatibility with Negative Examples (Constraint 7d):** This is the central constraint for learning. It is derived from **Lemma 1**, which states that if two state trajectories ($\tau, \tau'$) result in different optimal actions for some state, then they must lead to different RM nodes from the initial RM node. A pair of such trajectories is called a "negative example" (collected in set $E_l^-$). For each negative example, the SAT formulation enforces that the final RM nodes reached by processing $L(\tau)$ and $L(\tau')$ must be distinct. This constraint is crucial because it links observable policy differences (history policy $\pi_h$) to unobservable RM node differences. The paper defines how to compute the resulting RM node (vector $v_\tau$) for a given trajectory $\tau$ using matrix products of state-specific transition matrices $M_k$, which are themselves derived from the binary encodings of $\delta_u$ and $L$.
- **Non-stuttering (Optional Constraint 7e):** This constraint can be added if prior knowledge suggests that the underlying task is multi-stage and duration-insensitive (i.e., stutter-invariant). It ensures that once an RM node is reached via a proposition, observing the same proposition again will keep the RM in that same node.

#### 3.2. Sufficient Depth (Proposition 1)

A theoretical contribution is **Proposition 1**, which establishes that there exists a finite "sufficient depth" $l^* = |S|u_{\text{max}}^2$ for the history policy. This means that if Problem 1 is satisfiable at this depth $l^*$, it will also be satisfiable for any depth $l > l^*$. In other words, observing trajectories longer than $l^*$ will not introduce any new constraints that further reduce the set of feasible labeled RM models. The proof relies on constructing a synchronized labeled RM model from the true and learned RM models and showing that cycles in the corresponding product MDP can be removed without changing the RM nodes reached, effectively bounding the necessary trajectory length.

#### 3.3. Active Extension of the History Policy (Algorithm 1)

Recognizing the exponential growth of state trajectories with depth, the paper proposes an **Active Extension Algorithm** to mitigate the computational and memory burden of exhaustively collecting negative examples. This active learning strategy adopts a "volume-removal" approach, aiming to efficiently prune the set of candidate labeled RM models.

The algorithm proceeds iteratively:
1. **Initialize:** Start with a baseline set of negative examples from a shallow "burn-in" depth $l$.
2. **Subsample Candidates:** From the current set of feasible RM models (solutions to Problem 1), a subset $P_{\text{active}}$ is randomly sampled.
3. **Generate Candidate Trajectory Pairs:** For each candidate RM model in $P_{\text{active}}$, the algorithm uses randomized Depth First Search (DFS) to find pairs of trajectories $\{\tau, \tau'\}$ of length $l+1$ that lead to the *same* RM node according to that *candidate* model. These are potential query candidates.
4. **Evaluate Quality:** A "quality" metric is defined for each candidate pair $\{\tau, \tau'\}$. This metric quantifies how well the pair bisects the current hypothesis space (the $P_{\text{active}}$ set). High-quality pairs are those where roughly half of the candidate models predict that $\tau$ and $\tau'$ lead to the same RM node, while the other half predict they lead to different nodes. Such pairs are maximally informative for distinguishing between hypotheses.
5. **Query:** The top $B$ (budgeted) highest-quality trajectory pairs are selected. The *true* history policy (assumed available or estimable) is queried for these pairs. If a queried pair reveals that the true policy differentiates between $\tau$ and $\tau'$ (i.e., $\pi_h(a|s, \tau) \neq \pi_h(a|s, \tau')$), then it constitutes a new negative example.
6. **Refine:** The newly discovered negative examples are added to the SAT problem. The SAT solver is run incrementally, updating the set of feasible solutions $P_{\text{feasible}}$.
7. **Iterate/Terminate:** The process repeats, incrementing the depth $l$, until the set of feasible solutions converges to a single model (up to renaming) or a maximum depth/budget is reached.

This active approach seeks to reduce the number of queries and the size of the negative example set required to identify the true labeled RM, thereby enhancing computational efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed framework and the active extension algorithm were evaluated in grid world environments, specifically a 4x4 grid, with two distinct multi-stage tasks: "pick\_n\_drop" and "patrolABCD."

#### 4.1. Task 1: pick\_n\_drop

- **Task Description:** The robot's goal is to cyclically visit a pickup location, then a drop-off location, while avoiding a designated danger zone. The ground-truth reward machine has three nodes (start, picked up, dropped off) and associated labels for pickup, drop-off, and avoid zones, with all other states having a common "other" label.
- **Recovery without Active Learning:** Using a depth-9 expert history policy, the algorithm successfully recovered the ground-truth reward machine and its labeling function. The identified solutions (12 in total) were structurally equivalent, differing only by renaming of nodes and propositions. The algorithm correctly assigned distinct labels to the pickup, drop-off, and avoid locations, while grouping all other states under a single label.
- **Active Learning Performance:**
 - The active extension algorithm was initialized with a "burn-in" depth of $l=3$. At this shallow depth, the initial hypothesis space was large (over 150,000 feasible labeled RM models), from which 10,000 were retained for active sampling.
 - With an active sampling size of $N_{\text{active}}=100$ and a query budget $B=250$, the algorithm converged to the ground-truth solution set (up to renaming) in 100% of trials by depth 12.
 - With $N_{\text{active}}=50$, convergence was observed by depth 18.
 - A random baseline, which queries randomly generated state trajectory pairs, failed to find significant constraints even at depth 20, indicating the effectiveness of the quality-based sampling in the active approach.

#### 4.2. Task 2: patrolABCD

- **Task Description:** The robot is required to patrol four distinct rooms (A, B, C, D) in a specific sequential order (A $\rightarrow$ B $\rightarrow$ C $\rightarrow$ D). The ground-truth RM consists of four nodes, transitioning upon visiting each room.
- **Recovery without Active Learning:** Utilizing a depth-9 expert history policy, the algorithm successfully recovered the ground-truth labeled reward machine model and the correct clustering of grid cells into their respective propositions. This resulted in 36 policy-equivalent solutions, which account for permutations of node and labeling assignments given the anchoring constraint.
- **Active Learning Performance:**
 - The initial burn-in depth was set to $l=6$, resulting in 6895 unique history branches and a hypothesis space of 1152 distinct solutions.
 - With a query budget $B=250$ per depth increment and subsampling sizes $N_{\text{active}} \in \{100, 200\}$, the active method demonstrated faster convergence compared to the random baseline.
 - For $N_{\text{active}}=200$, 96.6% of trials converged to the ground-truth solution set by depth 13. For $N_{\text{active}}=100$, 83.3% converged by the same depth.
 - The random sampling baseline averaged 378 candidate solutions at depth 13, with a standard deviation two orders of magnitude larger than the active method, indicating its inefficiency.
- **Efficiency Gains (Memory and Computation):**
 - **Memory:** For the patrolABCD task, an exhaustive search at depth 9 required storing approximately 414 million negative examples, consuming 24.76 GB of memory. In contrast, the active extension algorithm, reaching a deeper horizon of depth 13, processed only 0.292 million negative examples, requiring 0.147 GB of memory. This represents a reduction in memory requirement by two orders of magnitude for negative examples.
 - **Runtime:** The exhaustive baseline at depth 9 exhibited a mean total runtime of 7185.53 seconds (predominantly SAT solving time). The active extension algorithm, while scaling to depth 13, achieved a mean total runtime of 3544.76 seconds, representing an approximate 2x speedup. The active method significantly reduced the SAT solving burden (2417.12 seconds on average, including the burn-in phase) compared to the exhaustive method (7158.73 seconds).

The results demonstrate that the active extension algorithm effectively alleviates both the memory and computational bottlenecks associated with exhaustive history policy expansion, while reliably recovering the ground-truth labeled reward machine models within practical limits.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of inverse reinforcement learning and task specification for autonomous systems, particularly in robotics. Its primary significance lies in establishing a foundational framework for jointly inferring both the reward machine (RM) transition structure and the labeling function directly from raw state trajectories and depth-limited history policies. This is achieved without requiring any prior observation of rewards, high-level labels (atomic propositions), or the internal states (nodes) of the automaton.

The potential impacts of this work include:

1. **Enhanced Autonomous Task Discovery:** By eliminating the reliance on human experts to manually define reward machines and their labeling functions, the framework enables robots to autonomously discover the latent logical structure of multi-stage tasks. This capability is crucial for deploying robots in complex, unstructured, or novel environments where pre-defined symbolic knowledge may not be available or easily specifiable.
2. **Increased Robustness and Adaptability:** Traditional methods are prone to errors and incompleteness in manual task specification. An inferred RM and labeling function could potentially adapt to variations in task execution or environment dynamics, leading to more robust and versatile robotic behaviors.
3. **Reduced Specification Burden:** The computational and human effort required for specifying complex multi-stage tasks can be substantially reduced. This could accelerate the development and deployment of sophisticated robotic applications by automating a critical part of the task design pipeline.
4. **Improved Computational Efficiency:** The proposed active extension algorithm addresses a major bottleneck of exhaustive search by intelligently querying informative trajectory pairs. The empirical results demonstrate substantial reductions in memory usage (two orders of magnitude) and computational runtime (approximately a 2x speedup) while still achieving reliable recovery of the ground-truth task structure. This makes the approach more tractable for larger state spaces and deeper task complexities.
5. **Theoretical Foundations for Identifiability:** The paper provides a theoretical guarantee regarding the sufficient depth ($l^*$) of trajectory information required to uniquely identify a policy-equivalent labeled reward machine. This contributes to the fundamental understanding of what information is necessary and sufficient for learning memory-based task representations.

**Future Directions and Broader Implications:**

While the current framework is a foundational step, it suggests several promising avenues for future research:

- **Robustness to Finite and Noisy Data:** The current assumption of access to the history policy induced by the *optimal* product policy can be relaxed. Future work could focus on estimating the history policy from finite state-action trajectories, potentially incorporating statistical methods to handle estimation errors and noise, similar to approaches in known labeling function settings.
- **Scalability to Richer Domains:** Extending the framework beyond tabular state-action spaces to continuous or high-dimensional perceptual observations is a critical challenge. This would likely involve learning labeling functions directly from raw perceptual representations, potentially leveraging advanced deep learning techniques and pre-trained models.
- **Refined Termination Criteria:** The current termination criterion requires finding solutions unique up to renaming. Integrating equivalence-testing procedures into the active learning loop could allow for earlier termination if all remaining candidate labeled RMs are policy-equivalent, even if not structurally identical. This could further improve computational efficiency in practice.
- **Unified Understanding of Memory:** This work contributes to the broader question of why memory is necessary in sequential decision-making, specifically focusing on memory related to task structure. A natural next step is to connect this framework with the literature on information states and learning Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs). Developing a unified understanding of different forms of memory requirements (e.g., for partial observability vs. task structure) in robot decision-making could lead to more comprehensive and intelligent autonomous agents.

In summary, this research offers a novel and computationally efficient approach to learning complex multi-stage task structures directly from observed behavior in information-scarce environments. Its contribution extends beyond practical application, laying theoretical groundwork for how task-relevant memory can be automatically inferred, paving the way for more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems.
