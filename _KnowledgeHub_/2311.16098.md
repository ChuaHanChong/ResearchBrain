# On Bringing Robots Home

> [!summary] Summary
> NYU and Meta researchers introduced Dobb·E, an open-source framework for training cost-effective robots to perform diverse manipulation tasks in real homes. The system achieved an 81% average success rate across 109 tasks in varied household environments, demonstrating efficient adaptation through self-supervised pretraining on a new in-domain dataset and rapid fine-tuning from limited human demonstrations.

## Problem

- Current robotics research is mostly confined to controlled lab settings, limiting generalization to complex, unpredictable real-world home environments.
- The goal of creating "generalist machines" – versatile domestic assistants capable of learning and adapting to diverse household tasks – remains unfulfilled.
- Existing methods for collecting robotic manipulation data are often inefficient, costly, and require expert operators, hindering large-scale deployment.

## Method

- Developed **Dobb·E**, a framework leveraging a low-cost **The Stick** tool for intuitive human demonstrations and a Hello Robot Stretch RE1 platform with an ==eye-in-hand== iPhone setup for data collection.
- Created the **Homes of New York (HoNY)** dataset, comprising 13 hours of RGB-D data from 22 lived-in homes, specifically designed for domestic manipulation tasks.
- Employed a ==pretraining-fine-tuning paradigm== using ==self-supervised learning (MoCo-v3)== on HoNY to create **Home Pretrained Representations (HPR)** with a ==ResNet34== encoder, followed by rapid ==fine-tuning== on new tasks with limited demonstrations.

## Results

- The Dobb·E framework achieved an average success rate of **81%** across **109** diverse manipulation tasks over 10 real homes, with **102 tasks** surpassing **50%** success.
- The Home Pretrained Representations (HPR) model significantly enhanced task success, outperforming vision models pretrained on larger but out-of-domain internet datasets by at least **23%**.
- A new task can be learned and a policy deployed within approximately **30 minutes**, requiring only about **91 seconds** of effective demonstration data per task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Domain-specific self-supervised pretraining (HPR) on in-domain data substantially improves task success rates (at least 23% over internet-trained models) for home robotics.
> - Accurate 6D odometry from consumer-grade smartphone sensors and high-quality depth data from LiDAR are critical for robust action prediction, especially in close-up interactions.
> - Real-world deployment reveals practical challenges often ignored in lab settings, including significant variations in lighting, sensor limitations with reflective surfaces, and robot hardware constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has made significant strides in various domains, ranging from industrial automation to specialized domestic machines like robot vacuums. However, the overarching goal of a "generalist machine"—a versatile domestic assistant capable of learning and adapting to diverse household tasks—remains largely unfulfilled. Current robotic research often operates within highly controlled industrial settings or academic labs with curated environments, objects, and lighting conditions. This prevailing paradigm has resulted in robotic algorithms that perform well in constrained scenarios but struggle when deployed in the messy, unpredictable, and varied environments of real homes. Existing domestic robots, while successful, are typically limited to performing a single task effectively.

"On Bringing Robots Home" directly addresses this fundamental challenge by initiating a "large-scale effort" to bridge the gap between lab-based robotics and real-world domestic deployment. The authors explicitly criticize the foundational assumptions of lab robotics and advocate for a paradigm shift towards evaluating and developing systems in diverse, uncurated household environments. Their work positions itself at the forefront of this shift by focusing on affordable yet versatile systems for learning robotic manipulation in real homes.

This research distinguishes itself from prior work in several key ways:
- **Real-world deployment and data:** Unlike many datasets collected in labs or short-term rental apartments, the Homes of New York (HoNY) dataset is amassed from 22 *lived-in* homes in New York City, capturing the true variability and clutter of domestic life. This makes the data uniquely representative of target environments.
- **Efficiency and user-centric data collection:** The introduction of "The Stick," a low-cost, intuitive demonstration tool, directly tackles inefficiencies in traditional robot data collection, which often involves unwieldy exoskeletons, complex teleoperation setups, or multiple robot deployments. This design choice aims to democratize data collection and make it accessible to non-experts.
- **Emphasis on generalization:** The methodology leverages large-scale pretraining on diverse home data combined with rapid fine-tuning for new tasks, aiming for high adaptability to novel environments and tasks without extensive re-engineering.
- **Open-source commitment:** The open-sourcing of hardware designs, software stack, models, and data fosters community collaboration and accelerates future research, moving beyond proprietary systems that limit scientific inquiry.

By directly confronting the challenge of real-world generalization in diverse, uncurated home environments, this work significantly contributes to the ongoing quest for truly capable and adaptable domestic robots, marking a crucial step beyond single-purpose machines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to advance the vision of "generalist machines" in homes – domestic assistants that can learn, adapt, and perform a wide range of tasks efficiently and safely, all while remaining cost-effective. The authors are motivated by the historical success of integrating single-task machines into homes (e.g., dishwashers, robot vacuums) and the persistent, decades-long pursuit of a truly versatile home robot. They contend that existing robotics research, often confined to controlled laboratory or industrial settings, fails to adequately address the complexities and variability inherent in real-world households.

To achieve their ambitious goal, the Dobb·E framework is built upon three core principles:
1. **Efficiency:** To enable rapid learning and deployment, the system embraces large-scale data collection coupled with modern machine learning tools, particularly self-supervised learning for foundational representations and quick fine-tuning for new tasks.
2. **Safety:** Recognizing the sensitivity of home environments, the robot is designed to learn new tasks from a small number of human demonstrations, rather than relying on potentially unsafe trial-and-error learning, which could lead to damage or accidents.
3. **User Comfort:** Acknowledging that robot operation should be accessible to non-experts, the research focuses on developing an ergonomic and intuitive demonstration collection tool ("The Stick"). This allows users to easily provide task-specific demonstrations in their own homes without needing to directly operate a complex robotic system.

The ultimate motivation is to accelerate the development of home robots and eventually realize the popular culture vision of a "robot butler in every home." By systematically tackling the challenges of data collection, model generalization, and real-world deployment in diverse home environments, the authors aim to lay a robust foundation for future research and practical applications in domestic robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Dobb·E framework employs a behavior cloning approach for visual imitation learning, integrating novel hardware, a unique dataset, and state-of-the-art machine learning techniques. The methodology is structured into four main stages: hardware design, dataset collection, policy learning, and real-world deployment.

#### 3.1. Hardware Design

The core innovation in data collection is "The Stick," an affordable ($25) reacher-grabber augmented with a 3D-printed iPhone mount. This tool allows human demonstrators to intuitively mimic robot movements without directly controlling the robot, making data collection user-friendly. An iPhone Pro (12 or newer) mounted on The Stick captures RGB images (1280x720 pixels), depth data (256x192 pixels) from its LiDAR sensor, and 6D pose (translation and rotation) from its internal odometry and gyroscope, all at 30 FPS using the Record3D app. Crucially, an identical iPhone mount is affixed to the Hello Robot Stretch RE1's dexterous wrist. This "eye-in-hand" configuration ensures that the camera's perspective on the gripper is identical between demonstration and execution, facilitating seamless sim-to-real transfer and eliminating the need for complex multi-camera setups or calibration. The Stretch RE1, chosen for its affordability, lightweight design (23 kg), two-hour battery life, and onboard Intel NUC computer, serves as the robotic platform. Minor hardware modifications include replacing standard gripper tips with small cylindrical tips, which improve manipulation of finer objects like door handles.

#### 3.2. Pretraining Dataset – Homes of New York (HoNY)

Leveraging The Stick, the team collected the HoNY dataset, comprising 13 hours of recording time (5620 demonstrations, 1.5 million frames) across 216 distinct "environments" within 22 real homes in New York City. Volunteers were instructed to collect data for eight broad task categories (e.g., button switching, door opening/closing, pick and place) and "play data" to encourage diverse interactions. The dataset includes RGB, depth, and 6D absolute poses. To address the lack of direct gripper status, a 3-layer ConvNet was trained on 500 manually labeled frames to predict the gripper distance (0-1), which was then applied to label the entire dataset. Rigorous quality control involved manual review to filter out bad demonstrations, noisy actions, and personal identifying information. HoNY is unique in its focus on first-person household interactions with high-quality depth information, differentiating it from many lab-centric or third-person datasets.

#### 3.3. Policy Learning with Home Pretrained Representations (HPR)

The Dobb·E policy learning leverages a pretraining-fine-tuning paradigm. A ResNet34 architecture, chosen for its performance and suitability for onboard robot computation, serves as the base for the visual encoder. This encoder is pretrained on the HoNY dataset using the MoCo-v3 self-supervised learning (SSL) algorithm for 60 epochs, resulting in the "Home Pretrained Representation" (HPR) model. For a new task in a novel home, HPR is then fine-tuned with a small number of new demonstrations. The downstream policy takes an RGB-D image (256x256 pixels, 4 channels) as input. HPR processes the RGB channels, while a median-pooling layer processes the depth channel. The resulting features are concatenated and fed into a two-layer neural network (MLP) to predict a 7-dimensional action vector (3D relative translation, 3D relative rotation in axis-angle, and 1D gripper value) at 3.75 Hz. The model is trained for 50 epochs with a mean-squared error (MSE) loss, with all parameters unfrozen and actions normalized.

#### 3.4. Deployment in Homes

For deployment, the robot is assumed to have navigated to the task vicinity. To teach a new task in a novel home, approximately 24 new demonstrations are collected using The Stick (taking about 5 minutes) from a varied grid of starting positions (e.g., 4x6 or 5x5) to promote spatial generalization. The R3D data is processed (5 minutes), and the HPR model is fine-tuned for 50 epochs on an RTX A4000 GPU (20 minutes). This entire process typically takes around 30 minutes from the start of data collection to a deployable policy. During robot execution, the iPhone streams RGB-D images via USB to the Stretch's onboard computer. The policy predicts actions, which are executed using a PyKDL-based inverse kinematics solver. The predicted gripper value is binarized with a task-specific threshold. Evaluations are conducted with 10 initial starting positions for each task, with manual resets between trials.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across 10 homes in the New York City area, encompassing 109 distinct tasks over 30 days, yielded several key findings:

- **Overall Performance and Simplicity Effectiveness:** Dobb·E achieved an impressive average success rate of 81% across all 109 tasks, with 102 tasks demonstrating a success rate of 50% or higher. This high performance was achieved using a relatively simple behavior cloning recipe, employing a ResNet model for visual representation and a two-layer neural network for action prediction. Remarkably, the system required only approximately 91 seconds of effective demonstration data per task, collected within a five-minute window, highlighting the efficiency of the framework.

- **Impact of Effective SSL Pretraining (HPR):** The Home Pretrained Representations (HPR) model, trained on the in-domain HoNY dataset using self-supervised learning, significantly outperformed other foundational vision models. HPR improved task success rates by at least 23% compared to models like R3M, VC1, and MVP, which were trained on much larger, but out-of-domain, internet datasets. While VC1 showed the closest performance, it exhibited a bimodal behavior, either performing well or failing entirely. This finding strongly validates the importance of domain-specific pretraining for generalizable representations in robotics.

- **Crucial Role of Odometry, Depth, and Demonstrator Experience:**
 - **Odometry:** The highly accurate 6D odometry derived from the iPhone's internal sensors (gyroscope and odometry) in The Stick proved critical. It provided much more robust and reliable action information compared to traditional structure-from-motion methods like OpenSfM, especially during close-up interactions where visual features might be scarce.
 - **Depth Perception:** The depth information from the iPhone's LiDAR sensor was found to be highly beneficial, significantly improving performance over RGB-only models. Depth perception aids in 3D reasoning, particularly for tasks involving contact with featureless objects, where precise distance estimation is crucial for gripper actions.
 - **Demonstrator Expertise:** Ablations showed that while some initial practice is required, novice users could effectively collect sufficient demonstrations to train successful robotic policies, making the data collection process scalable beyond expert operators.

- **Analysis of Failure Modes:** The experiments unveiled a "plethora of unique challenges absent or ignored in lab robotics":
 - **Lighting and Shadows:** Strong, unobserved shadows during execution often caused policies to behave erratically, leading to failures. Although the iPhone's low-light capabilities sometimes mitigated this, significant changes in lighting from demonstration to execution were problematic.
 - **Sensor Limitations:** The LiDAR-based depth sensor struggled with shiny and reflective objects, yielding noisy depth data and out-of-distribution values. Furthermore, reflections in mirrors could confuse the policy, as the demonstrator's reflection during data collection differed from the robot's reflection during execution.
 - **Robot Hardware Limitations:** The Hello Robot Stretch's physical constraints, such as its height limit (1m manipulation space) and bottom-heavy design, restricted the range of tasks (e.g., high-up light switches, pulling heavy objects from high positions) and affected its ability to apply force without tilting. Fine manipulation tasks like knob turning also suffered due to the need for precise gripper centering.
 - **Temporal Dependencies:** The single-step behavior cloning approach struggled with long-horizon tasks where visual observations at different points in time might appear similar (e.g., pick-and-place tasks where pick and place locations look identical). This caused the policy to get "confused" and fail to complete the sequence of actions.

- **Ablation Results:**
 - **Number of Demonstrations:** Increasing the number of demonstrations consistently improved task success rates until saturation, confirming the value of additional data.
 - **Depth Perception:** Depth input generally outperformed RGB-only input, particularly for tasks requiring precise interaction with surfaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"On Bringing Robots Home" marks a significant stride toward realizing versatile, general-purpose home robots. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating that an affordable, adaptable robotic system can achieve high success rates on a diverse set of manipulation tasks in complex, uncurated household environments. This moves beyond the long-standing limitation of lab-centric robotics and the single-task capabilities of existing home machines, setting a new benchmark for real-world performance.

The **Dobb·E framework** and its components have several potential impacts:

- **Accelerating Home Robotics Research:** By successfully deploying and testing a system in 22 diverse homes, the work provides concrete evidence that real-world deployment is feasible and offers invaluable insights into practical challenges. The authors' commitment to **open-sourcing all components** – including hardware designs for "The Stick," the HoNY dataset (RGB, depth, actions), HPR pretrained models, and the entire software stack for fine-tuning and robot deployment – is a monumental contribution. This open access dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for researchers, enabling faster iteration, comparative studies, and community-driven advancements in home robotics. The call for collaboration and provision of support resources further solidifies this commitment.
- **Practicality and Affordability:** The use of readily available and inexpensive components, such as a $25 reacher-grabber, iPhones for advanced sensing, and the commercially available Hello Robot Stretch, makes the Dobb·E system accessible. The rapid adaptation time – a new task learned and deployed within approximately 30 minutes – highlights its practical utility for non-expert users in a home setting. This addresses a critical need for systems that are both effective and deployable outside of specialized labs.
- **Unveiling Real-World Challenges:** The detailed analysis of failure modes (lighting/shadows, sensor limitations with reflective surfaces, robot hardware constraints, and the limitations of single-step policies for temporal tasks) provides a crucial roadmap for future research. These are challenges often overlooked in controlled lab environments but are paramount for successful home deployment. The paper effectively shifts the research focus towards these neglected but vital real-world problems.
- **Dataset Contribution:** The Homes of New York (HoNY) dataset is a valuable resource for the community. Its unique characteristics – being collected from diverse, lived-in homes, focusing on first-person interactions, and including high-quality depth information – make it highly relevant for training and evaluating robust robot learning models designed for domestic environments.
- **Foundational for Future Work:** The success of the HPR model, demonstrating the power of domain-specific pretraining, will likely influence future approaches to representation learning in robotics. The identified "open problems" (scaling to long-horizon tasks, incorporating memory, improving sensors, robustifying hardware) serve as a clear agenda for the next generation of research in domestic robotics, pushing the field towards more sophisticated and capable autonomous agents.

In essence, "On Bringing Robots Home" does more than just present a new system; it redefines the frontier of domestic robotics research by offering a practical, accessible, and comprehensive framework, while candidly outlining the remaining hurdles, thereby catalyzing future innovation in the field.
