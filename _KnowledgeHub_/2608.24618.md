# VIP: Variation-based Iterative-learning Planning for Robotic Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> VIP (variation-based iterative-learning planning) optimizes a robot's traversal-speed command as a continuous function of path arc length instead of finite trajectory variables. Planning objectives become system-energy functionals, and the command is updated from energy variations recorded during simulated or physical rollouts, needing no dynamic model. Complexity is O(n) per iteration; it cut replanning time 47-88% versus MPCC and polynomial planners and reduced swarm traversal time from 241s to 82s.

## Problem

- Prediction-based planners like MPC and polynomial or B-spline parameterizations scale badly: cost grows with prediction horizon, trajectory resolution, and number of coupled decision variables.
- Swarm planning inherits this: centralized joint optimization explodes with swarm size, distributed schemes hinge on communication, and fixed formations restrict passage through cluttered space.
- Iterative learning control assumes a repetitive task with a given reference and only reduces tracking error, leaving planning-level objectives such as traversal time outside the learning loop.

## Method

- Unified ==energy-variation formulation==: single-robot path-following error and swarm density error map onto one spatial energy system, so traversal time is minimized as a functional over arc length.
- Model-based **VIP** derives the ==functional gradient== via a ==Hamiltonian== and ==adjoint variable==; model-free **VIP** replaces it with an algebraic update driven by the recorded energy profile alone.
- Two rollout modes share one update law: model-in-the-loop simulated rollouts for offline planning, robot-in-the-loop physical traversals for online ==iterative learning==; ==Tube-RRT*== supplies geometry, **VIP** supplies speed.

## Results

- Across 60/80/100-obstacle forests, replanning time fell **47.4-87.7%** versus MPCC and polynomial baselines (**2.37-2.62ms** vs **4.98-5.44** and **17.66-21.32ms**), with traversal time within **1.4%** of MPCC and up to **6.8%** shorter than polynomial.
- Onboard planning cost scaled linearly: **0.28ms** for 50 waypoints, **0.52ms** for 100 on a workstation, **0.93ms** for 100 points on Jetson Orin during **150m** outdoor flights at **10m/s**.
- Iterative gains held on hardware: lifting-wing quadcopter traversal dropped **77.4s** to **42.7s** in simulation then **41.3s** in flight; three-drone swarm **241s** to **82s** over six iterations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing a command as a function over space, not as discretized waypoints, decouples plan resolution from problem dimension, so refinement costs linear time rather than a larger optimization program.
> - Splitting where-to-go from how-fast lets a geometric planner or virtual-tube controller own safety while a cheap learned speed profile owns efficiency, reusable across heterogeneous platforms.
> - Learning is between rollouts, not within one, so gains need repeated traversals of the same route and assume static environments; dynamic obstacles remain future work.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robotic systems are increasingly deployed in applications such as surveying, search and rescue, and last-mile delivery, necessitating robust motion planning capabilities. These applications often involve complex, obstacle-dense environments and limited onboard computational resources. The existing research landscape for robotic motion planning can be broadly categorized into prediction-based and reactive-based methods for single robots, and various centralized, distributed, and formation-based approaches for robotic swarms.

Prediction-based planners, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), formulate planning as a multi-step optimization problem over a finite horizon. While capable of incorporating system dynamics and environmental constraints, their computational cost typically increases with the prediction horizon, trajectory resolution, and number of decision variables. Methods using polynomial or B-spline parameterization aim to reduce variable count, but complex time optimization can still impose significant loads. Sampling-based planners, like the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA) or Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, improve efficiency by approximating the problem or limiting the solution space. However, real-time application for longer horizons remains a challenge for many prediction-based methods.

Reactive planners, including vector field (VF) and potential field methods, determine motion commands based on current state and local observations, generally requiring less online computation. However, their performance can be sensitive to local environmental structures and limited look-ahead information. Control Barrier Function (CBF) methods combine elements of both, providing safety guarantees but often requiring more computational resources.

For robotic swarms, the challenges are compounded. Centralized planners face rapidly increasing problem dimensions with swarm size. Distributed planners reduce central computation but are sensitive to communication conditions and local coordination rules. Formation-based methods provide structure but can limit maneuverability in cluttered environments. Furthermore, multi-robot planning paradigms are often categorized as bottom-up, where swarm behavior emerges from individual rules, or top-down, where collective motion is described by macroscopic variables (e.g., PDEs). While top-down approaches offer scalability, their formulation as general-purpose planning mechanisms can still involve demanding numerical optimization.

Iterative Learning Control (ILC) is a control methodology designed to improve system performance over repetitive tasks. ILC offers a function-space optimization perspective, allowing iterative solutions over the entire task domain. Both model-based and model-free ILC designs exist. However, conventional ILC primarily focuses on trajectory *tracking* with a given reference, rather than optimizing planning-level objectives like traversal time in navigation scenarios, where the trajectory itself is unknown.

This work fits into this landscape by addressing the need for a unified, computationally efficient, and scalable planning methodology that can directly optimize planning commands for both single robots and swarms, without the high computational burden and model dependence often associated with existing methods. It extends the concept of iterative learning from trajectory tracking to planning-oriented navigation by focusing on functional optimization of traversal commands.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel motion planning framework, Variation-based Iterative-learning Planning (VIP), that can efficiently generate and iteratively improve motion plans for single robots and robotic swarms. This framework aims to operate effectively in large, complex, and obstacle-dense environments, even under limited onboard computing resources.

The motivations for developing VIP stem from several limitations identified in the broader research landscape:

1. **High Computational Costs of Conventional Planning:** Existing prediction-based planning methods, such as Model Predictive Control and polynomial/B-spline trajectory generators, typically rely on finite-dimensional trajectory parameterization or increasingly long prediction horizons. This leads to a rapid increase in computational costs, especially for high-resolution trajectories or in multi-robot scenarios, making real-time, long-horizon optimization challenging for resource-constrained robotic platforms.

2. **Scalability Challenges in Multi-Robot Planning:** Extending motion planning from single robots to swarms significantly increases complexity. Centralized planners become computationally intractable with growing swarm size. Distributed planners, while alleviating central computation, often depend heavily on communication reliability, local coordination rules, and simplified assumptions, limiting their overall scalability. Current bottom-up approaches offer limited ability to directly optimize swarm-level objectives, while top-down approaches, despite their potential for scalability, still often require demanding numerical optimization.

3. **Limitations of Traditional Iterative Learning Control (ILC) for Navigation:** Conventional ILC is designed for repetitive *tracking* tasks with predefined reference trajectories. Direct application to robotic *navigation*, where the robot must determine its motion under environmental constraints and optimize objectives like traversal time, is not straightforward. Most ILC methods focus on reducing tracking errors, not on optimizing planning-level objectives. The improvement typically occurs at the execution layer, with planning remaining a separate process.

4. **Dependence on Accurate Models and Analytical Gradients:** Many optimization-based planning methods require explicit dynamic models and computationally expensive analytical gradient evaluations. This can be problematic in real-world scenarios where models may be uncertain, and online computation of complex derivatives is not feasible.

To address these motivations, the VIP framework sets forth the following objectives:
- To establish a **unified planning formulation** that can represent diverse performance objectives (single-robot path following and robotic swarm density regulation) through a common energy functional.
- To achieve **low computational complexity** by directly updating the planning command as a continuous function in an infinite-dimensional space, thereby avoiding the burden of horizon expansion and high-dimensional trajectory discretization.
- To develop a **model-free and online planning mechanism** that updates planning commands directly from energy variations recorded during robot-in-the-loop executions, without requiring explicit dynamic models or expensive analytical gradient computations.
- To demonstrate **effectiveness, computational efficiency, and scalability** across different planning objectives, robotic platforms, and swarm configurations through extensive simulations and real-world experiments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The VIP framework is designed for efficient motion planning by directly optimizing the planning command as a continuous function in an infinite-dimensional function space. The methodology encompasses a unified energy system formulation, a functional optimization approach, model-based and model-free iterative learning laws, and flexible implementation modes.

**Core Concept: Functional Optimization**
Instead of parameterizing trajectories with a finite number of discrete variables, VIP considers the planning command (specifically, a traversal-speed profile `v_t(l)` along a spatial arc length `l`) as a continuous function. Optimization then occurs directly within this function space. Planning objectives are translated into corresponding system-energy functionals, and the command is iteratively improved based on the *variation* of the energy response.

**Unified Energy System Formulation**
The paper unifies diverse planning objectives by mapping them to a common scalar energy function, `V`.
- **Single-Robot Formulation:** For a single robot, the motion command `v_c` is decomposed into a nominal component `v_n` that corrects path error and a traversal component `v_t` that advances the robot along the path. The energy `V_s` is defined as half the squared Euclidean distance to the closest point on the path, `V_s = 1/2 * ||e_p||^2`. The dynamics of `V_s` are characterized by a dissipative term proportional to `v_n` and a coupling term, modulated by a velocity saturation coefficient `κ_v`.
- **Robotic Swarm Formulation:** For a robotic swarm, the collective state is represented by a normalized density `ρ(t, p)`. The objective is to match a desired density `ρ_d(t, p)`. The energy `V_m` is defined as half the squared L2-norm of the density error `ϕ = ρ - ρ_d`. The collective velocity field `v_c` is similarly decomposed into a density-regulation component `v_n` and a traversal component `v_t`. The dynamics of `V_m` also show a dissipative term and a coupling term, modulated by an energy-weighted saturation coefficient `¯κ_v`. Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) is used to reconstruct swarm density from individual robot positions.
- **Common Energy Dynamics:** Both `V_s` and `V_m` are shown to conform to a unified energy model `dV/dt = -λ_1 * κ_E * v_n^p + ε * v_t^o * v_n^q + ε_1`, where `p > q`. This generalized energy function `V` forms the basis for the subsequent optimization.
- **Objective Function:** The navigation problem is formulated as minimizing an integral cost functional `J = integral(1/(κ_E * v_t) + k_V * V) dl`. This objective balances the local traversal-time cost (minimizing `1/(κ_E * v_t)`) with a penalty on the accumulated energy deviation (`k_V * V`), aiming for both efficiency and safety.

**Iterative Learning Laws**
VIP develops two types of learning laws:
- **Model-Based VIP:** This theoretical approach uses a Hamiltonian formulation to derive the exact functional gradient `F_n` with respect to `v_t`. The learning law `v_t,k+1 = v_t,k - b_1 * F_n,k` represents a steepest-descent step in function space. This derivation requires explicit knowledge of system dynamics, energy models, their derivatives, and an adjoint variable, serving as a theoretical reference for convergence and the qualitative behavior of the gradient.
- **Model-Free VIP:** This is the practical implementation, designed to avoid the computational burden of analytical gradients and explicit model dependence. It leverages the qualitative insight from the model-based analysis: the optimal traversal command generally increases when the energy `V` is low (indicating good regulation) and decreases when `V` is high (indicating a need for more regulation authority). The model-free learning law is `v_t,k+1 = v_t,k - b_3 * g(k_e * V_k)`, where `g(ζ)` is a scalar function. This function is designed to increase `v_t` if the normalized energy `ζ` is below an admissible band `[ζ_min, ζ_max]`, decrease `v_t` if `ζ` is above the band, and leave it unchanged within the band. This approach only requires the spatial profile of the recorded energy `V_k(l)` from the previous rollout.

**Computational Efficiency**
A key aspect of VIP's methodology is its computational efficiency. By performing functional optimization and pointwise scalar updates of the traversal command `v_t(l)`, the computational complexity per learning iteration is O(n), where `n` is the number of spatial discretization points. This is contrasted with traditional trajectory optimization methods that can have superlinear complexity due to solving coupled, high-dimensional problems. Memory complexity is also O(n).

**Implementation Modes**
The framework supports two modes of operation:
- **Model-in-the-loop (Offline):** A simulator or prediction model generates the energy profile for learning. This mode is suitable for initial convergence or when physical trials are expensive.
- **Robot-in-the-loop (Online):** The physical robot or swarm executes a traversal, records the energy profile, and then updates the traversal command for the next repeated traversal. This allows for continuous improvement and adaptation to real-world dynamics and uncertainties.

**Underlying Techniques:**
The methodology integrates several techniques, including the use of virtual tubes for defining safe navigation corridors, Kernel Density Estimation for multi-robot density fields, geometric decomposition of control commands, and Lyapunov-like functions for energy definition and analysis. The transformation between time and spatial domains (arc length) is central to formulating the problem as a spatial-domain optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents theoretical analyses, extensive simulations, and real-world experiments to demonstrate the capabilities of the VIP framework.

**Theoretical Findings:**
- **Unified Energy-Variation Formulation:** The paper successfully formulated single-robot path following and robotic swarm density regulation within a unified energy-based framework. This demonstrates that diverse task-dependent performance objectives can be represented by a common energy functional, allowing a consistent learning principle.
- **Computational Complexity:** The model-free VIP update law was shown to have a per-iteration computational complexity of O(n), where 'n' is the number of spatial discretization points. This linear scaling is a significant advantage over many conventional trajectory optimization methods that exhibit superlinear growth in computational burden.
- **Convergence Properties:**
 - For the model-based VIP, it was theoretically proven that the cost functional `J(v_t,k)` is monotonically non-increasing and convergent, with the L2-norm of the functional gradient `F_n,k` converging to zero, provided appropriate learning gain conditions.
 - For the model-free VIP, it was demonstrated that the cost sequence `J(v_t,k)` is monotonically non-increasing, and the L2-norm of the approximate gradient function `g_k` converges to zero. This implies that the learned energy approaches a pre-defined admissible band, which represents a neighborhood approximation of the optimal solution.

**Simulation Results:**
- **Single-Robot Autonomous Offline Planning:** Simulations in complex, obstacle-dense random forest environments (40m × 20m × 5m maps) showed that VIP enabled a single drone to navigate successfully. The learned traversal command adjusted speed based on local geometry and tracking requirements: faster in open areas and slower in narrow passages or turns, consistent with balancing traversal time and path adherence.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** The planning time was 0.28ms for 50 waypoints and 0.52ms for 100 waypoints, confirming the O(n) complexity. Average speed reached 2.98m/s (max 5m/s).
 - **Comparisons:** In a larger 230m × 200m × 30m environment, VIP was compared against MPCC and polynomial-based methods. While traversal time improvements varied (e.g., -1.31% to +1.37% vs. MPCC, -1.57% to +6.80% vs. Polynomial), VIP consistently achieved significant reductions in average replanning time (47.4-87.7% reductions compared to benchmarks), highlighting its efficiency for real-time applications requiring frequent updates.
- **Robotic Swarm Online Learning in a Virtual Tube:**
 - **Effectiveness:** In 2D and 3D virtual tube environments, VIP enabled robotic swarms (30 and 20 agents, respectively) to move faster along the tube while maintaining bounded spatial distribution and tube constraints. This demonstrated that macroscopic traversal-speed learning can non-conflictually coexist with microscopic distribution regulation.
 - **Traversal Time Reduction:** For a 30-agent swarm in a 2D tube, traversal time decreased over learning iterations, converging to a stable, lower value (e.g., from an initially conservative profile). This confirmed the iterative learning capabilities for collective motion.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- **Offline Flight in Unknown Environment (LiDAR-based Quadcopter):** A LiDAR-based quadcopter successfully traversed an approximately 150m outdoor field with trees and buildings. The drone achieved maximum speeds of about 10m/s. The onboard computation time for VIP was 0.93ms for 100 path points on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin, validating its lightweight nature and real-time responsiveness when integrated with onboard perception and localization modules. Repeated trials demonstrated repeatability and robustness to real-world uncertainties.
- **Online Learning with a Lifting-Wing Quadcopter:** Experiments on a lifting-wing quadcopter, a platform with complex aerodynamics, demonstrated VIP's applicability to systems with non-trivial dynamics without requiring explicit models. Traversal time was reduced from 77.4s to 42.7s during hardware-in-the-loop simulations, and further refined from 42.1s to 41.3s over two real-world iterations. This indicated effective transferability from simulation to physical platforms and continuous online refinement.
- **Swarm Online Learning in a Virtual Tube (Three Quadcopters):** Three quadcopters successfully flew in an outdoor virtual tube. VIP progressively increased the swarm's common traversal speed (from 0.5m/s to 2m/s) over five iterations, reducing traversal time from 241s to 82s. This confirmed VIP's ability to improve swarm-level traversal efficiency by learning a common profile, while the microscopic virtual-tube controller handled local spacing and boundary constraints. This supports the macro-micro decomposition strategy for swarm planning.

Collectively, the findings demonstrate that VIP can efficiently generate and iteratively improve motion plans for different planning objectives and robotic platforms. Its O(n) computational complexity, model-free nature, and ability to operate in both offline and online modes are supported by both theoretical analysis and empirical evidence across various complex scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VIP framework presents a motion planning methodology with several significant contributions and potential impacts on autonomous robotics:

1. **Unified and Generalizable Planning:** By formulating diverse planning objectives (single-robot path following, robotic swarm density regulation) within a common energy-variation framework, VIP offers a unified mathematical and algorithmic approach. This generality has the potential to simplify the design and integration of planning systems for a wide range of robotic tasks, reducing the need for specialized algorithms for each specific scenario or platform.

2. **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** The O(n) per-iteration computational complexity of VIP, achieved through functional optimization and pointwise updates, is a notable advancement. This efficiency is critical for deploying advanced planning capabilities on computationally constrained onboard robotic platforms and for enabling real-time replanning in dynamic, large-scale, or multi-robot environments, where traditional methods often become intractable.

3. **Robust Model-Free and Online Learning:** The model-free nature of VIP, which learns directly from recorded execution data, alleviates the dependency on accurate dynamic models and complex analytical gradient computations. This makes the framework more robust to modeling uncertainties, disturbances, and unmodeled dynamics inherent in real-world physical systems. The capability for robot-in-the-loop online learning allows continuous improvement of plans through repeated executions, facilitating adaptation and self-optimization in deployment.

4. **Scalability for Robotic Swarms:** VIP's approach to swarm planning, by learning a common macroscopic traversal command while relying on microscopic controllers for local regulation and safety, offers a scalable solution to multi-robot coordination. This macro-micro decomposition bypasses the computational bottleneck of optimizing individual trajectories for large swarms, paving the way for more complex and larger-scale swarm deployments in practical applications.

5. **Practical Applicability:** The extensive validation through both simulations and diverse real-world experiments on multiple platforms (LiDAR-based quadcopters, lifting-wing quadcopters, multi-quadcopter swarms) under various complex outdoor conditions underscores the practical deployability of VIP. The results demonstrate its ability to operate effectively alongside real-time perception, localization, and control systems, tolerating practical challenges such as sensing noise and actuation constraints.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework lays a foundation for future work in areas such as within-rollout adaptation, planning in dynamic environments, explicit incorporation of uncertainty-aware safety constraints, and scaling to even larger robot swarms with fully onboard learning. The principles established by VIP could also inspire new ways to integrate learning into other aspects of robotic autonomy.

In summary, VIP represents a step in addressing critical computational, scalability, and model-dependency challenges in robotic navigation. Its unified, efficient, and model-free learning approach has the potential to broaden the scope of autonomous robotic deployment in complex and dynamic real-world settings.
