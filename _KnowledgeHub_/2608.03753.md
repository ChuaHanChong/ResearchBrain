# GORDON: Graph-based Object-centric Rewards for Decomposition of Long-Horizon Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The GORDON framework generates dense, object-centric rewards from action-free video demonstrations, allowing for automatic subtask decomposition in long-horizon manipulation tasks. This approach achieves an average full-task success rate of 74.4% on four complex manipulation tasks, surpassing baseline methods by 35.3 percentage points.

## Problem

- Manual engineering of dense reward functions for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks is difficult and costly, while sparse rewards provide insufficient guidance.
- Reward learning from visual demonstrations is often brittle to task-irrelevant visual variations (e.g., background, viewpoint) and fails to focus on semantic object-state transitions.
- Existing methods for long-horizon tasks often require expensive action-labeled demonstrations, explicit task sketches, or predefined subgoals, limiting their applicability.

## Method

- Develops **GORDON**, a framework that learns dense, object-centric rewards from action-free video demonstrations using a ==Graph Neural Network==.
- Introduces an ==activity-aware weighted pooling== mechanism to focus on task-relevant object interactions, masking out incidental robot motion from graph embeddings.
- Automatically discovers subtask boundaries for long-horizon tasks by analyzing the ==temporal gradient of the learned reward==, enabling the training of sequential subpolicies.

## Results

- Achieved an average full-task success rate of **74.4%** on long-horizon manipulation tasks, representing a **35.3 percentage point** improvement over the best learned baseline.
- Demonstrated that the learned full-task reward profiles accurately identify subtask boundaries, enabling automatic task decomposition that aligns with ground-truth stages.
- GORDON's activity-aware robot masking enhanced reward discrimination (lowest "rho" of **0.629**) and robustness to bounding-box detection noise, outperforming variants without masking.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Activity-aware weighted pooling on object-centric graph representations effectively filters distracting robot motion and focuses on task-relevant object state changes for robust reward learning.
> - Combining temporal alignment with structural reconstruction in self-supervised pretraining yields a latent space that captures both task progression and scene structure, which is essential for dense rewards.
> - The temporal profile of a single learned long-horizon reward intrinsically contains enough information to automatically identify and segment constituent subtasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a significant challenge in reinforcement learning (RL) for robotics: enabling agents to acquire complex, long-horizon manipulation skills. Traditional RL approaches struggle with these tasks due to the difficulty of manually engineering dense reward functions, the limited guidance provided by sparse task-success signals, and the high cost associated with manually annotating subtasks in demonstrations.

A promising direction in this field is learning reward functions directly from visual demonstrations. However, existing methods that learn rewards from raw image pixels often exhibit brittleness. They can be sensitive to task-irrelevant visual variations such as background appearance, changing viewpoints, texture, and the visually dominant motion of the robot itself, which does not always directly correlate with task progression. Furthermore, in long-horizon tasks, success depends on a sequence of ordered object-state transitions rather than merely achieving proximity to a single final visual goal.

Object-centric representations have emerged as an alternative, providing an abstraction that can reduce sensitivity to irrelevant visual variations by focusing on detected entities and their relations. While prior object-centric reward learning methods, such as GraphIRL, have improved robustness by concentrating on object interactions, they have not explicitly distinguished between objects that drive semantic task progress and incidental robot motion. This distinction is particularly relevant in manipulation, where robot movement can visually dominate a scene while object-object relations define the true state of the task.

For long-horizon tasks specifically, previous approaches include hierarchical reinforcement learning, sequential policy learning, and imitation-based segmentation. These methods often require action-labeled demonstrations, explicit task sketches, or predefined sparse rewards. More recent visual decomposition methods can discover subgoals from phase changes in pretrained visual representations, but the detected boundaries may be tied to a fixed image-level embedding space, whose distance changes do not necessarily correspond to semantically meaningful subtask transitions. Other approaches leverage language models or vision-language models for task decomposition or reward generation, which introduce dependencies on additional priors or supervision sources.

This work, GORDON, positions itself by addressing these limitations. It proposes a graph-based object-centric reward learning framework that learns dense rewards from *action-free video demonstrations*, thereby reducing annotation costs. It specifically tackles the brittleness of pixel-based methods and the lack of task-relevant focus in simpler object-centric approaches by introducing an activity-aware weighted pooling mechanism. This mechanism emphasizes task-relevant objects while masking robot-dominated motion, aligning the learned reward more closely with semantic task progress. Crucially, for long-horizon tasks, GORDON uses the temporal profile of this learned reward to *automatically discover subtask boundaries* without manual segmentation or external language priors. This enables the training of subtask-specific rewards and specialized policies, which can then be composed sequentially to solve complex long-horizon tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to learn complex, long-horizon manipulation skills effectively using reinforcement learning, drawing supervision from readily available action-free video demonstrations.

The specific motivations and objectives driving the development of GORDON are:

- **Overcome Reward Design Complexity:** Manual design of dense reward functions for complex, multi-stage manipulation tasks is notoriously difficult and time-consuming. The authors aim to automate this process by learning rewards directly from demonstrations.
- **Address Sparse Reward Limitations:** Standard RL often relies on sparse rewards (e.g., a signal only upon task completion), which provide insufficient gradient information for agents to learn effective policies in high-dimensional, long-horizon settings. A key objective is to generate dense, continuous reward signals.
- **Reduce Annotation Cost:** Manual annotation of expert actions or subtask boundaries in demonstrations is expensive and labor-intensive. The research aims to leverage action-free video demonstrations, which are easier to acquire, for reward learning.
- **Enhance Robustness to Visual Variations:** Rewards learned directly from raw pixels are often sensitive to task-irrelevant visual factors like background changes, viewpoint shifts, object textures, and the visually prominent but often uninformative motion of the robot manipulator. An objective is to develop a representation that is robust to these factors.
- **Focus on Semantic Task Progress:** In long-horizon tasks, successful execution relies on a sequence of meaningful object-state transitions. Current methods often struggle to capture this semantic progression effectively. The motivation is to create a reward function that specifically emphasizes and tracks these task-relevant object changes and relations.
- **Enable Automatic Task Decomposition:** Long-horizon tasks naturally consist of multiple sequential subtasks. Manually segmenting demonstrations into these subtasks or defining subgoals is tedious. A core objective is to develop a mechanism that can automatically discover and leverage this inherent hierarchical structure from unsegmented demonstrations.
- **Facilitate Sequential Policy Learning:** Once subtasks are identified, the goal is to train specialized policies for each subtask with localized, more discriminative rewards, and then compose these policies sequentially to solve the complete long-horizon task more reliably than a single end-to-end policy.

By pursuing these objectives, the authors aim to advance the state of robot learning towards more autonomous and generalizable manipulation capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GORDON, the proposed Graph-based Object-centric Rewards for DecompositiON framework, consists of several interconnected components designed to learn dense, task-aligned rewards from action-free video demonstrations.

**Problem Formulation:** The method addresses learning a reward function, `r(s)`, for robotic manipulation tasks from a set of demonstration videos, `D = {V^k}`. The core idea is to learn an embedding function, `φ(·)`, that maps observations to a latent space where distances to demonstrated goal-state embeddings reflect progress toward task completion.

**A. Graph-Based Object-Centric Reward Learning:**
1. **Scene Graph Construction:**
 - For each video frame `I_t`, a set of `N_t` object instances is extracted using object detectors or simulator annotations.
 - The scene is represented as a fully connected graph `G_t = (V_t, E_t)`.
 - **Nodes (`v_i ∈ V_t`):** Each node corresponds to a detected object and is described by its semantic class and bounding-box geometry (2D or 3D, depending on the environment).
 - **Edges (`e_ij ∈ E_t`):** Edges encode pairwise spatial relations between objects, represented by Euclidean distances between their bounding boxes. This graph structure explicitly models relational information, distinguishing it from approaches that directly use bounding-box feature vectors.

2. **Graph Embedding Function (`φ(·)`):**
 - `φ` is composed of a Graph Neural Network (GNN) encoder, a permutation-invariant weighted pooling operator, and an MLP projection head.
 - The GNN processes node and edge features through message passing, producing node embeddings `{h_{t,i}}` for each object `i` in frame `t`.
 - These node embeddings are aggregated into a graph-level pooled representation `h_t` using a weighted average (Equation 1).
 - Finally, an MLP projection head converts `h_t` into the compact graph embedding `z_t = φ(G_t)`.

3. **Weighted Graph Pooling:** This mechanism is introduced to focus the representation on task-relevant scene structure.
 - **Pooling Weights (`w_{t,i}`):** Defined by Equation 2, which incorporates an `active_{t,i}` indicator and a `robot_{t,i}` indicator.
 - **Activity Indicator (`active_{t,i}`):** A binary value indicating whether object `i` is active (i.e., has significantly moved). It's computed from the temporal displacement of the bounding-box center, normalized by the box diagonal to ensure scale-invariance (Equation 3). Activity estimates are smoothed over time, and once an object becomes active, it remains active for the rest of the episode. The parameter `α` controls the relative weight assigned to active non-robot objects.
 - **Robot Indicator (`robot_{t,i}`):** A binary value indicating if the node corresponds to the robot. Robot nodes are masked by zeroing their features and removing incident edges before message passing, and are assigned a zero pooling weight.
 - **Purpose:** This pooling strategy ensures the graph representation is primarily driven by non-robot objects and their relations, and upweights entities whose state changes indicate task progress, thereby masking robot-dominated motion.

4. **Self-Supervised Pretraining:** The graph encoder is trained using two complementary self-supervised objectives:
 - **Temporal Alignment (L_tcc):** Uses the Temporal Cycle Consistency (TCC) loss to encourage embeddings of similar stages across different demonstrations to be close in the latent space, capturing task progression independently of timing variations.
 - **Structural Reconstruction (L_rec):** A lightweight attention-based object decoder `ψ(·)` predicts object bounding-box coordinates and class logits from the latent graph representation. The `L_rec` loss (Equation 4) combines penalties for bounding-box coordinate errors (L1), centroid errors, Generalized Intersection over Union (GIoU) errors, and object-class prediction errors.
 - **Combined Objective:** The final training objective `L = L_tcc + λ_rec L_rec` (Equation 5) balances temporal alignment with the preservation of object-level scene structure.

**B. Reinforcement Learning with Learned Rewards:**
1. **Dense Full-Task Reward (`˜r_full_t`):**
 - After pretraining, the graph encoder is frozen.
 - A goal embedding `z_full_g` is computed by averaging the embeddings of the final frames from all demonstration trajectories (Equation 6).
 - The dense full-task reward is defined as the negative squared Euclidean distance between the current scene embedding `z_t` and the average goal embedding `z_full_g` (Equation 7). Higher values indicate closer proximity to the demonstrated goal configuration.

2. **Terminal Bonus:** To address ambiguity near task completion, a lightweight MLP classifier `c(·)` is trained on top of the frozen graph embeddings to output a goal probability `p_t`. Frames within the final `q%` of demonstrations are labeled as positive goal states for training this classifier, providing a more robust signal than just the very last frame.
 - The final learned reward `r_full_t = ˜r_full_t + β · I[p_t ≥ τ_c]` (Equation 8) combines the dense distance-based shaping term with a classifier-gated terminal bonus (`β` controls the bonus weight, `τ_c` is a threshold).
 - For short-horizon tasks, this `r_full_t` is used directly to train a policy with RL.

**C. Automatic Subtask Discovery and Policy Composition (for Long-Horizon Tasks):**
1. **Subtask Discovery:**
 - For long-horizon tasks, the trained full-task reward model's temporal profile is analyzed.
 - Candidate subtask transition frames are identified by detecting significant changes in the reward gradient, which correspond to substantial shifts in the underlying object-centric scene configuration. The number of subtasks `M` is automatically determined.
 - This process segments the original demonstrations into `M` ordered subtask-specific datasets.

2. **Subtask Reward and Policy Learning:**
 - For each discovered subtask segment `m`, a separate subtask-specific encoder and reward model are trained using only the corresponding demonstration segments.
 - A local goal embedding for subtask `m` is computed from the final frames of its specific demonstration segments, defining a local reward `r^(m)`. This provides more discriminative feedback within each phase than the full-task reward.
 - A dedicated policy `π^(m)` is then optimized using `r^(m)` for each subtask.

3. **Subpolicy Training and Composition:**
 - At deployment, a sequential executor switches from policy `π^(m)` to `π^(m+1)` when the current subtask is detected as complete (e.g., via the local reward or a success predicate).
 - To ensure smooth transitions, each subpolicy is trained from perturbed initial states plausible for its phase. Collision-aware motion planning is used only to reposition the robot arm between subtasks.

**Experimental Setup:** The method is evaluated on seven manipulation tasks: one 2D task (MatchRegions from MAGICAL) and six 3D tasks (ManiSkill3 reimplementations of RLBench tasks). These include three short-horizon and four long-horizon tasks. Comparisons are made against hand-crafted environmental rewards (oracle and decomposed oracle), pixel-based (XIRL), and simpler object-centric (GraphIRL) learned reward baselines, as well as a language-guided model-based RL framework (RoboHorizon) for long-horizon tasks. Policies are trained with Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). Object detections are obtained from simulator annotations for reward representation construction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of GORDON on seven manipulation tasks demonstrates its effectiveness in learning dense, semantically aligned rewards and leveraging them for long-horizon task decomposition.

**Learned Reward Profiles:**
- Analysis of the full-task reward profiles for long-horizon tasks (e.g., PutItemInDrawer, PutShoesInBox, EmptyContainer) reveals distinct stage-like transitions.
- The boundaries inferred from significant changes in the reward gradient closely align with simulator-annotated subtask endings, even though these annotations are not used during reward learning. This indicates that GORDON's learned full-task reward profile inherently captures meaningful semantic task progression and enables automatic subtask discovery from unsegmented demonstrations.

**Policy Evaluation Across Tasks (Table I & II):**
- **Short-horizon tasks:** GORDON achieves high success rates, comparable to the environmental oracle policy. For instance, it scores 91.2% on MatchRegions, 92.5% on TakeUmbrellaOut, and 98.8% on PutRubbishInBin. This demonstrates effective transfer of the learned object-centric reward to policy training in simpler settings.
- **Long-horizon tasks:** This is where GORDON shows substantial advantages.
 - It achieves an average full-task success rate of 74.4% across the four challenging long-horizon tasks (PutItemInDrawer, PutShoesInBox, PutShoesOutBox, EmptyContainer).
 - This performance represents a 35.3 percentage point (p.p.) improvement over RoboHorizon, identified as the strongest learned or language-guided baseline.
 - It also surpasses the decomposed environmental-reward oracle by 25.4 p.p., indicating that the automatically discovered subtask structure and learned rewards are more effective than even manually engineered subtask rewards in some cases.
 - Crucially, single full-task environmental rewards, XIRL (pixel-based), and GraphIRL (simpler object-centric) baselines consistently fail to achieve full task completion (0% average success rate) in long-horizon tasks.
- **Cumulative Subtask Success Rates (Table II):**
 - Baselines, including the single full-task environmental reward, XIRL, and GraphIRL, often manage to complete the first stage of certain long-horizon tasks (e.g., opening a drawer or box) but experience sharp drops in success rates for subsequent cumulative stages. This suggests their reward signals provide insufficient stage-wise guidance for full task completion.
 - In contrast, GORDON maintains high conditional success rates across later stages of long-horizon tasks (e.g., 94.0% for the third subtask of PutShoesInBox given completion of the second). This indicates that the automatically discovered subtask structure, combined with subtask-specific rewards, provides reliable supervision for sequential policy learning, enabling high performance even in complex, multi-stage manipulation scenarios.
 - Remaining failures typically occur in the most demanding long-horizon tasks, such as PutShoesOutBox (requiring precise object placement outside a container) and EmptyContainer (requiring reliable sequential execution over multiple objects).

**Analysis and Ablation Studies (PutShoesInBox task):**
- **Reward Discrimination (Table III):** GORDON with robot masking achieved the strongest reward discrimination (lowest `ρ` value of 0.629), indicating a clear separation between successful and unsuccessful trajectories. This outperforms its 'robot included' variant (`ρ=0.760`), GraphIRL variants, and XIRL.
 - **Robustness:** Object-centric methods (GraphIRL and GORDON) are unaffected by visual appearance shifts, unlike pixel-based XIRL. GORDON's robot-masked variant maintains stability under moderate bounding-box detection noise (`ρ=0.583`), while the 'robot included' variant degrades (`ρ=0.985`), suggesting that robot masking enhances robustness to noisy detections.
- **Subtask-Level Reward Transfer (Table IV):** The dense learned rewards alone were sufficient for the initial "OpenBox" subtask but insufficient for contact-rich "PlaceFirstShoe" and "PlaceSecondShoe" stages. Adding a terminal bonus significantly improved subtask learning. The learned classifier-gated bonus used in GORDON yielded the best composed-policy performance (81.5% for PutShoesInBox), outperforming an oracle environmental bonus (58.0%). This highlights the complementary roles of dense shaping and a precise terminal bonus for complex subtask completion.
- **Training Objective Ablation (Table V):** Combining both temporal cycle-consistency (TCC) and reconstruction losses yielded the best reward discrimination (`ρ=0.629`). Using either objective alone degraded performance, suggesting they provide complementary supervision for learning a task-relevant latent representation.
- **RLBench vs. ManiSkill:** The ManiSkill3 reimplementation matched RLBench's performance on TakeUmbrellaOut (100% success) but significantly reduced training time and steps, validating ManiSkill3 as a more efficient platform for further evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GORDON framework represents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation and reinforcement learning, with several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Advancement in Long-Horizon Task Learning:** GORDON directly addresses the critical challenge of learning long-horizon manipulation skills, which has historically been a bottleneck for autonomous robotic systems. By demonstrating successful policy learning across complex, multi-stage tasks, it extends the practical capabilities of robots.
- **Reduced Reliance on Manual Reward Engineering:** The framework significantly reduces the burden of manual reward design, a notoriously difficult and time-consuming process for complex tasks. By learning dense, semantically meaningful rewards directly from demonstrations, GORDON moves towards more autonomous and less human-dependent robot learning pipelines.
- **Efficient Use of Demonstrations:** GORDON leverages action-free video demonstrations, which are far easier and cheaper to collect than action-labeled data or demonstrations with manual subtask annotations. This increases the accessibility of high-quality supervision for robot learning.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The use of object-centric graph representations, combined with activity-aware pooling and robot masking, makes the learned rewards inherently more robust to irrelevant visual distractions (like background changes, lighting variations) and incidental robot motion compared to pixel-based methods. This improved robustness has the potential to lead to better generalization across varying environments and object appearances.
- **Automatic Task Decomposition:** A key innovation is the ability to automatically discover subtask boundaries from the temporal profile of the learned reward, without any explicit prior knowledge, language models, or human segmentation. This automates a crucial step in tackling complex sequential tasks, making hierarchical learning more feasible and less reliant on manual oversight.
- **Improved Policy Learning Efficiency:** By decomposing long-horizon tasks into subtasks and training specialized policies with subtask-specific rewards, GORDON provides more targeted and discriminative feedback. This can lead to more efficient and reliable policy optimization compared to attempting to train a single policy for the entire complex task with a single, potentially ambiguous, full-task reward.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework's modularity, combining GNNs, self-supervised learning, and hierarchical policy composition, offers a strong foundation for future research. This could include exploring different graph representations, integrating more advanced object detection and tracking, or applying the automatic decomposition principle to even longer and more diverse manipulation tasks. The open-sourced ManiSkill3 implementation of benchmarks also facilitates reproducible research.
- **Broader Impact on Embodied AI:** The advancements in learning complex manipulation sequences from demonstrations contribute to the broader goal of embodied AI, where intelligent agents can learn to interact skillfully with the physical world. The ability to autonomously learn and decompose complex behaviors is a step towards more capable and adaptive robotic systems in various real-world applications.
