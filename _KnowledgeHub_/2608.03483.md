# Continue or Replan? Bernoulli-Continuation Policy Learning for Adaptive Horizon Execution

> [!summary] Summary
> The Bernoulli-Continuation Policy (BCP) framework enables adaptive execution-horizon selection in chunk-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, improving robotic manipulation success rates by up to 11.08% on low-success tasks while maintaining computational efficiency. This lightweight, plug-and-play policy learns when to replan in a single forward pass, keeping the base VLA frozen.

## Problem

- Chunk-based VLA models often use fixed execution horizons, leading to periodic, task-agnostic replanning that can misalign with critical manipulation stages and cause failures.
- Learning an adaptive execution horizon policy is challenged by the ordinal and prefix-sharing structure of candidate horizons, which standard classification ignores.
- Optimal execution horizons are unidentifiable per-chunk, and simple success/failure rewards in reinforcement learning can lead to policies that favor excessively short horizons, incurring high computational costs.

## Method

- Introduces the **Bernoulli-Continuation Policy (BCP)**, a lightweight, plug-and-play framework that attaches a compact ==Bernoulli-Continuation Head== to a frozen VLA model.
- The Bernoulli-Continuation Head models horizon selection as a sequence of Bernoulli continuation decisions, explicitly capturing the ordinal and prefix-sharing relationships between candidate horizons.
- Utilizes ==reinforcement learning== with a novel ==Replanning-Efficiency Reward (RER)== to optimize BCP, balancing task success with computational efficiency by rewarding successful, fewer VLA-call trajectories and penalizing inefficient ones.

## Results

- BCP improved the average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0's 13 low-success tasks by **+11.08%** (from **75.15%** to **86.23%**) for LingBot-VLA, and generalized to randomized settings with a **+9.92%** gain.
- On LIBERO and LIBERO-PRO benchmarks, BCP achieved **98.7%** average success rate on LIBERO tasks and a **+6.8%** gain over the baseline on LIBERO-PRO, demonstrating robustness.
- Real-robot experiments on AGIBOT G1 showed BCP increased grasping success from **74%** to **92%** and hanging mug success from **44%** to **84%**, while incurring a negligible **2.03 ms** per-query inference overhead and reducing overall estimated runtime.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task success in chunk-based VLA systems is heavily influenced by the strategic timing of replanning, with fixed horizons often leading to critical manipulation stages being executed with stale observations.
> - An inductive bias reflecting the ordinal and prefix-sharing nature of execution horizons, implemented via Bernoulli continuation decisions, is crucial for effective adaptive horizon learning.
> - A carefully designed reward function that explicitly trades off task success and replanning efficiency is necessary to train adaptive horizon policies that are both effective and computationally responsible.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, particularly in robotic manipulation. Recent VLA systems commonly employ "action chunking," where the model predicts a sequence of future actions (a chunk) rather than a single next action. This approach aims to reduce the frequency of computationally intensive model inferences and improve temporal consistency in robot movements.

However, a prevalent practice in these chunk-based VLA models is to execute a fixed number of actions, termed the "execution horizon," before re-observing the environment and replanning. This design leads to a periodic replanning schedule that operates independently of the robot's progress or the specific demands of the task.

This research identifies a limitation where this fixed-horizon approach can lead to failures. Specifically, if a critical manipulation stage, such as a precise grasp or placement, occurs without a replanning boundary immediately preceding it, the robot may execute this stage based on a "stale" action chunk derived from an earlier observation. This can result in accumulated errors and task failure.

Existing approaches to adaptive horizon execution include methods based on hand-designed reliability proxies (e.g., entropy or attention, which may require multiple chunk samplings) or dedicated mechanisms like cross-horizon consensus or closed-loop verification (which can involve complex procedures). This work positions itself as an alternative by proposing a lightweight, plug-and-play policy that directly learns an execution-horizon distribution in a single forward pass, aiming for efficiency and simplicity.

Furthermore, while reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied to improve VLA execution by fine-tuning the full VLA model or its action generation, this paper applies RL to a distinct problem: deciding *when* to stop executing a chunk and replan, rather than *how* to generate actions. This differentiates it from methods that update large action models, potentially incurring substantial training costs. The proposed method keeps the base VLA frozen, focusing on an orthogonal aspect of policy optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for adaptive execution-horizon selection in chunk-based VLA models, allowing robots to dynamically decide how many predicted actions to execute before replanning. This aims to mitigate failures caused by the misalignment between fixed replanning schedules and critical manipulation stages.

The motivation for this objective stems from a identified limitation in current chunk-based VLA systems. In such systems, the execution horizon is typically fixed throughout an episode, rendering replanning a periodic and task-agnostic event. This fixed schedule cannot guarantee that a replanning boundary will fall precisely before a precision-critical phase of a task, such as a delicate grasp or fine alignment. Consequently, the robot might be forced to execute these critical stages using an outdated understanding of the environment, leading to errors and task failures.

The authors demonstrate this limitation through a "phase-shift experiment" on RoboTwin 2.0. By varying the initial phase (ϕ) at which a fixed 50-step execution horizon begins, they show that the success rate of the LingBot-VLA model can vary by more than 11.30% across 50 tasks. This variation is attributed to whether replanning boundaries fortuitously align with critical manipulation moments. For instance, in a "Place Dual Shoes" task, one initial phase might lead to an accurate grasp and success, while another, with identical initial state, might result in an inaccurate gripper closure and failure, simply due to the timing of replanning. This indicates that task success is not solely dependent on the length of the execution horizon but on the strategic placement of replanning opportunities.

Learning an adaptive execution-horizon policy presents three specific challenges:
1. **Ordinal and Prefix-Sharing Structure**: Candidate execution horizons (e.g., 20, 30, 40 steps) are inherently ordered, and longer horizons subsume shorter ones (e.g., 40 steps includes the first 20 or 30 steps). A standard classification approach would treat these as independent categories, ignoring this structural relationship.
2. **Unidentifiable Per-Chunk Supervision**: The optimal execution horizon for any given chunk is not directly observable. The consequence of a horizon decision is coupled with subsequent chunks and decisions, meaning a single trajectory outcome could result from various sequences of horizon choices. This makes it difficult to assign unique ground-truth stopping labels to individual chunks for supervised learning.
3. **Success-Only Reward Limitation**: When using reinforcement learning, a binary success-or-failure reward alone can incentivize overly short horizons. While short horizons might lead to successful task completion by ensuring frequent replanning, they also incur excessive VLA calls, wasting computational resources without being explicitly penalized. This creates a trade-off between task success and computational efficiency.

To address these challenges, the research proposes a framework designed to make the execution horizon a learned, per-chunk decision, thereby enabling replanning boundaries to be placed where they are most beneficial for task execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces the Bernoulli-Continuation Policy (BCP), a lightweight and plug-and-play framework designed to adaptively select the execution horizon for VLA models while keeping the base VLA frozen.

**Problem Formulation:**
The system considers a pre-trained VLA model that, given an observation \(o_t\) and language instruction \(\ell\), predicts a fixed-length action chunk \(a_t = (a^t_1, \dots, a^t_H)\), where \(H\) is the prediction horizon. Instead of executing a fixed horizon \(E\), an execution-horizon policy \(\pi_\theta(\cdot | s_t)\) selects an adaptive execution horizon \(E_t\) from a discrete set of candidates \(\mathcal{E} = \{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_M\}\), where \(1 \le e_1 < \dots < e_M \le H\). The robot then executes the prefix \(a^t_{1:E_t}\) of the predicted chunk and queries the VLA again for a new action chunk and horizon decision. This means the VLA is responsible for action generation, and BCP is responsible for when to replan.

**Bernoulli-Continuation Head:**
The Bernoulli-Continuation Head is a compact module attached to the frozen VLA. It leverages the representations already computed by the VLA during action chunk prediction, avoiding an additional VLA forward pass. Its inputs include:
- Visual-language tokens \(F_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_v}\) for scene and instruction context.
- For each action step \(j\) within the chunk, the denoised action \(a^t_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a}\) and its final-step action-velocity feature \(u^t_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_u}\).

These action-level features are concatenated and projected, along with the visual-language tokens, into a shared \(d\)-dimensional space. A learnable [CLS] token is prepended to form an input sequence \(X_t\), which is then fed into a lightweight Transformer encoder. The output corresponding to the [CLS] token is mapped to \(M-1\) logits \(z_t = (z^t_1, \dots, z^t_{M-1})\).

Instead of using a standard softmax classifier, which treats horizons as independent classes, BCP models horizon selection as a sequence of Bernoulli continuation decisions. Each logit \(z^t_i\) is passed through a sigmoid function to yield a continue probability \(p^t_i = \sigma(z^t_i)\). Here, \(p^t_i\) represents the probability of continuing execution beyond horizon \(e_i\) to at least \(e_{i+1}\). The probability of selecting a specific horizon \(e_k\) is then formulated as:
\[
\pi_\theta(E_t = e_k | s_t) = \begin{cases} \left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1} p^t_i\right) (1 - p^t_k), & 1 \le k < M \\ \prod_{i=1}^{M-1} p^t_i, & k = M \end{cases}
\]
This factorization imposes an ordinal, prefix-sharing inductive bias. For example, executing for 40 steps and 50 steps share the same initial 40 actions. The decision mechanism reflects this by requiring successive "continue" decisions, ensuring that adjacent horizons have probabilistically linked choices.

**RL with Replanning-Efficiency Reward:**
Given the unobservability of optimal per-chunk horizons and the temporal coupling of decisions, BCP is trained using reinforcement learning, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al. 2024), while keeping the base VLA frozen.

For each task instance, \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) samples \(G-1\) adaptive trajectories. A crucial component is the inclusion of one reference trajectory \(\tau_{ref}\) executed under the fixed-horizon strategy. This reference trajectory anchors the advantage calculation, providing a stable baseline for comparison. Rewards are trajectory-level and broadcast to all decisions within a trajectory. Only the adaptive trajectories update BCP. The policy is optimized using a clipped objective function, where the advantage \(\hat{A}_i\) is group-normalized across the adaptive trajectories and the reference trajectory's reward.

To address the success-efficiency trade-off, a **Replanning-Efficiency Reward (RER)** is introduced. A sparse binary success reward would lead to policies that favor excessively short horizons (many VLA calls) to ensure success. RER addresses this by:
1. **Relative Efficiency Term (\(\eta_i\))**: Defined as \(\tanh(\log(C_{ref}/C_i))\), where \(C_i\) and \(C_{ref}\) are the VLA calls for an adaptive trajectory and the fixed-horizon reference, respectively. \(\eta_i > 0\) indicates fewer VLA calls than the reference.
2. **Final Reward (\(R_i\))**:
 \(R_i = S_i (1 + \delta_+ \max(\eta_i, 0) + \delta_- \min(\eta_i, 0))\)
 Here, \(S_i \in \{0, 1\}\) is the success indicator. This formulation prioritizes task success (a failed trajectory gets zero reward). Among successful trajectories, it rewards those with fewer VLA calls (\(\eta_i > 0\)) and mildly penalizes those with more VLA calls (\(\eta_i < 0\)), using asymmetric coefficients \(\delta_+ > \delta_- > 0\). This encourages BCP to replan when necessary for success but discourages superfluous VLA queries.

The RER also benefits group-based optimization by breaking ties in all-success groups (ranking by efficiency) and providing negative signals to failed adaptive trajectories when the reference succeeds, reducing gradient sparsity.

**Experimental Settings:**
Experiments were conducted on RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO, and LIBERO-PRO. Base VLA policies included LingBot-VLA, ABot-M0, ACT, and \(\pi_{0.5}\). BCP was trained under a "Clean" setting and evaluated under both "Clean" and "Randomized" settings for generalization. Real-world experiments were performed on an AGIBOT G1 robot. Training involved 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs for 300 steps with a group size of 8 and 256 parallel environments. Candidate horizons for RoboTwin 2.0 were (15, 20..., 50), and (2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10) for LIBERO with \(\pi_{0.5}\).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings across simulated and real-world environments, demonstrating the performance and efficiency of the Bernoulli-Continuation Policy (BCP).

**Performance on RoboTwin 2.0:**
- **Low-Success Tasks (13 tasks with <90% SR for LingBot-VLA):** BCP improved the average success rate for LingBot-VLA from 75.15% to 86.23%, an absolute gain of +11.08% under the Clean setting. This suggests that a significant portion of failures in fixed-horizon execution arises from timing misalignment rather than fundamental action generation errors.
- **Overall Performance (All 50 tasks):** For LingBot-VLA, BCP increased the average success rate from 89.88% to 93.94% (+4.06%) under the Clean setting, establishing a benchmark for VLAs.
- **Generalization to Randomized Setting:** Despite being trained only on the Clean setting, BCP generalized to the Randomized setting. For LingBot-VLA, it improved the average success rate on the 13 low-success tasks from 73.54% to 83.46% (+9.92%) and across all 50 tasks from 88.78% to 92.84% (+4.06%). This indicates that BCP learns task-stage-level knowledge about replanning rather than overfitting to specific visual conditions.
- **Base Policy Agnosticism:** Similar performance improvements were observed when BCP was applied to other base policies, ABot-M0 and ACT, across both Clean and Randomized settings.

**Performance on LIBERO and LIBERO-PRO:**
- **LIBERO:** With the \(\pi_{0.5}\) base policy, BCP achieved the highest average success rate of 98.7% across four LIBERO task suites, improving upon \(\pi_{0.5}\)'s 97.0% and outperforming comparison methods like AAC (+1.7%).
- **LIBERO-PRO:** On the more challenging LIBERO-PRO benchmark, which includes stronger position perturbations, BCP improved the average success rate from 30.9% to 37.7%, yielding a +6.8% gain over \(\pi_{0.5}\) and a +2.9% gain over AAC. This demonstrates BCP's robustness under challenging environmental variations.

**Runtime Efficiency:**
- BCP introduced a negligible per-query inference overhead of 2.03 ms for LingBot-VLA.
- While BCP slightly increased the average number of VLA calls (from 5.382 to 5.614), it reduced the average number of executed control steps per episode (from 269.075 to 248.102).
- The net effect was a decrease in overall estimated runtime, from 10.43 s to 10.24 s. This suggests BCP enables selective replanning without sacrificing runtime efficiency.
- Comparison with other adaptive strategies showed BCP achieved a favorable success rate-runtime trade-off, with the lowest runtime among evaluated methods due to the Replanning-Efficiency Reward.

**Ablation Studies (Hanging Mug task):**
- **SFT Policy:** Achieved a baseline success rate of 41%.
- **RL (Fixed-horizon):** Improved to 79%, indicating benefits of RL but with high computational overhead for fine-tuning the full VLA.
- **RL (Softmax Head):** Using a lightweight head with a softmax classifier achieved 78%, showing efficiency gains from a smaller module but similar success to fixed-horizon.
- **RL (BC Head):** Replacing softmax with the Bernoulli-Continuation Head improved success to 83%, highlighting the benefit of explicitly modeling the ordinal, prefix-sharing structure of horizons.
- **RL (BC Head) + RER (complete BCP):** Incorporating the Replanning-Efficiency Reward further raised the success rate to 87%, demonstrating the value of jointly accounting for task success and execution efficiency. The full BCP also achieved the lowest runtime (18.17s) compared to other RL variants.

**Qualitative Examples and Visualizations:**
Visual examples from RoboTwin 2.0 tasks ("Place Dual Shoes," "Place Bread Basket") illustrate how BCP adaptively shortens execution horizons to trigger replanning before critical manipulation stages. This allows the robot to refresh its observation and generate more accurate motions (e.g., corrected placement angle, precise grasping motion), leading to task success where a fixed-horizon strategy failed.

**Real-Robot Experiments (AGIBOT G1):**
- **Grasping Bottle:** BCP increased success from 74% (fixed-chunk) to 92%. The adaptive replanning during approach and manipulation stages provided more accurate grasping poses, preventing slippage.
- **Hanging Mug:** BCP achieved 84% success, a notable improvement over 44% (fixed-chunk) and 48% (AAC). BCP triggered replanning before the hanging stage, enabling more precise alignment of the mug handle with the rack hook.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work addresses a core limitation in the deployment of chunk-based Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation: the reliance on fixed execution horizons that can lead to misalignment between replanning schedules and critical task stages. By demonstrating that replanning *timing*, rather than just horizon length, significantly impacts task success, the research highlights a previously under-addressed failure mode.

The proposed Bernoulli-Continuation Policy (BCP) offers a lightweight, plug-and-play solution. Its design, which keeps the base VLA frozen and introduces a small, trainable head, has several implications:
- **Enhanced Performance**: BCP consistently improved success rates across diverse tasks and benchmarks (RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO, LIBERO-PRO) and on a real robot, with particularly notable gains on precision-sensitive, low-success tasks. This suggests a direct pathway to more robust and reliable robotic systems.
- **Improved Generalization**: The ability of BCP to generalize from clean training environments to randomized settings indicates that it learns fundamental knowledge about when to trust a predicted action chunk and when a fresh observation is critical, rather than merely memorizing task-specific conditions.
- **Computational Efficiency**: BCP's negligible per-query inference overhead and overall reduced runtime (despite slightly more VLA calls) make it a practical solution. It allows for adaptive behavior without incurring a substantial computational cost, which is important for real-world robotic deployment.
- **Principled Adaptive Control**: By formulating horizon selection as a sequence of continue-or-replan decisions and optimizing with a Replanning-Efficiency Reward, the method introduces a principled way to balance task success with computational cost, moving beyond heuristic or hand-tuned approaches.
- **Modularity and Accessibility**: As a plug-and-play framework, BCP can be integrated into existing chunk-based VLA systems without requiring extensive modifications or retraining of the large base models. This could accelerate its adoption and contribute to the development of more generalist robotic policies.

The findings have the potential to impact the broader field of robotic control and VLA research by:
- **Guiding Future VLA Designs**: Emphasizing the importance of adaptive replanning could influence the design principles of future VLA architectures, encouraging dynamic decision-making at inference time.
- **Improving Real-World Robot Reliability**: The demonstrated success on a real robot indicates BCP's applicability to practical robotic systems, potentially leading to more reliable autonomous manipulation in complex environments.
- **Advancing Reinforcement Learning in Robotics**: The novel application of RL for policy timing rather than action generation, coupled with a specialized reward function, contributes to the methodology of applying RL in robotics.

Future work directions include extending adaptive execution-horizon learning to World Action Models, which also generate action chunks for multi-step execution and could similarly benefit from dynamic replanning timing.
