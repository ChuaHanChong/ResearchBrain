# SSC: A Verifiable Structured Representation for Bimanual Manipulation Labelling

> [!summary] Summary
> The Structured Subtask Chain (SSC) offers a verifiable, structured representation for labelling bimanual manipulation demonstrations, combining the flexibility of natural language with machine-processable logic to improve data quality for robotic learning. This system identifies consistency anomalies in existing human annotations, flagging 1.3% of action cells in the BEHAVIOR-1K dataset, and utilizes vision-language models to resolve ambiguous action details, achieving up to 86.23% accuracy on challenging subtasks.

## Problem

- Natural language subtask labels for robotic manipulation demonstrations lack structural invariants, making automated verification and quality control difficult for large datasets.
- Rigid template formats, while structured, often suffer from over-segmentation, reduced readability, and inconsistencies, hindering annotation quality and utility for advanced VLA policies.
- There is a need for a labelling system that can be automatically checked for logical consistency to ensure the integrity and reliability of large-scale demonstration datasets for generalist robotic agents.

## Method

- Developed the **Structured Subtask Chain (SSC)** representation, modeling a manipulation demonstration as a sequence of alternating scene-graph states and actions, each annotated with a Structured Subtask Template (SST) entry.
- Designed a ==three-stage labelling pipeline==: SST construction from raw annotations and trajectory data, a ==state resolution cascade== (trajectory inference, annotation lookup, VL model queries) to resolve ambiguities, and ==consistency checking== against four state-transition invariants.
- Formalized the SST to encapsulate core action components (subject, predicate, object), flexible conditions, a base-motion field, and an after-state scene graph, with seven defined scene-graph configurations for bimanual states.

## Results

- Top-performing VL models like gpt55 and gem35f achieved **86.23%** and **85.51%** accuracy, respectively, on a 'trimmed-hard' subset of challenging bimanual actions, with multi-VLM ensembles showing modest gains up to **89.13%**.
- The consistency checker identified **31** anomalies (approximately **1.3%**) across **2,357** action cells in the BEHAVIOR-1K dataset, categorizing them into annotation-timing faults (I3) and hold/release symmetry violations (I1).
- Enabling the annotation-augmented VL (AVL) mode, which incorporates structured metadata, led to significant accuracy improvements (e.g., **+9.4** percentage points for gpt55 on the hard subset), demonstrating the value of layered information sources for query resolution.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining a structured state-transition model with flexible action descriptions allows for machine verifiability while maintaining human readability and consistency.
> - A hierarchical query resolution cascade, leveraging proprioceptive data, structured metadata, and large Vision-Language Models, effectively disambiguates uncertain action components in manipulation demonstrations.
> - Defining state-transition invariants enables automatic detection of logical inconsistencies and potential errors within human-annotated manipulation datasets, improving overall data quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in large-scale imitation learning for robotic manipulation, particularly for advanced vision-language-action (VLA) generalist policies. These policies, exemplified by models such as π$_{0.7}$ and LingBot-VLA, increasingly rely on demonstration datasets that are pre-segmented into dense, semantically labelled subtasks. The quality and structure of these subtask labels directly influence the effectiveness of downstream applications, including hierarchical policies, scalable automatic checks for VLA training data, and human review processes.

Currently, two primary types of subtask-label representations are prevalent, each with distinct limitations. Natural-language descriptions are readily interpretable by humans but lack structural invariants, making their automatic verification challenging due to inherent linguistic variability. Conversely, rigid template formats, such as the skill annotations in BEHAVIOR-1K, offer structure but are often linguistically over-segmented. This over-segmentation can complicate readability, hinder annotation consistency across tasks, lead to verification issues, and result in key components remaining unfilled in some task instances.

This research proposes the Structured Subtask Chain (SSC) to bridge the gap between these two extremes. It aims to provide a representation that is both structured for machine processing and flexible enough for readability and consistent human annotation. The proposed pipeline complements existing efforts in subtask-conditioned policies that require explicit sub-goals or structured reasoning (e.g., keypose-policy lines), manipulation dataset creation by focusing on "within-subtask" label organization and verification, and subtask labelling tools like VL-based annotators and temporal action segmentation by offering a structured state-transition framework with consistency checks. The work contributes to improving the underlying data infrastructure crucial for the advancement of generalist robotic manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel, verifiable structured representation for labelling bimanual manipulation demonstrations, thereby overcoming the limitations of existing labelling methodologies. This new representation, termed the Structured Subtask Chain (SSC), aims to serve as a middle ground between natural-language descriptions and rigid template formats.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical challenges observed in the current landscape of robotic manipulation research:
- **Support for Advanced Policies**: Contemporary VLA generalist policies and hierarchical control strategies for long-horizon manipulation tasks require detailed and structured subtask labels. These labels inform policy training and enable effective evaluation, yet existing formats often fail to provide the necessary granularity or verifiability.
- **Limitations of Natural Language Labels**: While natural language descriptions are intuitive for human understanding, their inherent linguistic variability makes automated verification difficult. This poses a significant barrier to scaling up data annotation and quality control for large datasets.
- **Drawbacks of Rigid Template Formats**: Existing rigid templates, while structured, often suffer from over-segmentation and overly complex linguistic logic. These issues can lead to inconsistencies across tasks, reduce human readability, and leave crucial fields underspecified or unfilled, further complicating verification and utility. For instance, BEHAVIOR-1K's skill annotations are cited as an example where over-segmentation impacts consistency and completeness.
- **Need for Verifiable Structure**: There is a clear need for a labelling system that can be automatically checked for logical consistency. This is essential for ensuring the integrity and reliability of large-scale demonstration datasets, which are prone to human annotation errors and inconsistencies.
- **Enhancing Readability and Consistency**: The proposed solution aims to maintain human readability by allowing labels to be rendered as natural language, while simultaneously enforcing structural invariants to ensure consistency and facilitate machine processing.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives include:
- Proposing the Structured Subtask Chain (SSC) as a state-transition representation where a demonstration is a sequence of Structured Subtask Template (SST) entries.
- Designing the SST to encapsulate core action components (subject, predicate, object), flexible conditions (adverbial modifiers), a separate base-motion field, and an after-state scene graph.
- Developing a three-stage labelling pipeline that supports vision-language (VL)–assisted functions: rendering SSTs into natural language, automatic verification of the chain against state-transition rules, and content completion for underspecified fields using a query resolution cascade.
- Instantiating and evaluating this pipeline on the BEHAVIOR-1K dataset to demonstrate its capability in logic verification and content completion, and to assess the performance of various VL models as candidate verifiers.
- Identifying and reporting anomalies within existing human annotations through the consistency checking mechanism.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the development of the Structured Subtask Chain (SSC) representation and an associated three-stage labelling pipeline designed for bimanual manipulation demonstrations.

#### 3.1. Framework Formalism: Structured Subtask Chain (SSC)

The core of the representation models a manipulation demonstration as a state-transition system. A demonstration is a sequence of alternating states and actions: $g_0 \xrightarrow{A_1} g_1 \xrightarrow{A_2} g_2 \cdots \xrightarrow{A_{K-1}} g_{K-1}$, where $g_k$ is a scene-graph snapshot and $A_k$ describes structural changes.

- **Structured Subtask Template (SST)**: Each transition is annotated with an SST entry $S_k = (\text{duration}_k, \text{motion}_k, A_k, g_k, Q_k)$.
 - $\text{duration}_k$: A half-open frame range $[s_k, e_k)$ defining the subtask interval.
 - $\text{motion}_k$: Indicates if the robot base translates during the interval (idle or move), inferred from disjoint signals compared to arm actions.
 - $A_k$: An ordered list of "ActionEntry" objects, detailing the observed actions for the transition from $g_{k-1}$ to $g_k$. Each `ActionEntry` includes:
 - **Subject (arm)**: Specifies the acting arm (left, right, coop, unsure).
 - **Predicate**: An action type (grasp, tool_use, contact, idle) combined with an action verb (e.g., pick, place, push).
 - **Object**: The primary target object.
 - **Conditions**: Flexible adverbial modifiers (e.g., spatial or instrumental phrases).
 - $g_k$: The scene-graph snapshot representing the state *after* $S_k$.
 - $Q_k$: A list of "PendingQuery" objects, recording any residual ambiguities in the entry.
- **Scene-Graph State Space**: A scene-graph snapshot $g$ is a triple $( \text{left}(g), \text{right}(g), \text{same}(g) )$.
 - $\text{left}(g), \text{right}(g)$: Object identifiers held by each hand (or $\perp$ for empty).
 - $\text{same}(g)$: A boolean or "unsure" indicator of whether the two hands hold the same physical object.
 - The framework defines seven configurations for the scene graph (E: both empty; L: only left holds; R: only right holds; D: different objects; D$_d$: same-named, confirmed different instances; S: same physical object (co-grasp); D$_?$: same-named, identity unresolved).

#### 3.2. Pipeline Overview (Three Stages)

The SSC pipeline processes each subtask interval in an online, interleaved manner, propagating resolved state information to subsequent steps.

- **Stage 1: SST Construction (Sec. 4.2)**
 - **Label Parsing**: Extracts action verb, type, object, conditions, and explicit arm assignments from the raw subtask annotation string using dataset-specific patterns.
 - **Trajectory Parsing**: Analyzes robot proprioception data (gripper command streams for open/close events, lower-body signals for base motion) to determine `gripper_events` and `motion_k`.
 - **SST Assembly**: Constructs the $S_k$ entry. It tentatively updates the running scene-graph state based on parsed actions and records any fields that cannot be uniquely determined (e.g., arm assignment, same-object identity) as typed `PendingQuery` entries in $Q_k$.
- **Stage 2: State Resolution Cascade (Sec. 4.3)**
 This cascade resolves queries $Q_k$ using progressively richer information sources. Resolutions for $S_k$ are finalized before proceeding to $S_{k+1}$, allowing resolved states to inform subsequent inferences.
 - **Level 1 (L1) – Trajectory Inference**: Utilizes trajectory signals (gripper events) and the preceding scene-graph state ($g_{k-1}$) to deterministically resolve queries. For example, a single gripper event identifies the acting arm, and a cooperative release is inferred from a co-grasped state.
 - **Level 2 (L2) – Annotation Lookup (Optional)**: If L1 cannot resolve a query, and if the dataset provides additional structured metadata (e.g., skill type, memory prefix, spatial prefix in BEHAVIOR-1K), L2 consults this metadata for disambiguation, particularly for same-object identity. This level is skipped if metadata is unreliable or absent for the queried field.
 - **Level 3 (L3) – Vision-Language (VL) Queries**: For any remaining ambiguities, L3 issues queries to a Vision-Language Model. Prompts are constructed based on the query kind (e.g., "which arm performs this action?" or "are the two hands on the same physical object?"). The VL model receives a multi-image input (e.g., start, middle, end frames of the subtask).
- **Stage 3: Subtask Chain Consistency Checking (Sec. 4.4)**
 After the entire chain of SST entries is constructed and resolved, a checker validates its consistency against four state-transition invariants. Violations are flagged as anomalies.
 - **(I1) Hold/release symmetry**: Ensures every object acquisition by an arm is matched by a corresponding release.
 - **(I2) Object-identity continuity**: Verifies that a held object persists across subtasks unless explicitly released.
 - **(I3) Gripper-event–window alignment**: Checks if the physical gripper open/close events for an action occur within its annotated frame range.
 - **(I4) Bimanual hand-over legality**: Validates hand-over actions, requiring simultaneous release by one arm and grasp by the other, with the object transitioning correctly.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Protocol

The pipeline was evaluated on BEHAVIOR-1K, a simulated bimanual dataset comprising 50 household tasks (3 episodes each), yielding 2,357 annotated action cells.
- **Operating Modes**:
 - **VL-only**: Cascade runs L1 $\rightarrow$ L3, relying solely on trajectory and VL verification.
 - **Annotation-augmented VL (AVL)**: Cascade runs L1 $\rightarrow$ L2 $\rightarrow$ L3, incorporating BEHAVIOR-1K's structured metadata (skill type, memory prefix, spatial prefix).
- **Reference Annotation**: A ground truth SSC was established by pooling votes from a panel of 13 VL models, followed by human review and override for all candidate labels.
- **Metrics**:
 - Per-model action-level accuracy on the (arm, action type, action verb, action obj) tuple against the reference.
 - Global SSC anomaly count (violations of I1-I4).
- **Models**: 13 state-of-the-art VL models from five providers were tested (e.g., gpt55, gem35f, qwen3vlp).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the SSC pipeline on the BEHAVIOR-1K dataset yielded several findings regarding the performance of VL models as verifiers and the utility of the consistency checking mechanism.

#### 4.1. Per-Model Action-Level Accuracy (VL-only mode)

- On a "trimmed-hard" subset of 138 challenging action cells, the top-performing VL models were gpt55 (86.23%) and gem35f (85.51%), with gpt54 and gem31p closely following (both 84.78%). The top six models demonstrated performance within approximately 3 percentage points of each other.
- Models at the lower end, such as qwen3vlp, gpt54mi, and o4mi, scored around 65.94% on this hard subset, indicating a significant performance gap from the leaders.
- The overall accuracy across all 2,357 action cells was high for most models, ranging from 99.19% (gpt55) to 97.84% (qwen3vlp), suggesting that most common actions are readily resolvable.

#### 4.2. Per-Task Difficulty Tiers

- Analysis of the 138-cell VL trimmed-hard subset revealed that most difficulties were concentrated in bimanual coordinated open, close, and push actions involving large containers (e.g., toolboxes, fridges, ovens, laptops).
- A "universal-failure" tier of 6 cells was identified where none of the 13 models could correctly resolve the action, and a "near-universal-failure" tier of 5 cells (1-2 solvers out of 13). This highlights specific manipulation scenarios that remain challenging for current VL models.
- The primary ambiguity driving these hard cases was often distinguishing between single-arm actions with a bracing non-acting arm versus truly cooperative bimanual actions.

#### 4.3. Multi-VLM Ensemble Exploration

- Canonical multi-model ensembles, combining insights from several VL models, demonstrated improved stability and accuracy. Representative trios, such as `dbao2p + qwen3vlf + gem35f` and `gpt54 + qwenvlx + gem35f`, achieved up to 89.13% accuracy on the VL trimmed-hard subset.
- Most top-performing trios exhibited provider diversity, suggesting benefits from combining models from different developers.
- The performance gains from ensembles were modest (a few percentage points), indicating that while useful, they do not entirely bridge the gap on the most challenging cases.

#### 4.4. SSC Logic Completeness and Anomaly Reports

- Applying the I1–I4 consistency checks to the annotated reference SSC across 150 task–episode pairs flagged 31 anomalies out of 2,357 action cells (approximately 1.3%).
- The anomalies were categorized as: 18 "annotation-timing" (I3), 9 "not-released-at-end" (I1), and 4 "release-without-hold" (I1).
- **Root Causes**:
 - **Out-of-Scope Behaviors**: Four tasks were flagged because they violated the framework's assumptions (e.g., gripper-less holding or non-empty terminal states). For example, Task 24 involved "hugging" an object without a gripper close event, triggering an I3 anomaly. Task 30 ended with objects still held, triggering an I1 anomaly. These were correctly identified as framework boundary issues rather than annotation errors.
 - **Annotation Faults**: Five tasks contained errors where the ground truth (GT) skill window did not correctly align with the actual gripper event that realized the action, triggering I3 anomalies. For instance, in Task 34, a grasp event occurred far outside its annotated window.
- These findings demonstrate the checker's ability to identify both situations where the framework's assumptions are challenged by real-world demonstrations and genuine inconsistencies or errors in human annotations.

#### 4.5. AVL Mode Ablation: Impact of Annotation Lookup

- When enabling Level 2 (annotation lookup) using BEHAVIOR-1K's structured metadata, significant accuracy improvements were observed. For instance, the strongest single model (gpt55) improved by +9.4 percentage points (from 86.23% to 95.65%) on the 138-cell VL-hard subset.
- Models like qwen3vlp showed a substantial gain of +29.7 percentage points, indicating that the L2 layer can resolve many ambiguities that were challenging for the VL model alone. This suggests that for some models, the AVL mode functions more as an annotation verification mechanism than a fresh reasoning process.
- The benefits of L2 are dependent on the availability and reliability of structured metadata within the dataset.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Structured Subtask Chain (SSC) and its associated labelling pipeline represent a contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for learning from demonstrations and VLA systems.

**Improved Data Quality and Reliability:** The primary significance lies in its ability to automatically verify subtask labels for consistency and logical coherence. By introducing state-transition invariants, the pipeline can detect subtle anomalies and inconsistencies in human annotations that might otherwise go unnoticed. This capability is critical for scaling up the curation of large, high-quality datasets, which are fundamental for training robust and generalizable robotic policies. The identification of both framework boundary violations and annotation faults provides actionable insights for dataset developers.

**Bridging Representation Gaps:** The SSC directly addresses the limitations of existing subtask label representations. It offers a structured format that can be programmatically processed by machines (e.g., for hierarchical policy learning) while retaining flexibility and the capacity to render as human-readable natural language. This "structured-yet-flexible" approach is essential for balancing machine interpretability with human understanding and annotation consistency.

**Enhanced Vision-Language Model Evaluation:** The framework provides a standardized and rigorous method for evaluating the performance of state-of-the-art VL models as verifiers for robotic manipulation tasks. The detailed per-model and per-task analyses illuminate the strengths and weaknesses of different VL architectures, particularly in challenging bimanual coordination and object interaction scenarios. This contributes to a deeper understanding of current VL model capabilities in grounded robotics contexts.

**Support for Complex Bimanual Manipulation:** By explicitly modeling bimanual actions, object states (scene graph), and separate base motions, the SSC provides a richer and more precise representation for complex manipulation tasks. This is crucial for developing advanced policies that require fine-grained coordination and state awareness.

**Foundation for Future Automation:** The research suggests potential for broader applicability beyond verification. The authors propose that paired with a temporal segmentation front end, the pipeline could extend from a verifier to an auto-annotator and verifier. This would significantly reduce the manual effort and cost associated with generating large-scale labelled datasets, accelerating the development cycle for new robotic capabilities.

**Extensibility and Adaptability:** The modular design of the SSC (e.g., universal verb vocabulary, typed query schema, explicit assumptions) allows for future expansion. New action types, additional sensory signals, or relaxations of current assumptions (e.g., handling gripper-less holding) can be integrated, ensuring the framework's adaptability to evolving research needs and more complex scenarios.

In summary, this work provides a robust and verifiable framework for structuring manipulation demonstrations, directly contributing to the foundational data infrastructure required for the next generation of generalist robotic agents.
