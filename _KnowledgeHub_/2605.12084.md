# Learning What Matters: Adaptive Information-Theoretic Objectives for Robot Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> Quasi-Optimal Experimental Design (QOED) offers an adaptive information-theoretic framework for robot exploration, automatically identifying critical parameters online and suppressing nuisance effects. Integrated with a learned differentiable dynamics model, QOED significantly improves parameter identification and dynamics prediction accuracy by up to 35.23%, leading to enhanced policy learning across various simulated and real-world robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Traditional Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design (BOED) in robot exploration requires expert-defined sets of critical parameters, which is challenging and prone to error in high-dimensional systems.
- Ignoring 'nuisance' parameters in BOED can distort information objectives and lead to inefficient exploration due to their correlation with critical parameters.
- Integrating BOED with modern model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) is difficult due to the lack of tractable likelihoods from learned neural network dynamics models for Fisher Information Matrix computation.

## Method

- Developed **Quasi-Optimal Experimental Design (QOED)** to adaptively identify critical and observable parameters online using ==Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) eigen-analysis== and a parameter selection process.
- Constructed an adaptive information objective using the ==Schur complement== of the FIM, which maximizes information gain about critical parameters while explicitly suppressing the confounding influence of nuisance parameters.
- Integrated **QOED** into a model-based policy optimization framework by learning a ==differentiable dynamics model== (a ==Transformer-based shortcut model==) to enable tractable FIM computation and policy updates.

## Results

- QOED consistently yielded stronger dynamics prediction RMSE (up to **35.23%** improvement over BOED) and competitive parameter estimation RMSE across various simulated tasks, demonstrating superior data informativeness.
- QOED significantly improved policy learning performance, outperforming BOED, QOED-AGNOSTIC, and other baselines in **normalized episodic task rewards** by leveraging more accurate dynamics estimates for policy optimization.
- Real-world experiments validated QOED's efficacy, achieving an **89% success rate** in a rod balance task and the highest success rate with lowest dynamics prediction RMSE in a wild wheeled navigation task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Eigenspace analysis of the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) effectively identifies the well-observed and identifiable subspace of parameters, allowing for automatic selection of critical parameters.
> - The Schur complement of the FIM provides a robust information-theoretic objective that effectively rewards information about critical parameters while implicitly accounting for and suppressing nuisance parameter effects.
> - A learned differentiable dynamics model can bridge the gap between theoretical information-theoretic objectives and data-driven model-based reinforcement learning, enabling practical application of BOED-like methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot exploration is a fundamental challenge in robotics, particularly due to the inherent expense and limitations of real-world interactions. The present work contributes to the field by addressing critical gaps in information-theoretic approaches to exploration, specifically Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design (BOED).

Traditional exploration methods in reinforcement learning (RL) broadly fall into uncertainty-driven and intrinsic-motivation (curiosity-driven) categories. Uncertainty-driven methods aim to reduce uncertainty about learned parameters or environmental stochasticity, often using Bayesian updates, low-dimensional approximations, or ensemble techniques. Curiosity-driven methods encourage agents to seek novel or unpredictable experiences. While these methods have shown utility, they often operate without explicit mechanisms for identifying which parameters are truly "learnable" or critical for the task.

BOED provides a principled framework for maximizing expected information gain about unknown parameters by selecting actions that are most informative. This approach has been applied in areas such as active perception, simulator calibration for sim-to-real transfer, and contact-rich manipulation. However, the efficacy of BOED heavily relies on two assumptions: (i) the existence of a compact, expert-defined set of critical parameters, and (ii) an accurate probabilistic model for predicting information gain, which often requires tractable likelihoods. In complex robotic systems, parameter identifiability varies, and domain expertise is often required to pre-specify critical parameters. Naively ignoring unselected parameters can lead to distorted information objectives, driving exploration towards uninformative or spurious directions. Furthermore, integrating BOED with modern model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) approaches, which learn dynamics models from data, has been challenging due to the difficulty of obtaining tractable likelihoods from learned neural network models.

This work addresses these limitations by proposing an adaptive information objective, Quasi-Optimal Experimental Design (QOED), which automatically identifies critical parameters online and mitigates the distorting influence of nuisance parameters. It further integrates this objective into a model-based policy optimization framework using a learned differentiable dynamics model, bridging the gap between sophisticated information-theoretic objectives and data-driven policy learning in robotics. This positions QOED as a development in creating more robust and efficient exploration strategies, particularly for high-dimensional robotic systems where parameter identifiability and observational complexities are prevalent.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with designing effective, learnable information-theoretic objectives for robot exploration, particularly within the framework of Bayesian Optimal Experimental Design (BOED). The authors identify two primary questions and corresponding challenges:

**Q1: Critical Parameter Subspace:** In high-dimensional robotic systems, many physical parameters are either weakly observable or entirely unidentifiable from the available data. Current BOED approaches typically assume an expert-curated, low-dimensional set of critical parameters. This reliance on prior domain knowledge can be restrictive and prone to error, as it's often unclear which parameters collected data can reliably reveal. The first objective is to develop an automatic mechanism for agents to identify these "critical" parameters online, without requiring expert assumptions. This aims to overcome the limitation that many parameter directions provide little information (low Fisher information), or that individual parameters may not be separable due to similar effects on system behavior.

**Q2: Subspace-Optimal Experimental Design:** Even after identifying a subset of critical parameters, simply optimizing information gain for these parameters in isolation (i.e., ignoring others) is often insufficient. Discarded "nuisance" parameters can still influence system dynamics and thereby affect the overall information objective. These unselected parameters can be correlated with the critical ones, potentially distorting the perceived information gain and leading exploration astray by overestimating the true information about the critical parameters. The second objective is to design an exploration objective that not only prioritizes information gain in the identified critical parameters but also actively suppresses the confounding influence of the remaining nuisance parameters. This ensures that the policy's exploration behavior remains robust and near-optimal relative to the ideal objective computed over the entire parameter space.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
1. **Develop an adaptive mechanism** to identify observable and identifiable parameter directions in complex robotic systems automatically.
2. **Construct an adaptive information objective** that emphasizes these critical parameters while explicitly accounting for and suppressing the distorting effects of nuisance parameters.
3. **Integrate this adaptive objective** into a model-based policy optimization framework, leveraging learned dynamics models to enable practical application in real-world robot learning.

These objectives collectively aim to improve the efficiency and reliability of robot exploration by making information-theoretic objectives more robust to high-dimensionality, parameter identifiability issues, and the presence of confounding nuisance variables.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, Quasi-Optimal Experimental Design (QOED), addresses the stated challenges by adaptively identifying critical parameters and constructing an information-theoretic objective that accounts for nuisance effects. QOED is integrated into a model-based policy optimization (MBPO) framework.

**A. Selecting Critical Parameters**
QOED identifies a compact and interpretable set of critical parameters through a three-step process:

1. **Parameter Estimation (Step I):**
 - The Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) is dependent on the current parameter values. Therefore, `ϕ` (hidden parameters) are initially estimated from observed trajectory data (`τ_obs_t`) by matching model rollouts to the observations.
 - This is formulated as a minimization problem (Equation 6) where `ϕ` is optimized to reduce the squared error between observed and predicted trajectories given fixed actions.
 - The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), a derivative-free optimizer, is employed for this estimation.
 - A Gaussian belief `p(ϕ) = N(μ_ϕ, Σ_ϕ)` is maintained, and `Σ_ϕ` is updated approximately using the inverse FIM, analogous to extended Kalman filtering.

2. **Identify the Well-Observed Subspace (Step II):**
 - Eigenspace analysis of the FIM is used to identify directions in the parameter space where data provides significant information.
 - The FIM `F_ϕ` is symmetric positive semidefinite, allowing for eigen-decomposition `F_ϕ = WΛW^⊤`, where eigenvalues `λ_i` indicate the amount of information along each eigenvector `w_i`.
 - Eigenvalues close to zero suggest low information content in those directions.
 - A threshold `δ_eig` (and a relative threshold `α_eig * max diag(Λ)`) is applied to filter eigenvalues, identifying a well-observed subspace spanned by eigenvectors `W_o` corresponding to `λ_i ≥ δ_eig`. The remaining directions are considered weakly observed or unobserved.

3. **Select Identifiable Parameter Coordinates (Step III):**
 - From the well-observed subspace, a compact and interpretable subset of individual parameter indices `k` is selected. This step aims to find physical parameters that strongly contribute to the observable directions and are not redundant.
 - This selection is formulated as an optimization problem (Equation 8) that maximizes `log det(W_ko W_ko^⊤)` (favoring diverse rows spanning the subspace) subject to a cosine similarity constraint `cos(r_i, r_j) ≤ δ_cos` between row vectors `r_j` of `W_o` (preventing selection of highly correlated parameters).
 - A lazy-greedy procedure is used for approximate solution.
 - The selected parameters `ϕ_k` are termed "critical (identifiable) parameters."
 - A baseline objective, **Agnostic QOED**, is defined as `tr(F_kk)`, which is the trace of the FIM restricted to these selected coordinates, ignoring discarded parameters.

**B. Adaptive Information-Theoretic Objective (QOED)**
This component addresses the influence of nuisance parameters.

- The FIM is block-partitioned into `F_kk` (critical-critical), `F_kk` (critical-nuisance), `F_kk` (nuisance-critical), and `F_kk` (nuisance-nuisance).
- The **QOED Objective** is defined using the Schur complement `I_k|k = F_kk - F_kk F_kk^-1 F_kk` (Equation 11), with the objective being `B_QOED(ϕ|τ_t) = tr(I_k|k)`.
- The Schur complement represents the covariance of the residual score after removing the best linear prediction of the critical score from the nuisance score, effectively rewarding information about critical parameters that cannot be predicted from non-critical ones.
- Algorithm 1 summarizes the computational flow for QOED objective calculation, including dynamic thresholding for eigenvalue splitting and the greedy selection process.
- **Theorem 1 (Quasi-optimality)** demonstrates that under conditions of bounded nuisance information (`η`) and limited coupling (`β`) between critical and nuisance directions, optimizing the QOED objective yields a constant-factor approximation to the ideal full BOED objective. This indicates that QOED is not generally quasi-optimal with a simple agnostic approach.

**C. Policy Learning with QOED**
QOED is integrated into an MBPO framework for policy learning.

1. **Learned Differentiable Dynamics Model:**
 - Instead of relying on potentially mismatched analytical physics models, a differentiable dynamics model `q_θ(s_t+1|s_t, a_t, ϕ)` is learned.
 - This model uses a "shortcut model" approach, mapping latent noise `δ` to a one-step state increment via a transport map `T_θ`.
 - The shortcut model is trained to minimize flow-matching and self-consistency losses (Appendix A).
 - The differentiable nature of `T_θ` allows for tractable estimation of the conditional log-density `log q_θ(s_t+1|s_t, a_t, ϕ)` (via change of variables), which is crucial for computing the FIM (Appendix B).
 - The dynamics model `T_θ` is parameterized by a Transformer network with state, action, and parameter embeddings and Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN).

2. **MBPO Pipeline:**
 - A PPO policy `π` is trained using a learned dynamics model `q_θ`.
 - The process involves pretraining `π` and `q_θ` in simulation with domain randomization.
 - During online deployment, an iterative loop is executed:
 - **Collect Data:** A transition `(s_t, a_t, ˆϕ, s_t+1)` is collected using the current policy, and `ˆϕ` is estimated using Equation 6.
 - **Update Dynamics:** The dynamics model `q_θ` is updated using the collected data and the shortcut model objective.
 - **Policy Optimization:** Imagined trajectories are rolled out from states in the dataset `D` using `q_θ` and `π`. The task reward is augmented with the QOED objective (Algorithm 1) to guide policy updates.
 - Exploration terminates when parameter posterior covariance `δ_var` and dynamics prediction error `δ_dyn` fall below specified thresholds.

This methodology provides a comprehensive approach to robot exploration by combining adaptive information objective design with data-driven model learning and policy optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of QOED was conducted across various simulated and real-world robotic tasks, addressing two main questions: (Q1) whether QOED collects informative data for parameter identification, and (Q2) whether it improves policy learning compared to state-of-the-art methods.

**A. QOED Yields Informative Data (Q1)**
- **Environments:** Simulated in MuJoCo environments including Unitree Go1 (Flat/Rough), Humanoid G1 (Flat/Rough), Clearpath Jackal (Flat/Rough), and Inspire-FTP Hand-Rotate. These cover both locomotion and manipulation with varying parameter dimensionalities (31-119).
- **Comparison:** QOED was compared against two ablations: QOED-AGNOSTIC (optimizing `tr(F_kk)` for identifiable parameters only, ignoring discarded ones) and BOED (canonical `tr(F_ϕ)` over all parameters). All methods used the same CEM optimizer and learned dynamics model.
- **Metrics:** Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of parameter estimates and dynamics predictions.
- **Results:**
 - Across all scenarios and noise levels (1σ, 2σ, 3σ), QOED consistently achieved the strongest dynamics-prediction RMSE and competitive parameter-estimation RMSE.
 - QOED improved dynamics prediction by **21.98%** over QOED-AGNOSTIC and by **35.23%** over BOED.
 - BOED generally yielded poor estimates and dynamics predictions because it allocates exploration effort to all parameter directions, including those difficult to learn or unobservable.
 - QOED-AGNOSTIC, while restricting to identifiable parameters, still underperformed QOED. This indicates that ignoring the influence of discarded (nuisance) parameters leads to degraded estimation and dynamics predictions due to correlations between critical and nuisance directions.
 - The improvement in dynamics prediction with QOED, despite similar aggregate parameter estimation RMSEs, was attributed to its focus on critical parameters that explain a disproportionate share of the dynamics prediction error (e.g., 65.9% for Go1, 53.1% for G1). For instance, QOED identified only 16 parameters (13.4% of total) for G1, highlighting how a compact identifiable subset can be highly explanatory.
 - QOED demonstrated robustness to hyperparameter choices (eigenvalue thresholds, cosine similarity).

**B. QOED Helps Policy Learning (Q2)**
- **Baselines and Ablations:** QOED was compared against SAC-ADAPT (adaptive reward weighting), DISAGREEMENT (ensemble-based uncertainty exploration), DOMAIN-RANDOM (PPO with domain randomization), QOED-PHYSICS (using ground-truth simulator physics via finite differences), QOED-AGNOSTIC, and BOED.
- **Metric:** Normalized episodic task reward (excluding exploration reward).
- **Results:**
 - QOED-PHYSICS, which had access to ground-truth physics, performed best across all environments, validating the QOED objective's efficacy when the dynamics model is accurate.
 - QOED, utilizing a learned dynamics model, also showed strong performance, often outperforming other baselines.
 - BOED consistently resulted in weaker policies due to its greedy information-gathering approach that does not account for parameter learnability.
 - QOED-AGNOSTIC underperformed QOED due to poorer dynamics estimates resulting from ignoring nuisance parameter influence, leading to miscalibrated physics during policy optimization.
 - DOMAIN-RANDOM and SAC-ADAPT generally performed less effectively, suggesting that broad randomization or simple adaptive reward schemes are less efficient than targeted information-theoretic exploration.
 - DISAGREEMENT was a strong baseline by targeting regions of high model uncertainty, but QOED, by conditioning on physics parameters, achieved further gains.
 - A performance drop for QOED in G1-Rough was noted, attributed to the learned dynamics model's unreliability during early learning in that specific challenging environment.

**C. Real-World Experiments**

1. **Rod Balance (Franka Emika Panda Arm):**
 - **Task:** Identify mass, inertia, and friction of cubes, then balance the highest-friction cube. This task focuses on Q1 (parameter identification accuracy).
 - **Results:** QOED achieved an 89% success rate, significantly higher than QOED-PHYSICS (42%), BOED (8%), and QOED-AGNOSTIC (17%). Failures were primarily due to center-of-mass misalignment. QOED's adaptive objective-adaptation strategy (first identifying mass/inertia, then friction) led to faster and more accurate parameter convergence. QOED-AGNOSTIC's failure was linked to its inability to account for friction's influence during mass estimation.

2. **Wild Wheeled Navigation (Clearpath Jackal Mobile Vehicle):**
 - **Task:** Navigate to a goal while avoiding obstacles in challenging scenarios: Motor Malfunction, Adversarial Force (towing a cart), and Forest. This evaluates both Q1 and Q2.
 - **Metrics:** Success rate, dynamics prediction RMSE, and normalized cumulative information gain.
 - **Results:** QOED achieved the highest success rate and the lowest dynamics prediction RMSE, quickly identifying key parameters for successful navigation. It also demonstrated the highest cumulative information gain. QOED adaptively diagnosed wheel-related effects and longitudinal force while suppressing other coupled directions. QOED with a learned dynamics model further improved performance over QOED-PHYSICS, leveraging the data efficiency of MBRL. DISAGREEMENT also performed strongly, while SAC-ADAPT exhibited uninformative wobbling behaviors.

**D. Discussions and Limitations**
The paper notes that SAC-based baselines might be more general but lack the specific physics parameterization required for QOED. The authors suggest learning a latent parameterization as future work. Real-world experiments highlighted the computational cost and challenges of real-time sim mirroring for QOED-PHYSICS, favoring the learned dynamics model. The trade-off between objective complexity and exploration efficiency remains an area for future investigation.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that QOED effectively identifies critical parameters and suppresses nuisance effects, leading to superior parameter identification, more accurate dynamics models, and enhanced policy learning in diverse robotic tasks, both in simulation and real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable advance in the field of robot exploration and model-based reinforcement learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Exploration Efficiency and Robustness:** By proposing Quasi-Optimal Experimental Design (QOED), the research offers a more principled and robust approach to information-theoretic exploration. Traditional BOED's reliance on pre-specified parameters and its susceptibility to nuisance factors are significantly mitigated. This directly leads to more efficient collection of informative data and reduced model error, which is critical in real-world robotics where interaction budgets are limited.

2. **Automated Critical Parameter Identification:** The ability of QOED to automatically identify critical, observable, and identifiable parameters online, through Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) eigen-analysis and parameter selection, reduces the need for extensive domain expertise. This feature is particularly impactful for complex, high-dimensional robotic systems where manual parameter selection can be challenging, error-prone, or even impractical.

3. **Improved Dynamics Model Learning:** QOED's adaptive information objective, by emphasizing critical parameters and suppressing nuisance effects, leads to more accurate and reliable learned dynamics models. This improvement in model fidelity is directly beneficial for model-based policy optimization (MBPO), allowing policies to be trained on more trustworthy simulated experiences.

4. **Bridging Theory and Practice in MBRL:** The integration of QOED with a learned differentiable dynamics model (shortcut model based on Transformer architecture) effectively bridges the gap between the theoretical rigor of BOED and the practical demands of modern data-driven MBRL. This allows for sophisticated information-theoretic objectives to be applied in settings where analytical physics models may be mismatched or unavailable. The results demonstrate that QOED with learned dynamics can outperform purely physics-based analytical models in certain real-world scenarios.

5. **Applicability Across Diverse Robotic Tasks:** The evaluation across a range of robot platforms (quadrupeds, humanoids, wheeled vehicles, dexterous hands) and tasks (locomotion, manipulation, navigation) in both simulation and real-world settings demonstrates the broad applicability and generality of the QOED framework.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The concepts introduced, particularly adaptive objective design and nuisance parameter suppression, provide a foundation for future work in several directions. The authors specifically mention extending QOED to meta-learning RL update rules or optimizing hyperparameters of differentiable algorithms. This suggests a broader paradigm for intelligent, data-efficient learning in complex systems beyond parameter identification, potentially influencing areas like adaptive control, system calibration, and skill acquisition.

In essence, this research contributes a method that makes intelligent exploration more accessible and effective for contemporary robotic systems, moving towards a future where robots can autonomously determine "what matters" for efficient learning and adaptation.
