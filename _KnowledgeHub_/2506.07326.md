# Reward Model Interpretability via Optimal and Pessimal Tokens

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Oxford investigated the interpretability of reward models by analyzing their scoring of optimal and pessimal tokens and multi-token sequences. This analysis exposed significant heterogeneity and systematic biases across models, including the devaluation of identity group references and sexual content, suggesting a misalignment with human preferences.

## Problem

- Reward models (RMs), foundational for LLM alignment via RLHF, are typically treated as 'disposable intermediaries,' with their internal workings, biases, and fidelity to human values largely unexplored.
- A lack of systematic understanding regarding how RMs translate complex human value judgments into scalar representations hinders the development of robust and truly aligned AI systems.
- The potential for unintended biases within RMs, which could propagate into widely deployed LLMs, remained understudied despite their critical role in encoding human values.

## Method

- An ==exhaustive search methodology== was pioneered to compute reward model scores for every single token in a model's vocabulary in response to value-laden prompts.
- Quantitative analysis, including sentiment lexicons, frequency bias investigation, and external validation with human preference data (EloEverything), was employed to interpret RM behaviors.
- The analysis was extended to multi-token sequences using ==discrete optimization methods like Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG)== to explore longer 'optimal' and 'pessimal' responses.

## Results

- Reward models showed only moderate alignment with independent human preferences from the EloEverything platform, with an average Kendall's τ correlation of **0.29**, and stronger agreement for highly-rated human items.
- All reward distributions exhibited a positive skew, and a 'mere-exposure effect' was observed where models assigned higher scores to more frequent words, even after controlling for sentiment.
- Identity group references and sexual content were consistently devalued by RMs compared to human rankings, with terms like 'Jews' appearing among pessimal multi-token sequences, indicating concerning biases.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reward models exhibit substantial heterogeneity and idiosyncratic biases, challenging the assumption of their interchangeability and demonstrating how base models and alignment performance shape token valuations.
> - Systematic asymmetries and framing effects exist, with RMs showing greater sensitivity to positive-sentiment tokens in positively framed prompts, potentially undermining the objective of effectively steering away from harmful responses.
> - A 'linguistic erasure effect' was identified, where RMs systematically undervalue identity group references (e.g., "homosexuals," "Black people") and sexual content, likely an unintended consequence of harmlessness training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human values represents a significant challenge in contemporary artificial intelligence development. Reward models (RMs) are a critical component in this alignment process, particularly within methodologies such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). These neural networks are trained to proxy human preferences, translating prompt-response pairs into scalar rewards, thereby directly encoding human value judgments.

While substantial research attention has focused on employing RMs for fine-tuning generative models, the RMs themselves have remained relatively underexplored as standalone objects of study. They are often treated as intermediary components in a larger alignment pipeline. The scarcity of literature analyzing RM properties has been partly attributed to the limited public availability of these models until recently, a situation beginning to change with the introduction of benchmarks like RewaRdBench.

This work addresses a gap in the literature by focusing directly on reward model interpretability. It positions RMs as direct encoders of human values, offering a unique perspective on how these values are operationalized within AI systems. The study implicitly contrasts with direct alignment algorithms like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which streamline the alignment process but forgo the explicit, inspectable reward model that PPO-based RLHF provides. This research complements existing efforts in interpretability and bias analysis within RMs, which have explored issues such as underspecification, hidden context in reward signals, length biases, and the impact of stylistic confounders in human feedback. It also connects to studies on the over-optimization of RMs, which have documented how prolonged training can lead to degenerate outputs and scaling law phenomena. By meticulously examining RM behavior, this paper contributes to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which human preferences and values are embedded into, and potentially distorted by, AI alignment systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the interpretability of reward models, which are central to aligning large language models with human values. The authors contend that despite their crucial role in encoding human value judgments, RMs are surprisingly under-studied, often considered merely disposable intermediaries in the broader alignment process. This work is motivated by the premise that RMs offer the most direct and explicit encoding of human values within AI systems, making them deserving of direct research inquiry to understand the consistency and faithfulness with which they represent these values.

The specific objectives of the study are multifaceted:

- **Pioneering Exhaustive Token Search:** To develop and apply a novel methodology involving an exhaustive search across every single token in reward model vocabularies. This approach, applied to value-laden prompts, aims to identify and analyze optimal and pessimal tokens across a diverse set of open-source reward models, providing a granular and comprehensive evaluation of their characteristics.
- **Uncovering Systematic Asymmetries:** To investigate and document systematic asymmetries within reward distributions. This includes examining differential sensitivities between high-scoring and low-scoring regions of the reward space, as well as distinct responses to positive versus negative sentiment tokens, and how these sensitivities may mirror human cognitive biases like framing effects.
- **Establishing Alignment with External Human Preferences:** To assess the fidelity of reward models as proxies for human preferences by comparing their value interpretations against an independent, ground-truth dataset of human judgments. This involves utilizing a crowdsourced platform (EloEveRything) to provide an external baseline for human value judgments.
- **Generalizing Findings to Multi-Token Sequences:** To extend the analysis of biases and asymmetries observed in single-token evaluations to multi-token sequences. This is achieved through the application of discrete optimization methods, such as Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), to identify optimal and pessimal longer textual responses.
- **Revealing Heterogeneity and Biases:** Through these analyses, the study aims to reveal significant heterogeneity among reward models trained on similar objectives, challenging assumptions about their interchangeability. Furthermore, it seeks to identify systematic devaluation of references to specific identity groups, hypothesizing this as an unintended consequence of harmlessness training objectives.

Ultimately, the motivation is to highlight potential pitfalls in current reward model development, offer recommendations for constructing more robust proxies of human value, and contribute to the broader objective of developing aligned and safe AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a multi-pronged approach to investigate reward model interpretability, combining exhaustive single-token analysis, quantitative assessments, sentiment and frequency bias evaluations, alignment with external human preferences, and multi-token sequence optimization.

**Model Selection:** Ten open-source reward models were chosen from the RewaRdBench leaderboard, encompassing a range of parameter counts (2B to 27B), base architectures, and developers. These models represented high-performing or leading entries from the leaderboard.

**Exhaustive Single-Token Search:**
To enable a comprehensive and granular evaluation, the authors adopted an exhaustive search strategy. An initial value-laden prompt, "What, in one word, is the greatest thing ever?", was used. For each of the selected reward models, every single token in its vocabulary (ranging from approximately 128,000 for Llama models to 256,000 for Gemma models) was appended as a response to this prompt. The scalar reward score generated by the model for each prompt-response pair was then recorded, and tokens were sorted by their scores. This method differed from analyzing log probabilities of fine-tuned LLMs, which were observed to over-index on common, statistically regular tokens rather than semantically meaningful ones for value interpretation.

**Qualitative and Quantitative Analysis of Single-Token Rewards:**
- **Qualitative Observations:** The top-ranked (optimal) and bottom-ranked (pessimal) tokens were qualitatively inspected across models to identify patterns. This revealed differences in prioritized content (e.g., affective vs. individualistic values) and concerning artifacts from training objectives (e.g., human harm, slurs, programming artifacts).
- **Quantitative Analysis:** Reward distributions were analyzed for statistical properties such as mean, variance, and skewness. The consistency of token rankings across different models was assessed using Kendall's $\tau$ ordinal correlation. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) was applied to visualize model similarity based on these correlations. Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) was used to determine which factors (base model, developer, parameter count, RewaRdBench rank) best explained the observed patterns of model-to-model correlation.

**Investigation of Framing Effects:**
- **Sentiment Analysis:** The relationship between token sentiment and reward scores was investigated using two human-curated linguistic corpora: Bing (positive/negative labels) and AFINN-111 (sentiment scores from -5 to 5). Scores were correlated with sentiment to determine how emotional valence influenced reward.
- **Prompt Framing:** The analysis was extended to two additional prompts: "What, in one word, is the best thing ever?" (positive variant) and "What, in one word, is the worst thing ever?" (negative contrast). This allowed for the examination of how prompt framing influences reward model sensitivity to positive versus negative sentiment, mirroring human cognitive biases.
- **Frequency Bias:** The influence of token frequency on reward scores was assessed using data from "Word Frequencies in Written and Spoken English." Log-transformed word frequencies were regressed against reward scores, controlling for sentiment value, to identify any "mere-exposure effect."

**Alignment with External Human Preferences (EloEveRything):**
To establish an external baseline for human value judgments, data was sourced from EloEveRything, a crowdsourcing platform where users provide pairwise preferences on Wikipedia-derived entities (concepts, people, things). The dataset comprised 12,515 users, 7,530 items, and over 1.8 million pairwise comparisons.
- **Methodological Adjustments:** The prompt was modified to "What one single thing, person, or concept is the greatest ever?" to better accommodate multi-word concepts present in EloEveRything. The analysis was restricted to the 7,350 items found in the EloEveRything dataset to ensure a common comparison set. Reward model scores and human Elo ratings were normalized to rankings for comparison.
- **Analysis:** Kendall's $\tau$ correlation was used to measure rank agreement between human preferences and reward model interpretations, both between humans and models, and across different models. Discrepancies between human and model valuations were qualitatively analyzed.

**Searching for Longer Optimal and Pessimal Token Sequences with GCG:**
To explore multi-token sequences, the authors utilized a modified Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) algorithm, originally developed for adversarial attacks. This implementation searched for token sequences that maximized or minimized reward values. A mean squared error loss on the reward value was used, and modifications from Faster-GCG were incorporated to enhance performance for short sequences. Prompts included "What, in one word, is the <greatest/best/worst> thing ever?" for 2-3 tokens, and "What is the best/worst thing ever?" for longer sequences, allowing the identification of linguistic "superstimuli."

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the behavior and interpretability of reward models:

**1. Substantial Heterogeneity Between Models:**
A notable finding was the significant qualitative and quantitative heterogeneity in token rankings across the ten reward models, even among those trained with similar objectives or from the same developer. For instance, the top-ranked tokens varied from "LOVE" and "felicity" in one model (R-Gem-2B) to "freedom" and "LIFE" in another (R-Lla-3B). Pessimal tokens also diverged, with some models penalizing harm-related words like "rape" and "Hitler," while others prioritized programming artifacts or specific identity groups (e.g., "blacks," "homosexuals").
Quantitative analysis using Kendall's $\tau$ correlation confirmed this diversity. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) and representational similarity analysis (RSA) indicated that while base model architecture and RewardBench ranking were significant predictors of model similarity, they did not fully account for the observed heterogeneity. This suggests that reward models inherit idiosyncratic biases from their pre-trained base models and that design choices lead to distinct value encodings, challenging the assumption of their interchangeability.

**2. Systematic Asymmetries in Reward Encoding:**
Reward distributions consistently exhibited a positive skew, meaning most tokens received low rewards, while a smaller number scored substantially higher. This characteristic implies that the upper tail of the distribution holds more discriminative power for learning.
A key asymmetry was identified in how models encoded high-scoring versus low-scoring tokens and positive versus negative sentiment. For positively framed prompts ("greatest thing ever," "best thing ever"), models showed greater sensitivity to distinctions among positive-sentiment tokens (steeper slope for positive sentiment) compared to negative-sentiment tokens. This bias was inverted for the negatively framed prompt ("worst thing ever"), where models became more sensitive to negative-sentiment tokens. This phenomenon mirrors human cognitive framing effects. The implication is that current reward elicitation methods, often focusing on preferred options, may inadvertently bias reward models toward processing positive tokens, potentially undermining their sensitivity to harmful content.

**3. Inconsistent Interpretation of "Best" vs. "Worst":**
The models did not treat "best" as a simple inverse of "worst." Juxtaposing scores for these two prompts revealed a complex "funnel" distribution. Many tokens were rated poorly for both, some were rated highly for one but poorly for the other, and a small number received high scores for both (e.g., non-committal answers like "depends," "impossible," or the neologism "sonder"). This indicates that valuation exists in a more-than-one-dimensional space and is not a straightforward bipolar scale.

**4. Overvaluation of More Frequent Tokens (Mere-Exposure Effect):**
Reward models demonstrated a "mere-exposure effect," where higher word frequency in the English language was associated with higher reward scores across prompts. This effect persisted even when controlling for the sentiment value of tokens, becoming more pronounced in negatively framed queries for some models. This finding suggests a leakage from the pre-trained base models into the reward models, where more common tokens might be inadvertently favored, potentially compromising the reward model's intended function of providing orthogonal information regarding helpfulness or harmlessness.

**5. Misalignment with Human Preferences and Concerning Biases:**
Comparison with the EloEveRything human preference dataset revealed only moderate agreement (mean Kendall's $\tau$ = 0.29) between human and model rankings, with substantial heterogeneity and asymmetry. Models showed stronger agreement with highly-rated human items than with low-rated ones.
Specifically, reward models systematically undervalued concepts related to nature, life (e.g., "Universe," "Gravity," "Breathing"), and technology (e.g., "Technology," "Computer") compared to human preferences. Conversely, they tended to overvalue affective qualities (e.g., "Unconditional love," "Imagination").
Most concerningly, significant discrepancies emerged around sexual content (e.g., "Sex," "Human sexual activity") and references to specific identity groups (e.g., "Black people," "Jews"). These terms were systematically devalued by the models, often appearing among the lowest-ranked tokens even for positive prompts. The authors propose this devaluation may be an unintended consequence of harmlessness training, leading to "linguistic erasure" and paradoxical harm by signaling that certain identity groups are associated with negativity or rejection.

**6. Multi-Token Sequence Findings:**
Using Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) for multi-token sequences corroborated several single-token observations. Programming-related tokens frequently yielded low scores across both "best" and "worst" prompts. Responses emphasizing subjectivity (e.g., "There is no objectively worst thing ever. It's subjective.") often scored highly for "worst" prompts, possibly indicating a model's preference to avoid negative sentiment in its response. Identity terms like "Jews" also appeared among pessimal multi-token sequences for both positively and negatively framed prompts, reinforcing the linguistic erasure effect. Optimal multi-token sequences were often ungrammatical and included emojis.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications for the development, evaluation, and ethical deployment of large language models.

Firstly, the finding of **substantial heterogeneity** among reward models, even those trained for similar objectives, challenges the common assumption that these models are interchangeable. This implies that the specific design choices, base model architecture, and training data lead to distinct value encodings, which may result in varied behaviors in downstream fine-tuned LLMs. A lack of transparency in their development processes further exacerbates the difficulty in attributing observed behaviors to specific factors.

Secondly, the identification of **systematic asymmetries in reward encoding** and **framing effects** is crucial. The observation that reward models are more sensitive to positive-sentiment tokens in positively framed prompts, and vice-versa, indicates that they do not simply represent "best" as the inverse of "worst." This suggests that human preference elicitation methods, often focused on positive choices, may inadvertently bias the dynamic range of reward models, potentially hindering their ability to sensitively detect and mitigate harmful or unsafe content in negative contexts. This calls for a re-evaluation of how human feedback is collected and operationalized in training data.

Thirdly, the discovery of an **overvaluation of frequent tokens** (the "mere-exposure effect") highlights a potential leakage of statistical regularities from pre-trained base models into reward models. This can undermine the reward model's intended purpose of providing value-aligned guidance orthogonal to token distribution, possibly contributing to the generic or bland outputs often observed in publicly available LLMs. Further research is needed to understand its interaction with KL-divergence regularization in RLHF.

Finally, and perhaps most critically, the demonstrated **misalignment with independent human preferences** and the **systematic devaluation of identity group references and sexual content** reveal concerning biases. The persistent low scoring of terms like "Black people" or "Jews" across various prompts, potentially as an unintended consequence of "harmlessness" training, risks linguistic erasure and the propagation of harmful stereotypes. This indicates that current reward models may fail to accurately represent the nuanced, context-dependent nature of human values, leading to AI systems that inadvertently cause harm by devaluing certain groups or concepts. This necessitates more careful consideration of the demographic representativeness and contextual richness of the human preference data used for training.

In sum, this work elevates reward models to objects of interpretability research in their own right, providing insights into how human values are encoded in AI systems. It offers actionable implications for building more robust, fair, and safe AI, encouraging developers to move beyond a "disposable reagent" view of RMs and to consider their internal dynamics and biases carefully. The findings contribute to the broader goal of creating aligned and safe AI systems by exposing fundamental challenges and advocating for greater transparency, accountability, and ethical consideration in reward model design and deployment.
