# Tactile Gym 2.0: Sim-to-real Deep Reinforcement Learning for Comparing Low-cost High-Resolution Robot Touch

> [!summary] Summary
> An adapted sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning framework enables zero-shot policy transfer for low-cost, high-resolution tactile sensors (TacTip, DIGIT, and DigiTac) on an affordable robot, revealing performance variations tied to sensor physical properties across object pushing, edge, and surface following tasks.

## Problem

- Lack of accessible and readily available high-resolution tactile sensing technologies and expensive robotic platforms impede broader research in tactile deep RL.
- Sim-to-real deep RL methods often remain confined to specific laboratory setups and sensor types, hindering generalizability and wider adoption.
- Previous claims about the generality of the Tactile Gym sim-to-real approach to various optical tactile sensors lacked empirical validation beyond the TacTip sensor.

## Method

- Integrated three low-cost, high-resolution optical tactile sensors (==DIGIT==, reduced ==TacTip==, and a ==DigiTac== hybrid) into an extended **Tactile Gym 2.0** simulator.
- Adapted the ==sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning== framework for ==zero-shot policy transfer== to an inexpensive Dobot MG400 4-DoF robot arm.
- Utilized a ==GAN== model for ==real-to-sim image translation== to bridge the reality gap between diverse real sensor outputs and simulated tactile images.

## Results

- Simulated deep RL policies achieved comparable high performance across all three sensor types (TacTip, DIGIT, DigiTac) for object pushing, edge following, and surface following tasks.
- Image translation using a GAN attained high Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) values (**0.9818-0.9956**) for all sensors, confirming accurate real-to-sim image transfer across diverse tactile images.
- In real-world tasks, object pushing errors were typically around **10 mm**, with TacTip showing slightly better accuracy (**9-13 mm**) compared to DigiTac and DIGIT (**11-16 mm**); the DIGIT failed to push very light objects due to insufficient tactile deformation. Edge and surface following showed errors between **0.6-1.8 mm**, but the DIGIT struggled with concave surfaces due to its stiffer, flatter sensing surface.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single sim-to-real deep RL approach can effectively generalize across different optical tactile sensor types (image-shading and marker-based) for manipulation tasks, despite their distinct raw tactile image outputs.
> - Performance differences between tactile sensors in physically interactive tasks are primarily attributable to their physical design characteristics, such as elastomer stiffness and sensing surface geometry, rather than fundamental sensing mechanisms.
> - Lowering hardware costs and providing open-source access to software and hardware developments significantly increases the accessibility and potential for acceleration of research in tactile deep reinforcement learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the intersection of deep reinforcement learning (RL) and high-resolution tactile sensing in robotics. The overarching goal in this field is to achieve human-like robot dexterity by integrating advanced deep learning techniques with granular tactile feedback. Recent advancements in applying deep learning to robot vision have demonstrated its potential for complex robot control. Extending this to tactile sensing, particularly with deep RL, offers a pathway for robots to learn intricate manipulation tasks, mirroring how humans acquire skills.

However, the authors identify several challenges that impede progress in tactile deep RL. Firstly, there is a lack of accessible and readily available high-resolution tactile sensing technologies, which limits the number of researchers who can contribute to this area. Secondly, laboratories with expertise in fabricating specific tactile sensors often maintain their focus on those particular technologies, leading to specialized but often isolated advancements. This siloed approach means that successful tactile deep RL methods developed within one laboratory, such as those demonstrated in, frequently remain confined to those specific setups and sensors, hindering broader field progression and efficiency. This contrasts with the open-source culture that has largely benefited AI research.

Existing efforts to address these challenges include Meta AI's open-source TACTO platform and PyTouch libraries for GelSight-based sensors like the DIGIT. Concurrently, the Bristol Robotics Laboratory has developed the TacTip family of sensors, and the Tactile Gym simulation environment. Tactile Gym has facilitated sim-to-real policy transfer via tactile image translation, enabling zero-shot performance in tasks requiring tactile feedback, such as object pushing and precise edge/surface following. While previous work claimed the Tactile Gym approach was applicable to a broad range of optical tactile sensors, this claim had only been empirically validated with the TacTip sensor itself.

This research aims to bridge these disparate developments by integrating GelSight-type sensors with the sim-to-real deep RL methods previously developed for the TacTip within the Tactile Gym framework. It also seeks to lower the barrier to entry for this research by adapting the benchmark to a more affordable robotic platform.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized challenges in advancing tactile deep RL, specifically the high cost of robot platforms, the lack of generalizable sim-to-real methods across diverse sensors, and the limited accessibility of specialized tactile hardware. The authors note that while previous work on Tactile Gym suggested its sim-to-real approach could apply to various high-resolution optical tactile sensors, including GelSight types, this generality had not been empirically demonstrated beyond the TacTip. This paper seeks to validate and expand that claim.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Extend the Tactile Gym Simulator:** To integrate three new low-cost, high-resolution optical tactile sensors—specifically the DIGIT (a GelSight-style sensor), a reduced form-factor TacTip sensor, and a novel DIGIT-TacTip hybrid sensor called DigiTac. This integration aims to create a unified platform that supports both major styles of optical tactile sensors (image-shading based and marker-based), making the platform more versatile and accessible to a wider research community.
2. **Demonstrate General Sim-to-Real Approach:** To validate that a single sim-to-real approach, previously demonstrated only with the TacTip, can effectively achieve robust real-world performance across these three distinct sensors, despite the significant differences in their tactile image outputs.
3. **Lower Research Entry Barrier:** To improve the accessibility of tactile deep RL research by adapting the proposed tasks to an inexpensive, commercially-available 4-degrees-of-freedom (DoF) robot arm, the DOBOT MG400. This modification aims to enable wider dissemination of the benchmark.
4. **Validate on Physically-Interactive Tasks:** To empirically validate the extended environment and the sim-to-real framework on three physically-interactive tasks that require tactile feedback: object pushing, edge following, and surface following.
5. **Compare Sensor Performance:** To provide an empirical comparison of the performance characteristics of these different optical tactile sensors within a sim-to-real context, thereby identifying their respective strengths and weaknesses in various manipulation scenarios. This comparison is intended to serve as a benchmark to inform future researchers in selecting and customizing tactile sensors.
6. **Open-Source Release:** To commit to openly releasing all software and hardware developments associated with this work, further aiding the dissemination and adoption of this benchmark by the broader robotics community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research builds upon the established sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning framework from Church et al., adapting it to a new, more accessible hardware platform and extending its compatibility to a wider range of tactile sensors.

**A. Tactile Robot System**
The physical platform consists of a low-cost desktop robot arm, the Dobot MG400, acting as the manipulator. This 4-DoF arm (x, y, z position, and rotation around z-axis) offers a reduced workspace and fewer degrees of freedom compared to industrial robots used in prior work (e.g., a 6-axis UR5). Its affordability ($2.7k) aims to lower the barrier to entry for researchers. Three distinct high-resolution optical tactile sensors are used as end-effectors:
- **TacTip:** A soft, curved, 3D-printed sensor with an internal array of marker-tipped pins, which amplify surface deformation.
- **DIGIT:** A GelSight-style sensor that uses image shading to detect contact, known for its low cost and compact size. It features a relatively stiffer elastomer compared to TacTip.
- **DigiTac:** A hybrid sensor that integrates a custom 3D-printed TacTip-style skin onto a DIGIT housing, combining the biomimetic soft skin of the TacTip with the camera and lighting system of the DIGIT.

**B. Tactile Sim-to-Real Deep RL Framework**
The core framework involves three phases:
1. **Online Learning in Simulation:** Deep RL policies are trained in the Tactile Gym simulator using images from simulated tactile sensors for tasks such as edge following, surface following, and object pushing.
2. **Domain Adaptation (Real-to-Sim Image Translation):** A generative adversarial network (GAN) model is trained to translate tactile images captured by a real sensor into images that resemble those from the simulated sensor. This step bridges the reality gap between real-world and simulated sensory data.
3. **Zero-shot Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer:** The policies learned in simulation are directly applied to the real robot hardware without further tuning, utilizing the GAN for real-time image translation.

To adapt this framework to the Dobot MG400's 4-DoF limitations, the tasks and data collection procedures were modified. For instance, surface following, which previously used roll and pitch for normal orientation, now relies on a custom 3D-printed flange to mount the sensor perpendicularly to the end effector, utilizing the robot's yaw DoF for orientation control. The simulation environment was extended to include detailed CAD models of the new TacTip, DIGIT, and DigiTac sensors, and their internal camera parameters and contact dynamics (friction, stiffness, damping) were adjusted to achieve realistic simulated performance.

**C. Deep RL with Different Tactile Dynamics in Simulation**
The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm from Stable-Baselines-3 is used for training the deep RL policies. The Tactile Gym simulates contact dynamics using rigid-body physics, rendering tactile information as depth images relative to the sensor's CAD model. Policies are learned for each of the three simulated sensor types, taking their distinct tactile observation spaces as input.

**D. Sim-to-real Transfer for Tactile Images**
A GAN is employed for real-to-sim image translation. The generator utilizes a U-net architecture for spatial feature inference, while the discriminator is a standard convolutional network with batch normalization. Due to varying sensor designs and illumination, image preprocessing and hyperparameters for the GAN model are fine-tuned for each sensor.

**E. Sim-to-real Data Collection**
Twelve distinct datasets (one for each sensor type and contact feature: edge or surface) are collected on the physical robot. Each training dataset contains 5000 tactile images, and each validation dataset contains 2000 images. Data is gathered by randomly contacting 3D-printed stimuli (straight edge and flat surface) while recording sensor poses. The ranges for random contact movement (position and rotation) are specified for each sensor, with adjustments made for the asymmetric designs of the DIGIT and DigiTac.

**F. Tactile Control Tasks**
Three tactile control tasks, adapted from previous work, are evaluated:
1. **Object Pushing:** The robot pushes an object along a predefined trajectory (straight, curved, sinusoidal) on a flat surface. The 2D action space includes x-position and rotation angle of the tool center point (TCP). Three objects (triangular prism, cube, cylinder) with different weights (185g to 363g) are used. Object movement is tracked via ArUco markers.
2. **Edge Following:** The robot slides the sensor along the edge of a planar object, maintaining a fixed distance between the edge and the TCP. The 2D action space involves x and y positions. Three stimuli with varied geometric features (straight, curved, right-angled corner) are used. Ground truth shapes are derived from CAD models.
3. **Surface Following:** The robot slides the sensor over a contacted surface, maintaining a fixed contact depth and orienting the TCP normal to the surface. The 2D action space comprises y-position and rotation angle. Three objects with closed-loop 2D side surfaces (planar, concave, convex) are used. Ground truth shapes are obtained from CAD models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of the extended Tactile Gym and sim-to-real framework yielded several findings across simulation and physical environments.

**A. RL Performance in the Simulated Environments**
Evaluation of policies trained using PPO in the simulated environment showed that all three integrated optical tactile sensors (TacTip, DIGIT, DigiTac) achieved comparable high final performances across the object pushing, edge following, and surface following tasks. Although minor differences in training performance were observed between sensors, these were attributed to varying contact dynamics resulting from the distinct tip shapes of the sensors, rather than fundamental limitations in policy learning. Crucially, it was not necessary to fine-tune training hyperparameters for each individual sensor, and the learned policies were transferred to the real robot without further adjustments.

**B. Image Translation for Sim-to-real Transfer**
The Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) was used to assess the quality of translated images for both edge and surface validation datasets. High SSIM values, approaching unity (0.9818 to 0.9956, with 1.0 being a perfect match), were obtained for all sensors. This indicates that the image-to-image translation GAN successfully produced accurate tactile images for real-to-sim transfer across TacTip-style (TacTip and DigiTac) and GelSight-style (DIGIT) sensors. While the DIGIT's image translation performance was marginally lower than the TacTip and DigiTac, this difference did not appear to impact overall task performance, which the authors attribute to the robustness provided by domain randomization during deep RL policy learning. The results validate the applicability of the image translation method beyond the TacTip family of sensors.

**C. RL Performance in the Physical Environments**

1. **Object Pushing:** The tactile robot successfully pushed objects (cube, cylinder, triangular prism) along straight, curved, and sinusoidal trajectories. Mean Euclidean distance errors for trajectories were typically around 10 mm, relative to an overall travel distance of 200-280 mm and sensor tip sizes of 20-40 mm.
 - The TacTip generally showed slightly superior performance, with accuracies between 9-13 mm.
 - The DigiTac and DIGIT exhibited similar accuracies, ranging from 11-16 mm.
 - The DIGIT, however, failed to push the triangular prism (the lightest object at 185g) across all trajectories. This failure was attributed to the DIGIT's relatively stiffer elastomer, which, with lighter objects, resulted in insufficient tactile deformation. This limited deformation likely caused the real tactile images to fall outside the distribution on which the GAN was trained, leading to inaccurate real-to-sim transfer.
 - Further experiments with the DIGIT pushing the triangular prism with additional weights (up to 150g) improved performance, supporting the hypothesis that increased contact force and corresponding deformation helped the tactile images align with the GAN's training distribution.

2. **Edge Following:** The robot successfully traced the edges of various planar objects, including straight, curved, and corner features, despite the sim-to-real image translation being trained solely on a straight edge.
 - The DigiTac and TacTip achieved mean Euclidean position errors of 0.6-1.4 mm.
 - The DIGIT showed errors between 0.9-1.8 mm.
 - The DIGIT's flatter, stiffer elastomer led to increased sensitivity to small deviations in penetration depth, occasionally resulting in larger localized errors. To mitigate high frictional forces from the necessary deeper contact, objects were coated with wax.

3. **Surface Following:** The tactile sensor successfully followed the curved surfaces of objects with vertical walls, generalizing from a planar surface training for the image translation.
 - The DigiTac and TacTip showed mean Euclidean position errors of 0.6-1.2 mm.
 - The DIGIT achieved the most accurate trajectory on the circular wall, with an error of 0.47 mm.
 - However, the DIGIT exhibited a tendency to get stuck and failed to follow concave surfaces (arch and flower objects). This was attributed to its flatter, stiffer sensing surface hindering smooth sliding over inward-curving geometries.

Overall, the experiments demonstrated zero-shot performance on real objects and trajectories not seen during simulation training for all three tactile sensors. The main differences in task performance were primarily linked to the physical construction and material properties of the sensors (e.g., stiffness, shape of the sensing surface) rather than fundamental limitations of their underlying sensing mechanisms or the sim-to-real framework itself.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of tactile robotics by addressing key barriers to progress in sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning with high-resolution touch sensors.

**Increased Accessibility:** The adoption of the low-cost DOBOT MG400 robot arm significantly lowers the financial barrier for researchers to engage with advanced tactile robotics. Combined with the open-sourcing of all software and hardware developments, this initiative promotes broader dissemination of the benchmark and enables a wider community of researchers to replicate and build upon this work. This is particularly important for institutions with limited budgets or for researchers new to the field.

**Enhanced Generality and Robustness of Sim-to-Real Methods:** The successful integration and validation of a single sim-to-real deep RL approach across three distinct optical tactile sensors—representing both GelSight-style (image-shading based) and TacTip-style (marker-based) sensing principles—demonstrates a notable advancement in the generality of these methods. The ability to achieve robust zero-shot performance on diverse real-world tasks, despite significant differences in sensor design and raw tactile image output, confirms the adaptability of the Tactile Gym framework. This finding suggests that researchers are less constrained by specific sensor types when developing deep RL policies for tactile manipulation.

**Empirical Sensor Comparison and Design Insights:** The detailed empirical comparison of the TacTip, DIGIT, and DigiTac sensors across various physically-interactive tasks provides valuable insights into their respective strengths and weaknesses. The findings highlight that performance differences primarily arise from physical characteristics such such as elastomer stiffness and sensing surface geometry, rather than the core sensing mechanism. For example, the DIGIT's stiffer, flatter surface was shown to be less suitable for following concave surfaces or pushing lighter objects without sufficient deformation, unlike the softer, domed TacTip and DigiTac. Such a benchmark aids researchers in making informed decisions when selecting or customizing tactile sensors for specific manipulation scenarios, guiding future sensor design and material choices.

**Facilitating Future Research and Complex Dexterous Manipulation:** By providing a general, accessible, and validated platform, this work opens avenues for developing more sophisticated sim-to-real deep RL policies for complex robot behaviors. The framework is poised to benefit research in prehensile and dexterous manipulation, allowing fundamental methods to be developed and refined in controlled, accessible environments before being scaled to more challenging applications involving multi-fingered robot hands and intricate contact dynamics. This accelerates the path towards achieving human-like robotic dexterity and more intelligent, adaptable robotic systems.
