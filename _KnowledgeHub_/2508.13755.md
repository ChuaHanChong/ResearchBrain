# Depth-Breadth Synergy in RLVR: Unlocking LLM Reasoning Gains with Adaptive Exploration

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, DARS-Breadth, improves large language model reasoning in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) by adaptively focusing on difficult problems and expanding training batch sizes. This dual approach boosts both Pass@K and Pass@1 performance across mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Existing Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) methods, particularly GRPO variants, exhibit a "cumulative advantage bias" that neglects hard-to-solve problems.
- This bias prevents large language models (LLMs) from effectively learning complex reasoning paths and limits their Pass@K performance.
- The impact of training "breadth" (batch size) in RLVR on LLM reasoning capabilities, especially Pass@1, has been largely underexplored, often defaulting to small fixed sizes.

## Method

- Proposed ==Difficulty Adaptive Rollout Sampling (DARS)== to re-balance cumulative advantage by adaptively performing multi-stage rollouts for low-accuracy, high-difficulty problems.
- Systematically scaled the ==training batch size ("breadth")== and utilized full-batch updates to enhance gradient stability and sustained exploration.
- Integrated DARS with large-breadth training into a unified =="DARS-Breadth" framework== to leverage the complementary benefits of both dimensions.

## Results

- DARS substantially improves Pass@K performance across mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and 7B models) with a **25.3%** reduction in inference cost compared to naive rollout scaling.
- Scaling training breadth dramatically boosts Pass@1 performance and sustains higher token-level entropy, indicating improved exploration.
- The DARS-Breadth framework achieves simultaneous improvements in both Pass@1 and Pass@K, outperforming individual optimizations and demonstrating generalized effectiveness on models like Llama-3.1-8B.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - GRPO-based RLVR suffers from a "cumulative advantage bias" that underweights hard problems; adaptively allocating more compute (multi-stage rollouts) to them rebalances the signal and improves Pass@K without excessive cost.
> - Increasing training "breadth" (batch size) acts as an implicit entropy regularization mechanism, stabilizing training, sustaining exploration, and boosting Pass@1 performance.
> - Optimizing "depth" (focus on hard problems) and "breadth" (data quantity per iteration) are orthogonal yet synergistic approaches to unlocking greater LLM reasoning capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research operates within the field of Large Language Models (LLMs) and their reasoning capabilities, particularly focusing on methods that employ Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). RLVR is characterized by its ability to provide automatic and deterministic correctness checks for LLM outputs, such as mathematical answers or code unit tests. This approach enables scalable verification without extensive manual labeling, distinguishing it from traditional instruction tuning or RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) pipelines that rely on reward models. Recent advancements in reasoning-centric LLMs, exemplified by models like OpenAI-o1, DeepSeek-R1, and Kimi-1.5, have largely been driven by large-scale RLVR. The paper positions RLVR as a promising direction for developing self-evolving LLMs.

Within RLVR, the GRPO (Group-Relative Policy Optimization) algorithm and its variants (e.g., Dr. GRPO, DAPO, VAPO) are recognized as leading approaches. This paper builds upon these foundations by identifying and addressing specific limitations in how current RLVR frameworks, particularly GRPO, manage exploration. Previous studies have increasingly focused on the Pass@K metric (the probability of at least one correct solution among K attempts) as a critical indicator of an LLM's intrinsic reasoning capacity, especially in the context of concerns about RLVR's performance ceiling relative to base models. This work contributes to understanding and improving both Pass@1 (single-attempt accuracy) and Pass@K performance in RLVR by systematically analyzing exploration strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the reasoning abilities of Large Language Models within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) framework. The authors identify that the full potential of RLVR is currently constrained by insufficient exploration along two distinct dimensions:
1. **Depth (Difficulty of Problems):** This refers to the capacity of an LLM to solve increasingly challenging problems. The paper's motivation in this area stems from an analysis of existing GRPO-based algorithms, which are found to exhibit a bias. Specifically, these algorithms disproportionately allocate learning attention to problems of medium difficulty, while down-weighting difficult, low-accuracy problems. These hard problems are considered crucial for truly advancing an LLM's reasoning skills and, consequently, its Pass@K performance. The current bias leads to a "gradient vanishing" problem for groups of hard problems where all rollouts yield incorrect answers, hindering the model's ability to learn from these instances.
2. **Breadth (Number of Training Instances):** This refers to the quantity of unique training instances processed in a single iteration of the RLVR process (i.e., batch size). The paper postulates that current RLVR frameworks inadequately explore the impact of scaling the number of training instances per iteration. Empirical observations suggest that simply increasing rollout size does not consistently improve Pass@K and can sometimes degrade performance for smaller models. Furthermore, the interplay between iteration instance quantity and continuous exploration capability is not fully understood.

The core motivations are therefore:
- To systematically analyze and quantify the "cumulative advantage bias" in GRPO and its variants, which limits the learning from difficult problems and caps Pass@K performance.
- To develop a method, Difficulty Adaptive Rollout Sampling (DARS), that explicitly re-weights difficult problems by allocating more computational resources (targeted, multi-stage rollouts) to them. This aims to expand the sparse reward signals available for challenging problems and improve Pass@K.
- To investigate the impact of increasing the breadth of training data (batch size) on LLM performance, particularly Pass@1, and continuous exploration, suggesting that it might act as an implicit entropy regularization.
- To demonstrate that "depth" (adaptive exploration for problem difficulty) and "breadth" (scaling iteration instances) are orthogonal and complementary dimensions, and that their synergistic combination can unlock simultaneous gains in both Pass@K and Pass@1 performance, moving beyond the limitations of existing RLVR approaches.
- To theoretically connect the proposed DARS-HW schedule to the Maximum Likelihood objective and provide a variance analysis comparing it to Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning (MaxRL).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces Difficulty Adaptive Rollout Sampling (DARS) to address the depth dimension and integrates it with an enhanced breadth scaling approach.

#### 3.1. Difficulty Adaptive Rollout Sampling (DARS)

DARS is designed to re-balance the cumulative advantage computation in RLVR by adaptively allocating more computational resources to difficult problems. It operates in two main phases:

**Phase 1: Pre-Rollout Difficulty Estimation**
- For each reasoning question $q_j$ in a data batch, DARS first performs a lightweight, initial rollout (first-stage rollout) consisting of $N_{pre}$ independent trajectories.
- From these rollouts, a binary reward $r_j^{(i)} \in \{0, 1\}$ (correct/incorrect) is obtained for each trajectory.
- The empirical accuracy $\hat{a}_j$ for question $q_j$ is then calculated as the average of these binary rewards.
- A scalar difficulty score $x_j$ is assigned to each question, defined as the complementary accuracy: $x_j = 1 - \hat{a}_j$. Thus, harder problems (low accuracy) receive a score closer to 1, and easier problems (high accuracy) receive a score closer to 0.

**Phase 2: Multi-Stage Rollout Re-Balancing**
- This phase dynamically decides how many additional trajectories $\Delta n_j$ should be allocated to each question $q_j$ to achieve a desired cumulative advantage. The goal is to up-weight the effective cumulative advantage for low-accuracy (difficult) problems.
- The maximum total rollout sampling for any question is capped at $N_{max}$ to control computational costs.
- Two re-balancing schedules are proposed:
 - **Equal-Treatment (ET) Schedule:** This schedule enforces that the rebalanced cumulative advantage for any question $q_j$ (where $\hat{a}_j < 0.5$) is equal to the cumulative advantage achieved by a medium-difficulty problem (accuracy 0.5) with the initial $N_{pre}$ rollouts. The formula for additional trajectories $\Delta n_j^{ET}$ is derived to achieve this equalization. Theoretically, this schedule induces an optimization objective proportional to the Log-Odds of success, $\nabla_{\theta} \log \frac{p}{1-p}$.
 - **Hardness-Weighted (HW) Schedule:** This schedule imposes a monotonically increasing re-weighting, allocating more rollouts to lower-accuracy problems. The target cumulative advantage for $q_j$ is set to be proportional to its difficulty score $x_j$ multiplied by the reference cumulative advantage of a medium-difficulty problem. The formula for additional trajectories $\Delta n_j^{HW}$ reflects this weighting. Theoretically, this schedule induces an optimization objective proportional to the Maximum Likelihood gradient, $\nabla_{\theta} \log p$.

The core idea of DARS is to shift computation towards problems where the model is currently struggling, allowing for more diverse exploration of solution paths for these challenging instances, and thereby improving Pass@K.

#### 3.2. Depth Synergy with Breadth Scaling (DARS-Breadth)

The dynamic and variable number of rollouts per question introduced by DARS's multi-stage sampling approach is incompatible with standard PPO mini-batch updates. To address this and leverage the benefits of increased breadth, the methodology integrates DARS with a large-breadth training scheme:
- Instead of PPO-style mini-batch updates, the training employs **full-batch gradient descent** across multiple PPO epochs. This ensures compatibility with DARS's dynamic allocation of rollouts.
- The batch size for this full-batch training is significantly increased.
- This large-breadth approach is theorized to offer two main advantages: (1) reduction of mini-batch gradient noise, leading to more accurate gradient directions, and (2) maintenance of high token-level entropy throughout training, acting as an implicit regularization against premature convergence and sustaining exploration for Pass@1.

The combined approach, DARS-Breadth, aims to unify the depth-adaptive sampling (from DARS) with breadth maximization through large batch sizes and full-batch updates.

#### 3.3. Training Target

The training objective used is a clipped objective function derived from GRPO, but without the KL penalty term and without response length handling, following the methodology of Dr. GRPO. This ensures consistency with a common and empirically strong RLVR baseline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the dynamics of RLVR training and the efficacy of the proposed DARS and DARS-Breadth methods.

#### 4.1. Cumulative Advantage Bias and Its Effects

- **GRPO Bias:** Analysis revealed that GRPO and its variants exhibit a cumulative advantage bias, funneling weight towards problems of medium difficulty and under-weighting high-difficulty, low-accuracy problems. This bias limits the improvement of Pass@K performance.
- **Naive Rollout Scaling:** Simply increasing the rollout size (Depth-Naive) did not consistently improve Pass@K. While it benefited Pass@1, for smaller models (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), it could even harm Pass@K performance.
- **Breadth Impact:** Increasing the training batch size (Breadth-Naive) significantly improved Pass@1 performance for all models. This was correlated with sustained high token-level entropy, suggesting that larger breadth acts as implicit entropy regularization, delaying premature convergence and fostering continuous exploration. However, Breadth-Naive could sometimes harm Pass@K for smaller models.

#### 4.2. DARS Effectiveness for Depth Exploration

- **Pass@K Improvement:** DARS, with both ET (Equal-Treatment) and HW (Hardness-Weighted) schedules, consistently improved Pass@K performance compared to the RLVR baseline and Depth-Naive approaches. This indicates that adaptively re-weighting difficult problems is effective in enhancing the model's ability to solve complex tasks when given multiple attempts.
- **Computational Efficiency:** DARS achieved higher Pass@K performance with substantially less inference cost compared to naively scaling the rollout size. DARS-ET reduced rollouts per prompt by approximately 52.5-60% and training time per step by 33.8-35.8% compared to Depth-Naive (N=32), while DARS-HW also showed reductions (25.3-37.2% fewer rollouts, 10.6-14.1% less time).
- **Implicit Objectives:** The ET schedule was found to induce a Log-Odds optimization objective, while the HW schedule induced a Maximum Likelihood objective, providing theoretical grounding for their different weighting behaviors. The HW schedule consistently yielded superior Pass@K curves.
- **Longer Thought:** DARS models consistently produced longer reasoning traces on challenging benchmarks (e.g., AIME 2024) compared to the GRPO baseline, along with higher accuracy, indicating that DARS stimulates deeper problem-solving processes.

#### 4.3. Depth-Breadth Synergy (DARS-Breadth)

- **Simultaneous Gains:** The combined approach, DARS-Breadth, demonstrated simultaneous improvements in both Pass@K and Pass@1. This finding supports the hypothesis that depth (adaptive exploration) and breadth (scaling iteration instances) are complementary dimensions. The DARS-Breadth curves consistently occupied the outermost envelope in Pass@1-Pass@K plots, indicating superior overall performance.
- **Superiority of HW Schedule in Synergy:** DARS-HW-Breadth generally outperformed DARS-ET-Breadth, particularly in Pass@K, reinforcing the observation that the HW schedule's emphasis on difficult samples is beneficial.

#### 4.4. Generalization and Robustness

- **Benchmark Performance:** DARS-Breadth consistently achieved the highest Pass@1 and Pass@K scores across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, MATH-500, OlympiadBench, AMC23, MinervaMath) for various Qwen2.5-Math models and Llama-3.1-8B.
- **Test-Time Scaling:** DARS consistently improved test-time scaling performance, evidenced by higher maj@K and Pass@K scores (e.g., AIME24 maj@16 saw gains of 4.6 points for 1.5B and 11.4 points for 7B models over baseline). This indicates a stronger underlying solution space.
- **Out-of-Domain Generalization:** DARS-HW-Breadth also showed improvements on non-mathematical tasks like GPQA-Diamond (scientific reasoning) and HumanEval (code generation), suggesting the mechanisms are not domain-specific.
- **Temperature Robustness:** The performance improvements from DARS were consistent across different sampling temperatures during evaluation.
- **Model Agnostic:** DARS was effective across different model sizes (1.5B, 7B, 8B) and even on the openPangu-7B architecture on Huawei Ascend NPU, indicating broad applicability.
- **Comparison to Static Allocation:** DARS (online adaptive allocation) outperformed Static DARS (fixed difficulty scores from base model), confirming that difficulty is policy-dependent and evolves during RL training.

#### 4.5. Theoretical Connection to Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning

- DARS-HW was theoretically shown to optimize an expected objective identical to Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning (MaxRL).
- Crucially, DARS-HW operates as a variance-reduced estimator compared to MaxRL. While MaxRL scales advantage algebraically (leading to O(1/u^2) variance growth for difficult problems $u \to 0$), DARS-HW achieves the same expected gradient by adaptively increasing rollouts (leading to O(1/u) variance growth), resulting in more stable and efficient optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to the field of Large Language Models and Reinforcement Learning, with implications for improving reasoning capabilities.

The primary significance lies in the systematic identification and empirical validation of two distinct yet complementary dimensions—**depth and breadth**—for enhancing LLM performance within the RLVR framework. By demonstrating that current GRPO-based methods suffer from a "cumulative advantage bias" that undervalues difficult problems, the research highlights a fundamental limitation in existing approaches. The introduction of **Difficulty Adaptive Rollout Sampling (DARS)** provides a method to counteract this bias, enabling models to learn from harder instances more effectively and improving Pass@K performance. This is particularly relevant for tasks requiring complex, multi-step reasoning, such as advanced mathematics and programming.

The finding that **large training breadth** significantly boosts Pass@1 performance and sustains token-level exploration introduces a simple yet impactful strategy for RLVR. The combined **DARS-Breadth** approach, which synergistically leverages both adaptive depth exploration and large-scale breadth, achieves simultaneous improvements in both Pass@K and Pass@1. This dual improvement is significant because Pass@1 is crucial for single-shot deployment scenarios, while Pass@K indicates the model's deeper reasoning capacity under more extensive sampling. The theoretical insight into DARS-HW's equivalence to Maximum Likelihood Reinforcement Learning, coupled with its superior variance properties, provides a more stable and efficient paradigm for optimizing models towards a Maximum Likelihood objective.

Potential impacts include:
- **More Capable LLMs:** By enabling LLMs to learn more effectively from difficult problems and maintain broad exploration, the methods presented could lead to the development of more robust and capable reasoning models.
- **Improved Efficiency:** DARS achieves better Pass@K with fewer rollouts compared to naive scaling, suggesting a more resource-efficient way to train advanced reasoning skills.
- **New Training Strategies:** The discovery of depth-breadth synergy opens avenues for designing more sophisticated RLVR training curricula, such as dynamically adjusting parameters (e.g., $N_{max}$) to transition from broad exploration to focused refinement, potentially maximizing both Pass@K and Pass@1 over the training lifecycle.
- **Broader Applicability:** The observed generalization of DARS to non-mathematical domains suggests its core principles could be applied to a wider range of reasoning-intensive tasks, thereby benefiting various applications of LLMs.
- **Enhanced Understanding of RLVR:** The analysis contributes to a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics of RLVR algorithms, particularly regarding exploration biases and the role of batch size in model learning and exploration.

Overall, this work presents a foundational advancement in RLVR methodology by addressing critical exploration limitations, leading to more robust and higher-performing LLMs across different reasoning tasks and deployment scenarios.
