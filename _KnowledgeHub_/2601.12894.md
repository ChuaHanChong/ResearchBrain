# Sparse ActionGen: Accelerating Diffusion Policy with Real-time Pruning

> [!summary] Summary
> Sparse ActionGen (SAG) is an adaptive method that prunes redundant computations in Diffusion Policy models for real-time robot manipulation. It achieves up to 4x speedup on various simulation and real-world tasks by dynamically generating pruning schedules based on observations and employing a "one-for-all" activation reusing strategy, maintaining high task success rates.

## Problem

- Diffusion Policy models face high computational costs due to their multi-step denoising process, limiting their action generation frequency and making them impractical for real-time visuomotor control (e.g., 50-1000 Hz).
- Existing acceleration methods often rely on static caching schedules that fail to adapt to dynamic robot-environment interactions, leading to inconsistent and suboptimal performance across different rollout iterations.
- Prior caching paradigms overlook significant computational redundancies that exist across different blocks within the Diffusion Transformer architecture.

## Method

- Sparse **ActionGen** (SAG) introduces a two-stage, rollout-adaptive prune-then-reuse pipeline that dynamically generates pruning schedules based on the current observation.
- A real-time diffusion pruner, an efficient observation-conditioned neural network, predicts a global binary pruning mask for all denoising steps and DiT blocks in a single forward pass before policy inference.
- A "one-for-all" reusing strategy is implemented, utilizing shared cache buffers across blocks of the same type to facilitate cross-block and cross-timestep activation reuse, minimizing global redundancy.

## Results

- Achieved average speedups of **3.63x** (Proficient Human demos) and **3.77x** (Mixed Human demos) on simulation benchmarks with over **90%** pruning, while maintaining average success rates of **84%** and **81%** respectively.
- Demonstrated real-world efficacy on a Franka robot, achieving high inference frequencies (**44.3**–**46.1** Hz) and improving success rates on challenging tasks like Assembly Line from **26%** (Full Computation) to **58%**.
- Outperformed or matched state-of-the-art caching and distillation methods by consistently delivering high speedups while largely preserving or improving task performance, showing robustness across diverse tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal computational sparsity patterns in Diffusion Policies are dynamic and vary significantly across different rollout iterations, necessitating environment-aware adaptation rather than static schedules.
> - Significant redundancies exist not only within individual blocks or timesteps but also across different Diffusion Transformer blocks, making a global 'one-for-all' activation reuse strategy highly effective.
> - An efficient, observation-conditioned pruner can predict optimal sparsity patterns a priori with minimal overhead, enabling real-time adaptation of computational resources.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Diffusion Policy models have emerged as a prominent approach in robotic control due to their capacity for modeling multi-modal action distributions. This characteristic has facilitated their integration into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, where they function as an action generation component for complex manipulation tasks. However, a significant limitation of Diffusion Policy models is the substantial computational demand associated with their multi-step denoising processes. This computational burden restricts their action generation frequency, rendering them impractical for real-time visuomotor control. For instance, achieving the required 50-1000 Hz for certain robotic arms necessitates an action generation speed well beyond what standard Diffusion Policy models can typically deliver (e.g., 20 Hz for a 50-step denoising process on an RTX 4090 GPU).

Existing research efforts have attempted to address this inference bottleneck. These methods generally fall into categories such as caching-based acceleration or distillation. Caching-based approaches, like EfficientVLA and BAC, aim to reduce computation by reusing redundant activations. However, a common limitation among many of these methods is their reliance on static caching schedules. These schedules are predefined offline and remain constant throughout the robot's interaction with the environment (the "rollout process"). The paper identifies that such static schedules fail to adapt to the dynamic nature of robot-environment interactions, leading to suboptimal performance variations across different rollout iterations. This is evidenced by experiments showing inconsistent performance of fixed schedules.

Distillation methods, such as One-Step Diffusion Policy, Consistency Policy (CP), and SDM Policy, aim to compress Diffusion Policies into faster student policies, often reducing the number of denoising steps. Other approaches like VDD compress policies into Mixture-of-Experts models. Streaming Policy, RNR-DP, and Falcon utilize partial denoising trajectories from previous iterations.

Sparse ActionGen (SAG) positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of static caching and offering an adaptive, real-time solution. It builds upon the concept of caching but introduces dynamic, environment-aware pruning to enable significant acceleration without performance degradation, differentiating itself from methods that use static schedules or solely rely on distillation for speedup. The work aims to make Diffusion Policy truly viable for high-frequency, real-time robotic control by dynamically managing computational resources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to significantly accelerate Diffusion Policy for real-time visuomotor control, making it practical for high-frequency robotic operations.

This objective is motivated by several key challenges and observations:

1. **Computational Bottleneck of Diffusion Policy:** Diffusion Policy models, while powerful in modeling multi-modal action distributions, inherently involve multi-step denoising processes. This iterative computation leads to a substantial latency in action generation, making them too slow for real-time robotic control applications that demand high execution frequencies (e.g., 50-1000 Hz for robot arms).
2. **Limitations of Existing Acceleration Methods (Static Schedules):** Prior caching-based acceleration techniques for diffusion models and Diffusion Policy, such as EfficientVLA and BAC, typically employ static caching schedules. These schedules are predetermined and do not adapt to the changing dynamics of the robot-environment interaction during a rollout. The authors demonstrate through empirical evaluation (leave-one-out analysis on fixed caching schedules) that the optimal computational sparsity pattern varies significantly across different rollout iterations. A schedule optimized for one iteration often performs poorly in others, leading to inconsistent and suboptimal trade-offs between performance and efficiency.
3. **Need for Rollout-Adaptive and Environment-Aware Adaptation:** To overcome the rigidity of static schedules, there is a clear motivation to develop an adaptive mechanism that can dynamically adjust the computational sparsity based on the current state of the robot and its environment. This adaptation should occur in real-time to maintain closed-loop visuomotor control.
4. **Minimizing Overhead of Adaptive Mechanisms:** Introducing a dynamic mechanism for pruning inherently risks adding computational overhead, which could negate the acceleration benefits. Therefore, a crucial motivation is to design an adaptive pruner that is highly parameter- and inference-efficient, capable of real-time prediction without significant additional cost.
5. **Addressing Overlooked Redundancies (Inter-Block and Inter-Timestep):** Existing block-wise caching paradigms primarily focus on redundancies within individual blocks or across timesteps in a localized manner. The authors observe and highlight the presence of significant redundancies *across different blocks* as well. This motivates the development of a global approach to resource allocation and activation reuse to maximize efficiency.

Based on these motivations, the specific objectives of SAG are:
- To propose a rollout-adaptive prune-then-reuse mechanism that identifies prunable computations globally and reuses cached activations on the fly during action diffusion.
- To parameterize an observation-conditioned diffusion pruner that predicts sparsity patterns *a priori* (before inference) based on current observations, enabling environment-aware adaptation.
- To instantiate this pruner with a highly parameter- and inference-efficient architectural design that generates a global pruning graph in a single forward pass for the entire denoising process.
- To introduce a "one-for-all" reusing strategy that reuses activations across both timesteps and blocks in a zig-zag manner, aiming to minimize global redundancy.
- To incorporate an end-to-end global sparsity loss during training, guiding the pruner to non-uniformly allocate computational resources across timesteps and blocks under a strict budget.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Sparse ActionGen (SAG) addresses the challenge of accelerating Diffusion Policy for real-time visuomotor control through a two-stage, rollout-adaptive prune-then-reuse pipeline. This methodology is designed to dynamically adapt to robot-environment interactions by identifying and reusing redundant computations.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Three-Level Redundancy Analysis

The authors frame the computational redundancy within the action diffusion process across three nested levels:
- **Rollout-Level:** Describes the iterative interaction where the policy generates an action $a_t$ based on observation $o_t$, leading to a new observation $o_{t+1}$.
- **Denoising-Level:** Within each rollout iteration, the action $a_t$ is obtained by performing $K$ reverse denoising steps using a conditional denoiser $f_\theta$.
- **Block-Level:** The denoiser $f_\theta$ is parameterized by a Diffusion Transformer (DiT). Each of its $L$ layers integrates temporal and observation conditioning via three blocks: self-attention (SA), cross-attention (CA), and a feed-forward network (FFN).

#### 3.2. Real-time Diffusion Pruner

The core of SAG's adaptive acceleration lies in its real-time diffusion pruner, $G_\psi$, which dynamically generates pruning schedules.

- **Motivation for Adaptation:** The paper empirically demonstrates that optimal caching schedules vary significantly across different rollout iterations, indicating the inadequacy of static schedules. This highlights the need for a mechanism that adapts to changing rollout dynamics.
- **Environment-aware Adaptation:** To enable dynamic adaptation, the pruner $G_\psi$ is parameterized and conditioned on the current observation $o_t$. This allows it to learn a direct mapping from environmental inputs to a predicted sparsity pattern, generating a binary pruning mask $M \in \{0, 1\}^{K \times 3L}$ *before* the policy inference. A mask value of 1 indicates a prunable computation for a specific block $b$ at timestep $k$. This approach avoids the computational overhead of profiling post-forward activations, which existing rule-based methods typically require.
- **Real-time Prediction (Efficient Architecture):** To minimize the overhead introduced by the parameterized pruner, $G_\psi$ is designed for parameter and inference efficiency.
 - It uses a single network to serve all DiT blocks, predicting the global sparsity pattern for all $K$ denoising steps and $3L$ blocks in a *single forward pass*.
 - Timestep and block indices are encoded using sinusoidal positional embeddings, which are then concatenated (rather than summed) to preserve their informational orthogonality.
 - A visual encoder processes the current observation $o_t$, and its embedding is concatenated with the spatiotemporal representation.
 - A three-layer MLP projects this combined feature vector to 2-dimensional logits, from which the binary pruning mask $M$ is obtained via an argmax operation. The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) is used to enable gradient flow through this non-differentiable operation during training.
- **Optimization Objective:** The pruner is trained to minimize a total loss $L_{total}$ comprising two components:
 - **Policy Fidelity Loss ($L_{fidelity}$):** This term ensures that the behavior of the pruned policy $\pi'$ aligns with the ground-truth actions $a^*$ from a reference dataset $D_{ref}$ (approximately 5% of the original training data). It is formulated as the mean absolute error between the pruned policy's output and the ground-truth action: $E_{(o,a^*) \sim D_{ref}} [||\pi'(o, M) - a^*||]$.
 - **Global Sparsity Loss ($L_{sparsity}$):** This loss enforces a target global pruning rate $\rho$ by penalizing the deviation of the overall sparsity degree of the mask $M$ from $\rho$. It uses a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) loss: $|| \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k,b} M_{k,b} - \rho ||$, where $N$ is the total number of prunable units ($K \times 3L$). This enables non-uniform allocation of computational resources across timesteps and blocks under a strict budget. The overall loss is $L_{total} = L_{fidelity} + \beta L_{sparsity}$, with $\beta=1$.

#### 3.3. One-for-All Reusing Strategy

For computations identified as prunable, SAG reuses previously cached activations. This strategy departs from traditional block-wise caching.

- **Motivation for Global Reuse:** The authors observe strong structural similarities in activations across *different blocks* (Figure 4a), indicating an overlooked source of redundancy in block-wise caching paradigms.
- **Mechanism:** Instead of maintaining independent cache buffers for each block, SAG utilizes a *shared buffer* $\tau$ for all blocks of the same type (e.g., all SA blocks share one buffer, all CA blocks share another, etc.).
 - When a block is executed (not pruned, $M^k_b = 0$), its output $d^k_b$ updates the corresponding shared buffer $\tau$.
 - When a block is pruned ($M^k_b = 1$), SAG reuses the activation stored in $\tau$ as its output.
- **Effect:** This "one-for-all" design facilitates cross-block and cross-timestep activation reuse in a "zig-zag" manner (Figure 4b), minimizing global redundancy. The computation for a hidden state $h$ passing through a block $b$ at step $k$ is formulated as $h^{\lfloor b/3 \rfloor}_k = h^{\lfloor b/3 \rfloor - 1}_k + (1 - M^k_b) \cdot d^k_b + M^k_b \cdot \tau$, with $\tau$ updated as $\tau = (1 - M^k_b) \cdot d^k_b + M^k_b \cdot \tau$. This strategy is applied during both the training and inference stages of the pruner.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Sparse ActionGen (SAG) encompassed a range of simulation benchmarks and real-world robotic tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness in accelerating Diffusion Policy without significant performance degradation.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance and Speedup

- **Simulation Benchmarks:** On Proficient Human (PH) and Mixed Human (MH) demonstration datasets across tasks like Lift, Can, Square, Transport, and Tool hang, SAG achieved average success rates of 84% (PH) and 81% (MH). It delivered speedups of 3.63× (PH) and 3.77× (MH), with pruning rates exceeding 90% of computations.
- **Multi-stage Kitchen Task:** For the more complex Kitchen task, SAG achieved a 100% success rate while providing a 4.03× speedup.
- **Real-world Evaluation:** On a Franka Research 3 robot performing tasks such as Stack Bowls, Insert Pen, and Assembly Line, SAG maintained high inference frequencies (44.3–46.1 Hz). It also demonstrated competitive success rates, notably increasing the Assembly Line task success from 26% (Full Computation) to 58%, indicating improved responsiveness in tasks requiring precision.

#### 4.2. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Baselines

SAG generally outperformed or matched the performance of various existing acceleration methods:
- **Caching-based Methods (EfficientVLA, L2C, BAC):** EfficientVLA, relying on static schedules, showed significant performance drops on complex tasks (e.g., 3% success on Kitchen). L2C provided a modest 1.28× speedup due to its fixed caching policy. BAC, while achieving good speedups, sometimes struggled on complex tasks like Transport due to its block-wise caching and inability to adapt.
- **Distillation and Other Acceleration Methods (DDIM, Falcon, SDP, CP):** DDIM provided a speedup of approximately 3.3× but sometimes with a slight performance decrease. Falcon and SDP achieved suboptimal speedups (1.13-2.85x for Falcon, 1.67-1.88x for SDP) due to coarser-grained strategies. Consistency Policy (CP) offered high speedups (e.g., 15x) but often suffered from severe performance degradation (e.g., 0% on Tool Hang, 15% on Can MH), attributed to excessive compression.
In contrast, SAG consistently achieved high speedups while largely preserving or improving task performance, demonstrating its effectiveness in adapting to rollout dynamics and globally optimizing computation.

#### 4.3. Compatibility with Efficient Samplers

SAG demonstrated compatibility with sampler-level acceleration methods. When combined with DPM-Solver++ (DPM50+SAG), it maintained the success rates of the stronger DPM50 schedule across most tasks and even improved performance on tasks like Square (MH), suggesting that SAG's pruning mechanism is complementary and does not merely mimic shorter denoising trajectories.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Real-time Diffusion Pruner:** Substituting the real-time pruner with a fixed caching schedule (i.e., removing environment-aware adaptation) resulted in a notable performance drop, with the average success rate decreasing by 6% on MH and Kitchen tasks. This validates the importance of dynamic, environment-aware pruning.
- **One-for-All Reusing Strategy:** Replacing the one-for-all strategy with a block-wise reusing strategy led to a significant 12% average performance drop. This finding supports the efficacy of reusing activations across both blocks and timesteps to reduce redundancy.
- **Global Sparsity Loss:** Removing the global sparsity loss (and using a block-wise uniform budget) resulted in a substantial performance collapse, with average success rates dropping by 37% on PH tasks and 32% on MH tasks, and 0% on some specific tasks. This indicates the critical role of non-uniform, global allocation of computational resources.

#### 4.5. Sensitivity Analysis

- **Pruning Rate:** SAG maintained an average success rate of 85% at both 80% and 91% pruning rates, indicating robust performance under aggressive computation reduction. At an extreme 97% pruning rate, the average success rate dropped to 51%, with failures predominantly observed in contact-sensitive tasks (Can, Transport variants), while Kitchen tasks remained robust. This suggests that the most extreme pruning levels may remove computations necessary for fine-grained corrections.

#### 4.6. Qualitative Results

- **Sparsity Patterns:** Visualizations of predicted sparsity patterns across rollout iterations (e.g., moving a kettle) showed large white regions indicating high sparsity. The patterns dynamically shifted with observations, demonstrating the pruner's ability to adapt. Denser computations were observed in the top and right areas of the sparsity map, corresponding to upper blocks and later denoising steps, suggesting these parts contribute more significantly to activation changes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Sparse ActionGen (SAG) method presents several significant contributions and holds potential for notable impact in the field of robotic control and artificial intelligence:

1. **Enabling Real-time Visuomotor Control with Diffusion Policies:** SAG directly addresses the primary bottleneck of Diffusion Policies, their high computational cost, by enabling substantial acceleration (up to 4x speedup) while maintaining or improving task performance. This makes Diffusion Policies, which are strong in modeling multi-modal action distributions, practical for real-time visuomotor control applications requiring high action frequencies, such as those involving advanced robotic manipulators.
2. **Advancement in Adaptive Computational Resource Management:** The introduction of a rollout-adaptive, real-time diffusion pruner represents a notable advancement over static caching schedules. By conditioning the pruner on current observations, SAG allows for dynamic and environment-aware allocation of computational resources, which is crucial for handling the interactive and often unpredictable nature of robot-environment dynamics. This adaptive approach ensures consistent performance across varied rollout iterations, unlike previous methods.
3. **Enhanced Efficiency through Global Optimization:** SAG's methodology refines the acceleration pipeline through a global perspective. The end-to-end global sparsity loss enables non-uniform and optimized allocation of computational resources across all denoising timesteps and DiT blocks. Furthermore, the "one-for-all" reusing strategy minimizes redundancy by leveraging similarities in activations across different blocks and timesteps, leading to more efficient caching and greater computational savings.
4. **Robustness and Generalizability:** The demonstrated effectiveness across a diverse set of robot manipulation tasks in both simulation (RoboMimic, Franka Kitchen) and real-world scenarios, including multi-stage and contact-sensitive tasks, indicates the robustness and generalizability of SAG. Its compatibility with existing efficient samplers further enhances its practical applicability.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages exploration of adaptive compute budgets for dynamic environments, more data-efficient training methods for pruners, and the extension of sparse action generation techniques to larger generalist policies and a wider range of hardware platforms and long-horizon tasks.

In summary, SAG contributes to making advanced, model-based robotic control more computationally efficient and adaptable to real-world operational constraints, thereby broadening the practical utility of Diffusion Policies in robotics.
