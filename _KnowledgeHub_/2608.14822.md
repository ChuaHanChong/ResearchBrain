# Imagining Recovery: Inference-Time Counterfactual Realignment for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> CoRe (Counterfactual Realignment) is a training-free, inference-time framework that recovers a frozen VLA from goal switches or physical perturbations by imagining a closed-loop rollout from a recent in-distribution state using synthesized observations, then physically restoring only the minimal set of displaced objects before returning control to the policy. Across simulators, backbones, and real hardware, CoRe lifts success rates by up to 85 points while cutting physical restorations by 42.2%.

## Problem

- Failure-conditioned retraining approaches need costly collection of perturbed or failed trajectories and must be repeated as new failure modes emerge, limiting scalability.
- Delegating recovery to external LLM/VLM agents or residual policies adds inference overhead and recovery quality depends entirely on that auxiliary module's own reliability.
- Reset- or rewind-based recovery to a fixed checkpoint or retrieved demonstration frame discards completed task progress or stays confined to previously seen states.

## Method

- ==Online suffix OOD detection== monitors the action-expert's suffix latent via a per-task Mahalanobis score over stochastic forward passes, triggering recovery without training a separate classifier.
- **Phase-aware recovery anchoring** scans the execution trace for the latest in-distribution frame using chunk-divergence comparisons, refining the anchor to sub-chunk control-step resolution via forward kinematics.
- ==Synthesized counterfactual continuation== rolls out the frozen policy from voxel-accumulated RGB-D observations and FK-rendered arm poses, then probe-based minimal restoration selects the smallest object subset preserving the imagined trajectory before physical handoff.

## Results

- On LangSwitch, **CoRe** raises three VLA backbones from **18.3-35.5%** to **82.6-92.7%** average success across 27 switch settings, with zero false triggers over 1,500 unswitched runs.
- On LIBERO-Long with perturbations, CoRe reaches **69.8-88.2%** success versus **12.8-43.5%** for bare baselines, and real-world xArm6 trials lift success from as low as **1/20** to **17-19/20** episodes.
- Ablations show CoRe prunes restorations to **0.67** objects per episode versus **1.16** for full restoration, and virtual probing is **2-5** times cheaper than physical testing while cutting collision/e-stop failures from **21** to **5** of 100 episodes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frozen VLA policies tolerate geometrically synthesized observations well enough that closed-loop counterfactual rollouts can substitute for a learned world model or privileged simulator access.
> - Planning recovery virtually before any physical motion turns costly, risky physical trial-and-error into cheap imagined probes, decoupling recovery-decision quality from execution cost.
> - Preserving completed task progress by anchoring recovery at the latest viable state, rather than resetting or rewinding, avoids re-doing already-finished subgoals and generalizes across both goal-switch and physical-perturbation failure modes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) models represent a notable advancement in robotic manipulation, offering enhanced flexibility and generality for robots to interpret diverse instructions and operate across varied objects, tasks, and environments. These models typically integrate large-scale vision-language representations with robotic action generation. However, a significant limitation of current VLA models is their fragility when encountering out-of-distribution (OOD) execution states, such as unexpected changes in task goals, scene configurations, or the robot's own state. This fragility arises primarily because these models are often trained exclusively on successful, forward-progressing demonstrations, which do not inherently encode corrective behaviors for deviations. Once the consistency between language, observation, and robot configuration is disrupted, VLA policies tend to lack autonomous recovery capabilities, leading to compounding errors and eventual task failure. Such disruptions are common in real-world deployments, where physical disturbances or mid-task instruction changes can occur.

The existing research landscape for VLA recovery broadly follows two main directions. The first approach involves augmenting nominal training data with failure-conditioned supervision. This supervision can be obtained from human interventions during deployment, from injecting perturbations into successful trajectories and appending corrective segments, or from synthesizing failed rollouts using simulators or learned world models. Policies are then post-trained using methods like behavioral cloning or preference optimization. A primary drawback of this approach is the requirement for costly failure data collection, which may need to be repeated as new failure modes emerge. The second direction delegates recovery responsibilities to an auxiliary module. These modules might include large language model (LLM) or vision-language model (VLM)-based agents for failure diagnosis and replanning, external supervisors providing textual or visual corrections, or learned residual policies that modify actions at runtime. However, these methods introduce additional training or inference overhead and rely on the auxiliary system's ability to consistently produce reliable, executable recovery decisions. Beyond these two primary directions, some methods involve resetting or rewinding execution to a predetermined state, such as a subtask checkpoint or a retrieved frame from prior demonstrations. These approaches risk discarding completed task progress, may fail to effectively escape the OOD state, or remain confined to a limited repertoire of known states. This current work aims to address these limitations by proposing an alternative recovery paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an inference-time framework, termed Counterfactual Realignment (CoRe), that enables frozen VLA models to recover from online disruptions without requiring policy retraining, failure-specific data, or external auxiliary models. The motivation for this objective stems from several observed limitations of existing VLA models and their recovery mechanisms.

First, VLA models, despite their advancements in flexibility and generality, exhibit brittleness when confronted with deviations from their training distribution. This fragility in the face of online perturbations (e.g., changes in scene, robot state, or task instruction) often leads to task failure because the policy lacks inherent corrective behaviors. The authors are motivated to create a mechanism that confers robustness to these models without altering their original learned policies.

Second, the current state of recovery methods often entails significant costs and risks. Approaches that rely on augmenting training data with failure cases necessitate extensive and often repetitive data collection, which is resource-intensive. Methods employing auxiliary modules introduce additional computational overhead and depend on the reliability of a separate, potentially complex system. The motivation for CoRe is to bypass these demands, offering a training-free solution that operates purely at inference time with a frozen VLA backbone.

Third, existing recovery strategies, particularly those involving resets or rollbacks, can discard valuable task progress that has already been achieved. Such an outcome is inefficient and diminishes the overall effectiveness of robotic agents. A key motivation for CoRe is to preserve completed task progress by identifying a suitable recovery anchor and planning a continuation from there.

Finally, the researchers are motivated to provide a unified approach that can handle diverse types of online disruptions, including both mid-episode instruction changes (language switches) and physical perturbations to the environment or robot state. By addressing these varied failure modes within a single framework, CoRe aims to increase the practical applicability and generalization of VLA models in dynamic, unpredictable environments. The underlying principle is to replace physical trial-and-error with virtual planning, enhancing both safety and efficiency of recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Counterfactual Realignment (CoRe) framework operates at inference time to recover a frozen VLA from online perturbations. The central principle involves generating a "counterfactual continuation" – a virtual rollout of what the VLA would do from a recent in-distribution state under the current target goal – and then performing a minimal physical realignment to rejoin this imagined trajectory. The framework consists of four main stages: Online Suffix OOD Detection, Phase-Aware Recovery Anchoring, Synthesized Counterfactual Continuation, and Probe-Based Minimal Restoration, followed by Physical Rearrangement and Handoff.

### 4. Online Suffix OOD Detection

The system continuously monitors the VLA's internal state to detect deviations. It focuses on the action-expert suffix latent, which is the final hidden state of the action head before motor decoding. For each policy query, `K` stochastic forward passes generate latent vectors, which are then mean-pooled into a vector `h_t`. A per-task Mahalanobis score `s_t` is computed, comparing `h_t` to statistical properties (mean and ridge-regularized covariance) derived from in-distribution successful rollouts for that specific task. Recovery is triggered if `s_t` exceeds a pre-calibrated high quantile threshold `τ_k` for `n_consec` consecutive queries. This approach avoids training a separate OOD detector and focuses on initiating autonomous realignment rather than merely aborting execution.

### 5. Phase-Aware Recovery Anchoring

Upon detection of an OOD state, CoRe identifies a "recovery anchor," which is defined as the latest trace frame from which recovery can commence while preserving all task progress completed up to that point. This involves scanning the recorded execution trace forward. At each frame, the system compares the action chunks predicted by the policy under the original instruction `ℓ` and the potentially new target instruction `ℓ'`. If the instruction has not changed (`ℓ = ℓ'`), the divergence metric `d_t` instead compares a clean, hypothetical rollout to the recorded configuration. The scan is designed to be "phase-aware," skipping frames where objects are still settling or the gripper is not clear, ensuring the anchor is in reproducible free space. The first frame where `d_t` exceeds a divergence threshold `δ` marks the "divergence onset." This onset is refined within the corresponding action chunk, and the arm configuration for the anchor is advanced by forward-kinematic integration based on the sub-chunk up to the refined onset.

### 6. Synthesized Counterfactual Continuation

From the identified recovery anchor, CoRe executes a closed-loop rollout of the frozen VLA policy under the target instruction `ℓ'`, forming a "counterfactual continuation" `τ_B`. This rollout is performed entirely on *synthesized observations*, eliminating the need for ground-truth renders from a simulator or physical robot motion.
- **Observation Synthesis:** Per-object voxel stores, accumulated from segmented RGB-D history, along with a world-frame background store, are used. For the third-person (exocentric) view, the real anchor frame is edited: the arm and any displaced objects are removed, the vacated regions are filled using background voxels, displaced objects are re-rendered at their imagined poses via z-buffered voxel splatting, and the arm is composited from a forward-kinematic (FK) render. For the first-person (egocentric) view, the entire image is reconstructed from the voxel stores at the imagined, moving arm viewpoint. Residual holes in both views are filled via inpainting.
- **Closed-loop Rollout:** In each imagined tick, synthesized observations are generated, the VLA `π` is queried, and the arm's pose is updated via FK. This process continues until the robot's tool-center-point (TCP) comes within a proximity threshold `d_prox` of any scene object, as FK alone cannot accurately model contact dynamics. The latest pre-contact frame from this virtual rollout becomes the "rejoin state" `c_star`, to which the physical system will be realigned.

### 7. Probe-Based Minimal Restoration

Not all displaced objects require physical restoration. CoRe identifies the minimal subset of objects whose restoration is necessary. An object is considered "displaced" if its pose deviates significantly from `c_star`. A "forced set `F`" of objects (e.g., those referenced by `ℓ'`, near task-relevant entities, or in a goal region) is always scheduled for restoration. For other displaced objects, CoRe evaluates candidate subsets `S` (starting with `F` and progressively increasing in size) through "virtual probes." Each probe involves a short counterfactual rollout from a "hybrid seed" where objects in `S` are at their `c_star` poses, while others retain their current live poses. The trajectory of this probe is compared to a full-restore reference trajectory using `d_arm(S)`. The first subset `S` whose `d_arm(S)` falls below a trajectory-equivalence threshold `ϵ` is selected as the minimal restoration set `S_star`.

### 8. Physical Rearrangement and Handoff

This is the only stage involving physical robot motion. Each object in `S_star` is returned to its rejoin pose through motion-planned pick-and-place operations, executed in top-first height order. Each placement is verified to within a tolerance `ϵ_verify`, with one retry allowed. Unrecoverable cases trigger a structured intervention request. Subsequently, the robot arm is moved to the rejoin configuration `q_star`. Finally, control is returned to the frozen VLA `π`. The execution trace is truncated at the recovery anchor, ensuring that any subsequent recoveries build upon the current one rather than regressing further.

### 9. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of CoRe was conducted across multiple simulators, VLA backbones, and real-world settings, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving robotic task success rates under various online perturbations.

### 10. Simulation Experiments

Two distinct benchmarks were utilized: LangSwitch for online language goal changes with an xArm6 robot in ManiSkill, and LIBERO-Long with Perturbation for physical disturbances with a Franka Panda robot in robosuite.

- **LangSwitch Tasks (Language Switches):** CoRe consistently improved the success rates across three VLA backbones (π₀, π₀.₅, GR00T N1.7). Bare backbones achieved average success rates ranging from 18.3% to 35.5%, while with CoRe, these rates increased to 82.6%–92.7%. All 27 individual switch settings across shallow, mid, and deep perturbation timings showed improvements. The "Conflict" switch family, where the new goal directly contradicted the state produced by the original task, remained the most challenging, but CoRe still provided substantial gains. Over 1,500 unswitched episodes, CoRe recorded no false positive OOD triggers.

- **LIBERO-Long with Perturbation Tasks (Physical Perturbations):** CoRe demonstrated notable improvements in post-perturbation success rates. Bare baselines achieved average success rates between 12.8% and 43.5%. With CoRe, these rates increased to 69.8%–88.2%. The "Arm-Only" variant, which incorporated a grasp-feasibility weighting for restorations, maintained rates between 65.0% and 81.9%. Analysis of perturbation timing revealed that bare policies exhibited high timing sensitivity under language switches, but CoRe mitigated this, significantly reducing the success rate spread across different timings. For physical perturbations, bare policies were already largely timing-invariant, and CoRe maintained this characteristic while substantially boosting overall success.

### 11. Real-World Experiments

CoRe was deployed on a physical xArm6 robot, fine-tuned with a π₀.₅ backbone, operating on pick, place, and push tasks with household objects. Five conditions, including three LangSwitch families and two physical perturbations (Arm Collision and Scene Rearrangement), were evaluated.

- The bare π₀.₅ policy, despite high nominal competence (19–20/20 successful episodes), completed a maximum of 9/20 episodes under any perturbation, collapsing to 1/20 for challenging language switches.
- CoRe elevated the success rate for every condition to 17–19/20 successful episodes (85–95%). This included a significant improvement from 1/20 to 18/20 successes for both "Re-skilling" and "Conflict" conditions. These gains were consistent across shallow, mid, and deep injection depths. All recoveries in the real-world setting were executed entirely on hardware. The median recovery involved only arm realignment, taking 8.8 seconds of physical motion.

### 12. Ablation Studies

- **Fidelity of Synthesized Counterfactual Rollouts:** The endpoint TCP divergence between synthesized and ground-truth rollouts from identical anchors was found to be low, with median values ranging from 0.36 cm to 3.28 cm. This remained below the pre-contact margins used for recovery decisions (2 cm for LangSwitch, 8 cm for LIBERO). This indicates that the geometrically synthesized observations are sufficiently accurate for policy execution in the imagined space, eliminating the need for learned world models.
- **Recovery Mechanism Ablation:** Compared to baselines like "Episode Reset" and "Anchor Rollback," CoRe (with counterfactual rejoining and minimal restoration) demonstrated slightly higher success rates (90.7% vs. ~88-89%) and reduced the number of post-handoff execution steps. The minimal restoration aspect of CoRe reduced the number of physically restored objects per episode from 1.16 (full restoration) to 0.67, with comparable success. In real-world "Scene Rearrangement" tasks, CoRe left 58% of above-tolerance displaced objects untouched, restoring an average of 1.15 out of 2.77 objects.
- **Cost and Safety of Virtual Probing:** Virtual planning (median 9.7 seconds per cycle, or 1.2 seconds per imagined action chunk) was found to be 2-5 times cheaper than executing a single physical manipulation (median 20.5 seconds of motion, 46.6 seconds wall clock). This virtual planning capability also improved safety, reducing physical collisions or emergency stops from 21/100 baseline episodes to 5/100 CoRe episodes in real-world tests, as exploratory contact was confined to imagination.

### 13. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Counterfactual Realignment (CoRe) holds several significant implications for the field of robotics and the deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

Firstly, CoRe directly addresses a critical challenge in VLA models: their inherent fragility to unexpected online disruptions. By providing a robust, autonomous recovery mechanism, the work significantly enhances the reliability and resilience of these models in dynamic and unpredictable environments. This increased robustness is essential for transitioning VLA models from controlled laboratory settings to real-world applications.

Secondly, a key contribution of CoRe is its training-free nature. Unlike many existing recovery methods that demand costly failure data collection, extensive policy retraining, or the integration of complex auxiliary learning systems, CoRe operates purely at inference time with a frozen VLA backbone. This characteristic substantially reduces the computational and data acquisition overhead associated with deploying and maintaining robust robotic systems, making advanced robotic capabilities more accessible and practical.

Thirdly, CoRe's ability to preserve completed task progress during recovery is a notable advantage. By identifying a viable recovery anchor and planning a continuation from it, the framework avoids discarding previously achieved subgoals, thereby ensuring more efficient task execution and reducing overall completion times compared to methods that require full resets or rollbacks.

Furthermore, the framework offers a unified approach to handling diverse failure modes, encompassing both mid-episode instruction changes (language switches) and physical perturbations to the environment or robot state. This versatility underscores CoRe's potential for broad applicability across a variety of robotic manipulation tasks and deployment scenarios.

The methodology's reliance on synthesized observations for counterfactual rollouts is also significant. It demonstrates that VLA policies can be robust to geometrically synthesized sensory inputs, enabling closed-loop virtual planning without the need for sophisticated learned world models or privileged access to simulator ground truth. This insight could pave the way for more efficient and generalizable virtual testing and planning paradigms in robotics.

Finally, by replacing physical trial-and-error with virtual planning, CoRe not only enhances the efficiency of recovery but also contributes to improved safety in robotic operations. Confining exploratory contacts to a virtual environment significantly reduces the risk of actual collisions, robot damage, or operator interventions, which is a crucial factor for the safe deployment of autonomous systems. Overall, CoRe represents a step towards developing more reliable, autonomous, and general-purpose robots capable of operating effectively in complex and changing environments.
