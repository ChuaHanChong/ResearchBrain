# Bridging Zero-shot Object Navigation and Foundation Models through Pixel-Guided Navigation Skill

> [!summary] Summary
> An RGB-centric system for zero-shot object navigation introduces a pixel-guided navigation skill (PixNav) combined with a Large Language Model (LLM) planner, allowing robots to locate novel objects without depth sensing. The approach effectively bridges internet-trained foundation models with embodied navigation tasks, achieving competitive performance while requiring only visual input.

## Problem

- Zero-shot object navigation for home-assistance robots is challenging, requiring identification and location of novel objects without prior specific training.
- Existing open-vocabulary navigation methods either struggle with multimodal alignment (RGB-based) or rely on expensive, map-based planning (requiring depth sensors and precise localization) that is disconnected from RGB-centric foundation models.
- Traditional learning-based robotic policies necessitate extensive, semantically annotated datasets, which are costly to generate and limit data diversity for each object instance.

## Method

- A hierarchical system leverages a low-level Pixel-guided Navigation skill (PixNav), a transformer-based policy trained to navigate towards a designated pixel using only historical RGB images.
- A high-level ==Large Language Model (LLM)== planner, integrated with a Vision-Language Model (VLM), guides long-horizon tasks by processing panoramic RGB observations, summarizing room layouts, clustering inferred room connections, and proposing exploration steps based on commonsense knowledge.
- Foundation models like GroundingDINO and SAM are used for object grounding and segmentation, providing the central pixel of a target object's mask as the goal for the low-level PixNav policy.

## Results

- The system achieved a **37.9%** Success Rate (SR) and **20.5%** Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) in zero-shot object navigation on the HM3D dataset, performing competitively with map-based methods while relying solely on RGB input.
- Ablation studies showed PixNav maintains high success rates (e.g., **0.71**-**0.80** SR for 1-3m tasks) even with significant changes to camera height and HFOV, indicating its strong generalization capability.
- The LLM planner demonstrated a **68.4%** accuracy in identifying the robot's current room and a **47.1%** accuracy in describing surrounding room clusters and their interconnections.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pixels serve as an effective intermediary to bridge the modality gap between RGB-trained foundation models and embodied navigation tasks, enabling a direct transfer of visual and linguistic knowledge.
> - The PixNav policy exhibits robust zero-shot generalization to varying camera settings (heights, Horizontal Fields of View) without retraining, suggesting it learns a universal representation for navigation.
> - Structured, step-by-step prompting can elicit foundational spatial awareness and organizational capabilities from LLMs, allowing them to perform self-localization and infer inter-room connections within indoor environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenging task of zero-shot object navigation for home-assistance robots. This domain requires robots to identify and locate novel objects without prior specific training for those objects. The broader landscape of robotics and artificial intelligence has seen significant advancements in foundation models, particularly in visual grounding and commonsense inference, driven by large-scale pre-training on internet data. However, a notable gap exists in translating these capabilities to embodied navigation tasks, specifically concerning robot locomotion.

Previous research in open-vocabulary object navigation has often fallen into two categories: approaches limited to a closed set of known objects or open-vocabulary methods that either struggle with multimodal alignment due to insufficient large-scale object navigation datasets (e.g., ZSON, SSNet) or rely on map-based planning (e.g., ESC, L3MVN, StructNav). Map-based approaches typically require accurate depth sensing and robust self-localization capabilities, which increase hardware costs and can be susceptible to environmental disturbances. This dependence creates a disjunction with foundation models, which are primarily trained on RGB images.

Simultaneously, foundation models in vision (e.g., GroundingDINO, Owl-ViT for detection; SAM for segmentation; RAM for tagging) and language (Large Language Models, LLMs, for understanding, generation, and planning) have demonstrated advanced perceptual and reasoning abilities. Vision-Language Models (VLMs) further bridge these modalities for general-purpose tasks. In the context of visual navigation, LLMs have been leveraged for high-level planning by transcribing environmental information into text, guiding exploration, and selecting targets.

This research aims to bridge the aforementioned gap by proposing a pure RGB-based navigation system that directly integrates knowledge from foundation models for both low-level locomotion and high-level planning. By using pixels as navigation goals, the approach seeks to connect the RGB-centric nature of foundation models with the requirements of embodied navigation, thereby offering a more direct and cost-effective solution compared to map-based paradigms. The work differentiates itself from prior LLM-based navigation methods by focusing on a specific step-by-step prompt template designed to elicit self-localization and structural memory organization capabilities from LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an intelligent navigation system for zero-shot object navigation that effectively utilizes the capabilities of modern foundation models while operating purely on RGB visual input. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

1. **Bridge the RGB-Map Modality Gap:** Foundation models are predominantly trained on vast datasets of internet RGB images. A significant challenge in embodied AI is to transfer this rich visual and linguistic knowledge to robots, whose locomotion systems often rely on map-based planning, which operates in a different geometric space (e.g., 3D occupancy maps). The authors hypothesize that pixels serve as an ideal intermediary, directly aligning with the RGB input of foundation models and enabling a more seamless transfer of knowledge.
2. **Overcome Limitations of Map-Based Planning:** Current map-based navigation approaches for zero-shot object navigation necessitate accurate depth sensing and precise localization. These requirements often lead to increased hardware complexity and cost for home-assistance robots. Furthermore, depth sensors can be prone to interference. The motivation is to reduce or eliminate this dependency by developing an RGB-based navigation policy, thereby making robotic systems more affordable and robust in diverse indoor environments.
3. **Enhance Versatility and Generalization:** Existing object-goal navigation policies often require learning specific multi-modal alignments for a predefined set of objects. The proposed "Pixel-guided Navigation skill" (PixNav) aims to be versatile by allowing any object, irrespective of its novelty, to be specified as a navigation target simply by indicating a pixel within its image representation. This approach abstracts away the need for explicit object recognition in the low-level policy, promoting generalization to arbitrary objects.
4. **Facilitate Large-Scale Data Collection:** Learning-based robotic policies often require extensive datasets for effective generalization. Traditional object-goal navigation demands unique object instances for each training trajectory, limiting data diversity. By using pixels as goals, PixNav can generate a multitude of diverse navigation trajectories from a single object instance (as each object comprises numerous pixels), significantly expanding the potential dataset size without requiring explicit semantic annotations for each trajectory. This reduces the cost and complexity of data generation.
5. **Enable Long-Horizon Navigation with Commonsense Reasoning:** For objects that are not immediately visible, a robot needs to explore its environment intelligently. Indoor environments often exhibit commonsense layouts (e.g., a bed is typically in a bedroom). The motivation is to leverage the implicit commonsense knowledge embedded within large language models (LLMs) to design a high-level planner that can strategize exploration sequences across rooms, thereby guiding the robot towards out-of-sight targets efficiently.
6. **Explore LLM Capabilities for Embodied Tasks:** The research aims to investigate and quantify the potential of LLMs in spatial reasoning tasks, specifically in self-localization and structural memory organization within indoor environments, by designing structured, step-by-step prompt templates.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system employs a hierarchical, RGB-centric strategy for zero-shot object navigation, combining a low-level pixel-guided navigation skill (PixNav) with a high-level Large Language Model (LLM)-based planner.

**A. Path-Planning with Pixel Navigation (PixNav)**

PixNav is a low-level, universal RGB-based navigation policy designed to execute primitive actions to navigate towards a designated pixel in the initial visual frame.

- **Action Space:** The policy operates in a discrete action space comprising six primitive actions: {Stop, MoveAhead, TurnLeft, TurnRight, LookUp, LookDown}.
- **Architecture:** A transformer-based network processes historical RGB images and the goal pixel.
 - **Goal Encoding:** The goal pixel (x_i, y_i) is converted into a binary mask image by setting a small bounding box (δ=2 pixels) around it to 1, concatenated channel-wise with the initial RGB image. A 4-channel ResNet18 extracts a 768-dimensional goal token.
 - **Observation Encoding:** A separate 3-channel ResNet18 processes each historical RGB observation image, yielding a sequence of 512-dimensional observation tokens.
 - **Goal Fusion Layer:** A 256-dimensional feature derived from the goal token is concatenated with each observation token.
 - **Transformer Decoder:** A 4-layer transformer decoder integrates global information from the fused tokens.
 - **Output Layers:** Three 1-layer MLPs are built upon the transformer's output tokens: a policy layer predicts the optimal action (π_θ), a tracking layer predicts the corresponding pixel in the current frame (f_θ), and a temporal distance layer predicts remaining time steps to the goal (d_θ).
- **Training Data Generation:** The system leverages the Habitat-Lab simulator with the HM3D dataset. To create large-scale demonstrations, the robot's initial position and a target pixel in the first RGB observation are randomly sampled. An underlying, off-the-shelf path planner (utilizing depth information) generates the optimal actions to reach the 3D coordinate corresponding to the sampled pixel. Over 100,000 trajectories, each up to 64 time steps, were generated, with RGB observations at 160x120 resolution. This pixel-centric data collection method bypasses the need for explicit semantic annotations for each trajectory.
- **Training Objective:** The entire network is trained via supervised learning using three loss functions:
 - **Imitation Learning Loss (L_il):** Cross-entropy loss for matching ground-truth optimal actions.
 - **Temporal Distance Loss (L_distance):** Mean Squared Error (MSE) for predicting the remaining time steps to the goal (scaled by 1/10).
 - **Tracking Loss (L_track):** MSE for predicting the corresponding pixel's location in subsequent frames.
 The total loss is a sum of these three components.

**B. Perception with Foundation Models**

For the high-level planning, the system integrates several foundation models to translate visual observations into semantic information.

- **Panoramic Observation:** The robot captures six distinct images by rotating to gain a comprehensive panoramic view of its surroundings.
- **Vision-Language Model (VLM) Translation:** The LLama-Adapter serves as a VLM, translating these visual observations into structured textual descriptions. The VLM is prompted with "Describe the room type in the image" and "Describe the objects with details in the image," converting each image into a paragraph detailing room type and objects.
- **Object Grounding and Segmentation:** When the LLM-based planner identifies a target object, GroundingDINO and SAM are employed to obtain a fine-grained segmentation mask for that object. The center pixel of this mask is then assigned as the goal for the low-level PixNav policy.

**C. Planning with Large-Language Model**

An LLM-based planner, utilizing GPT-4, guides the robot for long-horizon object navigation tasks by leveraging commonsense knowledge about indoor environments. The planning procedure involves three stages:

1. **Summarize:** The textual descriptions generated by the VLM for the six panoramic images are summarized by the LLM into a structured JSON format. Each entry includes an 'ID', 'Angle' (of observation), 'Type' (room type), and 'Objects' present.
2. **Clustering:** A step-by-step prompt guides the LLM to estimate the robot's current room and position, and subsequently infer inter-room connections. Rooms of the same type with inferred connections or adjacent observation angles are clustered. This process generates a graph-like representation of the local house layout.
3. **Planning:** A dedicated planning prompt is designed to instruct the LLM to propose the most optimal next step. This prompt balances exploitation (moving towards a likely room for the target object) and exploration (searching new areas). The LLM determines a subgoal (e.g., target room, or a general direction) based on the target object and the clustered environment graph. The specific goal for PixNav is typically set as the center pixel of the "floor" in the selected target room. The LLM re-plans periodically or upon completion of the current low-level navigation task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed method was evaluated through experiments in photorealistic indoor simulators (Habitat-Lab with HM3D dataset) and real-world environments.

**A. Benchmarks and Metrics**
- **Dataset:** HM3D dataset, comprising 80 scenes for training and 20 for validation.
- **Object Navigation Metrics:** Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) were used to assess overall navigation performance and path efficiency.
- **PixNav Metrics:** For evaluating the low-level PixNav policy, SR, SPL, and Distance to Goal (DTG) were measured for short-range tasks (1-3m and 3-5m geodesic distance from initial robot location to pixel goal). Each evaluation involved 500 episodes.

**B. Performance on Zero-Shot Object Navigation**
The proposed system's performance on zero-shot object navigation tasks was compared against several baselines (Table I):
- **Open-Set ObjectNav:** Our approach achieved a Success Rate (SR) of 37.9% and an SPL of 20.5%. This is competitive with map-based zero-shot methods like ESC (SR 39.2%, SPL 22.3%) and significantly outperforms RGB-based ZSON (SR 25.5%, SPL 12.6%), all while operating without depth sensing or explicit localization inputs.
- **Trajectory Visualization:** Visualizations demonstrated that PixNav can consistently track the initially designated pixel target despite changes in the robot's viewpoint and adaptively adjust its gaze (e.g., look down to avoid collisions). The LLM-based planner exhibited heuristic exploration behaviors, such as traversing between rooms and returning to corridors in a semantically guided manner. The strategy of using the center pixel of a room's floor as a goal for PixNav was identified as effective.

**C. Ablation Study on PixNav**
To assess the robustness and generalizability of the PixNav policy, ablation studies were conducted:
- **Camera Settings (Table II):** PixNav was evaluated under varied camera heights and Horizontal Fields of View (HFOV). Despite significant changes (e.g., 50% relative change in height, 25% relative change in HFOV), the policy maintained a high success rate in 1-3m navigation tasks (e.g., 0.71-0.80 SR, compared to 0.88 SR for default settings), with minor performance degradation in most cases. This suggests that PixNav possesses zero-shot generalization capabilities to new camera configurations, indicating it learns a universal representation for navigation.
- **Component Analysis (Table III):** Ablations on key architectural components of PixNav revealed:
 - Removing the tracking prediction, temporal distance prediction, or goal fusion network had a minor impact on 1-3m navigation scenarios.
 - However, for longer-range 3-5m scenarios, both tracking prediction (SR dropped from 0.42 to 0.33) and goal fusion (SR dropped from 0.42 to 0.34) significantly influenced performance. This indicates their importance in maintaining focus on the target pixel for long-term navigation.

**D. Analysis of LLM Planner**
To evaluate the LLM's spatial awareness, a quantitative analysis was performed comparing LLM responses to human evaluations for 100 episodes (Table IV):
- **Room Localization:** The LLM achieved an accuracy of 68.4% in identifying the robot's current room.
- **Room Clustering:** The LLM achieved an accuracy of 47.1% in describing the number and types of surrounding rooms and their interconnections.
These results indicate that the LLM, when guided by a structured prompt, exhibits a foundational level of spatial awareness and organizational capability regarding indoor environments.

**E. Sim2Real Transfer**
Real-world experiments were performed using an iRobot-Create3 mobile base equipped with an OAK-D camera, solely utilizing RGB observations. The robot's discrete action space was configured for basic movements. The real-world evaluations were reported to validate the functionality of both the PixNav policy and the LLM-planner, with further details provided via a project website.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an RGB-centric approach to zero-shot object navigation, contributing to the field of embodied AI by addressing key limitations of existing methods.

1. **Bridging Foundation Models and Embodied AI:** The introduction of the Pixel-guided Navigation skill (PixNav) offers a method to integrate powerful, RGB-trained foundation models directly into embodied navigation tasks. By specifying navigation goals as pixels, the framework circumvents the modality gap between internet-trained vision models and map-based locomotion systems, enabling a more direct application of advanced visual and language understanding capabilities to robotics.
2. **Reduced Hardware Requirements and Cost:** The pure RGB-based nature of the PixNav policy eliminates the dependence on expensive and potentially fragile depth sensors and precise localization systems. This has the potential to reduce the manufacturing cost and improve the robustness of home-assistance robots, making them more accessible for general applications.
3. **Enhanced Versatility and Generalization:** PixNav demonstrates versatility in navigating towards various objects, including novel ones, by abstracting the goal to a pixel. Its observed generalization capabilities across different camera settings (heights, HFOVs) suggest its potential as a robust, universal RGB-based path-planning module, reducing the need for extensive re-training for different robot platforms.
4. **Advancements in LLM for Robotics:** The systematic design of a step-by-step prompt template for the LLM planner reveals the potential of large language models in self-localization and structural memory organization within complex indoor environments. This work demonstrates how LLMs can provide high-level, commonsense-informed planning for long-horizon navigation tasks, even with limited explicit spatial mapping. This approach could be extended to other robotics planning and reasoning tasks.
5. **Efficient Data Collection:** The pixel-guided paradigm simplifies the generation of large-scale navigation datasets, as it allows for the creation of numerous diverse trajectories from single object instances without requiring explicit semantic annotations for each path. This could accelerate research and development in robot learning.

Despite current limitations in long-term path-planning performance, the approach lays a foundation for future research. Potential impacts include the development of more intelligent, adaptable, and cost-effective home-assistance robots that can navigate and interact with their environments based on high-level human instructions, without extensive prior knowledge of specific objects or environments. Future work aims to improve long-term performance through larger and more diverse datasets and the development of navigation-grounded vision-language models for planning, further extending the utility of foundation models in embodied AI.
