# Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima

> [!summary] Summary
> Zeroth-order optimization, using the standard two-point estimator, implicitly regularizes towards flat minima by effectively minimizing the original loss function plus a term proportional to the trace of its Hessian. This implicit bias provides a theoretical explanation for the observed generalization performance and practical utility of these methods, especially in high-dimensional machine learning tasks.

## Problem

- Many modern machine learning tasks, such as black-box attacks, reinforcement learning, and fine-tuning large language models, face challenges with inaccessible or computationally expensive gradient information.
- Despite empirical success and good generalization of zeroth-order (ZO) methods in high-dimensional settings, existing theoretical analyses primarily guarantee convergence to an arbitrary stationary point without explaining the 'quality' of solutions found.
- A theoretical gap existed in understanding why ZO methods generalize well and how they effectively navigate complex loss landscapes to find beneficial solutions.

## Method

- The authors rigorously analyze the standard two-point ==zeroth-order gradient== estimator, demonstrating it is an unbiased estimator for a smoothed function, f_λ(x).
- Through a Taylor expansion, they establish that minimizing f_λ(x) is approximately equivalent to minimizing the original loss f(x) plus a regularization term proportional to the trace of the Hessian, Tr(∇²f(x)).
- A novel theoretical framework, leveraging higher-order smoothness assumptions and advanced moment bounds via Isserlis' theorem, is developed to formally prove ZO's convergence to flat minima, defined as minimizers with the smallest trace of Hessian.

## Results

- Under convexity and three-times continuous differentiability, ZO is theoretically proven to converge to (O(ϵ/d2)O(ε/d^2)O(ϵ/d2), ϵ)-approximate flat minima with an iteration complexity of O(d4/ϵ2)O(d^4/ε^2)O(d4/ϵ2).
- Empirical evaluations on a synthetic test function, overparameterized convex classification (SVMs, logistic regression), and non-convex large language model fine-tuning (RoBERTa-Large) consistently show that ZO effectively reduces the trace of the Hessian, achieving flatter solutions than standard Gradient Descent.
- The observed reduction of the trace of Hessian to values much smaller than the problem dimension provides a crucial theoretical justification for the surprising effectiveness of ZO in high-dimensional tasks like LLM fine-tuning, where classical dimension dependence would typically predict poor performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inherent smoothing mechanism of the two-point zeroth-order gradient estimator acts as an implicit regularizer, explicitly penalizing the trace of the Hessian and guiding the optimization towards flatter minima.
> - Minimizing the implicitly smoothed objective (original loss plus Hessian trace) directly explains the 'implicit bias' of zeroth-order optimization towards solutions with better generalization properties.
> - The theoretical framework, which delves into higher-order derivatives and sophisticated moment calculations, can be extended to analyze the implicit regularization effects in other optimization methods, including first-order ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper makes a significant contribution at the intersection of zeroth-order optimization, implicit regularization, and the study of generalization in machine learning.

**Zeroth-Order Optimization (ZO) Context:** Zeroth-order methods are crucial for scenarios where gradient information is unavailable or computationally prohibitive. This includes black-box adversarial attacks, reinforcement learning, and, increasingly, fine-tuning large language models (LLMs). Traditional ZO theory primarily focuses on convergence to *any* stationary point. However, the practical success of ZO in high-dimensional, complex tasks like LLM fine-tuning (e.g., Malladi et al. showing ZO fine-tuning a 30-billion-parameter model on a single GPU) has outpaced theoretical explanations. This empirical effectiveness often requires a deeper understanding of *which* specific solutions ZO converges to, rather than just *that* it converges. Previous work has started to relax the dimension dependence of ZO convergence rates by relating it to the trace of the Hessian, but this connection needed more formal backing regarding *why* the trace would be small.

**Implicit Regularization and Flat Minima:** The concept of "flat minima" – regions in the loss landscape where the loss function changes slowly – has been widely studied in deep learning. Flat minima are empirically correlated with better generalization performance, especially for overparameterized models. Research on this topic has largely focused on first-order methods like Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM), Entropy-SGD, and Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA), exploring how they implicitly or explicitly navigate towards flatter regions. The "trace of Hessian" is a widely adopted metric to quantify sharpness. This paper extends this line of inquiry to zeroth-order optimization, which has largely been missing from the flat minima literature.

**Bridging Theory and Practice:** The paper aims to bridge a critical gap between the theoretical understanding of ZO and its empirical success. By demonstrating that ZO implicitly regularizes towards flat minima, it provides a theoretical underpinning for its observed generalization benefits, especially in high-dimensional settings like LLMs where a low trace of Hessian can effectively reduce the problematic dimension dependence in convergence rates. This work is novel in being the first to formally prove this implicit regularization effect for standard ZO.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the increasing prevalence and practical utility of zeroth-order optimization methods in modern machine learning, coupled with a gap in their theoretical understanding.

**Motivation:**
1. **Gradient Inaccessibility/Cost:** Many emerging machine learning problems, such as black-box attacks, reinforcement learning, and fine-tuning billions-of-parameter large language models (LLMs), face significant challenges with gradient computation. Backpropagation, while powerful, is memory-intensive and can be infeasible for massive models on limited hardware.
2. **Empirical Success of ZO:** Zeroth-order methods offer a compelling alternative, allowing gradient estimation purely from function evaluations. Empirical results, particularly in LLM fine-tuning, show that ZO can achieve comparable performance to gradient-based methods with drastically reduced memory requirements (e.g., 1 A100 GPU vs. 8 A100s for a 30B parameter model).
3. **Limitations of Existing Theory:** Current theoretical analyses of zeroth-order optimization primarily establish convergence to *an arbitrary stationary point*. They do not provide a "fine-grained characterization" of *which particular solutions* are reached when multiple optima exist. This leaves the empirical observation of good generalization for ZO unexplained from a theoretical perspective.

### 3. Key Objectives

1. **Formalize Implicit Regularization:** The primary objective is to formally investigate and prove the intuitive observation that standard zeroth-order optimization implicitly performs a type of regularization. The authors hypothesize that the smoothing mechanism inherent in the two-point gradient estimator introduces a term related to the trace of the Hessian.
2. **Prove Convergence to Flat Minima:** Based on this formalized intuition, the core objective is to theoretically demonstrate that zeroth-order optimization, using the standard two-point estimator, converges to "flat minima" – defined as minimizers that achieve the smallest trace of Hessian among all optimal solutions. This involves defining suitable notions of flat and approximate flat minima.
3. **Provide Convergence Rates:** To quantify this behavior, the authors aim to derive specific convergence rates (iteration complexity) for zeroth-order optimization to reach approximate flat minima under specified function properties (convexity and smoothness).
4. **Empirical Validation:** To support the theoretical claims, the paper aims to conduct empirical evaluations across different problem settings: a controlled test function, convex machine learning tasks (SVMs, logistic regression), and large-scale, non-convex language model fine-tuning.

In essence, the research seeks to move beyond simply proving convergence for ZO to understanding its *implicit bias* and how this bias contributes to its practical effectiveness and generalization capabilities.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines rigorous theoretical analysis with empirical validation across diverse problem domains.

#### 4.1. Zeroth-Order Optimization Algorithm

The paper focuses on the **standard two-point gradient estimator** (Algorithm 1), a widely used and fundamental zeroth-order method. This algorithm iteratively updates parameters `x` by sampling a standard Gaussian random vector `u` and estimating the gradient using finite differences of the loss function `f(x + λu)` and `f(x - λu)`. The smoothing parameter `λ` is crucial to this estimator. The core idea relies on the fact that this estimator is an unbiased gradient estimator for a *smoothed function* $f_\lambda(x):= E_u[f(x + \lambda u)]$.

#### 4.2. Theoretical Approach

- **Analysis of the Smoothed Function ($f_\lambda(x)$):** The starting point is a Taylor expansion of the smoothed function $f_\lambda(x)$. The paper leverages the approximation $f_\lambda(x) = f(x) + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \text{Tr}(\nabla^2 f(x)) + o(\lambda^2)$. This fundamental relation is key, as it shows that minimizing $f_\lambda(x)$ is approximately equivalent to minimizing the original loss $f(x)$ plus an additive regularization term related to the trace of the Hessian. This term is directly linked to the concept of "sharpness" or "flatness" of a minimum.
- **Definition of Flat Minima:** To formalize convergence, the authors introduce a precise, global definition of flat minima: `x*` is a flat minimum if it is a minimizer of `f(x)` that also achieves the *smallest trace of Hessian* among the entire set of minimizers `X*`. They also define `($\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2$)-approximate flat minima` to allow for practical convergence guarantees.
- **Assumptions for Analysis:**
 - **Smoothness (Assumption 3.3):** The function `f(x)` is assumed to be three times continuously differentiable with Lipschitz-continuous first, second, and third derivatives (L1, L2, L3 smoothness). These higher-order smoothness assumptions are necessary because the analysis involves second- and third-order derivatives (Trace of Hessian and its gradient). The paper acknowledges that these assumptions are for analysis, not for the algorithm itself which only uses zeroth-order information.
 - **Convexity (Assumption 3.4):** `f(x)` is assumed to be convex. This is crucial for guaranteeing global convergence and ensuring that `Tr($\nabla^2 f(x)$) >= 0`. The paper notes that extensions to locally convex settings or more general nonconvex scenarios are left for future work.
- **Refined Convergence Analysis:** Instead of treating the $\frac{\lambda^2}{2} \text{Tr}(\nabla^2 f(x))$ term as merely a bias to be controlled (as in standard ZO analysis), the authors treat $F(x):= f(x) + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \text{Tr}(\nabla^2 f(x))$ as the *implicit objective* being optimized.
 - This requires carefully bounding the difference between $f_\lambda(x)$ and $F(x)$ (Lemma 3.5).
 - It also necessitates a non-trivial bound on the second moment of the gradient estimator, $E\|g_\lambda(x, u)\|^2$, in terms of $\|\nabla F(x)\|^2$. This involves complex calculations using Isserlis' theorem for higher-order moments (up to 8th order), a significant departure from standard ZO analysis which typically only needs 4th-order moments.
 - The smoothness of $F(x)$ itself needs to be established (Lemma 3.6), given its dependence on second derivatives of $f(x)$.
- **Derivation of Iteration Complexity (Theorem 1 and Corollary 2):** By combining these refined bounds and standard optimization convergence arguments, the paper derives the iteration complexity required for ZO to reach $(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2)$-approximate flat minima.

#### 4.3. Empirical Approach

- **Controlled Test Function (Example 2.1):** A custom 2d-dimensional function `h(x) = (y^T z - 1)^2/2` is used, where the optimal solutions are known, and the trace of Hessian (`||y||^2 + ||z||^2`) can be easily analyzed. This allows for clear visualization of the optimization trajectory with respect to both loss and trace of Hessian.
- **Convex Machine Learning Tasks:** Binary classification tasks using overparameterized Support Vector Machines (SVMs) with squared hinge loss and Logistic Regression are employed. The `a5a` and `w5a` datasets from LIBSVM are used, with features mapped to a higher-dimensional space via a random matrix (`D=10,000`). This overparameterized setting ensures a diverse solution set where flatness can be meaningfully studied, and the trace of Hessian can be computed efficiently.
- **Non-convex Language Model Fine-tuning:** RoBERTa-Large (355M parameters) is fine-tuned on few-shot text classification datasets (SST-2, SST-5, TREC). This tests the theory in a high-dimensional, highly non-convex, and practically relevant setting. Due to the massive model size, the exact trace of Hessian is intractable; thus, it is *approximated* using the "expected sharpness" ($2/\delta^2 |E_u[f(x + \delta u)] - f(x)|$). Full-batch training is used to mitigate mini-batch noise.
- **Comparison:** In all empirical settings, the performance of Zeroth-Order Optimization is compared against standard Gradient Descent (GD), tracking training loss, test accuracy, and the (exact or approximated) trace of Hessian.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling theoretical and empirical evidence supporting its central hypothesis that zeroth-order optimization implicitly favors flat minima.

#### 5.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Implicit Hessian Regularization:** The core theoretical finding is that the standard two-point zeroth-order gradient estimator for a function $f(x)$ implicitly minimizes a smoothed function $f_\lambda(x)$ that effectively acts as $f(x) + \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \text{Tr}(\nabla^2 f(x)) + o(\lambda^2)$. This demonstrates that the trace of Hessian, a key metric for sharpness, is an inherent additive regularizer in ZO.
- **Convergence to Flat Minima:** Under the assumptions of convexity and three-times continuous differentiability with Lipschitz continuous first, second, and third derivatives (Assumptions 3.3 and 3.4), the paper *proves* that zeroth-order optimization with the two-point estimator converges to **($O(\epsilon/d^2)$, $\epsilon$)-approximate flat minima** (as defined in Definition 3.2). This means the algorithm finds a solution $\hat{x}$ such that its function value is close to the global minimum of $f(x)$ (up to $O(\epsilon/d^2)$) and its trace of Hessian is close to the minimum possible trace of Hessian among all minimizers (up to $\epsilon$).
- **Iteration Complexity:** The proven iteration complexity to achieve these approximate flat minima is $T = O(d^4/\epsilon^2)$, with a smoothing parameter $\lambda = O(\epsilon^{1/2}/d^{3/2})$. This is a novel result, providing the first formal proof of ZO's convergence to flat minima.
- **Generalizability of Framework:** The authors note that their proof framework can be extended to analyze the convergence of *first-order methods* (like SAM or gradient descent with perturbed points) to flat minima, suggesting broader applicability of their analytical techniques.
- **Relaxation of Assumptions:** The paper discusses how certain smoothness assumptions can be relaxed, albeit at the cost of slower convergence rates (e.g., from $O(d^4/\epsilon^2)$ to $O(\max\{d^5/\epsilon^2, d^4/\epsilon^3\})$ when relaxing third-order smoothness).

#### 5.2. Empirical Findings

- **Test Function (Example 2.1):** Experiments on the synthetic function clearly show that ZO significantly reduces the trace of the Hessian, unlike standard Gradient Descent or ZO with an infinitesimally small $\lambda$. The smoothing parameter $\lambda$ is demonstrated to directly control the trade-off: larger $\lambda$ leads to a more pronounced regularization effect on the trace of Hessian, but also larger errors in the loss function. This result is further supported by Proposition 2.2, which theoretically shows the "balance" metric (related to sharpness) converging to zero for ZO.
- **Convex Binary Classification:** On overparameterized SVMs and Logistic Regression, ZO consistently exhibits a decreasing trend in the trace of the Hessian during training, leading to flatter solutions. While GD achieves comparable final loss and accuracy, it does not demonstrate the same consistent reduction in the trace of Hessian. This aligns with the theoretical prediction of ZO seeking flatter minima.
- **Language Model Fine-tuning (RoBERTa-Large):** In the challenging non-convex setting of LLM fine-tuning, both GD and ZO are observed to decrease the *approximated* trace of Hessian. The paper attributes GD's behavior in this context to implicit regularization associated with large learning rates (as per existing literature), while ZO's behavior is consistent with the paper's theoretical insights.
- **Support for Prior Work:** The empirical observation that the trace of Hessian decreases and achieves values much smaller than the actual dimension `d` provides crucial support for assumptions made in prior works (e.g.,). These works suggest that if `Tr($\nabla^2 f(x)$) << dL1`, ZO can achieve dimension-independent convergence rates, explaining its surprising effectiveness in high-dimensional tasks like LLM fine-tuning despite the `d` dependence in classical ZO theory.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This work carries significant implications for both the theoretical understanding and practical application of optimization algorithms in machine learning.

#### 6.1. Deeper Theoretical Understanding of ZO

The most significant impact is providing a foundational theoretical explanation for a long-observed but poorly understood phenomenon: the generalization ability of zeroth-order methods. By formally proving that standard ZO implicitly regularizes towards flat minima, the paper moves beyond mere convergence guarantees to a deeper characterization of the *quality* of solutions found. This enhances the credibility of ZO as a robust optimization paradigm for complex, black-box problems.

#### 6.2. Explaining Empirical Success in High Dimensions (LLMs)

The findings directly address the question of why ZO performs remarkably well in high-dimensional tasks like LLM fine-tuning, despite classical theory predicting severe dimension dependence. By showing that ZO reduces the trace of the Hessian (a key factor in dimension-independent rates), the paper provides a crucial piece of the puzzle, unifying theory with the practical efficacy demonstrated in recent works like MeZO/MeZO++. This can guide future development and adoption of ZO techniques in large-scale AI.

#### 6.3. Informing Algorithmic Design

The explicit connection between ZO and Hessian-trace regularization can inspire new algorithmic designs. Researchers can now consider how to modify ZO (or even first-order methods) to either enhance or control this implicit regularization more effectively. The paper itself notes that its framework could be extended to analyze first-order methods like SAM, suggesting a cross-pollination of ideas between ZO and sharpness-aware optimization. This could lead to hybrid methods that combine the memory efficiency of ZO with explicit regularization for flatness.

#### 6.4. Advancing Optimization Theory

The detailed analytical techniques, particularly the use of Isserlis' theorem for higher-order moments in bounding the gradient estimator's variance, represent a non-trivial advancement in the theoretical toolbox for analyzing zeroth-order methods. This sophisticated analysis can serve as a blueprint for future theoretical work in constrained optimization, implicit regularization, and second-order stationary point analysis for gradient-free methods.

#### 6.5. Future Research Directions

The paper clearly outlines several promising avenues for future work, including:
- Extending the analysis to non-convex settings and refining the definition of computationally feasible flat minima for such cases.
- Investigating how different zeroth-order estimators or algorithmic modifications (e.g., variance reduction, momentum) might further improve convergence complexity or enhance flatness-seeking properties.
- Exploring the combination of zeroth-order and first-order methods to leverage their respective strengths.

In conclusion, "Zeroth-Order Optimization Finds Flat Minima" is a landmark paper that provides a much-needed theoretical foundation for the implicit regularization properties of zeroth-order optimization. It not only offers a deeper understanding of why these methods generalize well but also paves the way for the development of more principled and efficient optimization algorithms for the increasingly complex challenges in machine learning.
