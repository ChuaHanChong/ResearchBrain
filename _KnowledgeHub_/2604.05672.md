# A1: A Fully Transparent Open-Source, Adaptive and Efficient Truncated Vision-Language-Action Model

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from SYSU and MBZUAI developed A1, a fully open-source Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework designed for efficient, real-time robotic control. A1 adaptively accelerates both its VLM backbone and flow-matching action head, achieving up to a 72.3% reduction in inference time while maintaining high manipulation success rates across diverse real-world and simulated tasks.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models suffer from high computational cost and latency, primarily due to multi-billion-parameter VLM backbones and numerous iterative denoising steps in their action heads.
- The computational demands of current VLA models restrict real-time deployment on commodity hardware, necessitating expensive hardware and significant energy budgets.
- While VLM latency reduction has been explored, the action head often remains an unaddressed bottleneck, increasing overall inference time and hindering practical adoption.

## Method

- **A1** integrates a ==Molmo-7B VLM backbone== with a ==Qwen3-based flow-matching== (A1-FM) or MLP (A1-MLP) action head, pre-trained on a diverse, large-scale robotic trajectory corpus.
- A ==budget-aware adaptive inference mechanism== jointly accelerates the VLM and action head using ==multi-exit training== and ==early-termination== based on action-consistency thresholding.
- ==Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching== warm-starts denoising for the FM head across VLM layers, significantly reducing iterative denoising steps from 10 to 2 and decreasing per-episode inference time.

## Results

- **A1** achieved an average success rate of **96.6%** on the LIBERO benchmark and **56.7%** on real-world tasks across four robot platforms, outperforming baselines like π0 and π0.5.
- Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching reduced per-episode inference time by **72.3%** (from **37.8s** to **10.5s**) while maintaining a **96.4%** success rate, by reducing denoising steps from **10** to **2** and using warm-start initialization.
- The model demonstrated robust zero-shot generalization on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, achieving a **75.3%** success rate even under significant distribution shifts, exceeding OpenVLA-OFT (**69.6%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Flow-matching trajectories often converge to correct action modes within few denoising steps (fewer than three), indicating redundancy in extensive iterative denoising.
> - Robot actions often evolve smoothly across consecutive control steps, suggesting that full VLA re-evaluation at every timestep may be inefficient.
> - Intermediate hidden states within the VLM backbone can encode sufficient spatial and visual features to adequately seed downstream action prediction, allowing for earlier exits.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a significant paradigm for robotic manipulation in open-world environments. These models typically integrate large-scale Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to process multimodal observations and generate latent representations, which are then translated into motor commands by an action head, often based on diffusion or flow-matching techniques. This architecture enables robust generalization across various objects, instructions, and robot morphologies, leveraging the semantic understanding provided by pretrained VLMs and the expressive capabilities of generative decoders.

However, the practical deployment of VLA models is frequently constrained by high computational costs. State-of-the-art VLAs often utilize multi-billion-parameter VLM backbones and require 10–20 iterative denoising steps for their diffusion or flow-matching action heads. While existing research has explored methods to reduce VLM latency through techniques such as quantization, sparsity, or early-exit mechanisms, the computational overhead of the iterative action head often remains unaddressed, becoming a new bottleneck for real-time control. This leads to requirements for expensive hardware and substantial energy consumption, limiting widespread adoption in practical robotic systems.

This work, A1, positions itself within this landscape by focusing on joint acceleration of the *entire* VLA inference pipeline, rather than optimizing only the VLM backbone. It aims to provide an open-source, transparent, and efficient framework that specifically addresses the computational burden of both the VLM backbone and the iterative action head. The approach differentiates itself from prior efforts like DeeR-VLA by specifically addressing the heavy computational cost incurred by diffusion/flow-matching action heads across multiple time steps and by introducing a warm-starting mechanism for denoising. By offering a fully transparent and reproducible system, A1 also contributes to the broader research community by facilitating auditing, reproduction, and extension of its results.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop A1, a fully open-source, adaptive, and efficient Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework designed for low-cost, high-throughput inference without compromising manipulation success rates.

The motivation for this work stems from the practical limitations encountered during the deployment of existing VLA models for open-world robot manipulation. Current state-of-the-art VLA systems, while powerful, face significant constraints due to their high computational cost, which manifests as high latency and substantial compute requirements. These costs are primarily attributable to two components:
1. **Large VLM Backbones:** Many VLA models rely on multi-billion-parameter Vision-Language Model backbones, which demand extensive computational resources for inference.
2. **Iterative Action Heads:** The generative action heads, frequently based on diffusion or flow-matching techniques, typically necessitate numerous (e.g., 10–20) iterative denoising steps to generate precise motor commands. Even if VLM backbone latency is reduced, the action head can become the dominant bottleneck.

These factors make achieving real-time robotic control expensive on commodity hardware, thereby limiting the practical applicability and widespread adoption of advanced VLA models.

The authors derive their approach from three empirical observations:
1. **Trajectory Convergence:** Flow-matching trajectories, despite their iterative nature, often converge to a correct mode within a limited number of denoising steps (e.g., fewer than three), with additional iterations primarily contributing to precision refinement with diminishing returns.
2. **Action Redundancy:** In sequential control steps, many actions change smoothly, implying that only coarse updates are frequently necessary, and full re-computation of actions might be redundant.
3. **Layer-wise Coupling:** Intermediate hidden states within the VLM backbone may already encode sufficient spatial and visual features to initiate action prediction (e.g., to seed the flow-matching vector field), suggesting that evaluating the VLM backbone to its full depth is often not necessary.

These observations collectively suggest a guiding principle for efficient VLA inference: computational resources should be expended only when a change in the predicted action is significant. This principle motivates the development of a budget-aware adaptive inference mechanism that jointly accelerates both the VLM backbone and the action head. A further objective is to promote transparency and reproducibility in VLA research by releasing the complete training stack, including code, data processing pipelines, intermediate checkpoints, and evaluation scripts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The A1 framework comprises a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone and an action head, designed to integrate strong visual-semantic understanding with efficient action generation.

**A. Architecture and Action Head Bridging** The VLM backbone's weights are initialized from Molmo, a pre-trained model intended to provide robust visual-semantic and affordance-aware representations. A1 offers two implementations for its action head:
- **A1-FM (Flow Matching):** This variant uses a Qwen3 model with approximately 400M parameters as a flow-matching action expert. Actions `A_t` are supervised using a conditional flow matching loss (Equation 1), where the network learns to match a denoising vector field. During inference, actions are generated by integrating this learned vector field from random noise `A_0_t` to `A_t` using forward Euler integration (Equation 2). The action head is conditioned via KV-conditioned self-attention, integrating the VLM's prefix context.
- **A1 -MLP (Multi-Layer Perceptron):** This variant employs an MLP head that regresses continuous actions `A_t` from the hidden states `H_t` of dedicated action tokens (Equations 3, 4). These actions are supervised using an L1 loss (Equation 5).

**B. Adaptive Inference Acceleration** To reduce end-to-end inference latency, A1 incorporates a budget-aware adaptive inference scheme that jointly accelerates the VLM backbone and the action head.
- **Multi-Exit Training:** During training, a simple strategy involves randomly sampling a VLM layer index `i`. For the MLP action head, the loss `L^(i)` is supervised on actions predicted from the hidden state at layer `i`, rather than solely from the final layer. For the FM-based action head, the VLM is executed up to layer `i`, and the FM action head is activated accordingly. An alternative training approach supervises the action loss from all VLM layers to enhance stability.
- **Early-Termination Inference via Action-Consistency Thresholding:** This mechanism dynamically terminates the VLM's forward pass when predicted actions stabilize across layers. At inference time, actions `A_t^(i)` are computed at intermediate VLM layers. An early exit at layer `i` is triggered if the discrepancy `d(A_t^(i), A_t^(i-1))` between the current and previous layer's actions falls below a layer-specific threshold `η_i` (Equation 6). Discrepancy metrics considered include cosine similarity, L2 distance, and mean absolute deviation. The thresholds `η_i` are calibrated offline using training data. A desired compute budget is translated into a target early-exit probability distribution (e.g., an exponential distribution, Equation 8), which emphasizes earlier exits for greater computational savings. A filtered-quantile procedure (Equation 9) sets `η_i` values, ensuring disjoint assignment of samples across exit layers while matching the target proportions.
- **Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching (for A1-FM):** While early exiting the VLM backbone reduces its computation, the iterative nature of the flow-matching action head can still impose significant overhead if it runs independently at each exit layer. To mitigate this, A1 introduces Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching:
 1. The number of denoising steps `δ` for the FM action head is reduced to a small value (e.g., 2 steps) per layer.
 2. Instead of restarting denoising from random noise at each VLM layer, the output action chunk `A_1(i)_t` from the previous layer `i` is used as the warm-start initialization `A_0(i+1)_t` for denoising at the next layer `i+1` (Equation 10). This propagates the denoising process across layers, reducing overall computational cost while maintaining accuracy.

**C. Training Recipe**
The training pipeline consists of two stages:
- **Pre-Training Data Composition:** A large-scale robot-trajectory-only corpus is assembled, integrating:
 - **Open-source Robotic Datasets:** Including DROID, AgiBot, RoboCOIN, RoboMind, GM-100, and RoboChallenge. These provide diverse robot morphologies, task families, and scene statistics for broad generalization.
 - **Collected Robotic Datasets:** 15,951 in-house real-world trajectories from platforms like ARX, Franka, UR5, and AgiBot. This data specifically addresses distribution shifts between open-source datasets and target deployment environments.
 All datasets are converted to a consistent episodic format (`(o_t, s_t, ℓ, a_t)`), filtered for quality, and sampled using a hierarchical strategy (dataset-level and embodiment-balanced) to prevent bias.
- **Training Procedure:**
 - **Optimization:** The Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone is frozen. VLM components are optimized with a learning rate of 5 × 10
 −6, and the action head with 5 × 10
 −5, using the AdamW optimizer.
 - **Learning Rate Scheduling:** A linear warm-up phase (2,000 steps, VLM backbone frozen for the first 1,000 steps) is followed by cosine annealing decay.
 - **Data Augmentation:** Visual inputs receive image sharpening and random erasing. Proprioceptive states undergo action augmentation (state zero-out) with a probability of 0.5. State normalization is avoided to preserve intrinsic action space characteristics, and static/low-velocity frames are filtered.
- **Fine-Tuning:** Pre-trained checkpoints are fine-tuned on smaller, high-quality datasets for specific downstream tasks or robot embodiments, adjusting batch sizes and training steps as needed (e.g., LIBERO, VLABench, RoboChallenge).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of A1 encompassed computational cost analysis, simulation benchmarks, real-world robotic experiments, and ablation studies.

**A. Model Computational Cost Analysis** Analysis of the Molmo-7B VLM (28 layers) revealed a total inference cost of 11,074.39 GFLOPs for a sequence length of 352, with the CLIP vision encoder contributing 2,013.36 GFLOPs. Each LLM layer accounted for 323.61 GFLOPs. The MLP action head required 1.850 GFLOPs. The Flow Matching (FM) action head (Qwen3-400M) cost 0.493 GFLOPs per timestep, summing to 4.931 GFLOPs for 10 denoising steps. For A1-FM, a normal inference pass (δ=10 steps) required 11.130 TFLOPs. While an adaptive early-exit A1-FM (A1-FMe) with δ=10 denoising steps could reduce backbone computation, the iterative denoising at each exit layer resulted in an inference time of 4.44 seconds. In contrast, reducing denoising steps to δ=2 for A1-FMe significantly reduced inference time to 0.73 seconds, indicating that iterative denoising in the FM head constitutes a substantial computational bottleneck.

**B. Simulation Benchmark Results**
- **LIBERO:** A1 achieved an average success rate of 96.6%, with 99.8% on the OBJECT task suite. This performance was competitive with, and in some cases surpassed, established baselines such as π0 (94.2%) and π0.5 (96.9%).
- **VLABench:** A1 demonstrated an average success rate of 53.5%, outperforming π0.5 by 4% (49.5%). The model's failures were primarily attributed to object physics (e.g., objects falling) rather than a misunderstanding of task objectives, suggesting robust visual-language comprehension and affordance recognition.

**C. Real-World Experiment Results**
- **Real-world Manipulation (Table 2):** Across four distinct robotic platforms (Franka, AgiBot, OpenArm, Dobot-Arm) and seven diverse manipulation tasks, A1 achieved a mean success rate of 56.7%. This outcome surpassed π0.5 (47.5%) by 9.2% and π0 (40.8%) by 15.9%. A1 exhibited higher success rates in fine manipulation and long-horizon tasks, such as AgiBot's "pick glue" (80% vs. 60% for π0.5) and "clean table" (20% vs. 10% for π0.5). For few-shot learning, A1 achieved 50% success on "fruits (small)" with only 50 samples, outperforming baselines by 20–40%. Qualitative observations suggested A1 executed actions more accurately with reduced distraction from multiple objects.
- **RoboChallenge Results (Table 4):** On the RoboChallenge Table30 benchmark, A1 achieved an average success rate of 29.00%. This placed it competitively among open-source VLA models and surpassed other comparable open-source baselines, including π0 (28.33%), X-VLA (21.33%), and RDT-1B (15.00%). A1 demonstrated high success rates on precise and long-horizon tasks such as "Open Drawer" (100%), "Put Cup on Coaster" (90%), and "Stack Bowls" (80%).

**D. Ablation Study**
- **Effectiveness of Early-Termination Inference (A1 -MLP, Table 5):** Adaptive inference with A1 -MLP showed that a budget-aware early-exit criterion `c=1.0` maintained a 96.6% average success rate while reducing VLM layer computation by 15.6%. Progressively decreasing `c` to `0.1` increased computational reduction to 76.6% with a success rate of 92.3%. This suggests significant redundancy in VLM computation, allowing for substantial efficiency gains with minor performance degradation. In some instances (e.g., LIBERO-Spatial with `c=0.7`), reduced computation was associated with a higher success rate. Visualizations (Figure 4) indicated adaptive exiting, with simpler actions terminating early and complex actions requiring deeper layer processing.
- **Effectiveness of Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching (A1-FM, Table 6):** For A1-FM, the direct application of early-exit with full δ=10 denoising steps at each layer resulted in increased inference time (40.9s per episode for `c=1.0`) despite reduced VLM computation. The proposed Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching, which combined reduced denoising steps (δ=2) and warm-start initialization (Equation 10), significantly reduced inference time. For `c=1.0` and δ=2 with warm-start, the per-episode inference time decreased to 10.5 seconds (a 72.3% reduction compared to 40.9s), while maintaining a 96.4% success rate. Warm-start initialization also contributed to an improvement in success rate (from 95.4% to 96.4%).
- **Generalization Experiments (Table 7):** When evaluated zero-shot on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark, A1 -FM (without early-exit, δ=10) achieved a robust success rate of 75.3%. This result outperformed OpenVLA-OFT (69.6%), π0 (53.6%), and π0 -FAST (61.6%), demonstrating its zero-shot transfer capabilities despite significant distribution shifts. Real-world early-termination experiments on AgiBot further supported these findings, achieving 84.6% compute reduction for `c=0.4` while maintaining high accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The A1 framework represents an advancement in the field of Vision-Language-Action models by addressing the critical challenge of computational efficiency and deployment cost. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

Firstly, A1 introduces a methodology for **joint acceleration of both the VLM backbone and the iterative action head**, which is a distinguishing feature compared to prior work that often focused on optimizing only the backbone. The budget-aware adaptive inference scheme, including early-termination via action-consistency thresholding and Inter-Layer Truncated Flow Matching with warm-start denoising, effectively reduces inference latency and computational footprint. This capability is crucial for enabling real-time robotic control on hardware with constrained resources, thereby broadening the accessibility and deployability of advanced VLA policies.

Secondly, the commitment to providing a **fully open-source and transparent system** (including model weights, training/inference code, data processing scripts, and evaluation protocols) contributes significantly to research reproducibility and collective progress. This transparency allows the community to audit, verify, and extend the work, fostering a more collaborative and evidence-based development environment for embodied AI. The performance of A1 on benchmarks like RoboChallenge, where it surpasses other open-source baselines, demonstrates that transparent research can yield competitive results in real-world applications.

Thirdly, A1 demonstrates **robust generalization capabilities** across diverse simulation environments (LIBERO, VLABench) and multiple real-world robotic platforms (Franka, AgiBot, OpenArm, Dobot-Arm). Its competitive performance on complex tasks, including precise manipulation and long-horizon reasoning, suggests that the pre-training strategy on mixed open-source and collected robotic datasets, coupled with the adaptive inference mechanism, enables the model to effectively transfer skills to varied scenarios and embodiments. The strong zero-shot transfer results on LIBERO-Plus further underscore its potential for deployment in novel environments with distribution shifts.

In summary, A1 offers a technically rigorous and practically oriented approach to VLA model deployment. By mitigating the high computational cost bottleneck, fostering open science, and demonstrating strong empirical performance across a range of challenges, it has the potential to accelerate the development and real-world application of general-purpose robotic manipulation systems. Future work, as outlined by the authors, may involve exploring unsupervised affordance learning, integrating reinforcement learning to enhance accuracy, and addressing issues like network latency through asynchronous execution for smoother manipulation.
