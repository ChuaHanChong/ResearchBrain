# Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning for in-hand manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning framework was applied to in-hand manipulation tasks, showing that it improves the performance of fully-actuated robotic hands by refining model-free policies, achieving higher consecutive successes and rewards compared to pure PPO. The evaluation also highlighted its robustness to moderate object property changes but limited generalization across different objects, alongside increased computational overhead.

## Problem

- In-hand manipulation for robots is challenging, requiring dexterous control and robust handling of complex contact dynamics.
- Model-Free Reinforcement Learning (MFRL) demands extensive data, while Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) struggles with accurately modeling real-world physics for in-hand tasks.
- Integrating anticipatory mechanisms, similar to human dexterity and Model Predictive Control (MPC), into robotic learning remains a hurdle.

## Method

- The research applies a **Model-Based** Lookahead ==Reinforcement Learning== framework, integrating Model-Free and Model-Based components.
- A Model-Free Actor-Critic algorithm (PPO) trains the initial policy and value function in a GPU-accelerated simulation environment.
- A deterministic dynamics model is concurrently trained via supervised learning using extracted state-action-next state trajectories.
- For action selection, online planning (PPO-MPC) samples and evaluates short-horizon trajectories using the learned dynamics model and value function to guide the policy.

## Results

- Model-Based Lookahead RL (PPO-MPC) increased average reward and consecutive successes for fully-actuated systems, achieving, for instance, a **1.065** reward increase and **2.2** more consecutive successes for a cube manipulation task.
- The framework exhibited robustness to object property variations, maintaining performance advantages for cubes with densities up to **4x** the original, but failed to generalize a policy trained on one object to manipulate a different object (e.g., cube policy for egg).
- PPO-MPC incurred a higher computational cost due to online trajectory evaluation, resulting in increased overall runtime despite achieving task success in fewer simulation timesteps than pure PPO.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Model-Based Lookahead RL significantly improves in-hand manipulation performance for fully-actuated systems, provided a reasonably competent model-free base policy is in place.
> - The framework demonstrates robustness to moderate changes in object properties within the same task (e.g., density variations) but struggles with generalization to fundamentally different objects.
> - Achieving substantial benefits from the lookahead mechanism is contingent on the quality of the initial model-free policy, with limited gains observed for weak base policies or under-actuated systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

In-hand manipulation represents a significant challenge in robotics, primarily due to the intricate dynamics involved and the requirement for precise, dexterous control to handle diverse objects. Human workers currently surpass robots in tasks demanding dexterity, precision, and adaptability, especially those involving multiple tools and objects, such as in assembly lines. This disparity is often attributed to the inherent complexity differences between human hands and typical robotic grippers.

The development of complex robotic hands, exemplified by the Shadow Hand, introduces the additional challenge of teaching these systems to effectively manipulate objects. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for this, drawing inspiration from human learning processes. However, real-time human execution of dexterous tasks also involves anticipatory mechanisms, akin to Model Predictive Control (MPC), which rely on internal simulations of future sensory inputs.

Within the RL landscape, two primary approaches exist: Model-Free RL (MFRL) and Model-Based RL (MBRL). MFRL learns policies directly from environmental interaction without an explicit model of the system's dynamics. While this allows it to tackle complex, unknown dynamics, it often requires extensive data and computational resources for training. Conversely, MBRL learns a model of the environment's dynamics, which can then be used for planning and is typically more data-efficient. However, MBRL's effectiveness is heavily reliant on the accuracy of its learned dynamics model. For in-hand manipulation, accurately modeling contact points, forces, and the elasticity of robotic hands presents a substantial "reality gap," making purely MBRL or MPC approaches difficult to implement successfully.

This research positions itself within the evolving field of hybrid RL methods, which aim to combine the strengths of both MFRL and MBRL. These hybrid approaches seek to overcome the limitations of each paradigm by integrating learned policies and value functions from MFRL with a learned dynamics model from MBRL. Specifically, this work focuses on "Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning," a framework proposed in earlier research, which guides a trained policy using a dynamic model and value function through trajectory evaluation, similar to how MPC operates. The paper evaluates this specific hybrid method's applicability and effectiveness in the complex domain of dexterous in-hand manipulation, aiming to bridge the gap between theoretical advantages and practical robotic application.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulty of in-hand manipulation tasks for robotic systems. Despite the existence of advanced robotic hands, effectively controlling them to perform dexterous tasks remains a significant hurdle. Traditional RL methods, both model-free and model-based, face limitations in this context: Model-Free methods are data-intensive, and Model-Based methods struggle with the complexities of accurately modeling dynamic contact interactions in real-time. The paper acknowledges that human dexterity likely involves anticipatory mechanisms, which MBRL and MPC attempt to emulate.

Given these challenges, the **main objective** of this work is to apply and thoroughly evaluate a previously developed hybrid Reinforcement Learning framework, specifically Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning, to in-hand manipulation tasks. The goal is to verify if this hybrid approach can improve the performance of dexterous robotic hands in such tasks.

More **specific objectives** include:

- **Performance Comparison:** To quantitatively compare the performance of Model-Based Lookahead RL against a commonly used Model-Free RL algorithm (Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO) for in-hand manipulation.
- **Model Acquisition:** To successfully obtain the necessary components for the hybrid framework: a high-accuracy policy and value function trained via a Model-Free method, and an accurate dynamic model of the system.
- **Hybrid Integration and Evaluation:** To combine these models and assess whether the hybrid approach achieves superior performance compared to the standalone Model-Free RL policy across various scenarios, including different objects (cube, egg, parallelepiped) and different robotic hand configurations (fully-actuated and under-actuated systems).
- **Robustness and Generalization Analysis:** To analyze the hybrid model's robustness and ability to generalize when task parameters deviate from the training environment. This includes tests with altered object properties (e.g., density, size) and attempts to guide a policy trained on one object to manipulate a different object.
- **Computational Cost Assessment:** To determine and report the computational costs, specifically iterations per second and overall runtimes, associated with Model-Based Lookahead RL in comparison to its Model-Free counterpart.

These objectives collectively aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of the Model-Based Lookahead RL framework's utility, benefits, limitations, and practical implications for advancing robotic in-hand manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a simulation-based approach to analyze the Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning framework for in-hand manipulation.

**Simulation Environment and Robotic Hand:**
The study utilizes **Nvidia Isaac Gym**, a GPU-accelerated physics simulator known for its multi-environment capabilities, enabling parallel training (2^13 environments). This simulator provides detailed state vectors (position, orientation, velocity, acceleration) for simulated objects, obviating the need for external pose detectors. The **RH8DR robotic hand** from Seed Robotics is used, with URDF models provided for simulation.

**Robotic Hand Actuation Models:**
Two actuation configurations of the RH8DR hand are investigated:
1. **Fully-Actuated System:** Each of the 17 active Degrees of Freedom (DoF) is independently controlled using a PD controller, with position as the reference. The state vector comprises 61 variables: 2 variables per joint (position, velocity), 10 variables for the object (position, orientation, linear velocity), and 17 previous actions. The action space consists of 17 joint position values.
2. **Under-Actuated System:** This configuration mimics the real RH8DR hand, featuring 6 actuators (3 for the wrist, 2 for the thumb, 1 for the index, 1 for the middle, and 1 shared for the ring and little finger). Actuation for the fingers is torque-based, using a spring coefficient matrix (K) and a Synergy Coefficient matrix (S) to emulate tendon-like control and synergies. Wrist and thumb-palm joints are position-controlled. The state vector contains 50 variables (joint positions/velocities, object pose/velocity, 6 previous actions). The action space consists of 6 actuator values.

**Manipulation Task:**
The core task involves **consecutively rotating an object on the hand's palm to match various goal orientations**. An object is spawned on the hand with a random orientation, and a goal object (with another random orientation) is simultaneously presented. The robot must rotate its object to match the goal object's orientation within 600 timesteps. Upon success, a new goal orientation is provided. Failure occurs if the object falls from the palm. Performance metrics include consecutive successes and average episode length.
Three objects with varying geometric complexities are used: a **cube, an egg, and a parallelepiped**.

**Hybrid RL Framework (Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning):**
The framework integrates Model-Free and Model-Based components:

- **Training Phase:**
 - **Policy and Value Function Training:** A Model-Free Actor-Critic algorithm, **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, is used to train the policy ($\pi_{\theta_\pi}$) and value function ($V_{\theta_V}$). Training involves 2^13 parallel environments over 500,000 timesteps. The neural network architecture is a dense Feed Forward Network, separating into two heads for mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$).
 - **Reward Function:** The reward function incorporates rotation difference between object and target, a reach goal bonus, a small fall penalty, L2 regularization on the action space, and action smoothing.
 - **Dynamic Model Training:** Concurrently with policy training, a deterministic dynamics model ($f_{\theta_f}$) is trained using Supervised Learning with the Adam optimizer. The training data consists of extracted trajectories (state, action, next state) from the exploratory policy. The model predicts the next state given the current state and action. The network architecture for the dynamic model uses blocks of Linear Fully Connected Layer, Batch Normalization, ReLU activation, and a 20% dropout layer. For the fully-actuated system, a modular approach is used, where an initial "Single Joint Model" network predicts individual joint positions/velocities, simplifying the overall dynamics prediction due to the large action space. Inputs include finger joint positions/linear velocities and object position/linear velocity.

- **Evaluation Phase (Online Planning via MPC):**
 - After training, the hybrid model uses online planning, referred to as **PPO-MPC**.
 - **Trajectory Sampling:** From the current state, `N=1024` trajectories are simulated over a `H=2` step horizon. Actions are sampled from the learned policy ($\pi_{\theta_\pi}$), and next states are predicted by the learned dynamics model ($f_{\theta_f}$).
 - **Trajectory Evaluation:** Each sampled trajectory is evaluated by summing discounted rewards obtained from the environment (R) and incorporating the global information from the learned value function ($V_{\theta_V}$) at the terminal state of the horizon.
 - **Action Selection:** Instead of selecting the action from the single best trajectory (to mitigate approximation errors), the first actions of the `E=2` best trajectories are averaged to determine the action to be executed in the environment.

**Experimental Design:**
The methodology includes several tests:
1. **Baseline Comparison:** Evaluating PPO-MPC against pure PPO on the same tasks they were trained on (correct policy and dynamic model for corresponding task).
2. **Generalization Test (Object Property Change):** Manipulating a cube with varying densities (0.5ρ to 32ρ) using the model trained on the original cube.
3. **Cross-Task Generalization:** Using the policy trained on the cube to manipulate the egg and parallelepiped, aided by their respective dynamic models.
4. **Under-Actuated System Evaluation:** Testing PPO-MPC performance with the under-actuated hand configuration.
5. **Computational Cost Analysis:** Measuring iterations per second and runtime per success for both PPO and PPO-MPC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings regarding the application and performance of Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning (PPO-MPC) in in-hand manipulation tasks:

#### 4.1. Policy Training (PPO Baseline)

- **Fully-Actuated vs. Under-Actuated:** The fully-actuated system consistently achieved significantly higher Average Reward and Consecutive Successes across all objects compared to the under-actuated system. The under-actuated hand struggled with complex movements, especially for the cube and parallelepiped, often achieving close to zero consecutive successes.
- **Object Performance:** For the fully-actuated system, the egg surprisingly outperformed the cube despite its more complex geometry. This was attributed to the robot's manipulation strategy, which involved rolling the object on the palm with fingers, a technique more effective for rounder surfaces. The parallelepiped, with its varied dimensions, proved most challenging for the fully-actuated system.
- **Under-Actuated Object Performance:** In the under-actuated case, the cube and parallelepiped had similar poor performance, indicating that this system struggles with objects having straight sides and sharp edges.

#### 4.2. Dynamic Model Accuracy

- The trained dynamic models, for both fully-actuated (using a modular approach) and under-actuated systems, exhibited low prediction errors (RMSE and MAE) across train and test sets for joint position, joint velocity, object position, object orientation, and object velocity. This indicates that accurate models of the system dynamics were successfully learned.

#### 4.3. Model-Based Lookahead RL (PPO-MPC) Performance

- **On-Task Performance (Fully-Actuated System):** When using the correct policy and dynamic model for the task, PPO-MPC demonstrated a significant increase in both Average Reward and Consecutive Successes for the cube, egg, and parallelepiped compared to the pure PPO policy. For instance, the cube saw a reward increase of 1.065 and 2.2 more consecutive successes, while the egg had a 1.099 reward increase and 2.5 more consecutive successes. PPO-MPC also achieved goals in fewer simulated timesteps on average, although at a higher computational cost.
- **On-Task Performance (Under-Actuated System):** For the under-actuated system, the performance improvement from PPO-MPC was minimal for the cube and parallelepiped, which already had low baseline PPO performance. However, for the egg, PPO-MPC showed a noticeable improvement of 0.6 consecutive successes. This suggests that the benefits of trajectory evaluation are more pronounced when the base policy already exhibits a reasonable level of competence.
- **Generalization to Object Weight Change (Fully-Actuated Cube):**
 - The PPO policy generalized well to varying cube densities, even showing improved performance for densities 2ρ and 4ρ compared to the original 1ρ.
 - PPO-MPC maintained its performance advantage over PPO across most density changes, with significant improvements for 2ρ and 4ρ.
 - For extreme density changes (e.g., 32ρ), the performance gains from PPO-MPC diminished, but its performance did not fall below that of the pure PPO policy. This indicates a degree of robustness to changes in object dynamics when certain underlying system dynamics (e.g., joint kinematics) remain consistent.
- **Guiding Policy on Different Objects (Cube Policy for Egg/Parallelepiped):**
 - Neither the pure PPO policy (trained on cube) nor PPO-MPC (using the cube policy and respective object dynamic models) effectively generalized to manipulate the egg or parallelepiped. Both failed to achieve an average of even one consecutive success.
 - PPO-MPC provided only a very small, non-significant increase in Average Reward and no practical change in Consecutive Successes in these cross-object tasks. This result reinforces the observation that if the base policy is not adequately suited for the task, the guidance from trajectory evaluation offers minimal benefit.

#### 4.4. Computational Cost

- PPO-MPC incurred a higher computational cost compared to pure PPO. While PPO-MPC achieved success in fewer simulated timesteps, the process of trajectory evaluation significantly increased the overall runtime in seconds (due to lower iterations per second). This overhead is influenced by hyperparameters such as the horizon value.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic in-hand manipulation by providing an empirical evaluation of the Model-Based Lookahead Reinforcement Learning framework in a simulated environment. The findings offer insights into the practical applicability and limitations of combining Model-Free and Model-Based RL for dexterous tasks.

**Key areas of significance include:**

- **Validation of Hybrid RL for Dexterous Manipulation:** The work demonstrates that Model-Based Lookahead RL can enhance the performance of in-hand manipulation, particularly for fully-actuated systems and when a robust Model-Free policy already exists. This validates the premise that integrating a learned dynamics model and value function for short-horizon planning can effectively guide a reactive policy towards improved outcomes.
- **Insights into Generalization Capabilities:** The study provides nuanced insights into the generalization capabilities of such hybrid models. It shows that the framework can tolerate and even leverage moderate changes in object properties (like density) within the same task domain. However, it highlights limitations when attempting to transfer a policy trained on one object to manipulate a fundamentally different object without task-specific training, emphasizing the challenge of broad generalization in complex manipulation.
- **Dependency on Policy Quality:** A crucial finding is the strong dependency of the Model-Based Lookahead framework's performance gains on the quality of the underlying Model-Free policy. If the base policy is weak or struggles significantly with a task (as observed with the under-actuated system or cross-object generalization), the lookahead mechanism offers only marginal improvements. This suggests that effective hybrid design must still prioritize learning a competent base policy.
- **Highlighting Computational Trade-offs:** The research explicitly addresses the increased computational cost associated with trajectory evaluation in Model-Based Lookahead RL. While the method may reduce the number of simulated timesteps required for success, the overhead of online planning can lead to higher real-world execution times. This practical consideration is vital for deploying such systems in real-time robotic applications and points towards the need for further optimization in hardware and algorithms.
- **Implications for Real-World Robotics:** The use of an under-actuated hand model, designed to mimic a real robotic hand, provides a preliminary indication of challenges in transferring these methods to physical systems. The limited gains observed with the under-actuated model suggest that achieving high-quality base policies on hardware-constrained robots will be a prerequisite for significant benefits from model-based lookahead.

In summary, this work advances the understanding of how hybrid RL can be leveraged for complex robotic tasks. It underscores the potential of model-based guidance to refine model-free policies, while simultaneously identifying critical challenges related to generalization, computational efficiency, and the foundational requirement of a robust base policy for practical impact. These findings are important for guiding future research in developing more capable and adaptable robotic manipulation systems.
