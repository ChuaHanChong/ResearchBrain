# Parameter Efficient Fine-tuning via Explained Variance Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> Explained Variance Adaptation (EVA) from JKU Linz enhances LoRA for foundation models by employing a variance-optimal initialization based on incremental SVD of activations and an adaptive rank allocation strategy. This approach yields faster convergence and superior performance across diverse tasks (language, vision, RL), often with fewer trainable parameters than existing methods.

## Problem

- Fine-tuning large foundation models is computationally expensive, memory-intensive, and time-consuming.
- Existing Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA often use suboptimal initialization, hindering convergence and performance.
- Fixed rank allocation in LoRA can lead to inefficient parameter usage, as the importance of low-rank updates varies across model layers.

## Method

- Develops a ==variance-optimal initialization== for LoRA matrices (A) using incremental Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on activation vectors, which aligns them with principal components to maximize the expected gradient signal.
- Introduces an adaptive rank allocation mechanism that globally sorts singular values to redistribute the total rank budget, assigning more ranks to layers with higher activation variance.
- Leverages an incremental SVD approach to ensure the initialization process has negligible computational and memory overhead relative to full fine-tuning.

## Results

- EVA consistently achieves higher average performance across language generation (Llama-2/3.1, Gemma-2), language understanding (RoBERTa, DeBERTav3), image classification (DINOv2), and reinforcement learning (Decision Transformer) benchmarks.
- Experiments show EVA leads to larger initial gradient norms and faster convergence, empirically validating its theoretical claim of maximizing the expected gradient signal.
- The method establishes a new Pareto frontier in accuracy and efficiency, often reducing the total number of trainable parameters while improving or maintaining performance across various tasks and models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Initializing LoRA adapters along directions of maximum activation variance provably amplifies the expected gradient signal at the start of fine-tuning, leading to faster adaptation.
> - Adaptive allocation of low-rank parameters based on activation variance allows for more efficient use of the parameter budget, improving the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier.
> - The incremental SVD procedure offers a robust and computationally efficient method to perform variance-optimal initialization, making it practical for large models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research by Paischer et al. directly addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly evolving field of artificial intelligence: the efficient adaptation of large pre-trained **foundation models (FMs)**. FMs, such as large language models (LLMs) and vision transformers, have demonstrated unprecedented capabilities across various domains, from language generation to computer vision and reinforcement learning. However, their immense size, often comprising billions of parameters, makes traditional **full fine-tuning (FFT)** for specific downstream tasks prohibitively expensive in terms of computational resources, memory, and time.

This computational bottleneck has spurred the development of **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)** methods. PEFT techniques aim to adapt FMs to new tasks by training only a small subset of additional parameters or by modifying existing parameters with low-rank updates, while keeping the majority of the pre-trained weights frozen. Among PEFT methods, **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)** (Hu et al., 2022) has emerged as a particularly popular and effective approach due to its simplicity and ability to maintain high performance. LoRA introduces a pair of low-rank matrices (A and B) for each pre-trained weight matrix, significantly reducing the number of trainable parameters.

The current work by Paischer et al. fits into a growing line of research focused on enhancing LoRA's performance and efficiency, specifically by optimizing its initialization strategy and introducing adaptive rank allocation. Existing LoRA initialization methods typically rely on random initialization for matrix A and zero initialization for matrix B, or more recently, on Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of gradients or weight matrices (e.g., PiSSA, MiLoRA, OLoRA, LoRA-GA, CorDA). However, the authors argue that these existing SVD-based approaches do not *provably maximize the expected gradient signal* at the beginning of fine-tuning, which is crucial for fast adaptation and optimal convergence.

EVA distinguishes itself by proposing a *data-driven, variance-optimal initialization* scheme that explicitly aims to maximize this expected gradient signal. Furthermore, it integrates a sophisticated *adaptive rank allocation* mechanism, which allows for dynamic redistribution of the low-rank budget across different layers of the model based on their activation variance. This combination positions EVA as a method that not only improves initial learning dynamics but also optimizes parameter usage for better efficiency and performance, setting a new Pareto frontier in the PEFT landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel and superior initialization strategy for LoRA that addresses the limitations of existing methods, particularly their suboptimality in leveraging initial data signals. The authors explicitly aim to create an initialization scheme that **provably maximizes the expected gradient signal** at the very beginning of the fine-tuning process. This core objective is motivated by the understanding that a strong, stable gradient signal from the outset is critical for accelerating convergence and achieving higher performance in downstream tasks.

Beyond this central aim, several other key objectives and motivations drive the development of EVA:

- **Accelerating Fine-Tuning:** By providing an optimal starting point, EVA intends to make the fine-tuning process faster and more efficient, reducing the number of training steps required to reach peak performance.
- **Improving Downstream Performance:** The goal is not just faster training, but also achieving superior performance on a variety of downstream tasks across different modalities (language, vision, reinforcement learning).
- **Enhancing Parameter Efficiency through Adaptive Ranks:** While LoRA itself is parameter-efficient, a fixed rank allocation across all layers might not be optimal. The authors aim to develop a data-driven heuristic for adaptive rank allocation. This mechanism seeks to intelligently redistribute the total rank budget, assigning higher ranks to layers or components that capture more significant variance in activations. This ensures that the limited parameter budget is utilized where it matters most, potentially reducing the overall trainable parameter count while maintaining or improving performance.
- **Establishing a New Pareto Frontier:** The ultimate ambition is to demonstrate that EVA can simultaneously achieve better accuracy and higher efficiency (fewer trainable parameters), thus outperforming existing LoRA variants on a Pareto efficiency curve.
- **Addressing Computational Overhead:** While introducing a new initialization step, a practical motivation is to keep its computational overhead (both time and memory) negligible relative to the overall fine-tuning process, ensuring its practical applicability.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** The motivation includes providing a strong theoretical basis for why variance-optimal initialization amplifies gradient signals and contributes to better generalization, linking it to concepts like the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK).

In essence, the research is motivated by the continuous need to make large foundation models more accessible and adaptable without sacrificing performance, pushing the boundaries of what is possible with parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of Explained Variance Adaptation (EVA) lies in its novel data-driven initialization and adaptive rank allocation strategy for LoRA. The methodology can be broken down into three key components:

#### 3.1. Variance-Optimal Initialization via Incremental SVD on Activations

- **Core Idea:** The central hypothesis is that initializing LoRA matrices along directions that capture the most variance in activation vectors leads to a stronger and more stable gradient signal, thereby accelerating fine-tuning.
- **Targeted Initialization:** EVA focuses on initializing the LoRA matrix **A** (where the low-rank update is represented as `BAx`, with `x` being the activation input). It aligns the rows of A with the principal components of the activation vectors.
- **Incremental Singular Value Decomposition (SVD):** To achieve this without prohibitive memory costs associated with large datasets and models, EVA employs an *incremental SVD* approach. Instead of collecting all activation batches into a single large matrix, it processes minibatches of activation vectors sequentially. The method adapts the sequential Karhunen-Loeve algorithm (Ross et al., 2008), which incrementally updates the right-singular vectors (V) and singular values (Σ) in constant time and memory with respect to the dataset size.
- **Convergence Criterion:** The incremental SVD procedure continues until the right-singular vectors for each target weight matrix converge, as determined by a cosine similarity threshold (`τ`).
- **Theoretical Justification (Theorem 3.1 & 3.2):**
 - **Theorem 3.1** formally proves that the top `r` right-singular vectors of an activation matrix `X` optimally capture the maximum variance of activations under a rank-`r` projection and minimize the Frobenius norm reconstruction error. This establishes SVD as the "variance-optimal" choice.
 - **Theorem 3.2** extends this by demonstrating that initializing `A` with these top right-singular vectors *maximizes the expected squared gradient norm* (E[||∂L/∂B||²F] ∝ Tr(AΣAᵀ)). This provides the direct link between explained variance and amplified gradient signal, explaining why EVA leads to faster adaptation.
- **Connection to NTK:** The authors further connect EVA to the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory, suggesting that EVA approximately initializes LoRA adapters along the dominant principal directions of the NTK, which are associated with faster learning and more robust generalization.
- **Initialization of B:** Following prior work (Hayou et al., 2024), the LoRA matrix `B` is initialized with zeros, which avoids altering the pre-trained model weights at the start and is believed to have favorable properties for higher learning rates.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Rank Allocation

- **Leveraging Singular Values:** The singular values obtained from the incremental SVD provide a direct measure of the "explained variance" for each corresponding right-singular vector.
- **Global Sorting:** For a given total rank budget (`l = N * r`, where `N` is the number of target weight matrices and `r` is the base rank), EVA globally sorts all collected right-singular vectors (from all `N` matrices) according to their explained variance ratio.
- **Rank Redistribution:** It then selects the top `l` vectors from this globally sorted list. The rank assigned to each individual LoRA adapter (for a specific weight matrix) is determined by how many of its original singular vectors are present among these top `l` selections. This naturally allocates more rank budget to layers whose activation subspaces are more "informative" (i.e., explain more variance).
- **Hyperparameter ρ:** A hyperparameter `ρ ∈ [1, ∞)` is introduced to control the flexibility of rank redistribution. `ρ=1` results in a uniform rank distribution (like standard LoRA but with EVA's initialization). `ρ > 1` allows for more heterogeneous distributions, enabling some layers to receive more ranks than `r` (up to `rρ`) and others fewer, provided the total budget `l` is maintained.

#### 3.3. Computational Efficiency and Robustness

- **Negligible Overhead:** The incremental SVD process is designed to be computationally efficient. Although it's an additional step, it does not require gradient computation or storing optimizer states, leading to minimal memory overhead. The time complexity is `O(N * T * k² * b)`, where `N` is the number of weight matrices, `T` is the number of minibatches until convergence, `k` is the rank, and `b` is the batch size.
- **Batch Size and Order Invariance:** The authors empirically demonstrate that the incremental SVD convergence is robust to different batch sizes and the order of minibatches, ensuring the reliability of the initialization.

In summary, EVA's methodology intricately weaves together advanced numerical linear algebra techniques (incremental SVD) with a principled understanding of gradient dynamics and activation statistics, culminating in a powerful and adaptable PEFT initialization scheme.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Paischer et al. across a diverse range of domains consistently demonstrate the superior performance and efficiency of Explained Variance Adaptation (EVA) compared to traditional LoRA and other advanced initialization or rank redistribution methods.

#### 4.1. Consistent Performance Improvements Across Domains

EVA was evaluated on:
- **Language Generation:** Fine-tuning LLMs (Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.1-70B, Gemma-2-9B, Gemma-2-27B) on common sense reasoning benchmarks and mathematical reasoning tasks (MATH, GSM8K after MetaMathQA fine-tuning).
 - EVA consistently achieved the highest average performance across these tasks, often leading to performance on par with or better than competitors, even while reducing trainable parameters. For instance, EVA on Llama-3.1-70B yielded the highest average score (94.5) with over 15M fewer trainable parameters than standard LoRA.
- **Language Understanding:** Fine-tuning RoBERTa Large and DeBERTav3 Base on the GLUE benchmark.
 - EVA attained the highest average scores across all GLUE tasks for both models, demonstrating its effectiveness beyond generation tasks.
- **Image Classification:** Fine-tuning a DINOv2-g/14 model on the VTAB-1K benchmark (19 diverse image classification tasks).
 - EVA achieved the best average accuracy across all tasks, particularly excelling on "natural" (in-domain) image tasks.
- **Decision Making (Reinforcement Learning):** Fine-tuning a Decision Transformer on the Meta-World benchmark suite.
 - EVA significantly reduced the performance gap between LoRA and Full Fine-tuning (FFT), and when combined with DoRA (EVA+DoRA), it achieved the best average performance across all tasks.

#### 4.2. Faster Convergence and Stronger Gradient Signal

- **Gradient Norm and Training Loss:** Experiments (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B on MetaMathQA) showed that EVA exhibits the largest gradient norm at the beginning of training and converges faster than competing initialization methods, as evidenced by training loss curves (Figure 4, Figure 6). This empirically validates the theoretical claim of maximizing the expected gradient signal.

#### 4.3. Parameter Efficiency and Pareto Dominance

- **Adaptive Rank Reduction:** The adaptive rank allocation mechanism in EVA frequently led to a reduction in the total number of trainable parameters compared to methods using uniform ranks, while simultaneously improving or maintaining performance (Figure 2, Table 11). This is attributed to the redistribution of ranks from higher-dimensional feedforward weights to lower-dimensional attention weights, demonstrating EVA's ability to establish a new Pareto frontier in accuracy and efficiency.
- **Specifics of Redistribution:** The rank distribution patterns varied by model and task, with higher attention layers often receiving more ranks for DeBERTav3 Base, and earlier layers for RoBERTa Large (Figures 7-10).

#### 4.4. Negligible Computational Overhead and Robustness

- **Initialization Time:** The computational overhead for EVA's initialization was shown to be remarkably low, constituting merely 0.2% to 0.7% of the total training time for LLMs (Figure 5). This makes EVA highly practical.
- **Memory Usage:** The memory requirements for initialization were also minimal, as it does not involve storing gradients or optimizer states.
- **Invariance to Batch Size and Order:** The incremental SVD procedure used in EVA was empirically confirmed to converge consistently and be largely invariant to the batch size and the order of minibatches (Figure 5, Figure 11, Figure 12), highlighting its robustness.

#### 4.5. Impact of Initialization and Components

- **Closeness to Final Solution:** Analysis showed that EVA's initialization is "closer" to the final trained adapter weights (in terms of spectral norm and cosine similarity) than LoRA's random initialization, indicating that EVA starts with more relevant information (Table 6, Figure 12).
- **Importance of Major Components:** An ablation study comparing EVA (using major variance-explaining components) with "EVA-minor" (using least variance-explaining components) revealed that EVA significantly outperforms EVA-minor. This confirms the critical role of initializing along directions of high activation variance (Table 12).
- **Scale and Direction:** Ablation studies on VTAB-1K indicated that both scale and direction are crucial. Whitening (preserving direction, altering scale) improved performance on out-of-distribution tasks, while corrupting directions (random permutation/rotation) degraded performance, especially on structured tasks (Table 25).

In summary, the main findings strongly support EVA as a robust, efficient, and high-performing parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that consistently outperforms its predecessors across a wide array of tasks and model architectures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Explained Variance Adaptation (EVA) by Paischer et al. holds significant importance and potential impact across the landscape of deep learning and large-scale model deployment.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

EVA represents a substantial step forward in PEFT by providing a theoretically grounded and empirically validated initialization scheme for LoRA. Unlike previous methods that often relied on random initialization or less optimized SVD-based approaches, EVA directly targets the maximization of the expected gradient signal. This principled approach to initialization improves the fundamental learning dynamics of LoRA.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Performance and Efficiency

The consistent outperformance of EVA across diverse domains—including large language models, vision models, and reinforcement learning—demonstrates its broad applicability and effectiveness. By achieving faster convergence, higher average task performance, and often simultaneously reducing the number of trainable parameters through adaptive rank allocation, EVA offers a superior balance of accuracy and efficiency. This establishment of a new "Pareto frontier" is a critical practical benefit for practitioners facing computational constraints.

#### 5.3. Democratization of Foundation Models

As foundation models continue to grow in size and complexity, the cost and resources required for fine-tuning become a major barrier. EVA's ability to extract more performance and efficiency from fewer trainable parameters contributes to making these powerful models more accessible for adaptation, even with limited computational resources. This can accelerate innovation and application development across various industries and research fields.

#### 5.4. Deeper Understanding of Learning Dynamics

The theoretical underpinnings of EVA, particularly its connection between explained activation variance, gradient signal amplification, and links to the Neural Tangent Kernel, offer valuable insights into *why* certain initialization strategies are more effective. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how information flows and is processed within large neural networks during fine-tuning, potentially inspiring future research into more optimal learning algorithms.

#### 5.5. Practical Adoption and Future Research Directions

The authors' commitment to open-sourcing their code and integrating EVA into the widely used PEFT library significantly lowers the barrier to adoption for researchers and developers. This widespread availability will likely accelerate its integration into various projects and benchmarks. The paper also outlines several fruitful avenues for future research:
- **Incorporating Gradient Information:** Combining activation variance with direct gradient information could lead to even more potent initialization schemes.
- **Enhanced Interpretability:** Relating specific singular vectors to different forms of pre-trained knowledge could offer new ways to interpret how foundation models learn and adapt.
- **Combination with Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models:** Exploring EVA's utility in fine-tuning sparse MoE architectures could unlock further efficiencies for future generations of ultra-large models.

In conclusion, EVA is a significant contribution that refines a cornerstone PEFT technique, LoRA, through theoretically sound and empirically robust improvements in initialization and parameter allocation. Its impact will likely be felt broadly, making foundation models more adaptable, efficient, and accessible for the next wave of AI applications.
