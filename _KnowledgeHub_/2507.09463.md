# Influence of Static and Dynamic Downwash Interactions on Multi-Quadrotor Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Brown University conducted a comprehensive, data-driven analysis to characterize static and dynamic downwash effects on multi-quadrotor systems, meticulously mapping forces, moments, and velocities. They found that a lower quadrotor's thrust significantly decreases by up to 35% in stacked configurations and validated turbulent jet scaling laws for quadrotor wakes at relevant Reynolds numbers (Re "approx" 9000), allowing for a scalable reduced-order model.

## Problem

- Complex aerodynamic interactions, primarily downwash, destabilize multi-quadrotor systems operating in close proximity, limiting their use in dense formations.
- Existing mitigation strategies rely on conservative collision avoidance zones based on arbitrary separation distances, restricting operational capabilities in environments requiring compact operation.
- There is a lack of fundamental understanding and detailed characterization of downwash's spatial influence on quadrotor interactions, especially concerning unsteady forces, moments, and high-resolution velocity fields.

## Method

- A comprehensive experimental setup using two Crazyflie quadrotors, 6-DOF force/torque transducers, and an x-z translation stage was employed for precise relative positioning and static interaction measurements.
- High-resolution ==Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV)== was used to characterize single and multi-vehicle flow fields, combining near-field and far-field data into composite velocity fields.
- Dynamic interaction studies utilized a "CrazyRail" linear acceleration platform to simulate rapid approach/retreat scenarios, measuring ==phase-averaged forces and moments== on a stationary lower quadrotor.

## Results

- The lower quadrotor experienced a thrust reduction to **65%** of nominal hover thrust at closest vertical separation (unsteadiness lingering to "approx" 17 vertical, "approx" 4.5 horizontal normalized separation), with dynamic experiments showing further reduced thrust during approach vs retreat phases in offset configurations.
- Pitch moments peaked at a horizontal separation of **2 times** the motor-to-body center distance (∆x/l = 2), with the lower quadrotor experiencing **three times** the moment observed by the upper quadrotor, and unsteady moments decaying by ∆x/l "approx" 4.
- Single quadrotor wakes showed individual rotor jets merging into a single turbulent jet at approximately **6.5 times** the motor-to-body center distance (z/l = 6.5), with far-field characteristics aligning with classical turbulent jet scaling laws.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The lower quadrotor experiences the most substantial aerodynamic interference from downwash, including significant thrust reduction and heightened unsteadiness in both forces and moments.
> - Quadrotor wakes exhibit characteristics consistent with classical turbulent jet theory in the far-field, demonstrating self-similarity and linear decay/growth of velocity and width, even at intermediate Reynolds numbers (Re "approx" 9000).
> - Dynamic interactions reveal velocity-dependent behaviors, with approach phases leading to more pronounced thrust reduction and destabilizing pitch moments compared to retreat phases, particularly in offset configurations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The operation of multiple quadrotors in close proximity is hindered by complex aerodynamic interactions, primarily downwash effects, which can destabilize vehicles and degrade performance. While multi-quadrotor systems have found applications in fields such as search and rescue, precision agriculture, and infrastructure inspection, their capability to operate in dense formations is restricted. Traditionally, these systems employ conservative strategies, such as maintaining large collision avoidance zones, to mitigate these effects. This approach limits their applicability in environments requiring compact operation, such as aerial docking or collaborative object transport.

Previous research has acknowledged the downwash effect in helicopter aerodynamics and begun exploring its impact on small-scale aerial vehicles like quadrotors. Existing work on downwash in quadrotors has primarily focused on collision avoidance by modeling quadrotor volumes as simple shapes (cylinders or ellipsoids) with predefined separation distances. However, these models often lack integration of detailed flight physics and are based on arbitrary separation values, leaving a gap in the fundamental understanding of the spatial influence of downwash. Studies measuring rotor wake decay have often reported results in dimensional units, complicating generalization across different vehicle scales, or have relied on low-bandwidth, low-resolution velocity probes. Numerical simulations, while capable of capturing complex wake structures, are often computationally intensive and limited in the flow field area they can cover.

This paper addresses these limitations by providing a comprehensive, data-driven analysis of static and dynamic downwash effects. It aims to characterize forces, moments, and velocities associated with downwash using high-resolution measurement techniques and normalized parameters, making the findings scalable and reproducible. Rather than focusing on avoiding downwash regions, this work seeks to understand and characterize these interactions, laying a foundation for developing strategies that enable quadrotors to operate effectively *within* these regions. This approach is intended to inform physics-based modeling and learning-based control strategies, thereby expanding the operational envelope and improving the robustness of multi-quadrotor control for dense formations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to provide a comprehensive, data-driven analysis of the downwash effect in both single and multi-quadrotor configurations. This analysis specifically focuses on characterizing, analyzing, and understanding the forces, moments, and velocities generated by these aerodynamic interactions.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and limitations in current multi-quadrotor operations:

- **Aerodynamic Interference:** Flying multiple quadrotors in close proximity creates complex aerodynamic interactions, primarily downwash, which can destabilize vehicles and degrade performance. This issue poses a significant challenge for advancing multi-robot aerial systems.
- **Limitations of Current Strategies:** Traditional multi-quadrotor systems rely on conservative strategies, such as large collision avoidance zones. While these prevent immediate collisions, they severely restrict the operational capabilities of quadrotor teams, particularly in dense environments or for applications requiring tight formations (e.g., aerial docking, large-scale trajectory planning, collaborative transport).
- **Gap in Fundamental Understanding:** Despite recognition of the downwash effect, detailed research into its impact on small-scale aerial vehicles, such as quadrotors, has been limited. A critical gap exists in understanding the spatial influence of downwash on quadrotor interactions at a fundamental level, particularly regarding forces, moments, velocity fields, and unsteady measurements.
- **Need for Physics-Based Modeling:** Accurate and detailed characterization of downwash effects is necessary to establish an empirical basis for aerodynamic interactions. This empirical foundation is crucial for developing physics-based models and enhancing learning-based approaches, which can lead to more sample-efficient learning and robust control strategies for dense formation flight.
- **Scalability and Generalization:** Previous studies have often presented results in dimensional units for specific quadrotors, making it difficult to scale findings to other vehicles or reproduce data in different contexts. The researchers aim to address this by systematically characterizing and analyzing normalized flow variables.
- **Unsteady Effects:** The unsteadiness and turbulence associated with downwash are recognized as destabilizing factors. Quantifying these unsteady effects is motivated by the need to develop proactive corrective measures for maintaining stability, optimizing path planning, and controlling dense formation flight.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines two primary contributions:
1. To characterize the effect of downwash on forces, moments, and velocities acting on a pair of quadrotors flying in close proximity as a function of their relative separation. This includes developing an algebraic reduced-order model of quadrotor downwash.
2. To quantify the unsteadiness (turbulence) due to downwash to better understand its destabilizing effects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the aerodynamic influence of downwash on close-proximity flight for two Crazyflie quadrotors, specifically the effect of the upper quadrotor's downwash on the lower quadrotor. The study considers two primary configurations: Case I (vertically aligned, stacked configuration) and Case II (horizontally offset configuration).

**A. Experimental Setup**

The experimental setup was conducted in a wind-free environment and consisted of:
- **Quadrotors:** Two Crazyflie quadrotors were used. All four motors on both quadrotors were operated with a constant 4V DC voltage supply, generating thrust sufficient for steady-state hover in free flight, corresponding to approximately 320 Hz rotor rotation.
- **Translation Stage:** A custom x-z translation stage allowed precise relative positioning of the quadrotors. Quadrotor (B) was fixed, while quadrotor (A) traversed a range of horizontal (∆x) and vertical (∆z) separations with a position accuracy of 100 µm.
- **Force/Torque Transducers:** Both quadrotors were mounted on 6-degrees-of-freedom (DOF) ATI Nano-17 force/torque transducers to measure aerodynamic interactions.
- **Normalization:** Thrust measurements (F_z) were normalized by the quadrotor's weight (W ≈ 0.265 N). Pitch moments (M_y) were normalized by Wl, where l is the horizontal distance from the quadrotor's body center to a motor (32.5 mm). Velocities were normalized by the induced velocity (U_i), calculated using actuator disk theory (U_i = sqrt(F_z / (2ρAn))).

**B. Experiment Methods**

Two sets of experiments were conducted:

1. **Force & Moment Measurements (Static Interactions):**
 - **Relative Position Matrix:** The relative position (∆x, ∆z) was varied systematically over a rectangular matrix: 0.1 m ≤ ∆x ≤ 0.6 m (horizontal) and 0.1 m ≤ ∆z ≤ 1.1 m (vertical). The larger vertical range was chosen based on existing models suggesting a more significant axial downwash development.
 - **Data Acquisition:** Force and moment measurements from both quadrotors were recorded at a sampling rate of 20 kHz for 30 seconds at each relative position. Each case was repeated three times, and standard deviations were computed.

2. **Velocity Measurements (Particle Image Velocimetry - PIV):**
 - **Configurations:** Velocity measurements focused on extreme instances of downwash effects:
 - Stacked (Case I) at closest vertical separation (∆z/l = 4) and a farther separation (∆z/l = 12.5).
 - Offset (Case II) at closest vertical separation (∆z/l = 4) and a farther separation (∆z/l = 12.5).
 - Single quadrotor: A baseline case to characterize flow fields without inter-vehicle interaction.
 - **PIV System:**
 - **Tracers:** Small (∼ µm) droplets of Di-Ethyl-Hexyl-Sebacic-Acid-Ester (DEHS) were suspended in the flow field.
 - **Illumination:** A Quantel Evergreen dual-cavity Nd:Yag laser (532 nm, max 200 mJ @ 15 Hz) illuminated tracer particles. The laser sheet was aligned to pass through the center of the front two rotors.
 - **Imaging:** A LaVision Imager sCMOS camera (2560 × 2160 pixels) with a Nikon 35mm lens provided a field of view of 400 × 300 mm (x × z).
 - **Data Processing:** 1000 image pairs (70 seconds of data) were processed using DaVis v10 (LaVision) with multipass correlations (final interrogation area of 32 × 32 pixels, 75% overlap).
 - **Composite Flow Fields:** To cover larger vertical ranges (near-field and far-field), the quadrotors were raised and lowered on the translation stage while keeping their relative position, laser sheet, and camera fixed. Average velocity fields from overlapping sections (minimum 100 mm overlap) were combined to form composite flow fields.

**C. Dynamic Quadrotor Interactions:**
- **Setup:** A precision linear dynamic acceleration platform, "CrazyRail," was developed. This allowed a hovering quadrotor (C) to rapidly approach and retreat from a stationary hovering quadrotor (D) positioned below.
- **Measurement:** The stationary lower quadrotor (D) was equipped with an ATI Nano-17 force/torque transducer.
- **Configurations:** Measurements were performed in both stacked (∆x/l = 0) and offset (∆x/l = 2) configurations.
- **Motion Profile:** Quadrotor (C) followed a prescribed sinusoidal trajectory defined by:
 - Oscillation amplitude (A): 2.5 cm – 9 cm
 - Minimum separation distance (∆z_min): 0.5 cm – 7 cm
 - Motion frequency (f): 0.1 Hz – 1 Hz
 - The separation distance was ∆z(t) = ∆z_min + A(1 + sin(ωt)), where ω = 2πf. The rate of change of separation, ∆ż(t), was Aω cos(ωt).
- **Data Acquisition & Processing:** Each trial involved a 48-cycle sinusoidal motion. The initial and final four ramp-up/down cycles were removed. Force and moments were phase-averaged across the forty stable oscillations to generate time-series data representing steady-state dynamic interactions. Static data (no relative motion) was also collected as a baseline for comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Forces & Moments (Static Interactions)**

- **Lower Quadrotor Thrust:** When the quadrotors were in a stacked configuration at the closest vertical separation (∆z/l = 4), the lower quadrotor experienced a significant thrust reduction, dropping to 65% of its nominal hover thrust. Its thrust recovered to nominal levels at approximately ∆x/l ≈ 3 horizontally and ∆z/l ≈ 19 vertically. These limits represent the areas of significant downwash influence, consistent with models suggesting downwash is more pronounced axially.
- **Upper Quadrotor Thrust:** The upper, downwash-generating quadrotor experienced only a slight reduction in thrust, retaining 90% of its original hover thrust even at the closest vertical separation.
- **Unsteadiness (Thrust):** The standard deviation of the normalized thrust (F'z) indicated that unsteadiness lingered until ∆z/l ≈ 17 and ∆x/l ≈ 4.5. When quadrotors were vertically aligned, unsteadiness remained pronounced up to ∆z/l ≈ 14. The lower quadrotor experienced significantly higher magnitudes of unsteadiness (approximately two orders of magnitude greater) compared to the upper quadrotor. Beyond ∆z/l > 17 and ∆x/l > 4.5, unsteadiness was considered negligible.
- **Pitch Moment:** Both quadrotors began to experience a pitch moment when horizontally displaced. The pitch moment (M_y) peaked at a horizontal separation of ∆x/l = 2. At this configuration, the upper quadrotor's downwash partially offset, creating the largest moment arm on the lower quadrotor. The upper quadrotor experienced only one-third of the pitch moment observed by the lower quadrotor.
- **Unsteadiness (Pitch Moment):** The standard deviation of the pitch moment (M'y) was non-zero even without horizontal displacement. Unsteady moments peaked at ∆x/l = 2 and decayed to negligible levels by ∆x/l ≈ 4. For vertical separations ∆z/l > 16, the flow became established, leading to a steady presence. Similar to thrust fluctuations, moment fluctuations were approximately two orders of magnitude smaller for the upper quadrotor.

**B. Velocity Fields**

1. **Single Vehicle Near-field Flow Characteristics:**
 - **Flow Structures:** Inflow velocity was observed above the rotors, roughly one-fifth of the induced velocity below. Rotor jets appeared as high-speed annuli below the blades with a low-speed core below the hub. A dead zone existed between the rotor jets immediately below the quadrotor body. These observations were consistent with existing CFD simulations and experimental studies.
 - **Jet Merging:** Individual rotor jets merged into a single turbulent jet at approximately z/l = 6.5 (≈ 2.3 motor-to-motor distances) along the centerline. Beyond this merge point, the maximum downwash speed occurred at the centerline and expanded downstream into the far-field.
 - **Lateral Velocity:** The time-averaged lateral velocity (¯v) was zero at the centerline. The flow moved inward below the rotors, but beyond the merge point, the polarity switched, and the merged jet spread outwards with diminishing intensity.
 - **Velocity Profiles:** Axial velocity profiles transitioned from multiple localized peaks (near-field) to two dominant peaks (bimodal, 5 ≤ z/l ≤ 6.5), and then to a single peak at the centerline (z/l ≥ 6.5) with radial tapering and lateral broadening.

2. **Single Vehicle Far-field Flow Characteristics - Comparison with Turbulent Jet Theory:**
 - **Turbulent Jet Scaling:** Far-field flow measurements (z/l > 6.5) were compared with classical turbulent jet theory. The jet half-width (r_1/2) was observed to grow linearly with axial distance (z), and the centerline velocity (u_c) and maximum axial velocity (u_max) overlapped and decayed linearly with z, consistent with turbulent jet behavior.
 - **Self-Similarity:** The scaled axial and lateral velocities demonstrated self-similarity, aligning with theoretical predictions for turbulent jets.
 - **Reynolds Number:** At the merge point (z/l = 6.5), where the turbulent jet achieved full merger, the centerline velocity was approximately half the induced velocity, and the jet half-width was roughly l. The calculated Reynolds number (Re) at this point was approximately 9000. This is significant because self-similarity, typically associated with high Reynolds numbers (10^4-10^5), was demonstrated to persist even at this lower intermediate Reynolds number, indicating dominant turbulent mixing mechanisms.
 - **Reduced-Order Model:** The validation of scaling laws for forces, moments, and velocities established a framework defined by four critical parameters: merge point (z_m/l), induced velocity (U_i), characteristic arm length (l), and virtual origin (z_0). This framework is applicable to quadrotors with similar rotor spacing and, with determined parameters, to heterogeneous multirotor systems.

3. **Multi-vehicle Flow Field Characteristics:**
 - **Stacked Configuration (Case I):**
 - **Larger Vertical Separation (∆z/l = 12.5):** The upper quadrotor's wake spread out and homogenized more before reaching the lower quadrotor, resulting in a wider jet and smoother inflow for the bottom vehicle, leading to larger magnitudes of velocities below the lower quadrotor.
 - **Closer Vertical Separation (∆z/l = 4):** The lower quadrotor operated within the upper quadrotor's near-field wake, characterized by high-velocity, non-uniform flow structures. This interfered with the lower quadrotor's ability to generate its own clean downwash, reducing its effective downwash velocity and resulting in lower velocity magnitudes. The far-field profiles resembled flatter, more uniform velocity distributions compared to the unimodal profiles of a single quadrotor.
 - **Offset Configuration (Case II - ∆x/l = 2):**
 - The horizontal offset resulted in an asymmetry in the lower quadrotor's wake. The strongest part of the upper quadrotor's wake did not directly impact the center of the lower quadrotor, shifting regions of peak velocity away from the lower quadrotor’s axial centerline. This asymmetry became more pronounced at shorter vertical separations.

**C. Dynamic Quadrotor Interactions**

- **Approach vs. Retreat Phases:** The approach phase (negative velocity, quadrotor (C) moving closer to (D)) consistently resulted in lower thrust for quadrotor (D) compared to the retreat phase (positive velocity, quadrotor (C) moving away from (D)).
- **Stacked Configuration:** Dynamic approaches and retreats in the stacked configuration generated symmetrical force patterns, resulting in negligible pitch moments centered around zero, irrespective of separation. The thrust range was wider due to complete four-motor overlap.
- **Offset Configuration:** The offset configuration induced substantial destabilizing pitch moments on the lower quadrotor (D). These moments decreased in magnitude with increasing separation and were more pronounced (higher magnitudes) during the approach phase than the retreat phase, reflecting velocity-dependent behavior and aerodynamic asymmetry.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of multi-quadrotor system aerodynamics by providing a detailed, data-driven characterization of static and dynamic downwash interactions. The findings carry several implications:

- **Enhanced Fundamental Understanding:** The comprehensive mapping of forces, moments, and velocities in both near-field and far-field regions provides a foundational empirical basis for understanding aerodynamic interactions in dense quadrotor formations. This moves beyond generalized avoidance strategies to a more detailed comprehension of interactions *within* regions of downwash influence.
- **Development of Physics-Based Models:** The validation of turbulent jet scaling principles for quadrotor wakes, particularly at Reynolds numbers (Re ≈ 9000) relevant to small-scale UAVs, is significant. This finding enables the development of robust, reduced-order algebraic models for downwash, which can be integrated into control systems and simulation environments. Such models can predict aerodynamic behavior more accurately than purely empirical or computationally intensive fluid dynamics models.
- **Scalability and Generalization:** The use of normalized quantities and the identification of a scalable framework defined by critical parameters (merge point, induced velocity, arm length, virtual origin) allow for the generalization of these findings. This framework can be applied to a range of quadrotors, and potentially extended to heterogeneous multirotor systems, facilitating broader applicability in multi-robot research.
- **Improved Control Strategies:** The characterization of unsteady downwash effects, including both thrust and moment fluctuations, is crucial for developing robust control strategies. By understanding how forces and moments vary with relative separation and velocity (approach vs. retreat), controllers can be designed to anticipate and proactively counteract dynamic load variations, thereby enhancing stability and performance in close-proximity flight.
- **Expanded Operational Envelope:** A deeper understanding of downwash effects can enable quadrotors to operate safely and efficiently in denser formations. This has the potential to unlock new applications and improve existing ones, such as aerial docking, collaborative transport of objects, large-scale trajectory planning, and operation in confined or complex environments where tight maneuvers are required.
- **Open-Access Dataset:** The creation of a valuable open-access dataset of downwash characteristics (forces, moments, velocities) serves as a resource for the multi-robot community, facilitating further research, model validation, and the development of more sophisticated algorithms for multi-UAV cooperation.
