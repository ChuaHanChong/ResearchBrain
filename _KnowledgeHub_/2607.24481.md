# ArmnetBench v0.1: Parallel Real-World Evaluation of Manipulation Policies on a Low-Cost Arm Farm

> [!summary] Summary
> ArmnetBench v0.1 introduces a framework for parallel real-world evaluation of robot manipulation policies using a managed fleet of low-cost robotic cells. This initiative provides a standardized benchmark, reducing per-trial evaluation costs and releasing a substantial, graded dataset of policy rollouts for future research.

## Problem

- Real-world evaluation of generalist robot manipulation policies faces high costs due to hardware, setup, and human operator involvement, limiting studies to few trials on single robots.
- Small-scale evaluations on single robots often obscure genuine policy improvements, making direct comparisons difficult and hindering independent reproduction due to unrecorded experimental variables.
- Simulation offers a cost-effective alternative but is subject to the 'sim-to-real' gap, where policy performance does not reliably translate to physical robots.

## Method

- Developed the **Armnet arm farm**, a managed fleet of low-cost ==SO-101== robotic cells, each equipped with cameras and a Raspberry Pi, designed for parallel policy execution under light on-site supervision.
- Implemented a standardized evaluation protocol where policies run in ==isolated containers==, and operators manually reset scenes and provide a ==three-way quality score== (successful, suboptimal, failure) for each rollout.
- Released a comprehensive evaluation corpus of 3,118 core labelled episodes (including 600 human demonstrations and 2,518 policy rollouts) to support diverse downstream robot learning applications.

## Results

- Evaluated seven manipulation policies across **12 tasks** (**8** single-arm, **4** bimanual) with each policy trained or fine-tuned on an identical budget of **50 demonstrations** per task.
- The π0.5 policy achieved the highest overall strict success rate at **47.6%** (**51.5%** including suboptimal completions), leading in both single-arm (**45.4%**) and bimanual tasks (**52.1%**).
- Performance varied significantly by task, with π`transfer_cube`.5 achieving **86%** strict success on `transfer_cube` (bimanual) but no policy achieving any success on the contact-rich `cable_clip` task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parallel evaluation on a low-cost arm farm effectively reduces the per-trial cost and enables a larger scale of real-world robotic experiments, addressing a critical bottleneck in robotics research.
> - A standardized benchmark with graded scoring (successful, suboptimal, failure) provides richer data beyond binary outcomes, offering a more nuanced assessment of policy performance.
> - The relative performance of manipulation policies can be embodiment-dependent, as observed by shifts in policy rankings between single-arm and bimanual tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robot manipulation policies is constrained by the challenges associated with real-world evaluation. Traditional evaluation methods often involve significant costs due to the need for physical hardware, diverse trial setups, and human operators for configuration, resetting, and scoring. This typically limits studies to a small number of trials on a single robot, which can obscure the significance of policy improvements and hinder independent reproduction due to unrecorded experimental variables such as camera placement or object initialization.

While simulation offers a cost-effective and reproducible alternative for evaluation, it is subject to the sim-to-real gap, where performance in simulation may not directly translate to physical robots. Existing physical benchmarks attempt to address this by standardizing hardware or scenes. Examples include REPLAB (Yang et al., 2019) providing a reproducible low-cost cell, SceneReplica (Khargonkar et al., 2024) offering replicable scenes, FurnitureBench (Heo et al., 2025) focusing on long-horizon assembly, FMB (Luo et al., 2025) for functional manipulation, and VLA-REPLICA (Huang et al., 2026) for reproducible Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model evaluation using the SO-101 cell. However, many of these primarily provide blueprints for independent setup, which can still be resource-intensive or limited in scale. Other approaches involve auto-evaluation, such as AutoEval (Zhou et al., 2025) which automates resets and success detection on a single robot, or distributed evaluation, exemplified by RoboArena (Atreya et al., 2025) which pools heterogeneous labs and employs preference scoring due to variability across sites, and RoboDojo (Chen et al., 2026) which integrates simulation with standardized remote services for bimanual platforms. Advances in low-cost robotics hardware, such as ALOHA (Zhao et al., 2023) demonstrating bimanual manipulation capabilities, and efforts in large-scale dataset collection like DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) and Open X-Embodiment (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024), contribute to the ecosystem for developing and testing robot manipulation policies.

ArmnetBench v0.1 distinguishes itself within this landscape by combining a managed fleet of low-cost robotic cells with an emphasis on parallel evaluation under light on-site supervision. This approach is designed to reduce the per-trial cost of real-world evaluation, support broad coverage across various tasks and policy types, and enable both single-arm and bimanual configurations. A core contribution is the release of a large, graded evaluation corpus collected under controlled conditions, allowing for more nuanced assessment beyond binary success/failure. This work aims to bridge the gap between high-cost, limited-scale physical evaluations and purely simulated environments, offering a reproducible and scalable platform for real-world robotics research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for ArmnetBench v0.1 stems from the existing bottleneck in the real-world evaluation of generalist robot manipulation policies. The cost associated with each policy rollout, including the requirement for physical hardware, the time-consuming process of setting up and resetting experimental scenes, and the need for human operators to score outcomes, significantly limits the scope of research. Many studies are confined to a few dozen trials on a single robot in a single laboratory, which can make it difficult to discern genuine policy improvements from experimental noise and complicates direct comparisons between different research efforts. Furthermore, unarticulated experimental choices, such as specific camera placements, lighting conditions, object initialization procedures, or policy checkpoint selections, can inflate reported success rates and impede independent replication of results.

While simulated environments offer a reproducible and low-cost alternative, they are constrained by the "sim-to-real" gap, meaning that policies optimized in simulation may not perform equivalently in physical settings. Existing physical benchmarks, while avoiding the sim-to-real challenge, often necessitate compromises such as fewer tasks, limited trials, a single robot embodiment, or the use of coarse binary scoring (success/failure), thereby restricting the granularity of evaluation.

The key objectives of ArmnetBench v0.1 are:
1. **Reduce Per-Trial Evaluation Cost:** To address the high cost of real-world evaluation, the project aims to minimize the per-trial expenditure by utilizing a parallel fleet of low-cost SO-101 robotic cells. Each cell integrates 3D-printable components, affordable cameras, a Raspberry Pi, and network-controlled power.
2. **Enable Broad and Reproducible Evaluation:** The system is designed to support a wide range of evaluation scenarios, including diverse tasks, multiple policy architectures, and both single-arm and bimanual robot configurations. Policies are run in isolated containers under a standardized protocol to enhance reproducibility.
3. **Validate the Arm Farm Infrastructure:** A crucial objective for v0.1 is to validate the entire arm farm system, from hardware and software to the operational protocol, as a robust and scalable evaluation substrate.
4. **Establish Baseline Policy Performance:** To provide an initial comparative analysis, ArmnetBench v0.1 aims to compare seven distinct manipulation policies across 12 tasks, all trained or fine-tuned on an identical budget of 50 demonstrations per task. This is intended to establish baselines for future iterations and comparisons, rather than to determine the absolute performance ceiling of each method.
5. **Generate a Labelled Evaluation Corpus:** A central objective is to produce and release a comprehensive dataset of scored policy rollouts and reference demonstrations. This corpus, featuring a three-way quality label (successful, suboptimal, failure), is intended to support not only direct policy evaluation but also downstream machine learning tasks, such as reward modeling, training predictive world models, and developing policies capable of learning from mixed-quality data.

Through these objectives, ArmnetBench v0.1 seeks to contribute to overcoming fundamental limitations in real-world robot manipulation research by providing a more accessible, scalable, and data-rich evaluation platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ArmnetBench v0.1 evaluation was conducted on the Armnet arm farm, a managed fleet of low-cost SO-101 robotic cells designed for parallel real-world policy evaluation.

**Armnet Arm Farm Infrastructure:**
The farm comprises multiple cells, each equipped with either one or two SO-101 5-DoF follower arms, three cameras, and a Raspberry Pi 5 edge device. These components are integrated within a fixed A3 cardboard workspace that provides a consistent backdrop. Observations collected include camera views (front, top, wrist for single-arm; top, left_wrist, right_wrist for bimanual), five arm-joint positions, and one gripper position per arm. Actions are specified as target arm-joint and gripper positions. A networked Shelly plug facilitates remote power scheduling and recovery for each cell. Object positions within the workspace are randomized between rollouts for task variability, while the nominal cell setup is fixed for each task.

**Cell Hardware and Configuration:**
- **Arms:** One or two SO-101 follower arms per cell.
- **Cameras:** Single-arm cells use top, front, and wrist cameras. Bimanual cells use top, left_wrist, and right_wrist cameras. Context cameras (top/front) are Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 units (IMX708) mounted on Arducam pan/tilt units. Wrist cameras are Innomaker U20CAM 1080p USB models. All cameras record at 20 fps with a 16:9 aspect ratio, using resolutions of 1024x576 for context cameras and 1280x720 for wrist cameras. Pan/tilt values are fixed per cell configuration.
- **Lighting:** Ambient office lighting is used, with cells positioned away from windows and all room lights on. No specific calibration or matching of illumination across cells was performed.
- **Cost:** The approximate per-cell bill of materials (indicative mid-2026 US retail) is $359 for a single-arm cell and $477 for a bimanual cell.

**Evaluation Execution Protocol:**
A co-located workstation manages the arm farm, building and executing policy containers, performing inference, and handling real-time control over the local network. A cloud backend schedules jobs and streams logs and video feeds for operator monitoring.
- **Submission:** Users submit a policy image, embodiment type, and task.
- **Execution:** The workstation builds the policy image and runs it in an isolated container, ensuring policy dependencies are separate from cell software. The running policy sends commands to the corresponding edge Raspberry Pi connected to an arm.
- **Supervision:** Operators monitor the fleet via a control panel, which displays progress, scoring controls, and live-streaming logs and video. Operators are actively involved in resetting the scene and scoring each rollout; cells operate autonomously between these manual interventions.

**v0.1 Deployment:**
The v0.1 evaluation utilized three co-located cells: two single-arm (cell-1, cell-3) and one bimanual (cell-8). Tasks were partitioned such that all seven policies for a given task were evaluated on the same physical cell, maintaining a consistent nominal setup for that task. While the cloud backend supports geographically distributed cells, this capability was not exercised in v0.1. Operator active time per rollout (reset + score) was estimated at approximately 10 seconds.

**ArmnetBench Benchmark Design:**
- **Tasks:** The benchmark comprises 12 tabletop manipulation tasks, including 8 single-arm and 4 bimanual tasks. These tasks cover diverse manipulation challenges such as stacking, insertion, handling deformable objects, pick-and-place, and interacting with articulated objects (e.g., `block_stack`, `cable_clip`, `fold_tea_towel`). Each task is supported by 50 human-teleoperated reference demonstrations, which serve as training or fine-tuning data for the evaluated policies.
- **Policies:** Seven distinct manipulation policies were evaluated: two specialist imitation learning policies (ACT and Diffusion Policy) and five vision-language-action models (SmolVLA, π
0, π
0.5, GR00T N1.7, and MolmoAct 2). Each policy was trained or fine-tuned specifically for each task using the same 50 human demonstrations. The exact policy checkpoint for every task-policy pair is released.
- **Evaluation Protocol and Scoring:** Each task-policy pair aimed for 30 rollouts (with two pairs having 29 due to manual reset errors). Before each rollout, an operator manually reset the scene and randomized object placement within predefined task-specific ranges. Initial states were sampled independently and not matched across policies. Rollout termination was determined by operator judgment, as no standardized wall-clock limits were enforced. Rollouts were scored by the on-site operator using a three-way quality label: "successful" (task goal achieved cleanly), "suboptimal" (goal achieved but with poor quality), or "failure" (goal not achieved). The "suboptimal" label was used conservatively, applied to only 3.5% of policy rollouts. The 50 human demonstrations per task were deemed "successful" by construction. Successful and suboptimal rollouts were trimmed to remove idle frames. In total, v0.1 collected 3,118 core labelled episodes (600 human demonstrations and 2,518 policy rollouts), plus an additional 600 auxiliary policy rollouts released as data but not included in the core benchmark. All episodes include 3 synchronized camera views at 20 fps.

**Data Formats and Downstream Uses:**
The core benchmark data is released in two formats:
- **LeRobot v3.0:** Stores synchronized state, action, packed AV1 video, sparse terminal reward, and done fields, along with episode metadata (success, success_class, policy_type, policy_repo_id).
- **RoboMeter:** Provides one video per camera, the quality_label, and an embedding of the task instruction.
These labelled trajectories are intended to support various downstream robot learning applications, including reward modeling, training quality-conditioned policies on mixed-quality data, and developing action-conditioned world models or video-generation modules for robot policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ArmnetBench v0.1 evaluated seven manipulation policies across 12 tasks (8 single-arm, 4 bimanual), with each policy trained or fine-tuned on 50 demonstrations per task. The evaluation comprised 2,518 policy rollouts, scored with a three-way quality label (successful, suboptimal, failure).

**Overall Policy Performance:** The evaluation reported both a strict success rate (counting only "successful" rollouts) and a more lenient success+suboptimal rate. Across all 12 tasks, the policy π0.5 demonstrated the highest strict success rate at 47.6% (51.5% including suboptimal completions) over 359 rollouts. This policy also led in both single-arm tasks (45.4% strict success) and bimanual tasks (52.1% strict success).

The overall strict success rates for the other evaluated policies were:
- π
0: 35.1% (40.4% with suboptimal)
- GR00T N1.7: 29.4% (33.1% with suboptimal)
- Diffusion Policy: 26.7% (29.7% with suboptimal)
- ACT: 19.2% (21.1% with suboptimal)
- MolmoAct 2: 18.9% (21.7% with suboptimal)
- SmolVLA: 15.0% (19.2% with suboptimal)

**Embodiment-Dependent Performance:**
The relative performance of policies was observed to be embodiment-dependent. For bimanual tasks, Diffusion Policy's performance rose, positioning it second among policies, while ACT's performance decreased significantly to 2.5%. This indicates that performance rankings observed on single-arm tasks may not extrapolate to bimanual scenarios.

**Per-Task Success Rates:** A detailed breakdown of strict success rates per task highlighted varying difficulties and policy strengths:
- **`cable_clip`:** No policy achieved any success on this contact-rich task.
- **High-Performing Tasks/Policies:**
 - `ring_insert`: GR00T N1.7 achieved 60% strict success.
 - `tool_insert`: π0.5 achieved 53% strict success.
 - `tool_removal`: Diffusion Policy achieved 63% strict success.
 - `cable_unclip`: π0.5 achieved 70% strict success.
 - `eye_drops_to_basket`: Several policies performed well, with ACT, π 0, π 0.5, GR00T N1.7, and MolmoAct 2 achieving between 63% and 70% strict success.
 - `eye_drops_to_shelf`: π0 achieved 76% strict success, and π0.5 achieved 70%.
 - `fold_tea_towel` (bimanual): π0.5 achieved 70% strict success.
 - `open_lamp_door` (bimanual): Diffusion Policy achieved 73% strict success.
 - `transfer_cube` (bimanual): π0.5 achieved the highest strict success rate across all tasks at 86%.

The "suboptimal" label was sparingly used, appearing in 89 of 2,518 policy rollouts (3.5%), suggesting that for the majority of evaluations, outcomes were largely categorized as either clean success or failure.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ArmnetBench v0.1 demonstrates a methodology for real-world policy evaluation on a fleet of low-cost SO-101 robotic cells, managed under light on-site supervision. This approach addresses a recognized bottleneck in the development of generalist robot manipulation policies by offering a framework designed to reduce the per-trial cost and increase the scale of real-world evaluation.

The key contributions and their potential impact include:

1. **Scalable Real-World Evaluation Infrastructure:** The Armnet arm farm provides a managed fleet, enabling parallel execution of policy rollouts. This infrastructure can allow researchers to conduct a larger number of real-world trials than typically feasible with single-robot setups, potentially accelerating development cycles and providing more statistically significant results for policy comparisons.
2. **Standardized Benchmark and Protocol:** ArmnetBench v0.1 establishes a 12-task suite and a shared-budget evaluation protocol. By fixing parameters such as the number of training demonstrations (50 per task) and providing the exact policy checkpoints, the benchmark aims to improve the reproducibility and comparability of robot manipulation research. This can foster a more objective environment for assessing advancements in policies.
3. **High-Quality, Graded Evaluation Corpus:** The release of 3,118 core labelled episodes, with a three-way quality scoring (successful, suboptimal, failure), represents a significant dataset for the robotics community. This graded labelling goes beyond typical binary success/failure metrics, providing richer information about policy performance.
4. **Support for Downstream Robot Learning:** The quality-labelled trajectories within the dataset are intended to serve as valuable resources for training and fine-tuning a range of downstream robot learning models. This includes developing more sophisticated reward models, training predictive world models that can learn from varied outcome qualities, and enabling policies to learn effectively from datasets comprising mixed-quality demonstrations. The synchronised observations, actions, and videos can also be utilized for action-conditioned world models and video-generation modules within robot policies.
5. **Baseline for Policy Comparison:** The presented leaderboard provides an initial comparative assessment of seven diverse policies under a shared data budget. This baseline can serve as a reference point for future research, enabling new policies to be evaluated against established methods within a consistent framework.

**Acknowledged Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations that inform future work and contextualize the current findings:
- **Reproducibility:** While execution reproducibility is strong due to released container images and policy checkpoints, cross-cell physical reproducibility remains untested, as each task ran on a single cell. This leaves open questions about the consistency of results across nominally identical hardware setups.
- **Uncontrolled Factors:** Manual reset errors (leading to two dropped episodes), unmeasured initial-state variation (preventing spatial success analysis), and unstandardized rollout durations (relying on operator judgment for termination) introduce variability that could affect comparability.
- **Environmental and Object Changes:** Ambient office lighting is not calibrated, and objects experienced wear and tear during evaluation, potentially altering task difficulty over time. Physical cell changes, such as camera misalignment or blur on specific cells, could also have impacted observed success rates.

Future work explicitly targets addressing these limitations by aiming to reduce operator time through automated scene resets, logging initial object positions for precise state verification, and using reward models to automate or assist scoring. Additional goals include expanding the task suite, standardizing lighting with per-cell lightboxes, implementing camera-framing checks, and conducting cross-cell policy evaluations to empirically measure reproducibility. These planned improvements suggest the ArmnetBench framework is a dynamic initiative with the potential to evolve into an even more robust and comprehensive platform for real-world robot manipulation research.
