# Rectified LpJEPA: Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures with Sparse and Maximum-Entropy Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> Rectified LpJEPA is introduced as a method for Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures to learn sparse and non-negative representations. This approach leverages Rectified Distribution Matching Regularization and Rectified Generalized Gaussian distributions, achieving competitive downstream performance on image classification while maintaining explicit and analytical control over representation sparsity.

## Problem

- Existing Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) primarily employ regularization strategies that favor dense, isotropic Gaussian representations, thereby limiting the diversity of learnable feature structures.
- Integrating the benefits of sparse and non-negative codes, observed in neuroscience and certain deep learning contexts for efficiency and interpretability, into JEPA frameworks with analytical control was a standing challenge.
- Preventing representational collapse in self-supervised learning requires robust regularization beyond mere invariance, with current methods often not explicitly encouraging sparsity.

## Method

- The authors propose Rectified **LpJEPA**, which integrates Rectified Distribution Matching Regularization (RDMReg) into the JEPA framework to induce sparsity and non-negativity in learned features.
- RDMReg aligns rectified feature representations (output of `ReLU(z)`) with a novel family of target distributions called Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGG) distributions, which are constructed to model rectified random variables and explicitly feature a probability mass at zero.
- The method employs the sliced 2-Wasserstein distance on one-dimensional projections (via the Cramér–Wold device) to enforce distribution matching, while the RGG parameters (location μ and shape p) provide analytical control over the expected L0 norm (sparsity) of the representations.

## Results

- Rectified LpJEPA consistently achieved controllable sparsity, with empirical L0 norms closely tracking theoretical predictions across variations in RGG parameters and different backbone architectures.
- The method attained competitive linear probe accuracy on ImageNet-100 and ImageNet-1K (100 epochs) compared to both dense and sparse baselines, while producing significantly sparser representations.
- Learned representations exhibited improved statistical independence (lower normalized Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion values) and high R´enyi information dimension entropy, indicating efficient and robust coding, along with strong transferability across various downstream datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGG) distributions can serve as maximum entropy distributions under expected L_p norm constraints on positive values, providing a principled theoretical target for inducing sparse and non-negative codes.
> - Applying rectification directly to the features (`ReLU(z)`) before regularization is critical for achieving a favorable sparsity-performance tradeoff, as direct matching to the RGG via the continuous mapping theorem is more effective than post-hoc rectification.
> - The framework provides analytical control over the sparsity level of learned representations, demonstrating that parameters of the target RGG distribution directly correlate with the empirical L0 norm.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of self-supervised representation learning, a paradigm aimed at enabling machine intelligence to learn informative data representations without explicit human-provided labels. A prominent category within this field is Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs), which learn representations by enforcing consistency between multiple, augmented views of the same underlying data in a latent space. JEPAs operate by decoupling learning from observation-level reconstruction or density estimation, allowing for flexibility in encoding task-relevant information.

A known challenge in JEPAs is representational collapse, where models learn trivial or low-rank representations if invariance alone is enforced. Recent advancements, such as LeJEPA (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025), address this by employing distribution-matching techniques. LeJEPA introduces the Sliced Isotropic Gaussian Regularization (SIGReg) loss, which aligns one-dimensional projected feature marginals to a univariate Gaussian distribution. This regularization encourages the full representation to approximate an isotropic Gaussian distribution, which is characterized by maximum entropy under expected L2 norm constraints. This approach extends beyond second-order regularization methods, like VICReg (Bardes et al., 2022), by penalizing higher-order distributional discrepancies.

However, a limitation of current Gaussian-based regularization approaches is their inherent tendency to favor dense representations. This restriction limits the range of expressible representational structures and contrasts with observations across various scientific disciplines. In neuroscience, signal processing, and certain deep learning contexts, sparse and non-negative codes are frequently identified as efficient and interpretable forms of representation (Olshausen & Field, 1996; Donoho, 2006; Lee & Seung, 1999; Glorot et al., 2011).

This research addresses this gap by seeking to integrate the benefits of sparse and non-negative representations into the JEPA framework. It aims to develop a regularization method that not only prevents collapse but also explicitly encourages sparsity, thereby generalizing existing dense-representation JEPAs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel methodology, Rectified LpJEPA, that enables Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) to learn sparse and non-negative representations. This objective is driven by several motivations:

1. **Addressing Limitations of Existing JEPA Regularization:** Current JEPA regularization strategies, particularly those based on isotropic Gaussian distributions (like in LeJEPA), inherently favor dense representations. While effective at preventing collapse, these methods limit the diversity of representational structures that can be learned.
2. **Biological and Computational Efficiency of Sparsity:** Across various scientific fields, including neuroscience, signal processing, and deep learning, sparse and non-negative codes have been recognized for their efficiency and interpretability. For instance, neural systems are believed to encode sensory inputs into sparse, non-negative activations, indicating a fundamental organizing principle for efficient information processing. L1 regularization in sparse coding and non-negative matrix factorization in signal processing similarly leverage sparsity and non-negativity for robust and interpretable decompositions. In deep learning, rectified non-linearities (e.g., ReLU) induce non-negativity and support sparsity, contributing to the success of modern neural networks.
3. **Desire for Controllable Sparsity:** The researchers aim to develop a method that provides explicit and analytical control over the degree of sparsity in learned representations. This control, particularly over the expected L0 norm, is sought to enable a systematic exploration of sparsity-performance tradeoffs.
4. **Preservation of Task-Relevant Information:** While enforcing sparsity, a critical motivation is to ensure that the learned representations retain sufficient task-relevant information, preventing a loss of discriminative power for downstream tasks. The goal is to achieve sparsity as a principled inductive bias rather than a heuristic constraint that might degrade performance.
5. **Generalization of Existing JEPAs:** The proposed approach seeks to strictly generalize prior Gaussian-based JEPAs, positioning them as specific instances within a broader family of regularization techniques. This aims to provide a unified framework for learning various representational structures, including sparse ones.

In summary, the motivation stems from the observed benefits of sparse, non-negative representations, the limitations of existing JEPA regularization in achieving them, and the desire to create a principled, controllable mechanism for inducing such properties while maintaining high-quality representations for machine learning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology involves introducing **Rectified Distribution Matching Regularization (RDMReg)** into Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs). This regularization aligns rectified feature representations with a novel family of target distributions called **Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGG) distributions**.

#### 3.1. Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGG) Distributions

The RGG distribution is central to inducing sparsity and non-negativity. It is developed in stages:

- **Generalized Gaussian Distribution (GNp(μ, σ)):** This is a continuous probability distribution defined by parameters μ (location), σ (scale), and p (shape, L_p norm parameter). It generalizes the Laplace distribution (p=1) and the Gaussian distribution (p=2). Its probability density function (PDF) allows for varying tail behaviors.
- **Truncated Generalized Gaussian Distribution (TGNp(μ, σ, S)):** This is a restriction of the GNp distribution to a specific support set S (e.g., (0, ∞)). The authors demonstrate that the multivariate version of TGNp (product of i.i.d. TGNp random variables) is the maximum differential entropy distribution under expected L_p norm constraints over its support (0, ∞)^d. This property ensures that the continuous components of the target distribution are as "random" as possible under the given constraints, promoting diversity.
- **Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGNp(μ, σ)):** This is the final target distribution, constructed as a mixture of a discrete Dirac measure δ₀ at zero and a Truncated Generalized Gaussian distribution TGNp(μ, σ, (0, ∞)). This mixture allows for a concentrated probability mass at zero, which explicitly induces sparsity. The RGNp distribution effectively models rectified random variables (i.e., `ReLU(X)` where `X ~ GNp`).
 - **Sparsity Control:** The expected L0 norm of a multivariate RGNp distribution is analytically derived as `d * Φ_GNp(0,1)(μ/σ)`. This equation demonstrates that the degree of sparsity (fraction of zero entries) can be explicitly controlled by tuning the location parameter μ and the shape parameter p (and indirectly by σ).
 - **Entropy Characterization:** Due to the discrete component (Dirac measure) at zero, the RGG distribution is a mixed measure (Lebesgue + Dirac), making standard differential entropy ill-defined. The authors employ R´enyi information dimension d(ξ) and d(ξ)-dimensional entropy to quantify uncertainty. They show that rectification preserves the maximal entropy property of the original distribution, up to scaling by the R´enyi information dimension and constant offsets.

#### 3.2. Rectified LpJEPA Architecture and Loss Function

The proposed **Rectified LpJEPA** architecture integrates the RDMReg loss with the standard JEPA framework:

1. **Encoder-Projector Design:** Two augmented views (x, x') of an input are processed by an encoder (e.g., ResNet, ViT) followed by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) projector.
2. **Rectification:** A ReLU nonlinearity is applied to the output of the projector, `ReLU(z)` and `ReLU(z')`, ensuring non-negativity and inducing sparsity in the learned representations.
3. **Loss Components:** The overall self-supervised learning objective consists of two parts:
 - **Invariance Term:** `min_θ E_z,z' [||ReLU(z) - ReLU(z')||^2_2]`. This term encourages the rectified representations of different views of the same data to be similar, promoting view-invariance.
 - **Rectified Distribution Matching Regularization (RDMReg):** This regularization term enforces that the distribution of the rectified features `ReLU(z)` (and `ReLU(z')`) matches the target RGG distribution.
 - **Cram´er–Wold Device:** High-dimensional distribution matching is approximated by matching one-dimensional linear projections. Random projection vectors `c` are sampled uniformly from the L2 sphere.
 - **Necessity of Two-Sample Testing:** Unlike Gaussian distributions, the RGG family is not closed under linear projections. This means that projecting an RGG-distributed variable does not necessarily yield another RGG distribution. Therefore, the RDMReg uses a two-sample hypothesis test on the projected marginals to compare the feature distribution with the target RGG distribution.
 - **Sliced 2-Wasserstein Distance:** The specific distribution-matching loss chosen is the sliced 2-Wasserstein distance, which measures the distance between the sorted empirical cumulative distribution functions of the projected feature samples and the projected RGG target samples.
 - **Connection to Non-Negative VCReg:** The authors establish a theoretical link, showing that if the projected feature distribution matches the RGG target along the eigenvectors of the centered feature covariance matrix, then the centered covariance becomes isotropic. This implies that RDMReg implicitly encourages a form of non-negative covariance regularization, similar to VICReg but applied to rectified features.

#### 3.3. Hyperparameter Choices for the Target Distribution

The parameters {μ, σ, p} of the RGG target distribution collectively determine the L0 sparsity. Two strategies for selecting σ are explored: `σ_GN` (which ensures the variance of the pre-rectified Generalized Gaussian is 1) and `σ_RGN` (which ensures the variance of the post-rectified RGG is 1). `σ_GN` is adopted as the default, as it simplifies theoretical analysis and empirically yields favorable sparsity-performance tradeoffs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Rectified LpJEPA on image classification benchmarks yielded several findings:

1. **Necessity of Rectification:** Comparisons with ablations demonstrated that applying rectification directly to the learned features (`z+ = ReLU(z)`) and regularizing them towards Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGG) distributions (termed `RGNp | z+`) achieved the most favorable sparsity-performance tradeoffs. Configurations without feature rectification or with non-rectified target distributions resulted in dense representations or substantial performance drops, respectively. The continuous mapping theorem further supported that direct matching to the RGG is more effective than post-hoc rectification of features matched to a Generalized Gaussian.
2. **Controllable Sparsity:** Rectified LpJEPA exhibited controllable sparsity, with the empirical L0 norms of learned features closely tracking the theoretical predictions derived from Proposition 3.5. This consistency was observed across variations in the mean shift parameter μ and the shape parameter p, and across different backbone architectures (ResNet, ViT, ConvNeXt). This demonstrates that the RGG parameters provide explicit control over the sparsity level.
3. **Sparsity and Performance Tradeoff:** The relationship between sparsity and downstream performance was analyzed, revealing a Pareto frontier. Performance decayed gradually as the number of zero entries in the representations increased. A sharp decline in performance was observed only when approximately 95% of the feature entries were zero, suggesting that substantial sparsity can be achieved without severely impacting task-relevant information.
4. **Pair-wise Independence:** Rectified LpJEPA consistently achieved lower normalized Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion (nHSIC) values compared to baselines like VICReg and Non-Negative VICReg. This indicates that the method encourages representations to be closer to statistically independent, going beyond merely reducing second-order correlations.
5. **R´enyi Information Dimension and Entropy:** The analysis of d(ξ)-dimensional entropy showed a clear Pareto frontier between entropy and sparsity in the learned representations. Given the lower nHSIC values, the sum of marginal d(ξ)-dimensional entropies for Rectified LpJEPA is inferred to be a more accurate approximation of joint entropy, implying that the method learns high-entropy features that are also sparse.
6. **Pretraining and Transfer Evaluations:**
 - **ImageNet-100:** Rectified LpJEPA achieved competitive downstream linear probe accuracy on ImageNet-100 compared to both dense (SimCLR, VICReg, LeJEPA) and sparse (NCL-ReLU, NVICReg-ReLU) baselines, while simultaneously learning sparser representations.
 - **ImageNet-1K (100 Epochs):** In a larger-scale, shorter training regime, Rectified LpJEPA with mixed random and eigenvector projections performed comparably to or slightly better than VICReg in encoder accuracy and significantly improved projector accuracy. This suggests that incorporating eigenvector-based projections can accelerate the reduction of second-order dependencies.
 - **Transferability:** The method demonstrated strong transferability, achieving competitive accuracy in both few-shot and full-shot linear probing across a range of downstream datasets (e.g., DTD, CIFAR-10, Flowers-102, Food-101, Oxford-IIIT Pets).
 - **Dataset-Adaptive Sparsity:** Pretrained Rectified LpJEPA representations exhibited distinct sparsity patterns across different out-of-distribution datasets, suggesting that sparsity statistics could serve as a proxy for distinguishing in-distribution from OOD inputs.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** The Rectified Distribution Matching Regularization (RDMReg) was found to be only marginally slower than SIGReg (used in LeJEPA) in wall-clock time and utilized comparable GPU memory, indicating its practical applicability.
7. **Qualitative Analysis (k-Nearest Neighbors & Attribution Maps):**
 - **Semantic Coherence:** Even in highly sparse regimes, Rectified LpJEPA consistently retrieved semantically coherent nearest neighbors for unambiguous images.
 - **Information Preservation:** For images with multiple objects (e.g., a cat on a laptop), extremely sparse representations could cause the model to focus predominantly on one object (e.g., the laptop). However, when the distracting background was removed by cropping, the model's focus shifted to the primary object (the cat), demonstrating that information was preserved rather than lost, and that the representations were adapting to salient features in the scene. Attribution maps supported these observations by showing shifts in spatial attention corresponding to neighbor retrieval patterns under varying sparsity levels.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents Rectified LpJEPA as a novel approach within self-supervised representation learning, offering several contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Principled Sparsity Induction:** The introduction of Rectified Distribution Matching Regularization (RDMReg) with Rectified Generalized Gaussian (RGG) distributions provides a principled and theoretically grounded method for inducing sparsity in learned representations. Unlike heuristic sparsity methods, this approach offers analytical control over the expected L0 norm, linking regularization parameters directly to the degree of sparsity.
2. **Generalization of JEPAs:** Rectified LpJEPA strictly generalizes prior Gaussian-based JEPAs, such as LeJEPA, by incorporating a richer family of target distributions. This expansion broadens the scope of representational structures that JEPAs can learn, extending their applicability beyond dense, isotropic Gaussian embeddings.
3. **Improved Representational Properties:** The research demonstrates that Rectified LpJEPA can learn sparse, non-negative representations that not only achieve competitive downstream performance but also exhibit improved statistical independence (lower nHSIC) and high entropy (in terms of R´enyi information dimension). These properties are often associated with efficient and robust coding in biological and computational systems.
4. **Enhanced Interpretability and Efficiency:** Sparse representations are generally considered more interpretable as fewer features are active. This could lead to a better understanding of what models learn. Additionally, sparsity can contribute to computational efficiency by potentially enabling more compact storage and faster inference for downstream tasks, especially if sparse-aware hardware or algorithms are utilized.
5. **New Avenues for JEPA Research:** By demonstrating that sparsity can be achieved through target distribution design while preserving task-relevant information, this work opens up new directions for fundamental research on JEPA regularizers. Future studies could explore other target distributions, alternative metrics for sparsity, and the interplay between sparsity, disentanglement, and robustness in self-supervised learning.
6. **Potential for Out-of-Distribution Detection:** The observed dataset-adaptive sparsity patterns suggest that sparsity statistics could potentially serve as a lightweight proxy for distinguishing in-distribution training data from out-of-distribution inputs, which is a critical area in robust AI.

Overall, Rectified LpJEPA advances the understanding and capability of self-supervised learning by integrating a crucial aspect of efficient coding—sparsity—into a prominent architectural framework, potentially paving the way for more efficient, interpretable, and biologically plausible machine learning models.
