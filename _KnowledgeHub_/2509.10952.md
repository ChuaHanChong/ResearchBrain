# ImMimic: Cross-Domain Imitation from Human Videos via Mapping and Interpolation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology developed ImMimic, a framework for teaching robots complex manipulation skills from human videos by adapting both visual and action spaces. It utilizes Dynamic Time Warping and MixUp-based interpolation to bridge the human-robot domain gap, resulting in higher task success rates and smoother robot trajectories across various end-effectors compared to prior methods.

## Problem

- A substantial "domain gap" exists between human videos and robot execution (visual appearance, embodiment, physics, action spaces), hindering direct skill transfer.
- Existing robot learning methods from human videos often simplify tasks, require extensive preprocessing, or fail to effectively leverage human action information for robust adaptation.
- The high cost and time associated with collecting robot-specific demonstration data limit the scalability and generalizability of robot skill acquisition.

## Method

- An embodiment-agnostic ==co-training== framework, built on a ==Diffusion Policy==, that jointly optimizes policies using both human video demonstrations and limited robot data.
- A ==hand pose retargeting== system that converts human hand movements into robot-compatible action commands through an optimization problem.
- A ==mapping-guided MixUp== strategy that employs ==Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)== for precise human-robot trajectory alignment and progressive interpolation to create a continuum of intermediate domains.

## Results

- **ImMimic**, particularly with action-based mapping, achieved consistently higher success rates across four manipulation tasks and four diverse robot embodiments, reaching **100%** in tasks like Pick and Place.
- Robot trajectories generated by **ImMimic** demonstrated improved smoothness, indicated by higher **Spectral Arc Length (SPARC)** scores, compared to robot-only policies and vanilla co-training baselines.
- The method exhibited superior sample efficiency, achieving high success rates (e.g., **1.0 SR**) with significantly fewer robot demonstrations (e.g., **5**) when augmented with human videos, compared to robot-only approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Retargeted human actions provide a more robust and informative signal for bridging the human-robot domain gap than purely visual features, leading to superior performance with action-based mapping.
> - A progressive interpolation strategy within MixUp creates a smoother "domain flow" for adaptation, enabling more stable and effective learning than static mixing.
> - Effective human-to-robot transfer is influenced not only by "human-mimetic" end-effector design but also by factors like arm kinematics and mounting conditions, which significantly impact the effective action distance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly moving towards leveraging large-scale, readily available data sources to overcome the notorious challenges of data collection in robotics. Human videos, depicting everyday manipulation activities, represent a vast and largely untapped reservoir of supervisory signals for robot skill acquisition. However, directly transferring skills from human videos to robots is hampered by a "domain gap," which encompasses stark differences in visual appearance, embodiment morphology, physical constraints, and action spaces between humans and robots.

Existing approaches to bridge this domain gap in imitation learning from human demonstrations can be broadly categorized:

- **Data Preprocessing and Simplification:** Some methods attempt to mitigate visual differences by masking out embodiments in images or simplify the action space to basic 3D translations to reduce the embodiment-specific action gap. While effective in specific scenarios, these approaches often limit the complexity of skills that can be learned or require significant manual effort.
- **Latent Space Adaptation:** Another line of research focuses on learning domain-invariant representations by adapting the latent spaces of visual inputs from both human and robot domains using unsupervised learning objectives. However, these methods often overlook the crucial role of human actions, instead learning the action decoder solely from robot demonstrations, which can be data-inefficient for skill learning.
- **Two-Stage Methods:** Many works adopt a two-stage approach, where a policy is first pretrained on human data and then fine-tuned with a small amount of robot-specific data. While beneficial, this can lead to a "catastrophic forgetting" problem or suboptimal transfer if the initial human-based policy is not well-aligned with the robot's capabilities.
- **Co-Training:** Recent studies, such as EgoMimic, have shown the benefits of jointly optimizing on both human and robot data in a co-training framework. However, these methods often still rely on heavy visual preprocessing or simplified action spaces, leaving the fundamental domain shift largely unaddressed in a generalized manner.
- **Dexterous Retargeting:** Techniques for translating human hand poses into robot joint poses are essential for bridging the morphological gap. While effective for teleoperation, where continuous human guidance refines the robot's actions, simply retargeting human trajectories to train a policy often exposes a residual "domain gap" between the retargeted commands and actual robot execution due to unaddressed visual, kinematic, and physical disparities.
- **Domain Adaptation Techniques:** Beyond robotics, general machine learning has explored domain adaptation using adversarial feature adaptation, cycle-consistent data translation, or methods that insert intermediate domains (e.g., via MixUp).

ImMimic specifically addresses the limitations of prior co-training and retargeting approaches by proposing an "embodiment-agnostic" framework that deeply integrates domain adaptation. It recognizes that while retargeted human hand trajectories are valuable, they still suffer from the underlying domain gap. Its novelty lies in leveraging these retargeted actions as informative *action labels* within a sophisticated domain adaptation strategy. By applying **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)** for precise human-robot trajectory alignment and a **MixUp-based interpolation** to create a continuum of intermediate domains, ImMimic goes beyond simple visual preprocessing or separate latent space alignment. It simultaneously adapts both the visual observations and the action spaces, creating a smoother transition from the human source domain to the robot target domain within a unified co-training pipeline. This positions ImMimic as a significant advancement in making human videos a truly scalable and effective data source for learning complex, dexterous robot manipulation across diverse hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the ImMimic research is to **enable robots to learn diverse manipulation tasks from abundant human videos, thereby offering a scalable alternative to costly and time-consuming robot-specific data collection.** The core motivation stems from the inherent difficulty and expense associated with gathering high-quality robot demonstration data in real-world environments. Human videos, on the other hand, are plentiful and provide rich examples of complex manipulation.

However, the authors identify a **"substantial domain gap"** as the primary obstacle to effectively leveraging human videos for robot learning. This gap manifests across multiple dimensions:
- **Visual appearance:** Humans and robots look drastically different.
- **Embodiment structure:** The kinematic and morphological differences between human hands/arms and robot end-effectors are significant.
- **Physical constraints:** The unique dynamics and physical interactions of human bodies versus robot hardware.
- **Action space:** The ways humans and robots execute similar actions can vary considerably.

To overcome this fundamental challenge, the paper sets out to achieve several specific objectives:
1. **Develop an embodiment-agnostic co-training framework:** The goal is to create a single framework capable of learning jointly from human demonstration videos and a limited amount of teleoperated robot demonstrations, without being tied to a specific robot morphology.
2. **Utilize retargeted human hand trajectories as informative action labels:** Beyond just visual context, the research aims to extract and leverage the rich action information inherent in human hand movements, transforming them into a format directly usable by the robot. This addresses a common oversight in prior work that often neglected human actions during domain adaptation.
3. **Create intermediate domains for robust adaptation:** The framework seeks to establish a smooth "bridge" between the human (source) and robot (target) domains by interpolating data points, rather than attempting a direct, abrupt mapping. This is inspired by techniques that show improved adaptation via continuous domain transitions.
4. **Establish an effective mapping between human and robot data for interpolation:** To ensure meaningful interpolation, a precise alignment between corresponding human and robot trajectories is crucial. This objective leads to the exploration of different mapping strategies, specifically focusing on action- and visual-based similarities.

By achieving these objectives, ImMimic aims to provide a more generalizable, robust, and data-efficient method for robot skill learning from heterogeneous data sources, making robot manipulation more accessible and scalable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ImMimic proposes an "embodiment-agnostic co-training framework" that intelligently integrates human videos with a small set of robot demonstrations. The framework is built upon a Diffusion Policy backbone and comprises three main technical components: Hand Pose Retargeting, Co-Training, and Mapping-guided MixUp.

#### 3.1. Hand Pose Retargeting System

To make human actions interpretable for robots, human hand movements from videos are first converted into the robot's action space.
- **Hand and Wrist Pose Estimation:** For human videos, MediaPipe is used to detect and crop human hands. The cropped regions are then fed into FrankMocap, which estimates precise 3D positions of 21 hand joints within the local wrist frame. The 6D pose of the wrist in the camera frame is recovered by projecting these joints into a depth map and solving a Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem. For real-time teleoperation, a more stable depth-based wrist estimation and vector retargeting (aligning finger segment orientations) is used (Appendix B).
- **Retargeting:** The estimated human keypoints are mapped to robot joint angles via an optimization problem. This process minimizes the squared difference between the human keypoint positions and the robot's forward kinematics outputs, while also penalizing large changes in robot joint angles to ensure temporal smoothness (Equation 1). This ensures that human demonstrations are translated into a format compatible with robot control.

#### 3.2. Co-Training

ImMimic adopts a co-training strategy, building on the Diffusion Policy, to jointly optimize the policy using both human and robot data.
- **Policy Backbone:** The framework uses a Diffusion Policy, known for its ability to predict complex action sequences.
- **Robot Prediction Loss ($L_{robot}$):** For robot demonstrations, the policy learns to predict future actions based on an observation history containing agent-view images, wrist-view images (processed by separate ResNet encoders $f_\theta^a, f_\theta^w$), and proprioception (end-effector pose, finger joint positions). The loss minimizes the $\ell_2$ difference between predicted and actual robot actions.
- **Human Prediction Loss ($L_{human}$):** For human videos, the policy takes agent-view images (processed by the same $f_\theta^a$) and the *retargeted human actions* ($a_t^{h \to r}$) as input for its condition. It then predicts future retargeted actions, minimizing an $\ell_2$ loss. Notably, the retargeted human actions serve as "action labels" and as a proxy for proprioception for human data.
- **Co-training Loss:** The total loss is a sum of $L_{robot}$ and $L_{human}$, with an equal proportion of human and robot data in each training batch. This ensures the policy continuously learns from both domains.

#### 3.3. Mapping-guided MixUp

This is the core domain adaptation mechanism. It creates intermediate domains by interpolating between human and robot data, guided by a precise temporal mapping.
- **Mapping with Dynamic Time Warping (DTW):** To establish correspondences between human and robot demonstration *sequences*, DTW is employed. DTW aligns sequences with varying temporal dynamics, finding the best matching paths. Two strategies are explored for defining the distance metric for DTW:
 - **Action-based Mapping:** The distance between a retargeted human demonstration and a robot demonstration is calculated as a weighted sum of differences in translation, hand pose, and orientation. This focuses on kinematic and action similarity.
 - **Visual-based Mapping:** The distance is computed based on the L2 norm of visual features extracted from a pretrained encoder, aligning frames based on visual appearance.
 - The DTW mapping $M_{h \to r}(t)$ identifies robot timesteps that best correspond to a given human timestep $t$.
- **MixUp-based Interpolation:** Inspired by MixUp, ImMimic interpolates both the condition inputs and the predicted actions between paired human and robot samples. For each human timestep $t$, a corresponding robot timestep $t'$ (from $M_{h \to r}(t)$) is sampled, and a mixed sample is generated:
 - $z_{mix} = \alpha \cdot z_t^h + (1 - \alpha) \cdot z_{t'}^r$ (interpolated condition input)
 - $a_{mix} = \alpha \cdot a_{t:t+k}^{h \to r} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot a_{t':t'+k}^r$ (interpolated predicted action)
 - A **progressive interpolation strategy** is adopted, where the mixing coefficient $\alpha$ gradually decreases during training (from 1 to 0). This allows for a smoother transition from purely human data to robot-like data, enabling gradual domain adaptation, akin to "domain flow".

#### 3.4. Inference

During testing, actions are predicted at a fixed frequency. An upsample rate $\gamma$ (determined by the ratio of robot to human demonstration durations, Appendix A.2) is applied to predicted actions to match the robot's execution speed. Temporal ensembling is used to average overlapping predictions for smooth, continuous motion.

#### 3.5. Experiment Setup

- **Hardware:** Experiments are conducted on a Franka Emika Panda robot arm equipped with four diverse end-effectors: Robotiq 2F-85 (2-finger gripper), Fin Ray Gripper (2-finger soft gripper), Allegro Hand (4-finger dexterous hand), and Ability Hand (5-finger dexterous hand). This extensive hardware suite allows for robust evaluation of embodiment transfer.
- **Tasks:** Four manipulation tasks are used, ranging from basic (Pick and Place, Push) to tool-based (Hammer, Flip), demanding increasing levels of control dexterity.
- **Baselines:** The method is compared against several baselines, including Robot-only, Two-stage Fine-Tuning, Vanilla Co-Training, and variants of MixUp with different mapping strategies (Random, Visual, Action).
- **Metrics:** Performance is evaluated using Success Rate (SR), Trajectory Smoothness (SPARC), and Action Distance (AD) between human and robot trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of ImMimic across diverse robotic embodiments and manipulation tasks yielded several key findings:

1. **Enhanced Task Success and Smoothness:** ImMimic-A (Action-based mapping) consistently achieved higher success rates across all four tasks and all four robotic embodiments compared to all baselines, including Robot-Only, Two-Stage Fine-Tuning, and Vanilla Co-Training (Table 1, 2). For instance, in Pick and Place, ImMimic-A reached 100% success rate on all embodiments, significantly outperforming baselines. Furthermore, ImMimic demonstrated improved trajectory smoothness, reflected by higher Spectral Arc Length (SPARC) scores, indicating more fluid and controlled robot motions compared to robot-only policies and vanilla co-training (Table 3). This highlights that effectively leveraging human videos through ImMimic's approach leads to more robust and refined robot behaviors.

2. **Superiority of Action-based Mapping:** A crucial finding is that action-based mapping (ImMimic-A) consistently outperformed visual-based mapping (ImMimic-V) and random mapping (Table 2). This suggests that retargeted human *actions* provide a more structurally similar and informative signal for bridging the human-robot domain gap than purely visual features. The authors demonstrate that action-based mapping is more accurate and robust, especially under visual or action disturbances in long-horizon video retrieval tasks (Fig. 6, Fig. C.1). Poor visual mapping, in contrast, can lead to undesirable robot behaviors like motion traps (Fig. 7e). This emphasizes the importance of aligning the underlying motion dynamics and kinematics.

3. **Consistent Improvement Across Embodiments:** ImMimic-A consistently improved policy performance across all tested end-effectors, from simple 2-finger grippers (Robotiq, FR) to complex dexterous hands (Allegro, Ability) (Table 1). This demonstrates the framework's "embodiment-agnostic" nature and its ability to adapt to diverse hardware configurations, though the magnitude of gains varied.

4. **Embodiment Design and Action Distance Insights:**
 - Despite the overall improvements, certain embodiment-task combinations remained challenging (e.g., Hammer with Ability Hand, Flip with Allegro Hand), often due to physical limitations of the robot hardware such as short thumbs, limited wrist flexibility, or insufficient gripping force (Fig. 7c,d,f,g).
 - Counter-intuitively, more "human-mimetic" dexterous hands (Allegro, Ability) did not necessarily result in smaller average action distances to human demonstrations; instead, they showed larger distances compared to the simpler grippers (Table E.3).
 - The study observed a correlation where policies benefited more from human videos when the average action distance between human and robot was smaller, regardless of the end-effector's mechanical structure. This suggests that factors like hand-arm mounting conditions and arm kinematics significantly influence the *effective* action distance and retargeting quality, not just the hand design itself. These observations offer valuable insights for future robot end-effector design.

5. **Importance of Scale and Diversity in Human Demonstrations:** The quantity and diversity of human videos directly impact learning performance and sample efficiency. Increasing the number of human demonstrations significantly boosted success rates, allowing ImMimic-A to achieve high performance with a substantially smaller amount of robot data compared to robot-only baselines (Fig. 4, 5). For example, with 100 human videos, ImMimic-A achieved 1.0 SR with just 5 robot demonstrations, while Robot-Only required 20 demonstrations to achieve lower success rates.

6. **Efficacy of Progressive MixUp:** The progressive interpolation strategy, where the mixing coefficient $\alpha$ linearly decreases during training, was found to be more effective than sampling $\alpha$ from a $\beta$-distribution (Table E.2). This confirms that a controlled, gradual adaptation creates a smoother "domain flow" between human and robot data, as visualized by t-SNE embeddings (Fig. E.3), enabling more stable and effective learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ImMimic framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenge of learning from human demonstrations. Its potential impact is multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Scalability of Robot Skill Acquisition:** By providing a robust method to leverage abundant, readily available human video data, ImMimic drastically reduces the reliance on expensive and laborious robot-specific data collection. This is a critical step towards democratizing robot skill acquisition, making it feasible for a wider range of tasks and users. It allows for rapid prototyping and deployment of new robot skills without extensive expert teleoperation.

2. **Robustness and Generalization Across Embodiments:** The framework's demonstrated ability to consistently improve task success rates and execution smoothness across four distinct robotic end-effectors, ranging from simple grippers to complex dexterous hands, is highly significant. This "embodiment-agnostic" quality is crucial for building adaptable robotic systems that can operate in diverse environments with varying hardware, moving beyond single-robot, single-task solutions.

3. **Refined Understanding of Human-to-Robot Transfer:** The key insight that retargeted human *actions* provide more informative and robust supervisory signals for domain adaptation than visual features alone is a profound contribution. This shifts the focus from purely visual alignment to kinematic and dynamic correspondence, offering a more principled way to bridge the action gap. This finding guides future research to prioritize action-centric understanding in imitation learning from human data.

4. **Guidance for Future Robot Hardware Design:** The empirical observations regarding the nuanced relationship between embodiment design, effective action distance, and policy performance offer valuable feedback for hardware engineers. The finding that a "human-mimetic" appearance doesn't automatically translate to better transfer, and that factors like arm kinematics and mounting configurations are equally critical, can inform the development of more functionally adaptable and efficient robotic end-effectors and integrated arm-hand systems. Suggestions like increased thumb length, additional wrist degrees of freedom, and adaptive grasping mechanisms directly emerge from this research.

5. **Advancement in Domain Adaptation for Robotics:** ImMimic's novel integration of DTW-based mapping with progressive MixUp interpolation provides a powerful, continuous domain adaptation mechanism. This technique creates a smooth manifold between source (human) and target (robot) domains, preventing abrupt shifts and enabling more effective knowledge transfer. This contributes new methodological insights to the broader domain adaptation literature within machine learning.

6. **Broader Impact on AI and Robotics Applications:** By enabling robots to more effectively acquire complex manipulation skills from human demonstrations, ImMimic can accelerate progress in various application domains. This includes household robots performing daily chores, industrial robots executing intricate assembly tasks, and assistive robots helping individuals with disabilities. The ability to learn from "watching humans" brings us closer to truly intelligent and autonomous robotic agents that can integrate seamlessly into human environments.

In conclusion, ImMimic is a timely and impactful contribution that tackles a central problem in robot learning with an elegant and effective solution. Its focus on systematic domain adaptation of both visual and action spaces, validated by extensive real-world experiments, positions it as a foundational work for future research in scalable and generalizable robot skill learning from human observation.
