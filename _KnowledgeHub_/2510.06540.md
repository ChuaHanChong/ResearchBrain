# Scalable Policy-Based RL Algorithms for POMDPs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign developed a framework for solving Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) by approximating them as finite-state "Superstate MDPs" using truncated histories, applying standard policy optimization with linear function approximation. The work provides tighter theoretical guarantees, demonstrating an exponential decay of approximation error with history length and establishing finite-time performance bounds for standard Temporal Difference learning without requiring computationally intensive m-step methods.

## Problem

- Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) are computationally intractable due to their continuous belief state space, making exact solutions infeasible for most real-world applications.
- Heuristic methods for solving POMDPs, while empirically successful, often lack robust theoretical performance guarantees and convergence proofs.
- Existing approximations of POMDPs as finite-state MDPs struggled with providing convergence guarantees for standard Temporal Difference (TD) learning, required computationally expensive m-step TD, or lacked finite-time bounds for linear function approximation.

## Method

- Approximating POMDPs as finite-state "==Superstate MDPs==" by representing states as `l`-length histories of action-observation pairs, thereby discretizing the state space.
- Applying a policy optimization algorithm that utilizes standard Temporal Difference (TD) learning with linear function approximation to evaluate policies within the constructed Superstate MDP.
- Establishing theoretical guarantees, including a uniform filter stability condition and a novel algebraic identity, to justify the convergence of standard TD learning and bound approximation errors.

## Results

- Tighter theoretical bounds were derived, quantifying the exponential decay of the optimal value function difference between the original POMDP and its Superstate MDP approximation with increasing history length `l`.
- Finite-time performance bounds were established for the policy optimization algorithm using linear function approximation, enhancing scalability and providing stronger guarantees compared to asymptotic methods.
- Convergence guarantees for standard Temporal Difference (TD) learning in the policy evaluation step were proven, enabling efficient policy optimization that shows improved performance with longer histories on a FrozenLake environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The uniform filter stability condition is crucial for demonstrating that belief states corresponding to identical finite histories remain close, enabling the Superstate MDP approximation.
> - A newly introduced algebraic identity provides a more refined method for bounding sums of product differences, leading to tighter theoretical guarantees for approximation errors.
> - Standard Temporal Difference (TD) learning can effectively evaluate policies within Superstate MDPs despite a mismatch between the true POMDP sampling and the approximate MDP updates, without requiring computationally expensive modified TD methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides a framework for sequential decision-making, typically modeled as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). However, many real-world scenarios involve partial observability, where the agent does not have direct access to the true underlying state of the environment. These problems are modeled as Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs). Examples span robotic control, autonomous driving, personalized recommendations, and medical diagnosis.

Solving POMDPs presents significant computational challenges. The "belief state" formulation, which transforms a POMDP into a fully observable MDP where states are continuous probability distributions over the original hidden states, resolves the partial observability but introduces a continuous state space, making exact solutions PSPACE-complete and computationally intractable.

To address this intractability, various approximate approaches have been explored. These often involve heuristic methods that base decisions on limited information, such as current observations only or a finite history of past observations and actions. While these methods demonstrate empirical success, they frequently lack rigorous theoretical performance guarantees.

In contrast, fully observable MDPs benefit from a rich body of RL algorithms (e.g., Q-learning, actor-critic, policy gradient) with established sample complexity and convergence bounds. The present work seeks to bridge this gap by investigating whether standard RL algorithms, proven effective for MDPs, can be adapted to approximately solve POMDPs by treating finite histories as MDP states, and importantly, by establishing theoretical performance bounds for such an adaptation.

Prior research has also explored approximating POMDPs as finite-state MDPs by using finite memory or approximate information states. For instance, Kara and Yüksel (2023) and Cayci et al. (2024) have considered similar transformations to "Superstate MDPs." However, these approaches often faced limitations, such as difficulties in proving convergence for Q-learning with linear function approximation, reliance on strong ergodicity assumptions, or the need for computationally more expensive m-step Temporal Difference (TD) learning for policy evaluation. This paper differentiates itself by aiming for tighter theoretical guarantees, supporting standard TD learning, extending to linear function approximation, and providing finite-time performance bounds for policy optimization in this context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop scalable policy-based reinforcement learning algorithms that can approximately solve Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) by transforming them into finite-state Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), specifically referred to as "Superstate MDPs," and to provide rigorous theoretical guarantees for this approach.

This objective is motivated by several key challenges and limitations in the existing literature:

1. **Computational Intractability of POMDPs:** Exact solutions for POMDPs are computationally prohibitive due to the continuous nature of belief states and the PSPACE-completeness of the problem. This necessitates approximate methods for practical applications.
2. **Lack of Theoretical Guarantees for Heuristic Approaches:** While empirical methods based on finite histories or memory-based controllers have shown promise in handling partial observability, many of these approaches lack formal theoretical guarantees regarding their performance or convergence properties.
3. **Leveraging Standard RL Algorithms:** The success and theoretical backing of standard RL algorithms for fully observable MDPs motivate the question of whether these established techniques can be effectively applied to POMDPs through an appropriate approximation, and whether such an application can be accompanied by robust theoretical performance bounds.
4. **Limitations of Existing Superstate MDP Approximations:**
- **Unclear Convergence and Performance of Standard TD Learning:** Previous work on approximating POMDPs as Superstate MDPs has not fully clarified whether standard Temporal Difference (TD) learning, used for policy evaluation, would converge or perform well when the underlying true dynamics are POMDPs.
- **Computational Expense of Modified TD Learning:** Some prior approaches, such as Cayci et al. (2024), address the challenge of sampling mismatch (where learning samples originate from the true POMDP but target the Superstate MDP) by employing computationally more expensive m-step TD learning. This work aims to establish convergence guarantees for standard TD learning, thereby avoiding this additional overhead.
- **Scalability Challenges with Function Approximation:** Q-learning approaches in prior work (e.g., Kara and Yüksel, 2023) faced difficulties in proving convergence with linear function approximation, limiting their scalability to large state spaces. Additionally, they often relied on restrictive ergodicity assumptions and provided only asymptotic convergence guarantees. This research aims to develop finite-time performance bounds for policy optimization with linear function approximation, enabling scalability and offering stronger guarantees.
5. **Need for Tighter Approximation Guarantees:** Existing theoretical bounds on the difference between the optimal value functions of the original POMDP and its Superstate MDP approximation (e.g., in Kara and Yüksel, 2023; Abel et al., 2016; Subramanian and Mahajan, 2019; Cayci et al., 2024) were relatively loose. A motivation is to derive tighter, worst-case bounds that more accurately quantify the approximation error, especially the exponential decay of error with history length.
6. **Development of General Algebraic Tools:** The need for improved bounding techniques motivates the introduction of a novel algebraic identity that can be broadly applied to refine bounds in various analyses, including those beyond the immediate scope of this paper.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this research involves approximating a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) into a finite-state Markov Decision Process (MDP), termed a "Superstate MDP," and then applying a scalable policy-based reinforcement learning algorithm with linear function approximation to solve this Superstate MDP.

#### 3.1. Superstate MDP Approximation

1. **Finite History Representation:** The continuous belief state space of the POMDP is addressed by replacing the true belief state with a "Superstate," which is defined as a finite history of the last `l` action-observation pairs. A "grouping operator" `G(H_t)` truncates any full history `H_t` to its `l`-length suffix. This transforms the infinite-state POMDP into a finite-state Superstate MDP, whose states are these `l`-length histories.
2. **Defining Superstate MDP Dynamics:** For any Superstate `B` and action `a`, the Superstate MDP defines:
- An approximate reward `~r(B, a)` as the expected reward given the belief state `π(s|B)`.
- Approximate transition probabilities `~P(B' | B, a)` to a next Superstate `B'`, which are calculated by marginalizing over all possible actual observations `y` and next hidden states `s'`, considering `G(B ∥ {y, a}) = B'`.
3. **Theoretical Guarantees for Approximation:**
- **Uniform Filter Stability Condition (Assumption 1):** This crucial assumption states that any new observation sufficiently informs the agent to reduce differences between any two prior beliefs, ensuring the belief state "forgets" initial uncertainty. It is quantified by a contraction factor `ρ ∈ (0, 1)`. This condition is necessary to relate belief states to their corresponding superstates.
- **Belief State Convergence (Lemma 1):** Leveraging Assumption 1, the authors prove that belief states corresponding to the same Superstate (i.e., having identical `l`-length histories) are close in total variation distance. The distance decreases exponentially with `l`.
- **Novel Algebraic Identity (Lemma 2):** A general algebraic inequality is introduced to bound differences of sums of products of vectors. This identity provides tighter bounds than traditional methods based on decomposition and triangle inequality.
- **Optimality Gap (Theorem 2):** Using Lemma 1 and Lemma 2, the authors derive a theoretical guarantee that quantifies the difference between the optimal value function of the original POMDP and the optimal value function of the Superstate MDP. This difference is shown to decay exponentially with the history length `l`, providing a tighter bound than prior work.

#### 3.2. Policy Optimization Algorithm for Superstate MDP

To learn the optimal policy for the constructed Superstate MDP, the research proposes a Policy Optimization algorithm (Algorithm 2) that integrates standard Temporal Difference (TD) learning with linear function approximation for policy evaluation.

1. **Linear Function Approximation:** The Q-function `Q(B, a)` for the Superstate MDP is approximated linearly as `ϕ^T(B, a)θ`, where `ϕ(B, a)` is a feature vector for a Superstate-action pair, and `θ` is a parameter vector constrained within a ball of finite radius `R`.
2. **Approximate TD Learning (Algorithm 1 - Inner Loop):** For a fixed policy `μ`, the algorithm uses TD(0) to learn the Q-function parameters `θ`.
- The agent interacts with the *true* POMDP, generating sequences of `(s_t, a_t, r_t, y_{t+1})`.
- However, the TD updates are performed on the Superstates `G(H_t)` and `G(H_{t+1})`. The update rule for `θ` is: `θ_{t+1/2} = θ_t + ϵ_t [r_t + γϕ^T(G(H_{t+1}), a_{t+1})θ_t − ϕ^T(G(H_t), a_t)θ_t] ⋅ ϕ(G(H_t), a_t)`.
- A "burn-in" period `l'` is introduced, meaning updates only occur after `t ≥ l'`, to allow the history to become sufficiently long and belief states to contract.
- The critical aspect is that this standard TD learning operates despite a "sampling mismatch" (samples are from POMDP, updates for MDP), which is justified by the proven closeness of belief states corresponding to the same superstate.
3. **Policy Update (Outer Loop):** The policy `μ_i` is updated using an exponential update rule, adapted from the POLITEX algorithm (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2019). Specifically, `μ_i(a | B) ∝ exp(η Σ_{j=1}^{i-1} Q^{μ_j}_{τ+l'}(B, a))`, where `η` is a learning rate and `Q^{μ_j}_{τ+l'}` is the Q-function learned in the previous iteration. This part extends POLITEX to the discounted reward setting.
4. **Convergence Analysis of TD Learning (Lemma 3 & 4):**
- **Contraction Mapping (Lemma 3):** Under Assumption 1 and sufficient exploration by the policy, it is shown that the Superstate MDP transition operator admits a contraction mapping, which is fundamental for TD learning convergence.
- **Finite-Time Bounds for TD Learning (Lemma 4):** This lemma provides explicit finite-time error bounds for Algorithm 1, quantifying the difference between the learned Q-function and the true Q-function of the Superstate MDP. It incorporates terms related to the function approximation error and the error introduced by the history approximation (i.e., `(1-ρ)^l`).
5. **Regret Analysis (Theorem 3):** The overall performance of the algorithm is quantified using a notion of regret `R_T`, defined as the cumulative difference between the optimal POMDP value function and the value function of the learned policy over `M` episodes, each of `τ + l'` steps. The theorem establishes an upper bound on this regret, showing how it depends on the history approximation error (ξ_HA) and the function approximation error (ξ_FA), decaying with `T`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

This research yields several findings, primarily focusing on theoretical guarantees for the proposed framework:

1. **Tighter Approximation Guarantees for POMDP-to-Superstate MDP Transformation:** The paper presents theoretical bounds (Theorem 2) on the difference between the optimal value function of the original POMDP and its approximated Superstate MDP. This difference is shown to decrease exponentially with the length `l` of the truncated history, represented by `(1-ρ)^l`. These bounds are reported to be tighter and stronger (worst-case rather than expected difference) compared to previous work that yielded polynomial or squared inverse discount factor bounds.
2. **Novel Algebraic Identity:** Lemma 2 introduces a general algebraic result for bounding expressions of the form `Σ (a_i b_i - c_i d_i)`. This identity provides a refined approach for obtaining tighter guarantees, and its utility is demonstrated by showing its application to improve bounds in other relevant analyses (e.g., Subramanian and Mahajan, 2019).
3. **Convergence Guarantees for Standard Policy Optimization without M-step TD:** A significant finding is the establishment of convergence guarantees for a standard policy optimization algorithm (alternating policy evaluation via TD learning and policy improvement) applied to the Superstate MDP. This addresses the challenge of sampling mismatch, where samples are drawn from the true POMDP but updates are for the approximate MDP, without resorting to the computationally more expensive `m`-step TD learning employed in prior work like Cayci et al. (2024). This indicates a path toward more efficient learning.
4. **Finite-Time Performance Bounds with Linear Function Approximation:** The research develops finite-time performance bounds for the Policy Optimization Algorithm (Algorithm 2) in the linear function approximation setting (Lemma 4 and Theorem 3). This enables the approach to scale to large state spaces, which is crucial for practical applications. Crucially, this analysis avoids restrictive ergodicity assumptions and provides finite-time rather than asymptotic convergence guarantees, distinguishing it from some prior Q-learning based methods (e.g., Kara and Yüksel, 2023).
5. **Extension of POLITEX Algorithm to Discounted Rewards:** The policy update rule in Algorithm 2 leverages an extension of the POLITEX algorithm, originally designed for the average reward setting, to the discounted reward problem, broadening its applicability within RL.
6. **Quantified Regret Analysis:** Theorem 3 provides an analytical upper bound on the regret of the proposed algorithm with respect to the optimal value function of the original POMDP. This regret bound explicitly decomposes the error into components attributable to linear function approximation (ξ_FA) and the approximation arising from using a truncated history (ξ_HA), offering insights into the sources of performance degradation.
7. **Empirical Observations:** Experimental evaluation on a partially observable FrozenLake-v1 environment demonstrates that increasing the history length `k` generally improves performance, particularly in the presence of observation noise. Longer histories help mitigate the negative effects of higher noise levels. A comparison on a simple POMDP suggests that the proposed algorithm can achieve comparable performance to `m`-step TD learning approaches (e.g., Cayci et al., 2024) with potentially lower computational complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of reinforcement learning and its applications:

1. **Enhanced Scalability and Practicality for POMDPs:** By demonstrating that standard policy optimization algorithms with linear function approximation can effectively approximate optimal POMDP policies with provable guarantees, this research offers a more scalable and practical approach to addressing partial observability. This can facilitate the deployment of RL in complex real-world systems where exact POMDP solutions are intractable and previous approximate methods lacked strong theoretical backing or were computationally demanding.
2. **Strengthened Theoretical Foundation:** The derivation of tighter approximation bounds, particularly the exponential decay of error with history length, and the introduction of a novel general-purpose algebraic identity (Lemma 2), advance the theoretical understanding of POMDP approximations. The explicit quantification of finite-time errors for standard TD learning in a non-Markovian setting is a notable theoretical contribution that can influence future analyses in related domains.
3. **Improved Computational Efficiency:** The ability to establish convergence guarantees for standard TD learning in the policy evaluation step, rather than relying on more computationally intensive `m`-step TD learning, represents a step toward more efficient algorithms for partially observable reinforcement learning. This could lead to faster training times and reduced computational resources in practical implementations.
4. **Broader Applicability:** The proposed framework can be applied to a diverse range of sequential decision-making problems under uncertainty. These include areas such as robotics control, autonomous navigation, personalized content delivery, medical diagnosis, and other domains where the underlying state is not fully observable and decisions must be made based on partial and noisy information.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in several directions. The analysis of function approximation, particularly with linear features, encourages further exploration of more expressive approximators, such as deep recurrent neural networks (e.g., LSTMs, Transformers). Integrating such advanced architectures with the rigorous theoretical framework established here could lead to algorithms with even tighter approximation bounds and superior empirical performance, bridging the gap between deep reinforcement learning's empirical success and formal guarantees in POMDPs.
6. **Guidance for Algorithm Design:** The detailed regret analysis, which explicitly delineates errors due to history approximation and function approximation, provides valuable insights for algorithm design. This enables practitioners to make informed decisions about trade-offs between history length, feature set complexity, and expected performance, guiding the development of more robust and optimized POMDP solvers.
