# Explicit Language Memory for Long-Horizon Planning in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model is proposed that incorporates an explicit natural-language memory module, improving phase consistency and reducing skipped steps in long-horizon robotic tasks and leading to enhanced overall success rates across simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle with the non-Markovian nature of long-horizon tasks, often leading to temporal inconsistencies and skipped stages.
- Lack of robust mechanisms for closed-loop error correction in multi-stage tasks causes execution errors to accumulate, leading to task failures.
- Sparse expert demonstrations limit the compositional generalization abilities of VLA models across diverse and complex robotic tasks.

## Method

- Introduces a **hierarchical VLA architecture** that decomposes the robotic control policy into a high-level semantic reasoning component and a low-level continuous action generation component.
- Integrates an ==explicit natural-language memory== module that recursively captures historical events, current task state, and near-future intent using a ==rolling-compression rule== to maintain temporal consistency.
- Employs an ==automated supervision pipeline== to generate target language memory and subtask instructions from expert episodes, enabling independent training of the high-level VLM and low-level VLA branches.

## Results

- Improved average stage success rates on BEHAVIOR-1K "turning-on-radio" task from **30.0%** to **40.0%**, and on Genie Sim 3.0 "single-package sorting" from **41.7%** to **63.9%**.
- Demonstrated enhanced phase consistency, significantly reducing premature stage transitions and skipped steps in multi-stage tasks across simulation environments.
- Provided evidence of in-context failure recovery on a real XLeRobot, where the memory model could record grasp failures and issue subtasks to re-attempt the action.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit natural-language memory improves phase consistency by ensuring the agent advances only when observational evidence supports the completion of the current subtask milestone.
> - Decoupling high-level semantic reasoning from low-level continuous action generation helps preserve the VLM backbone's semantic understanding and enhances overall task interpretability.
> - The structured memory allows for in-context failure recovery by recording observed failures and conditioning subsequent predictions to issue corrective subtasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) models represent a paradigm for integrating visual perception, language comprehension, and robotic control within a unified framework. These models leverage semantic knowledge derived from large vision-language models (VLMs) to enable robots to understand and execute tasks based on natural language instructions and visual observations. This approach offers the potential for generalizable policies that can adapt to various tasks and robotic platforms.

However, existing VLA models encounter challenges when applied to long-horizon tasks, which typically involve multiple sequential stages and complex temporal dependencies. These challenges include:
- **Sparse Expert Demonstrations:** The difficulty in acquiring comprehensive expert data limits compositional generalization across diverse tasks.
- **Non-Markovian Nature:** Long-horizon tasks often exhibit non-Markovian properties, meaning that current observations alone may be insufficient to determine the correct action, requiring awareness of past states or actions. Policies conditioned solely on instantaneous observations can struggle with temporal consistency.
- **Accumulation of Execution Errors:** Limited mechanisms for closed-loop error correction can lead to the propagation and accumulation of errors throughout a multi-stage task.
- **Weakening of Semantic Representations:** End-to-end action fine-tuning in monolithic VLA models may dilute or degrade the high-level semantic understanding capabilities inherited from the VLM backbones.

Prior work has explored hierarchical planning by leveraging language models to coordinate pre-trained robot skills (e.g., SayCan, Inner Monologue) or by incorporating reasoning traces for current decisions (e.g., Embodied Chain-of-Thought, dVLA, DualCoT-VLA). Other approaches have focused on representing temporal context through perceptual history compression (e.g., HAMLET) or by maintaining cognitive memories (e.g., MemoryVLA, MAP-VLA). Large-scale VLA models such as RT-1, RT-2, and OpenVLA demonstrate success in short-horizon control and scaling, while flow-based policies like π0 and π0.5 focus on generating continuous action trajectories and integrating semantic prediction.

This work contributes to the landscape by addressing the challenges of long-horizon tasks through a novel hierarchical VLA architecture that integrates an explicit natural-language memory module. It positions itself by focusing on maintaining task history as a compact, explicit, and interpretable language memory, distinct from approaches that primarily rely on visual history concatenation or implicit memory representations. The proposed method aims to improve temporal consistency and robustness by enabling recursive semantic state tracking and decoupling it from low-level continuous action generation, offering a structured approach to long-horizon robot planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance and robustness of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in executing complex, long-horizon robotic tasks. The motivation stems from several identified limitations of existing VLA models when faced with such tasks:

1. **Addressing Sparse Expert Demonstrations and Cross-Task Generalization:** Current VLA models often rely on extensive expert demonstrations, which are difficult to acquire for a broad range of complex, multi-stage tasks. This scarcity of data limits the ability of models to generalize compositionally across different tasks or variations of the same task. The research aims to improve generalization by structuring the planning process such that high-level semantic reasoning is separated from low-level control, potentially making the system more adaptable.

2. **Mitigating the Non-Markovian Nature of Long-Horizon Tasks:** Many real-world tasks are non-Markovian, meaning the optimal next action depends not only on the current observation but also on the sequence of past events or the overall task progress. For example, grasping a specific object may be required *before* pressing a button, even if both actions are visually plausible at different stages. Policies that condition solely on current observations can suffer from temporal inconsistency, leading to skipped stages, repeated actions, or proceeding after an unobserved failure. The objective is to introduce a mechanism that maintains an explicit, persistent record of task history to ensure temporal consistency and phase progression.

3. **Enabling Closed-Loop Error Correction and Preventing Error Accumulation:** In long-horizon tasks, small execution errors can accumulate over time, leading to task failure. Existing VLA models often lack robust mechanisms for error detection, diagnosis, and recovery within a closed-loop execution. The motivation is to provide an internal representation that can track the state of execution, identify when a subtask has failed or not been completed, and allow for dynamic correction.

4. **Preserving High-Level Semantic Representations:** End-to-end fine-tuning of large vision-language model (VLM) backbones for low-level action prediction can sometimes weaken their inherent high-level semantic understanding capabilities. This study aims to decouple the high-level semantic reasoning from low-level continuous control. This separation is intended to allow the VLM backbone to maintain its high-level language understanding and semantic representations while a dedicated low-level VLA handles precise continuous control.

To address these issues, the central hypothesis is that maintaining task history as an explicit natural-language memory can significantly improve phase consistency, ensuring the agent advances only when observational evidence supports the completion of the current milestone. This approach seeks to provide a compact, interpretable, and stable semantic interface for the low-level VLA, thereby improving overall task success and interpretability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a hierarchical long-horizon VLA architecture that integrates an explicit language-memory module. This architecture decouples the robotic control policy into a high-level semantic reasoning component and a low-level continuous action generation component.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Policy Decomposition

The overall policy `π` is decomposed into two branches:
- **High-level Semantic Branch (πHL):** This branch is responsible for semantic reasoning. Given the current visual and proprioceptive observation (`ot `), the global task instruction (`l`), and the previous language memory (`m t-1 `), it predicts an updated language memory (`mt `) and a concise subtask instruction (`st `). This branch determines "what state the task is in" and "what should be done next."
- **Low-level Action Branch (πLL):** This branch handles continuous control. Conditioned on the current observation (`ot `) and the subtask instruction (`st `) generated by the high-level branch, it predicts a continuous action chunk (`a t:t+H `) for a fixed horizon `H`. This branch decides "how to execute the selected subtask."

This decomposition allows for separate optimization targets for semantic planning and continuous control, as depicted in a recurrent loop where the high-level VLM updates memory and subtask, and the low-level VLA generates actions whose execution produces the next observation.

#### 3.2. Recursive Language Memory

The core of the high-level branch is the language memory `mt `. This memory is a structured natural-language summary that captures:
- Historical events.
- The current task state.
- Near-future intent.

It does not narrate every frame but selectively retains information crucial for disambiguating decisions, such as completed stages, evidence of completion, relevant object attributes, observed failures, and the next pending milestone.

The memory operates under a **rolling-compression rule**:
- **Pre-completion:** While a subtask is active, memory preserves details (e.g., category, color, location) that help identify its target.
- **Post-completion:** Upon completion, these details are compressed into a concise past-tense statement, and attention shifts to the next action. This mechanism ensures the memory remains bounded in length while semantically anchoring phase progression.
The high-level model updates memory only when the current observation provides sufficient evidence that the active milestone has changed.

#### 3.3. Automated Memory Supervision

To train the high-level branch, a supervision pipeline is developed to generate target memory (`mt `) and subtask (`st `) for each sampled observation. This process involves:
1. **Expert Episodes:** Each expert episode includes a video recording and manually provided skill-level annotations (skill names, descriptions, start, and end timestamps).
2. **Frame Division and Skill Indexing:** The video frames are aligned with the active skill index based on timestamps.
3. **VLM-based Annotation:** A general-purpose VLM (e.g., hosted Gemini 3.5 Flash API or locally deployed Qwen3.5-9B) receives the current frame and the complete skill annotation for the episode. It then outputs two fields:
 - A concise subtask (`st `) in the format "action + target object + context" (without an agent subject).
 - A first-person language memory (`mt `) that describes completed skills in the past tense and transitions to the next skill with a future-oriented statement. The prompt guiding the VLM annotation explicitly requests the use of natural object names and visible attributes, avoiding implementation-specific terms. These generated `(mt, st)` pairs serve as supervised targets for the high-level policy.

#### 3.4. Independent Branch Training

The two branches are trained independently to avoid the need for manually balancing semantic generation and continuous control losses within a single monolithic objective:
- **High-level Branch Training:** The high-level VLM uses PaliGemma, a VLM from the family employed by the π0.5 baseline. It is trained autoregressively to predict both the updated memory (`mt `) and the subtask (`st `). The objective function `LHL ` is a negative log-likelihood over the predicted memory and subtask, conditioned on the current observation, previous memory, and global instruction.
- **Low-level Branch Training:** The low-level VLA receives `ot ` and the target (or predicted) `st ` to generate an action chunk. Following the π0.5 action expert, it is optimized using a flow-matching action objective. The supervised action term `LLL ` minimizes the squared L2 norm between the predicted action chunk and the ground-truth action chunk, conditioned on the observation and subtask.

During training, PaliGemma and the VLM backbone within the low-level VLA are adapted using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), while a 300M-parameter Gemma action expert is fully fine-tuned. AdamW is used for optimization with a specific learning rate schedule.

#### 3.5. Closed-Loop Inference

At deployment, the memory `mt ` is initialized as empty. The robot operates in a loop:
1. Acquire current observation (`ot `).
2. The high-level branch (`πHL `) updates the memory (`mt `) and predicts the subtask (`st `) based on `ot `, the global instruction (`l`), and the previous memory (`m t-1 `).
3. The low-level branch (`πLL `) generates an action chunk (`a t:t+H `) using `ot ` and `st `.
4. The action chunk is executed in the environment.
5. The process repeats until the high-level state indicates task completion.

This closed-loop inference generates an interpretable log of memory and subtasks, which aids in diagnosing decision failures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate whether language memory improves long-horizon VLA performance and how memory context affects action decisions. Evaluations were conducted across BEHAVIOR-1K, Genie Sim 3.0, and a real XLeRobot platform. The primary metric was stage-wise success rate, which provides insight into where failures occur within multi-stage tasks.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

**BEHAVIOR-1K (Turning-on-radio task):**
- The baseline π0.5 achieved an average stage success rate of 30.0%.
- The proposed π0.5
- language memory model improved the average success rate to 40.0%.
- Specific stage improvements: MoveTo remained at 100.0% for both. PickUp significantly improved from 10.0% to 30.0%. PressOn remained challenging, increasing from 0.0% to 10.0%. PlaceOn increased from 10.0% to 20.0%.

**Genie Sim 3.0 (Package sorting tasks):**
- **Single-package sorting:**
 - Baseline π0.5: 41.7% average stage success.
 - π0.5
- language memory: 63.9% average stage success.
 - Significant gains observed in Follow (50.0% to 100.0%), PickUp-1 (50.0% to 100.0%), and Inside-1 (placing on scan table, 50.0% to 83.3%). Other stages (Upright, PickUp-2, Inside-2) showed no change or slight improvements.
- **Continuous package sorting (4 packages):**
 - Baseline π0.5: 31.3% average stage success.
 - π0.5
- language memory: 46.9% average stage success.
 - The baseline achieved 100.0% for Upright-1 but failed to complete Upright-3 and Upright-4. The memory model improved Upright-2 from 25.0% to 37.5%, Upright-3 from 0.0% to 37.5%, and achieved 12.5% for Upright-4.

**Real XLeRobot (Pick-and-place task):**
- This was a shorter task with 25 training trajectories and 20 evaluation trials.
- Baseline π0.5: 62.5% average stage success.
- π0.5
- language memory: 66.3% average stage success.
- Follow remained at 100.0%. PickUp, MoveTo, and Place stages each increased from 50.0% to 55.0%. The smaller improvement aligns with the expectation that explicit history has less impact on tasks with fewer stages.

#### 4.2. Phase Consistency and Skipped Steps

Qualitative analysis demonstrated that explicit language memory improved phase consistency and reduced skipped steps.
- In the "turning on radio" task, the baseline without memory prematurely advanced from "pickup" to "place the radio on the table" before the required "button press." The memory model, conversely, recorded "radio approached and picked up" and maintained "pressing the button" as the next intent, advancing only after observing evidence for that milestone.
- Similarly, in package sorting, the baseline could skip barcode scanning and attempt to place the package directly into the basket. The memory policy prevented this by not advancing to the basket stage until its context explicitly recorded that the package was placed in the scanning area and picked up again. These observations support the interpretation that memory enforces stage ordering.

#### 4.3. Semantic Planning Versus Motor Control

The results also highlight a distinction between semantic planning and motor control. For instance, the low success rate for the "PressOn" stage in BEHAVIOR-1K (10.0% with memory) indicates that even when memory correctly identifies button pressing as the next subtask, the execution can still fail due to challenges like button localization, geometric alignment, force application, or action timing. This suggests that language memory primarily addresses "what to do" and "which stage is active," but does not directly enhance "how to execute contact-rich motions." This distinction can facilitate diagnosing failures as either high-level semantic errors or low-level control errors.

#### 4.4. In-Context Failure Recovery

The real-robot logs provided evidence for in-context failure recovery. When a grasp failed, the baseline might enter an inconsistent state until timeout. In contrast, the memory model could record the failure (e.g., "object not secured"), issue a subtask to reopen the gripper, and re-attempt the grasp. This behavior was not due to a separate recovery controller but emerged from the textual failure state conditioning subsequent predictions. This suggests that history can enable adaptive behavior by making failed attempts available for future decisions.

Overall, the observed benefits include:
1. Reduction of ambiguity at subtask boundaries by explicitly tracking completed and pending events.
2. Discouragement of premature stage transitions through evidence-conditioned memory updates.
3. Improved localization of decision failures due to readable memory and subtask outputs.
4. Contextual representation of failed actions for potential recovery.

#### 4.5. Limitations

The study acknowledged several limitations:
- **Real-robot evaluation scope:** The real-robot experiments were limited to a short pick-and-place task with a small dataset (25 training trajectories, 20 evaluation trials), not demonstrating performance on minute-scale, multi-object, or dynamic real-world scenarios.
- **Statistical stability:** Simulation results reported stage success rates without variation across random seeds or confidence intervals, suggesting that the statistical robustness of the improvements warrants further measurement.
- **Increased complexity and latency:** The hierarchical design adds a high-level VLM call before low-level action generation, increasing computational overhead, end-to-end latency, and deployment complexity compared to a monolithic architecture.
- **Motor control limitations:** Language memory cannot compensate for insufficient low-level data or inherent control precision limitations, as evidenced by the low success rate in contact-rich actions like "PressOn."
- **Recovery data:** Robust recovery mechanisms would require collecting and labeling diverse failure and recovery trajectories, alongside successful actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces an explicit language-memory component for long-horizon VLA planning, representing a significant step towards more robust and interpretable robot behaviors in complex, multi-stage tasks. The proposed hierarchical architecture, which separates high-level semantic state tracking from low-level continuous action generation, directly addresses several critical limitations of existing VLA models.

The key contributions and their potential impact include:

1. **Enhanced Temporal Consistency and Robustness:** By converting discrete temporal observations into a coherent textual memory sequence, the model effectively tackles the non-Markovian nature of long-horizon tasks. The recursive updating of language memory and subtask instructions, anchored by previous memory, enables persistent temporal tracking and dynamic correction. This mechanism significantly improves stage-wise success rates across various simulation environments and a real-world task by reducing premature stage transitions and skipped steps, as demonstrated by the quantitative results. This offers a path to more reliable robot operation in real-world scenarios.

2. **Improved Interpretability and Diagnosability:** The explicit natural-language memory provides an interpretable semantic account of the decision process. Both the memory (`mt `) and subtask (`st `) are visible text outputs, which generates an actionable log that can be directly aligned with action failures. This allows for clearer diagnosis of whether a failure stems from a high-level semantic misjudgment or a low-level motor control issue, rather than treating all failures as an opaque end-to-end outcome. This enhanced interpretability is crucial for debugging, development, and building trust in autonomous systems.

3. **Facilitating In-Context Failure Recovery:** The language memory's ability to record observed failures and persist this information across decisions demonstrates a form of in-context adaptation. By conditioning subsequent predictions on a textual failure state (e.g., "object not secured"), the model can issue corrective subtasks, such as re-attempting a grasp. This capability, emerging without a dedicated recovery controller, indicates a promising avenue for robots to self-correct and recover from minor execution errors, leading to more resilient task completion.

4. **Decoupled Optimization and Scalability:** The independent training of high-level and low-level branches simplifies the optimization process by avoiding the need to balance conflicting semantic and control losses in a monolithic model. This architectural separation may also contribute to better utilization of large VLM backbones for semantic reasoning without degrading their representations through excessive fine-tuning for low-level actions. The automated supervision pipeline for memory generation from expert videos further streamlines the data preparation process for such hierarchical systems.

While the approach introduces increased computational complexity due to the hierarchical structure, the benefits in terms of semantic understanding, temporal consistency, and basic recovery capabilities suggest a significant step toward developing more capable and generalizable long-horizon robot policies. Future work, exploring multimodal memory and broader failure recovery demonstrations, could further enhance the utility and robustness of this explicit language-memory paradigm, ultimately advancing the field of embodied AI and robotic manipulation.
