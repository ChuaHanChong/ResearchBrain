# $τ_0$-WM: A Unified Video-Action World Model for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> τ₀-WM, a unified video-action world model, integrates policy learning and action-conditioned simulation using a shared video diffusion backbone. This model leverages heterogeneous interaction data and a test-time computation strategy to achieve superior success rates in long-horizon, fine-grained real-robot manipulation tasks, demonstrating up to a 17% improvement on unseen tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation faces uncertainty regarding the physical consequences of actions, requiring models that can predict future states and evaluate outcomes.
- Existing approaches struggle to effectively combine diverse data sources (real-robot, UMI, egocentric videos) due to varying levels of action supervision and visual richness.
- Many manipulation tasks require robustness beyond feed-forward action prediction, necessitating mechanisms to propose, evaluate, and revise actions before physical execution.

## Method

- A unified video-action ==world model== (τ₀-WM) built on a shared video ==diffusion== backbone, integrating both a Video Action Model (VAM) for action generation and an Action-Conditioned Video Simulator (ACVS) for evaluating future consequences.
- Training on approximately 27,300 hours of heterogeneous data (real-robot, UMI-style, egocentric human videos) using modality-specific supervision masks to leverage diverse information without complete annotation.
- A coarse-to-fine test-time computation (TTC) strategy that samples candidate actions, applies a Re-==denoising== Consistency Score (RCS) filter, and performs Low-quality Action Rectification (LAR) using ACVS for unreliable actions, enabling action refinement before execution.

## Results

- τ₀-WM achieved superior average success rates on four precision-sensitive, long-horizon real-robot manipulation tasks (e.g., Toolbox, Faucet), outperforming baselines and demonstrating robust performance across varied complexities.
- Ablation studies showed that pre-training with the complete heterogeneous data corpus significantly improved zero-shot performance (e.g., 0.55 vs. 0.14 success rate for pen-to-holder task) and fine-tuned performance (0.83 vs. 0.70 for object-wipe-place) compared to training solely on real-robot data.
- The test-time computation strategy increased average success rates from 0.43 to 0.60 on single-attempt tasks, with the Low-quality Action Rectification (LAR) contributing more significantly than Re-denoising Consistency Score (RCS), especially for tasks requiring precise placement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unifying VAM and ACVS around a shared video diffusion backbone allows for a common predictive representation that supports both policy learning and action evaluation, improving model coherence.
> - Heterogeneous data integration, facilitated by modality-specific supervision masks, enhances general-purpose manipulation priors and visual understanding, leading to better generalization to unseen tasks.
> - Test-time computation, involving self-consistency checks and future-conditioned action rectification, can measurably improve action quality and success rates by allowing the robot to 'imagine' and 'critique' actions pre-execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation research is characterized by the challenge of enabling robots to perform complex tasks in uncertain physical environments. This requires models that can not only infer appropriate actions but also anticipate and evaluate the future consequences of those actions before physical execution. This work builds upon and integrates two distinct but related lines of research: Robotic Video Action Models (VAMs) and Action-Conditioned Video Simulators (ACVS).

**Robotic Video Action Models (VAMs)** focus on introducing future forecasting into robot control by jointly predicting videos and actions. Recent advancements often leverage pre-trained video-generation diffusion models, adopting a joint-denoising paradigm to generate future visual latents and action chunks concurrently. These models aim to provide dynamics-aware representations for manipulation. Prior VAMs typically utilize future prediction either as an auxiliary objective for policy learning or as an optional visual output.

**Action-Conditioned Video Simulators (ACVS)**, on the other hand, employ video models to act as simulators for decision-making. Earlier methods in visual foresight learned action-conditioned video predictors and used model-predictive control to select actions based on predicted futures matching a goal. With the advent of large-scale video generation, contemporary systems condition video models on robot actions or end-effector trajectories to predict manipulation rollouts, evaluate policies, or support reinforcement learning. These simulators are often distinct modules from policy generation.

This paper positions τ₀-WM as a unified video-action world model that integrates policy learning, video prediction, and action evaluation within a single, future-predictive framework. It differentiates itself by:
1. **Unifying VAM and ACVS:** Instead of treating future prediction as merely an auxiliary objective or a separate simulation module, τ₀-WM builds both action generation (VAM) and action-conditioned simulation (ACVS) around a shared video diffusion backbone. This allows for a common predictive representation.
2. **Heterogeneous Data Integration:** It addresses the challenge of combining diverse data sources—real-robot demonstrations (high action fidelity but limited diversity), UMI-style demonstrations (broader behaviors, weaker action signals), and egocentric human videos (large-scale visual dynamics, no robot actions)—through modality-specific supervision masks. This aims to leverage the strengths of each data type.
3. **Test-Time Computation for Action Improvement:** The framework utilizes the learned world model at deployment for explicit action selection and refinement, moving beyond a simple feed-forward prediction. This involves evaluating imagined futures and rectifying low-quality actions before physical execution.

By unifying these components and leveraging diverse data with test-time reasoning, τ₀-WM aims to develop a more general and robust manipulation system compared to approaches that treat policy learning and dynamics modeling as separate problems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified world model for robotic manipulation that can generate executable actions, anticipate their future consequences, and evaluate these consequences before physical execution. The motivation stems from several core challenges in contemporary robotic manipulation:

1. **Uncertain Physical Consequences:** Robotic manipulation inherently involves acting under uncertainty. A robot must not only determine the action likely to fulfill a given instruction but also predict how that action will alter the scene through contact, object motion, and multi-step interactions. Traditional reactive policies often struggle with this predictive aspect, leading to brittle behavior in complex environments.
2. **Coupling Prediction with Executability:** While predictive control and world models have a long history, their application to real robots requires coupling this predictive capability with an action interface that is directly executable by a specific robot embodiment, controller, and sensing stack. Bridging this gap is crucial for practical deployment.
3. **Data Heterogeneity:** The data required to learn both predictive capabilities and executable actions exists in diverse forms.
 - **Broad Interaction Data:** Egocentric videos and human interaction trajectories offer extensive visual dynamics, covering how objects move and tasks unfold. However, this data typically lacks robot-specific action labels.
 - **Grounded Robot Data:** Robot demonstrations provide precise action grounding, linking observations to continuous actions for a specific embodiment. Yet, such data is expensive to collect and often covers a limited range of objects, environments, and tasks.
 - Training solely on robot data yields grounded but narrow policies, while training only on broad video data results in predictive but action-ungrounded models. A general manipulation system must effectively utilize broad interaction data without sacrificing the executable action grounding necessary for deployment.
4. **Improving Decision Making Before Execution:** Many manipulation tasks require precision and robustness, where a single feed-forward action prediction might be insufficient. The ability to propose, imagine, and revise actions computationally before committing to physical execution could significantly improve success rates, especially in challenging long-horizon or fine-grained tasks.

The authors propose that by placing future observations, robot actions, and task progress within a shared predictive model, and by allowing each data source to supervise only the signals it contains, a more general and capable manipulation system can be achieved. The goal is for the representation to function not merely as an auxiliary feature for policy learning but as an interface for proposing actions, imagining consequences, and revising decisions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

τ₀-WM is presented as a unified video-action world model that integrates policy learning, video prediction, and action evaluation through a shared predictive framework. Its architecture is built upon a video diffusion backbone and provides two primary interfaces: a Video Action Model (VAM) and an Action-Conditioned Video Simulator (ACVS).

**A. Overall Framework**
The core idea is to leverage a shared video diffusion backbone for both generating actions and simulating future visual outcomes. This allows for complementary training objectives from diverse data sources and enables test-time computation to refine actions.

**B. Data Sources and Unified Supervision**
The model is trained on a substantial corpus of approximately 27,300 hours of heterogeneous interaction data, segmented into four categories:
1. **Real-robot teleoperation (17.8K hours):** Collected on AGIBOT-G01, ARX, and dual-arm Franka systems. Provides reliable, deployment-aligned continuous action supervision.
2. **UMI-style demonstrations (6.5K hours):** Uses handheld gripper-like devices for collection in diverse environments. Offers rich visual interaction data with weaker, action-like signals.
3. **Egocentric human interaction videos (3.0K hours):** Open-source videos providing broad coverage of everyday manipulation behaviors and visual dynamics, but without robot-compatible action labels.
4. **Rollout or failure trajectories:** Incorporated to supervise task progress and identify low-quality outcomes, enhancing simulator learning by exposing off-distribution actions and recovery behaviors.

To accommodate the varying levels of supervision from these sources, τ₀-WM employs **modality-specific supervision masks**. For each training sample, a mask specifies which inputs are observed, which targets are predicted, and which losses are active. This enables joint training across all data sources, ensuring each sample contributes relevant information without requiring complete annotation.

**C. Video Action Model (VAM)**
The VAM serves as the policy-facing interface, jointly predicting future visual latents and executable robot actions.
- **Problem Formulation:** Given a current multi-view observation (oᵢ), language instruction (p), and robot state (sᵢ), VAM predicts a future latent trajectory (ˆzᵢ₊₁:ᵢ₊Hᵥ) and a continuous action chunk (ˆaᵢ:ᵢ₊Hₐ₋₁).
- **Architecture:** VAM is based on the Wan2.2-TI2V-5B architecture.
 - A Wan VAE encodes camera views into latent tensors. Multi-view inputs are concatenated to form a temporal latent canvas.
 - The architecture comprises two coupled components:
 - **Video Branch:** Uses the 5B-parameter Wan video DiT backbone to predict future latent trajectories via conditional denoising.
 - **Action Branch:** A 0.5B-parameter DiT-style action decoder coupled to the video transformer. It models temporal dependencies within the action horizon and cross-attends to intermediate video features from the video branch. These video features are conditioned on visual context and language instruction, providing instruction-aware and dynamics-relevant representations for action generation.
- **Joint Flow-Matching Objective:** VAM optimizes a flow-matching objective for both future video latents and action chunks. The loss function includes terms for video latent prediction and action chunk prediction, with supervision masks ensuring only available data contributes to specific loss components.

**D. Action-Conditioned Video Simulator (ACVS)**
The ACVS functions as the evaluation interface, estimating the future consequences of a candidate action without physical execution.
- **Problem Formulation:** Given memory observations (oᵢ₋M:ᵢ), a language instruction (p), and a candidate action chunk (āᵢ:ᵢ₊Hₐ₋₁), ACVS predicts future video latents (ˆzᵢ₊₁:ᵢ₊Hᵥ) and dense reward scores (ˆrᵢ:ᵢ₊Hₐ₋₁). It treats the candidate action as a clean condition.
- **Architecture:** ACVS reuses the Wan VAE and the video transformer backbone from VAM but removes the Action DiT policy branch.
 - Memory and current observations form clean latent context, while future latent slots are denoised by the video backbone.
 - Candidate actions are conditioned by projecting action blocks into lightweight MLPs that are then injected into the diffusion-time embedding and AdaLN modulation embedding, broadcasting across spatial tokens and camera views for corresponding future slots.
- **Reward and Progress Scoring:** ACVS predicts a dense reward trajectory by decomposing manipulation tasks into subtasks and assigning progress labels at that level. Frame-level rewards are estimated through Monte Carlo propagation. Failure data is explicitly incorporated with negative rewards for failed subtask segments, enabling the simulator to learn to identify actions leading to unsuccessful outcomes.
- **Training Objective:** ACVS uses a flow-matching formulation to jointly supervise future video latents and dense reward trajectories, similar to VAM's objective but with terms for video latent prediction and reward prediction.

**E. Test-Time Computation (TTC)**
τ₀-WM implements a coarse-to-fine test-time computation strategy to select and rectify actions before execution. This procedure allocates additional computation for action selection when needed, preserving real-time performance in most situations.
1. **Sampling Candidate Actions:** VAM initially samples multiple action chunks (N candidates).
2. **Re-denoising Consistency Score (RCS):** For each candidate, a lightweight self-consistency filter is applied. This involves re-noising the action at random flow timesteps and evaluating it with VAM's action vector field to compute an average re-denoising error. The RCS is defined as the negative of this error, favoring actions more consistent with the learned conditional action manifold. The candidate with the highest RCS is initially selected.
3. **Low-quality Action Rectification (LAR):** If the selected candidate's RCS falls below a predefined reliability threshold (γ), ACVS is invoked for more expensive rollout-based evaluation.
 - ACVS evaluates all N candidate actions, predicting an imagined rollout (ˆz⁽ⁱ⁾) and a dense reward trajectory (ˆr⁽ⁱ⁾) for each.
 - A rollout value (J⁽ⁱ⁾) is computed as the maximum task progress achieved by the imagined rollout.
 - The highest-value rollout (j⋆) is selected as the most promising future.
 - Instead of executing the corresponding action (ā⁽ʲ⋆⁾), the selected rollout latent (ˆz⁽ʲ⋆⁾) is converted into an additional future condition. VAM is then re-queried with this future condition to generate a refined action chunk (ã), explicitly guiding the policy toward the high-value imagined future.

This proposal-evaluation-revision procedure allows τ₀-WM to improve robot actions directly through future prediction before physical execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of τ₀-WM focused on its performance on long-horizon, fine-grained real-robot manipulation tasks, the effectiveness of heterogeneous pre-training, and the benefits of its test-time computation strategy. Experiments spanned three robot embodiments (AGIBOT-G01, ARX, dual-arm Franka system) and involved language-conditioned, multi-view packing and assembly tasks. Task success rate was the primary metric.

**A. Main Results: Policy Performance**
τ₀-WM was evaluated on four precision-sensitive manipulation tasks (Toolbox, School Bag, Faucet, Badminton) that were explicitly excluded from the pre-training corpus. These tasks demanded long-horizon reasoning, multi-stage object interaction, and precise geometric alignment.
- **Superior Success Rate:** τ₀-WM achieved the highest average success rate among the evaluated baselines (π₀.₅ and Fast-WAM) and performed best on most individual tasks.
- **Consistent Performance:** While π₀.₅ performed competitively on simpler tasks like Toolbox, its performance diminished on tasks requiring more complex coordination and fine-grained manipulation. τ₀-WM maintained strong performance across all four tasks, including the challenging Faucet task which required accurate hose alignment and secure attachment.
- **Qualitative Observations:** The authors observed that τ₀-WM often performed additional corrective actions (e.g., pushing or pressing a tool further) after initial insertion in the Toolbox task, leading to a more robust final state, whereas baselines sometimes terminated prematurely. This behavior is attributed to the explicit modeling of future visual outcomes, encouraging optimization for the quality of the final scene configuration.

**B. Ablation Studies: Pre-training Data Composition**
The impact of the heterogeneous pre-training data mixture was assessed by comparing a τ₀-WM model trained solely on real-robot teleoperation data against one trained on the complete corpus (Robot + UMI + Egocentric data). Evaluations were conducted in both zero-shot and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) settings.
- **Zero-Shot Performance:** For the zero-shot task (pen-to-holder), the model trained with the complete corpus achieved an average success rate of 0.55, significantly outperforming the robot-only trained model (0.14). This suggests that UMI and egocentric interaction data contribute to improved general-purpose manipulation priors and visual understanding, enhancing generalization to unseen tasks.
- **Fine-Tuned Performance:** In the SFT task (object-wipe-place), the complete corpus-trained model also showed better performance (0.83 average success rate) compared to the robot-only model (0.70). The benefit was particularly noticeable under cluttered conditions (0.75 vs. 0.55), indicating improved robustness beyond faster adaptation.

**C. Ablation Studies: Test-Time Computation (TTC)**
The proposed test-time computation strategy was ablated on two tasks (Tissue→Box and Pen→Box) under a strict single-attempt protocol (no retries).
- **Re-denoising Consistency Score (RCS):** Implementing only the lightweight RCS filter increased the average success rate from 0.43 (without TTC) to 0.50. This indicates that a portion of failures can be mitigated by selecting more self-consistent action samples.
- **Low-quality Action Rectification (LAR):** Further enabling LAR, which involves ACVS for rollout-based evaluation and action rectification, improved the average success rate to 0.60. This demonstrates the benefit of leveraging imagined futures to refine actions.
- **Comparison with Guidance Approaches:** τ₀-WM's RCS + LAR strategy consistently outperformed existing generation-time guidance approaches like Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) and Action Coherence Guidance (ACG). For example, on Pen→Box, RCS+LAR achieved 0.50 success rate compared to CFG (0.15) and ACG (0.35). The greater improvement on Pen→Box suggests that future-conditioned rectification is particularly effective for tasks demanding precise object placement and alignment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in τ₀-WM offers several contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, demonstrating a unified approach that integrates previously distinct functionalities.

The primary significance lies in the **unified video-action world model architecture**. By building both a Video Action Model (VAM) for policy learning and an Action-Conditioned Video Simulator (ACVS) for evaluation around a shared video diffusion backbone, the research establishes a common predictive representation. This integration allows for a more cohesive learning framework where dynamics modeling directly informs and evaluates action generation, rather than serving as a detached auxiliary task. This approach has the potential to lead to more robust and capable robot policies.

The methodology for **integrating heterogeneous interaction data** from real robots, UMI-style demonstrations, and egocentric human videos, using modality-specific supervision masks, is a notable contribution. This addresses a critical challenge in scaling robotic learning by allowing models to leverage the strengths of various data sources—high-fidelity action grounding from real robots, behavioral diversity from UMI, and broad visual dynamics from human videos—without being constrained by their individual limitations (e.g., lack of actions in egocentric videos or limited diversity in robot data). This could accelerate the development of more general-purpose manipulation foundation models.

Furthermore, the introduction of a **test-time proposal-evaluation-revision procedure** is significant. By employing a coarse-to-fine strategy involving Re-denoising Consistency Score (RCS) for initial selection and Low-quality Action Rectification (LAR) via ACVS for unreliable candidates, τ₀-WM demonstrates a mechanism for improving action quality before physical execution. This approach moves beyond purely feed-forward policies and enables robots to "imagine" and "critique" their actions, leading to enhanced performance on challenging, fine-grained tasks. The experimental results show that this test-time computation yields measurable improvements in success rates, particularly in single-attempt scenarios requiring high precision.

The potential impact of τ₀-WM extends to several areas:
- **General-Purpose Manipulation:** The ability to learn from diverse data sources and unify perception, action, and prediction could contribute to the development of more general and adaptable robotic manipulation systems, reducing the need for extensive task-specific data collection.
- **Robustness in Real-World Scenarios:** The explicit modeling of future visual outcomes and the test-time action rectification mechanism could enhance robot robustness in complex, uncertain, and cluttered real-world environments, where errors can be costly.
- **Foundation Models for Robotics:** By demonstrating how a shared predictive representation can unify different aspects of robotic learning, τ₀-WM contributes to the broader effort of building foundation models for physical AI, potentially enabling robots to perform a wider range of tasks with greater autonomy.

Future work suggested by the authors includes incorporating additional sensing modalities like tactile feedback for contact-rich interactions, developing more reliable and longer-horizon deployment-time reasoning mechanisms, and extending predictive modeling to even longer temporal horizons and more complex manipulation scenarios. These directions suggest an ongoing research trajectory towards more sophisticated and reliable robot capabilities.
