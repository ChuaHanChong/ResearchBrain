# From Activation to Causality: Discovery of Causal Visual Representations in the Human Brain

> [!summary] Summary
> A research team from Weizmann Institute of Science and MIT developed BrainCause, an automated framework to causally validate visual concept representations in the human brain, moving beyond mere correlational activation. This framework integrates generative AI to synthesize precisely controlled stimuli, significantly reducing the false positive rate of activation-based methods from approximately 70% to 23% and improving true positive identification by 12.1 percentage points.

## Problem

- Existing fMRI studies often identify brain regions that activate strongly for a given concept but fail to establish a causal link, potentially due to confounding visual or semantic features.
- Activation-based methods can lead to a high rate of false positives, incorrectly identifying regions as representing a concept.
- Traditional fMRI struggles to achieve fine-grained concept localization and disentangle representations beyond broad visual categories.

## Method

- **BrainCause**, an automated framework, systematically generates three types of stimuli (positive, semantic negative, and counterfactual negative) using large language models and text-to-image AI to isolate target concepts.
- The framework computes a 'causal score' for each voxel, combining positive activation with specificity against semantically related alternatives and responses to counterfactual edits.
- It validates discovered concept representations using both generated and measured fMRI data, and suggests follow-up experiments to address data gaps for promising but unconfirmed findings.

## Results

- BrainCause significantly reduced the false positive rate from approximately **70%** for activation-based methods to **23%**, while increasing the true positive rate from **26.6%** to **38.7%**.
- The framework achieved substantial improvements in causal specificity scores, with the semantic negative score on generated data improving from -0.44 (MindSimulator) to 0.62 (BrainCause).
- It successfully recovered and localized known functional areas (e.g., **99%** alignment for Bodies, **90%** for Faces) and discovered new, causally supported representations for fine-grained concepts like 'tools' and 'animal faces' within high-level visual cortex.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Methods relying solely on activation maximization exhibit a high false positive rate (around 70%) when causally evaluated, underscoring the limitations of correlation-based analysis.
> - Leveraging advanced generative AI allows for the creation of controlled stimuli (semantic negatives and counterfactuals) that are critical for disentangling genuine concept representation from correlated features.
> - A systematic causal validation approach can refine brain mapping, reduce ambiguities, and reveal more specific, fine-grained functional organizations in the visual cortex.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The study addresses a fundamental challenge in neuroscience: understanding how visual concepts are represented in the human brain. For decades, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been instrumental in identifying brain regions selective for broad visual categories such as faces, places, bodies, and words. These studies traditionally rely on "category contrasts," where a brain region's response to a target category is compared against its response to other categories. More recently, computational approaches leveraging image-to-fMRI encoding models have extended this paradigm, enabling predictions of brain activity for a wider array of images and facilitating the discovery of more nuanced representations beyond predefined categories.

Despite these advancements, a key limitation remains: strong activation of a brain region in response to a visual concept does not definitively prove that the region *causally represents* that concept. The activation might instead be driven by visual or semantic cues that frequently co-occur with the concept (e.g., background, color, object pose). This work builds upon the foundation of fMRI-based localization and computational brain modeling but introduces a critical shift from mere correlational activation to causal validation of neural representations. It integrates recent advancements in generative AI (large language models and text-to-image models) with image-to-fMRI encoding models to synthesize highly controlled stimuli, directly addressing the challenge of disentangling true concept representation from correlated factors. This approach positions the work as a bridge between high-throughput computational discovery and the rigorous causal validation traditionally sought through methods like lesion studies, but tailored for visual concept representation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a systematic framework for discovering and causally validating visual concept representations in the human brain, moving beyond mere activation-based correlations. The authors identify several key motivations:

- **Addressing the Correlation-Causation Gap:** A central motivation is the recognition that existing fMRI studies, including those using advanced encoding models, often identify brain regions that *activate* strongly for a given concept but do not establish a *causal* link. The observed activation might be a confound of correlated visual or semantic features (e.g., a region responding to "outdoor scenes" when investigating "animals," if animals are predominantly pictured outdoors). The framework aims to provide a method to distinguish genuine concept representation from such spurious correlations.
- **Improving the Specificity and Reliability of Discoveries:** Many current methods may yield "false positives" – regions incorrectly identified as representing a concept due to reliance on activation alone. The goal is to reduce these false positives by introducing targeted causal testing, thereby leading to more reliable and interpretable mappings between visual concepts and brain activity.
- **Enabling Fine-Grained Concept Localization:** Traditional fMRI studies often focus on broad categories. The motivation includes extending discovery to a wider and more fine-grained set of visual concepts (e.g., specific body parts, types of text) that might not be sufficiently represented or disentangled in existing datasets or through traditional contrast methods.
- **Guiding Future Experimental Design:** Beyond discovery, the authors aim to create a framework that can inform and guide future fMRI experiments. This involves identifying concepts that are underrepresented in existing datasets, proposing specific stimuli for further testing, and pinpointing which types of "negative control" stimuli would be most informative to collect. This closes the loop between computational analysis and experimental neuroscience.
- **Leveraging Advanced AI Models:** The work is motivated by the recent advancements in generative AI, which allow for the synthesis and precise editing of images with fine-grained semantic control. These capabilities are leveraged to create the necessary controlled stimuli for causal testing, which would be challenging or impossible to achieve with traditional image retrieval alone.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **BrainCause**, an automated framework composed of three main stages: (i) Causal Dataset Generation, (ii) Concept-Selective Representation Search, and (iii) Validation and Follow-Up Experiment Design. The framework takes a target visual concept and a subject’s image-fMRI dataset as input, and outputs candidate voxel sets along with confidence estimates.

#### 3.1. Causal Dataset Generation

This stage constructs a specialized image-fMRI dataset for the target concept, designed to isolate the concept from correlated visual and semantic factors. This involves both generation using advanced AI models and retrieval from existing datasets.

- **Positive Images:** A large language model (Gemma-3-27B-IT) generates diverse prompts describing the target concept. A text-to-image model (FLUX.2) synthesizes 200 training and 100 validation images per concept from these prompts.
- **Semantic Negative Images:** The language model proposes 10 "counter concepts" that are semantically or visually related to the target but distinct (e.g., for "human hands," alternatives like "human body" or "robot hands"). For each counter concept, the LLM generates 10 prompts, explicitly ensuring the target concept is not mentioned. The text-to-image model synthesizes images, and a vision-language model (Qwen3-VL-8B) verifies the *absence* of the target concept in these images, yielding 80-100 semantic negatives for training and validation.
- **Counterfactual Negative Images:** For 50 training and 20 validation positive images, the language model generates 10 edit instructions per image. An image editing model (FLUX.2) modifies the positive images to remove or replace the target concept while largely preserving other image content (e.g., replacing a human face with an animal face, or removing it entirely). The VLM verifies the successful removal/replacement of the target concept, resulting in 400-500 counterfactual negatives.

All generated images are then passed through a pre-trained image-to-fMRI encoding model (from Beliy et al.), which predicts the corresponding brain responses (predicted fMRI) for each subject. Additionally, BrainCause retrieves positive and semantic-negative images from the measured image-fMRI dataset (Natural Scenes Dataset, NSD) using a CLIP-based retrieval strategy, augmented with VLM verification to ensure concept presence/absence.

#### 3.2. Concept-Selective Representation Search

For each voxel, BrainCause computes three scores based on the generated and retrieved stimuli:

- **Positive Score ($S_{pos}$):** Average activation of the voxel to positive images, measuring response strength.
- **Semantic-Negative Score ($S_{neg}$):** Difference between the positive score and the average activation of the 10 "hardest" semantic negative images (those that activate the voxel most strongly). A high score indicates specificity against semantically related alternatives.
- **Counterfactual Score ($S_{edit}$):** Average difference between the activation for each positive image and its "hardest" counterfactual edited counterpart. A high score indicates that the voxel's response significantly decreases when the concept is removed while other image elements are preserved.

A voxel's overall **causal score ($S_{causal}$)** is defined as the average of its semantic-negative and counterfactual scores. Candidate concept representations are then identified as sets of voxels with positive causal scores, or a predefined number of top-ranked voxels. The discovered region's overall scores are computed by averaging individual voxel scores within the set.

#### 3.3. Validation and Follow-Up Experiment Design

The framework determines a "final verdict" for each concept based on two main factors:

- **Causal Evidence:** This is assessed using the causal scores on both the generated evaluation dataset and the measured-data evaluation set. Statistical tests provide p-values for each score, indicating the confidence of the causal evidence.
- **Concept Coverage in Measured Data:** This quantifies the fraction of positive and semantic-negative images for a concept that could be successfully retrieved and verified from the measured fMRI dataset. This metric indicates how informative the existing measured data is for validating the concept.

Based on these, BrainCause makes a decision: high causal evidence with high measured-data coverage leads to a high-confidence discovery. Low causal evidence leads to rejection. If causal evidence is strong on generated data but measured-data coverage is low, the finding is considered promising but insufficiently validated, triggering recommendations for follow-up fMRI experiments with specific positive examples, semantic negatives, and counterfactual edits to fill data gaps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings, evaluated primarily on the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) across four subjects.

- **High False Positive Rate in Activation-Based Methods:** A crucial finding is that methods based solely on activation maximization exhibit a high false positive rate. For 260 visual concepts, approximately 70% of regions discovered by activation-based methods (e.g., MindSimulator) were identified as false positives under causal evaluation, meaning they responded strongly to the target concept but also maintained high activation for correlated semantic negatives or counterfactual edits (Figure 4a). This confirms the authors' premise that activation alone is insufficient for establishing concept representation.

- **Causal Ranking Reduces False Positives and Enhances Specificity:** BrainCause, by ranking voxels based on a combined causal score, significantly reduced the false positive rate to 23% while increasing the true positive rate (concepts genuinely represented) from 26.6% to 38.7% (Figure 4b). Quantitative comparisons (Table 1) showed that BrainCause achieves comparable activation scores to activation-based methods but substantially improves causal specificity scores across generated semantic negatives, measured semantic negatives, and counterfactual edits. For instance, the semantic negative score on generated data improved from -0.44 (MindSimulator) to 0.62 (BrainCause). This indicates that BrainCause identifies regions that are not only strongly activated by a concept but also specifically responsive to it over correlated alternatives.

- **Alignment with Known Functional Regions:** The framework successfully recovered and localized well-established functional areas for broad visual categories. The top-ranked voxels by BrainCause's causal score showed high alignment with known NSD functional regions for Bodies (99% for top 100 voxels), Faces (90%), Places (74%), and Words (99%) (Table 2). This validation step confirms the method's ability to identify previously known representations reliably.

- **Discovery of Fine-Grained Concept Representations:** BrainCause identified localized and causally supported representations for a broad range of fine-grained concepts beyond traditional categories. Examples include "tools" localizing near body-part and action-related regions, and "animal faces" localizing within known face-selective areas (Figure 5). The analysis also revealed distinct, fine-grained patterns for related concepts, such as human face, human hands, and human legs across face and body-selective regions, and for handwritten text, symbolic signs, and logos within word- and object-related visual areas (Figure 6). This demonstrates the framework's capability to uncover nuanced functional organization in high-level visual cortex.

- **Consistency Across Subjects:** Despite individual variability in brain organization, the causally discovered regions for concepts like "Animal," "Human Interaction," and "Hands in Action" showed clear correspondence in their spatial localization across different subjects (Appendix A.1, Figure S1). This inter-subject consistency strengthens the validity and robustness of the identified causal representations.

- **Analysis of Limitations and False Positives:** While reducing false positives, some remain. These often involve broad image properties like "sky" or "reflection," where semantic-negative generation can be challenging. The authors note cases where LLMs/VLMs failed to generate truly negative examples that exclude the target concept while maintaining semantic similarity (Appendix A.7, Figure S4). This highlights current limitations in generative AI models for creating perfectly controlled stimuli for very abstract or pervasive concepts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for computational neuroscience, experimental design, and the understanding of visual cognition.

- **Advancing Causal Understanding in Neuroscience:** By systematically addressing the correlation-causation gap in visual representation discovery, BrainCause provides a methodological advancement that moves beyond mere observed activation. It establishes a more rigorous standard for claiming that a brain region represents a specific visual concept, enhancing the reliability and interpretability of findings in fMRI studies. This approach brings a form of "causal intervention" to computational fMRI analysis, which has historically been challenging to implement at scale.

- **Refining Brain Mapping and Functional Localization:** The ability to identify fine-grained, causally supported representations allows for a more detailed and accurate mapping of visual cortex. This can lead to a deeper understanding of how complex visual information is hierarchically and semantically organized in the human brain, potentially revealing new functional specializations that were previously obscured by co-occurring factors.

- **Bridging Computational and Experimental Neuroscience:** BrainCause offers a practical framework that closes the loop between computational analysis and experimental design. By identifying gaps in existing measured data (e.g., insufficient positive examples or critical negative controls) and proposing targeted stimuli for follow-up experiments, it provides an actionable roadmap for neuroscientists. This could lead to more efficient and hypothesis-driven fMRI studies, maximizing the utility of valuable scanning time and resources.

- **Leveraging AI for Scientific Discovery:** The work demonstrates a powerful application of advanced generative AI and large language models (LLMs/VLMs) as tools for scientific discovery, not just data analysis. By using these models to intelligently synthesize and edit stimuli, and to reason about counterfactuals, the framework creates an automated experimental design pipeline that can scale to hundreds of concepts, something previously infeasible. This highlights the growing potential for AI to accelerate and refine research in complex domains like neuroscience.

- **Future Directions and Iterative Refinement:** While acknowledging current limitations in AI model capabilities (e.g., semantic negative generation failures), the framework lays the groundwork for future iterative approaches. The ability to analyze failure modes (e.g., false positives linked to broad image properties) can guide further improvements in stimulus generation and causal testing strategies, leading to increasingly robust and comprehensive discovery pipelines.
