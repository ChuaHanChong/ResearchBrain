# Deep Whole-body Parkour

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a unified deep reinforcement learning framework, Deep Whole-body Parkour, allowing humanoid robots to execute dynamic, multi-contact parkour maneuvers by integrating depth perception with whole-body motion tracking. The system achieved robust adaptation to varying environmental conditions and initial robot positions, demonstrated through successful deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot performing skills such as vaulting and climbing.

## Problem

- Perceptive locomotion methods for legged robots were limited to pedal gaits, restricting whole-body multi-contact interactions with environments.
- General motion tracking approaches, while capable of reproducing complex whole-body skills, lacked environment awareness and could not adapt to real-world geometry or variations.
- A gap existed in enabling humanoids to perform dynamic, contact-rich maneuvers on unstructured terrain while perceiving and adapting to the environment's geometry.

## Method

- A deep reinforcement learning framework was developed that integrates exteroceptive depth perception directly into a whole-body motion tracking policy.
- A custom dataset of human parkour motions on physical obstacles was curated and retargeted to the robot, combined with high-fidelity digitized environments.
- Training utilized a massively parallel grouped ray-caster for efficient simulation and an adaptive sampling strategy with stuck detection for robust curriculum learning.

## Results

- Successfully deployed on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating robust execution of complex maneuvers like 'kneel climb' and 'dive roll' in diverse real-world environments without external odometry.
- Policies trained with depth vision achieved a 100% success rate within an aggressive 1.2m x 1.2m out-of-distribution starting position range, significantly outperforming vision-less baselines.
- The system exhibited robustness to unseen distractors in simulation, maintaining high success rates even when novel objects were introduced into the scene.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating depth vision provides a crucial non-trivial benefit, enabling policies to correct for significant deviations in initial robot position and adapt to various environmental conditions.
> - A novel 'relative frame' reward design allows for global adaptation to obstacles while maintaining local skill fidelity, essential for complex, interaction-intensive motions.
> - Robust simulation-to-real-world transfer for depth perception can be achieved by intentionally adding Gaussian noise and patched artifacts to simulated images, mirroring real-world camera issues.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical bifurcation in the field of humanoid control, seeking to bridge two dominant, yet previously disconnected, paradigms: perceptive locomotion and general motion tracking.

**Perceptive Locomotion** focuses on enabling legged robots to navigate complex, unstructured terrain. By integrating proprioceptive data (internal body state) with exteroceptive observations (e.g., depth images, height maps), deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has allowed robots to adapt their gait in real-time. Recent advancements have demonstrated remarkable agility, with quadrupeds and humanoids performing dynamic parkour behaviors like leaping over gaps and climbing obstacles. However, as the authors highlight, this paradigm is fundamentally limited to *pedal gaits*, meaning robots primarily interact with the environment through their feet. The upper body is typically used for balance, not for active, contact-rich interaction. This severely restricts the range of traversable environments and complex maneuvers humanoids could perform.

**General Motion Tracking**, rooted in computer graphics and animation, emphasizes the reproduction of complex, agile skills by imitating reference motion data. Works like DeepMimic and AMP have showcased physically simulated avatars mastering diverse skills, from backflips to martial arts and parkour vaults. More recently, these imitation-based policies have successfully transferred to physical humanoids. The critical limitation here is that these approaches are largely *environment-agnostic*. They blindly track a pre-recorded trajectory without perceiving or reacting to the real-world geometry. A robot attempting a vault might perfectly execute the kinematics but fail due to incorrect timing or hand placement relative to an obstacle it cannot "see."

"Deep Whole-body Parkour" directly confronts this limitation by proposing a unified framework that *integrates exteroceptive depth perception into a whole-body motion tracking framework*. This represents a significant conceptual leap, moving beyond reactive reflexes (perceptive locomotion) and blind reproduction (general motion tracking) to achieve *perceptive general motion control*. The work aims to enable robots not just to mimic a motion, but to *adapt* it dynamically based on the visual occupancy of the environment. This positions the paper at the forefront of research seeking to unlock truly versatile and intelligent humanoid robot behavior in complex, real-world scenarios. It builds upon the successes of prior DRL-based locomotion and motion tracking, while strategically addressing their inherent limitations to create a more comprehensive and robust control system.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind "Deep Whole-body Parkour" stems from the observed limitations of current humanoid control paradigms. The authors identify a clear gap:
1. **Perceptive locomotion is limited to pedal gaits:** While robots can adapt to uneven terrain using their feet, they cannot utilize their full body (e.g., hands, torso) for multi-contact interactions like vaulting, scrambling, or hand-assisted climbing. This restricts their traversability in geometrically constrained environments.
2. **General motion tracking is environment-agnostic:** These methods can reproduce complex whole-body skills but lack the ability to perceive and adapt to the environment. They cannot adjust their movements to the precise geometry of obstacles, leading to failures if the initial conditions or obstacle dimensions deviate from the pre-recorded trajectory.

Given these challenges, the **key objectives** of this work are:

- **To unite perceptive locomotion and general motion tracking:** Develop a framework that enables *perceptive general motion control*, where exteroceptive sensing is directly integrated into whole-body motion tracking.
- **To enable dynamic, multi-contact skills on unstructured terrain:** Allow a humanoid robot to perform highly dynamic, non-locomotion tasks such as vaulting, dive-rolling, kneeling, and climbing, requiring precise interaction with the environment using multiple body parts.
- **To achieve robust adaptation to environmental variations:** Design a policy that can proactively adjust its approach trajectory, foot placement, and hand contacts based on visual feedback (depth perception) to tolerate significant variance in initial conditions (e.g., varying distance or angle to an obstacle).
- **To train a single policy for multiple distinct motions:** Demonstrate the generalizability and non-trivial benefit of integrating perception by having one policy successfully execute various complex maneuvers across different terrestrial features.
- **To ensure real-world deployability:** Create a system that can be deployed on a physical humanoid robot (Unitree G1) in diverse indoor and outdoor environments, emphasizing robustness without reliance on external odometry or precise calibration.

Ultimately, the motivation is to significantly expand a humanoid robot's traversability and capabilities, moving towards human-level agility and interaction in complex, contact-rich environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in "Deep Whole-body Parkour" is a sophisticated, data-driven approach that combines high-fidelity human motion capture, accurate environment digitization, massively parallel reinforcement learning, and careful simulation-to-real-world transfer techniques.

**A. Dataset Curation and Environment Generation:**
To overcome the limitations of existing datasets lacking dynamic, whole-body interactions with complex geometry, the authors curated a custom dataset. Expert human actors performed parkour maneuvers on physical obstacles, recorded by an optical motion capture system. Simultaneously, a LiDAR-enabled iPad Pro digitized the physical scene, ensuring high-fidelity spatial correspondence between the captured motion and the environment. This raw human motion was then retargeted to the Unitree G1 humanoid robot using the GMR framework, involving optimization-based kinematic filtering and manual adjustments to ensure physical feasibility.
For training, the scanned meshes were post-processed to isolate functional geometry (obstacles, platforms) and procedurally instantiated in NVIDIA Isaac Lab. This created a massive-parallel training environment where motion-terrain pairs were randomized in placement, forcing the agent to learn to condition its behavior on local obstacle geometry rather than global scene context.
A key innovation for scalability is the **Massively Parallel Grouped Ray-Caster**. Standard sensor rendering in large-scale simulations struggles with thousands of parallel environments and complex moving articulations without "ghost" perceptions. The authors developed a custom, highly optimized GPU-accelerated ray-caster utilizing Nvidia Warp. It employs mesh instancing and a collision grouping mechanism, assigning static terrain a universal group ID and each robot a unique group ID. A precomputed mapping from group indices to mesh instance IDs significantly reduces the search space during ray-marching (Algorithm 1), resulting in a **10x increase in rendering speed** compared to a naive approach.

**B. Training Settings:**
The policy is trained using Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) with an asymmetric Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. Both proprioceptive data and exteroceptive depth images are used as inputs.
Crucially, the reward design incorporates a novel **relative frame** concept. This frame $T_{rel}$ combines the planar (x-y) position and yaw angle of the robot's actual root with the vertical (z) position, roll, and pitch angles from the reference motion. This allows the robot to adjust its horizontal approach to an obstacle while maintaining the desired whole-body orientation and posture of the reference skill (Figure 3). Reward terms are defined to track global root position/rotation, local link positions/rotations (for key links), and link linear/angular velocities, along with penalties for high action rates, joint limits, undesired contacts, and torque limits.
To facilitate curriculum learning and handle variable-length reference trajectories, an **adaptive sampling** strategy is implemented. Motions are discretized into 1.0s bins, and a failure counter is incremented for the bin corresponding to a failure timestep. Smoothed failure scores are then used to construct a probability distribution for sampling future training reset states (Algorithm 2), ensuring the agent prioritizes learning from high-failure scenarios. An **early timeout with stuck detection** mechanism further prevents training on unsolvable situations by truncating trajectories where the robot becomes stuck at the episode's beginning.
The policy network's inputs include future joint/velocity references (relative to the reference transform), future base position reference (in reference transform), future base rotation reference (in actual robot transform), a single noised depth image, and 8 frames of historical proprioception. Notably, the policy network *does not* receive odometry information. The critic network, used for value function prediction, receives additional privileged information such as actual link transforms and height scan data.

**C. Bridging Sim-to-Real Gap in Depth Perception:**
To ensure successful deployment, the training in simulation closely mimics real-world depth camera characteristics. While the policy trains with synchronized depth images, real-world deployment on a RealSense D435i at 50Hz required specific adaptations. The authors utilize a GPU-based OpenCV implementation for inpainting to meet the 50Hz requirement for filtering. To robustly bridge the sim-to-real gap, Gaussian noise and patched artifacts are intentionally added to simulated depth images, replicating common real-world issues like reflection, motion blur, or stereo algorithm errors (Figure 4).

**D. Deployment:**
The trained policy is deployed onboard the Unitree G1 humanoid robot (29-DOF) using ROS2, with ONNX (CPU version) for neural network acceleration. A state machine mechanism selects the appropriate motion reference. Critical to deployment is the fact that the policy is designed to operate *without any odometry system* providing global position error. Instead, it uses joint/velocity references as trained, and relative rotation differences based on real-time IMU readings after an initial heading alignment of the motion reference to the robot's actual heading. This minimalist approach to external sensing simplifies real-world integration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings that validate the efficacy and robustness of their Deep Whole-body Parkour framework, particularly highlighting the crucial role of integrated depth perception.

**A. Real-World Experiment:**
The system was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot (29-DOF) in diverse real-world indoor and outdoor environments. The robot demonstrated robust execution of complex, multi-contact parkour maneuvers, including 'kneel climb,' 'roll vault,' 'dive roll,' and 'jump sit.' A key achievement here is the ability to operate without an accurate odometry or localization system; the robot simply walks to a rough starting point, and the perceptive policy takes over. This practical demonstration underscores the robustness of the sim-to-real transfer and the viability of their perception-driven control approach.

**B. Non-Trivial Benefit of Depth Vision:**
The integration of depth vision provides a significant "non-trivial" benefit, primarily in **robustness to initial conditions**.
- **Positional Correction:** Experiments showed that policies trained with depth vision could automatically correct for substantial deviations in initial robot position. Even when initialized across a 0.5m × 0.5m horizontal range around the reference motion's starting point, all robots in a batch converged to the target obstacle and successfully completed the task (Figure 7). This positional variance drastically converges over time (Figure 5), illustrating the policy's ability to adjust its approach gait (e.g., shortening or lengthening steps) based on visual feedback.
- **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Start Points:** An aggressive out-of-distribution test involved a grid search around the starting position, covering a 1.2m × 1.2m space. The system demonstrated a **100% success rate** within this wide range, only dropping at the very boundaries (Figure 6). This is a dramatic improvement over environment-agnostic motion tracking, which would fail catastrophically with such perturbations.

**C. Vision Robustness:**
The policy also exhibited **robustness to unseen distractors** in the environment. Various novel objects (e.g., "wide distractor," "plane distractor," "wall distractor") were introduced into the scene during simulation tests, which had never been seen during training (Figure 8).
- For most distractors, no drop in success rate was observed.
- Quantitative evaluation using Mean Positional Joint Position Error (MPJPE) (Table II) showed that while "wide distractor" and "plane distractor" led to a larger MPJPE compared to an undisturbed scene, the robot still successfully completed the motions. The "plane distractor," which significantly altered the potential motion dynamics, had the most pronounced impact on MPJPE, yet the policy still managed the task. This indicates that the visual system effectively processes novel visual information without being overly sensitive to minor, non-critical changes.

**D. Ablation on the Training Recipe:**
Ablation studies clarified the contribution of key components:
- **Training without depth input (BeyondMimic baseline):** This variant was found to be *not robust to initial position perturbation*. It required an exact starting position, leading to catastrophic failures during scene interaction when perturbations were introduced (Table I). This underscores the absolute necessity of depth vision for adaptable interaction.
- **Training without stuck detection:** While still completing motions, this variant resulted in slightly higher MPJPE errors (Table I), indicating that the stuck detection mechanism is beneficial for training efficiency and overall performance by preventing the policy from learning from unrecoverable states.
- **Local frame reward (vs. relative frame):** Policies trained only with local frame rewards tracked locally more accurately but performed worse globally in interaction-intensive cases (Table I). This confirms the importance of the authors' "relative frame" definition, which allows for global adaptation while maintaining local skill fidelity.
The full proposed pipeline consistently achieved the best performance and robustness across all evaluated metrics and conditions, particularly under initial position randomization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"Deep Whole-body Parkour" represents a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, with profound implications for the capabilities and autonomy of these complex machines.

**1. Unification of Paradigms:** The most significant contribution is the successful unification of perceptive locomotion and general motion tracking. By integrating exteroceptive depth perception directly into a whole-body motion tracking framework, the authors have created "perceptive general motion control." This synergy overcomes the limitations of previous approaches, where robots were either visually aware but restricted to pedal gaits or capable of complex whole-body motions but blind to their environment. This breakthrough unlocks a new dimension of intelligent physical interaction for humanoids.

**2. Enhanced Humanoid Agility and Traversability:** The framework dramatically expands the range of dynamic, multi-contact maneuvers a humanoid robot can perform. Skills like vaulting, dive-rolling, kneeling, and climbing, which require precise coordination of the entire body with environmental features, were previously out of reach for robust, autonomous execution on unstructured terrain. This work moves humanoids beyond simple walking or running, enabling them to navigate much more complex and challenging environments akin to human capabilities.

**3. Robustness and Adaptability in Unstructured Environments:** The demonstrated robustness to initial conditions and environmental variations (e.g., different starting distances, unseen distractors) is critical for real-world deployment. The policy's ability to proactively adjust its approach based on visual feedback transforms fragile, fixed-trajectory tracking into robust, spatially-aware capabilities. This adaptive nature is crucial for robots operating outside of controlled laboratory settings, where precise pre-calibration is often impractical.

**4. Scalable Training Infrastructure:** The development of a custom, massively parallel grouped ray-caster, along with adaptive sampling and stuck detection, provides a powerful and efficient infrastructure for training complex, contact-rich humanoid behaviors. This scalable training pipeline is essential for expanding the repertoire of skills and accelerating future research in whole-body control.

**5. Reduced Deployment Complexity:** The ability to deploy the policy without an accurate global odometry or localization system significantly simplifies real-world implementation. By leveraging relative frame tracking and onboard IMU/depth sensing, the robot can operate more autonomously without relying on external infrastructure or intricate setup procedures.

**6. Foundation for Future AI in Robotics:** This work establishes a strong baseline for developing general-purpose humanoid controllers capable of mastering contact-rich environments in a fully learned, end-to-end manner. It lays the groundwork for future research into autonomous skill selection, where a robot could intelligently choose and adapt appropriate parkour maneuvers based on its perception of the environment. The open-sourced infrastructure further encourages collaboration and accelerates advancements in the field.

In summary, "Deep Whole-body Parkour" represents a pivotal step towards creating truly agile, intelligent, and autonomous humanoid robots that can interact with the physical world in a highly dynamic and adaptable manner.
