# Think or Not Think: A Study of Explicit Thinking in Rule-Based Visual Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> A study of thinking in rule-based visual reinforcement fine-tuning for multi-modal large language models reveals that explicit reasoning processes are not always necessary and can even be detrimental, especially for visual perception tasks. For 2B models, a "no-thinking" strategy achieved a 3.14% higher average accuracy in image classification and offered significant computational efficiency over thinking-based methods.

## Problem

- Conventional rule-based reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for large language models (LLMs) heavily relies on explicit "thinking" processes like Chain-of-Thought, assuming this translates universally to Multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs).
- Thinking-based RFT introduces significant computational overhead, including longer fine-tuning times and higher GPU memory requirements, due to generating lengthy reasoning responses.
- The necessity and specific impact of explicit thinking during MLLM RFT were largely unexplored, especially considering observations of "overthinking" phenomena during LLM/MLLM inference.

## Method

- Introduced =="No-Thinking-RFT"== where models are strictly prompted to output direct answers without explicit reasoning, utilizing an ==equality-based accuracy reward== to penalize extraneous text.
- Proposed =="Think-After-Answer"== and =="Adaptive-Thinking"== variants to investigate the timing and strategic adoption of thinking, all implemented within the GRPO framework.
- Conducted systematic comparative studies across diverse MLLM models (Qwen2-VL-2B/7B, InternVL2.5) and a wide range of visual and multi-modal tasks, including image classification and various reasoning benchmarks.

## Results

- No-Thinking-RFT consistently outperformed Thinking-RFT for visual perception tasks, achieving an average of **3.14%** higher accuracy for **2B** models in image classification.
- No-Thinking-RFT demonstrated substantial computational efficiency, with significantly faster fine-tuning and inference times, and quicker reward convergence compared to Thinking-RFT.
- Thinking-RFT responses frequently showed inconsistencies between the generated reasoning and the final answer, particularly in mid-sized models on reasoning tasks, impacting overall accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal "thinking" strategy in MLLM RFT is highly dependent on both the model's inherent capability and the specific task complexity; smaller models and visual perception tasks often do not benefit from explicit thinking.
> - Explicit thinking processes that occur *before* verifiable answers can hinder reward convergence during fine-tuning, suggesting a "fine-tuning overthinking" phenomenon.
> - MLLMs can adaptively learn to decide *when* to engage in a thinking process during RFT, converging to strategies that align with their capabilities and task requirements.
