# Think or Not Think: A Study of Explicit Thinking in Rule-Based Visual Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> A study of thinking in rule-based visual reinforcement fine-tuning for multi-modal large language models reveals that explicit reasoning processes are not always necessary and can even be detrimental, especially for visual perception tasks. For 2B models, a "no-thinking" strategy achieved a 3.14% higher average accuracy in image classification and offered significant computational efficiency over thinking-based methods.

## Problem

- Conventional rule-based reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for large language models (LLMs) heavily relies on explicit "thinking" processes like Chain-of-Thought, assuming this translates universally to Multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs).
- Thinking-based RFT introduces significant computational overhead, including longer fine-tuning times and higher GPU memory requirements, due to generating lengthy reasoning responses.
- The necessity and specific impact of explicit thinking during MLLM RFT were largely unexplored, especially considering observations of "overthinking" phenomena during LLM/MLLM inference.

## Method

- Introduced =="No-Thinking-RFT"== where models are strictly prompted to output direct answers without explicit reasoning, utilizing an ==equality-based accuracy reward== to penalize extraneous text.
- Proposed =="Think-After-Answer"== and =="Adaptive-Thinking"== variants to investigate the timing and strategic adoption of thinking, all implemented within the GRPO framework.
- Conducted systematic comparative studies across diverse MLLM models (Qwen2-VL-2B/7B, InternVL2.5) and a wide range of visual and multi-modal tasks, including image classification and various reasoning benchmarks.

## Results

- No-Thinking-RFT consistently outperformed Thinking-RFT for visual perception tasks, achieving an average of **3.14%** higher accuracy for **2B** models in image classification.
- No-Thinking-RFT demonstrated substantial computational efficiency, with significantly faster fine-tuning and inference times, and quicker reward convergence compared to Thinking-RFT.
- Thinking-RFT responses frequently showed inconsistencies between the generated reasoning and the final answer, particularly in mid-sized models on reasoning tasks, impacting overall accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal "thinking" strategy in MLLM RFT is highly dependent on both the model's inherent capability and the specific task complexity; smaller models and visual perception tasks often do not benefit from explicit thinking.
> - Explicit thinking processes that occur *before* verifiable answers can hinder reward convergence during fine-tuning, suggesting a "fine-tuning overthinking" phenomenon.
> - MLLMs can adaptively learn to decide *when* to engage in a thinking process during RFT, converging to strategies that align with their capabilities and task requirements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the crucial intersection of multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) and reinforcement learning (RL) for model fine-tuning. The broader landscape can be understood through several key developments:

Firstly, **Rule-Based Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) in LLMs** has gained significant traction. Pioneering works like Deepseek-R1, Kimi K1.5, and OpenAI O1 have demonstrated that RFT, by leveraging verifiable rewards, can effectively incentivize reasoning capabilities in large language models, often outperforming traditional supervised fine-tuning (SFT). A central tenet of these successes was the belief that an explicit "thinking process" (e.g., Chain-of-Thought, CoT) generated by the model before answering was crucial for solution exploration and achieving "aha moments."

Secondly, the rapid emergence of **MLLMs** has extended these concepts to multi-modal domains, allowing models to process and reason over both text and images. Researchers have naturally sought to apply RFT principles to MLLMs, aiming to replicate the reasoning and "aha moment" effects observed in their text-only counterparts (e.g., Vision-R1, Visual-RFT, MM-Eureka). However, the specific role and necessity of explicit thinking within MLLM RFT had remained largely unexplored.

Thirdly, concurrent research in **"overthinking" phenomena** in both LLMs and MLLMs at the *inference stage* has introduced a counter-narrative. Studies have suggested that excessive or unnecessary reasoning can sometimes degrade performance, particularly for simpler tasks or when reasoning leads to hallucinations or inconsistencies. This raises a critical question: if overthinking can harm inference, could explicit thinking *during the fine-tuning process* also be detrimental in certain contexts?

This paper directly addresses this gap by rigorously investigating the impact of explicit thinking *during the RFT fine-tuning process* for MLLMs. It challenges the conventional assumption that explicit thinking is universally beneficial, particularly for models with varying capabilities and across diverse tasks. By introducing "No-Thinking-RFT" and exploring adaptive strategies, the work aims to refine our understanding of effective RFT for MLLMs, moving beyond simple replication of LLM RFT phenomena to a more nuanced, efficiency-aware approach. The application to image classification, where MLLMs have historically struggled compared to specialized vision models, further broadens the scope of RFT applicability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to thoroughly investigate the necessity and benefits of an explicit thinking process in rule-based reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) for multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) across a spectrum of tasks and model sizes.

The motivations driving this investigation are multi-faceted:

- **Challenging Conventional Wisdom:** Rule-based RFT has shown remarkable success in LLMs, largely attributed to incentivizing an explicit thinking process before generating answers, which is believed to facilitate deeper solution exploration and lead to "aha moments." This perception has widely influenced the extension of RFT to MLLMs, with many efforts focusing on reproducing these explicit thinking behaviors. However, the authors question whether this explicit thinking is *always* essential and advantageous, especially for smaller MLLMs or specific task types.

- **Addressing Computational Inefficiency:** Thinking-based RFT typically involves generating lengthy reasoning responses, which significantly increases fine-tuning time and GPU memory requirements compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT). This computational overhead necessitates a critical examination of whether the benefits of explicit thinking justify its costs.

- **Bridging the Gap with Inference-Time "Overthinking" Studies:** Recent research has highlighted that "overthinking" during MLLM and LLM *inference* can sometimes degrade performance, particularly on commonsense tasks where elaborate reasoning is not required or can lead to errors. While these studies focus on inference, their findings prompt the authors to explore whether a similar phenomenon might occur *during the RFT fine-tuning stage*, potentially hindering learning or convergence.

- **Exploring MLLM Capabilities for Image Classification:** The paper initially extends Thinking-RFT to image classification, an area where MLLMs have traditionally shown weaker performance compared to specialized vision models due to pre-training data limitations. This application serves as a concrete case study to observe RFT's impact and subsequently question the role of thinking within this context.

Based on these motivations, the specific objectives are:

1. **Extend Thinking-RFT to Image Classification:** To apply the existing thinking-based RFT paradigm to image classification tasks for MLLMs, using verifiable rewards (class labels) and evaluate its performance against SFT.
2. **Introduce and Evaluate No-Thinking-RFT:** To propose an RFT approach that explicitly discourages any thinking process, utilizing a direct-answer prompt and a strict equality-based accuracy reward. The goal is to compare its performance and efficiency against Thinking-RFT.
3. **Investigate the Impact of Thinking Order (Think-After-Answer):** To test the hypothesis that explicit thinking *before* verifiable answers might hinder reward convergence and reduce performance by proposing a variant where thinking occurs *after* the answer is generated.
4. **Explore Adaptive Thinking Strategies (Adaptive-Thinking):** To conduct a pilot study on whether MLLMs can learn to adaptively decide when to engage in a thinking process based on problem requirements and their own capabilities during RFT.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic comparative study using various RFT methodologies on diverse MLLM models and tasks.

#### 3.1. Core Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) Framework

- **Optimization Algorithm:** The authors adopt Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant of policy gradient methods, for all RFT experiments. GRPO, following Deepseek-R1, estimates relative advantage by sampling a group of responses and normalizing their rewards, offering computational efficiency over methods requiring a separate critic model.
- **Training Strategy:** All RFT methods utilize R1-zero training, meaning they apply RL directly to base models *without* initial supervised fine-tuning (SFT), following established practices to isolate the effects of RL.

#### 3.2. RFT Variants

- **Thinking-RFT (Baseline):**
 - **Instruction Prompt:** Encourages the model to generate a thinking process within `<think>` tags before providing the final answer in `<answer>` tags.
 - **Reward Function:** A binary reward (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) composed of two parts: a *format reward* (ensuring correct tag usage) and an *accuracy reward* (verifying the answer in the `<answer>` tag against the ground truth).

- **No-Thinking-RFT (Proposed):**
 - **Instruction Prompt:** Explicitly instructs the model to "directly output the answer," omitting any thinking process.
 - **Reward Function:** Eliminates the format reward and relies solely on a strict *equality accuracy reward*. A score of 1 is granted only if the model's output precisely matches the ground truth, effectively penalizing any extraneous text, including reasoning. This design inherently discourages thought generation and shortens responses.

- **Think-After-Answer (Hypothesis Testing):**
 - **Instruction Prompt:** Prompts the MLLM to first output the answer in `<answer>` tags, followed by a brief reasoning process in `<reason>` tags.
 - **Reward Function:** Maintains the Thinking-RFT reward structure (format + accuracy). This variant is designed to test if placing thinking *after* the verifiable answer alleviates potential negative impacts on reward convergence.

- **Adaptive-Thinking (Pilot Study):**
 - **Instruction Prompt:** Asks the model to first assess if a problem requires thinking. If so, it generates a thinking process before answering; otherwise, it answers directly.
 - **Reward Function:** A format reward of 1 is given for *either* the thinking format (`<think>...</think><answer>...</answer>`) or the direct answering format (`<answer>...</answer>`). The accuracy reward remains the same as Thinking-RFT. This setup allows the model to learn and converge to a preferred response strategy.

#### 3.3. Experimental Tasks and Datasets

The methodologies are evaluated on a comprehensive suite of visual and multi-modal tasks:

- **Image Classification:** A case study on 11 diverse public benchmarks (e.g., Caltech101, ImageNet, Food101, DTD, EuroSAT, UCF101). Experiments include few-shot learning, base-to-new generalization, and open-set classification.
- **General Visual Reasoning:**
 - **Visual Perception/Spatial Understanding:** SAT (training) and CVBench (testing), which involves tasks like counting, relation, depth, and distance.
 - **Multi-Modal Math Reasoning:** Math-40K (training) and MathVista, MathVision (testing).
 - **Visual Puzzle Reasoning:** PuzzleVQA (in-domain) and AlgoPuzzleVQA (out-of-domain).
- **Other Tasks (in Appendix):** Referring expression comprehension (Refcoco/+/g, LISA-Grounding) and few-shot object detection (COCO subset).

#### 3.4. Model Architectures and Setup

- **Base Models:** Primarily Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct. Additional experiments use InternVL2.5 (1B, 4B) and Qwen2-5-VL-Instruct (3B, 7B) to ensure generalizability across different MLLM types and sizes.
- **Implementation Details:** All parameters are fine-tuned. Training is conducted on 8 A100 GPUs with a batch size of 1 per GPU and 2-step gradient accumulation. Specific hyperparameters (β=0.04, learning rate=1e-6, temperature=1.0) and image/response lengths are standardized across methods for fair comparison.

#### 3.5. Analysis Techniques

- **Quantitative Metrics:** Accuracy for classification and reasoning tasks, fine-tuning/inference time, convergence speed (accuracy reward curves).
- **Qualitative Analysis:** Examination of model responses for trivial reasoning, inconsistencies between thinking and answer tags, and adaptive strategy adoption. GPT-4o is used to extract answers from thinking tags for consistency checks.
- **Parameter Change Analysis:** L1 norm of parameter differences to understand where changes occur (modality, module, layer levels) with different RFT strategies.

This comprehensive approach allows for a robust investigation into the role of explicit thinking in MLLM RFT, moving beyond simple performance comparisons to deeper insights into learning dynamics and model behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several significant findings across various tasks and model scales, challenging prevailing assumptions about explicit thinking in MLLM RFT:

#### 4.1. Findings from Image Classification Tasks (Case Study)

- **Superiority of RFT over SFT:** Both Thinking-RFT and No-Thinking-RFT significantly outperformed traditional supervised fine-tuning (SFT) across most classification datasets in few-shot and base-to-new generalization settings. This validates RFT's effectiveness in teaching MLLMs downstream knowledge beyond simple memorization.
- **No-Thinking-RFT Outperforms Thinking-RFT in Classification:** For 2B models, No-Thinking-RFT consistently surpassed Thinking-RFT in 10 out of 11 datasets, achieving a 3.14% higher average accuracy. This performance advantage, though narrower, persisted for 7B models. This suggests that for visual perception tasks like classification, explicit thinking is often unnecessary and can even be detrimental.
- **"Free-Lunch" Phenomenon:** Both Thinking-RFT and No-Thinking-RFT exhibited cross-dataset generalization. Fine-tuning an MLLM on one dataset often improved its performance on other datasets with different data distributions and class names, countering the "catastrophic forgetting" observed in some VLM fine-tuning methods. No-Thinking-RFT generally showed better cross-dataset generalization than Thinking-RFT.
- **Efficiency and Convergence:** No-Thinking-RFT demonstrated substantial computational efficiency advantages, requiring significantly less fine-tuning and inference time and faster reward convergence compared to Thinking-RFT. This is primarily due to the shorter, direct responses generated by No-Thinking-RFT.

#### 4.2. Key Findings from General Visual Reasoning Tasks

The evaluation on tasks like spatial reasoning (CVBench), multi-modal math (MathVista, MathVision), and visual puzzles (PuzzleVQA, AlgoPuzzleVQA) across 2B and 7B Qwen2-VL-Instruct models led to four crucial insights:

- **Finding 1: Small Models Struggle with Meaningful Thinking:** For models with limited capabilities (e.g., 2B), Thinking-RFT often led to trivial or unhelpful reasoning processes. Consequently, No-Thinking-RFT significantly outperformed Thinking-RFT on most benchmarks for 2B models, including visual perception and many math subtasks, while also being faster. This implies that weak models cannot generate high-quality Chain-of-Thought (CoT) beneficial for RFT.
- **Finding 2: Task-Dependent Necessity of Thinking:** Visual perception (spatial reasoning, classification) and puzzle tasks generally *do not require explicit thinking* during RFT. No-Thinking-RFT either matched or outperformed Thinking-RFT across both 2B and 7B models on these tasks. Conversely, multi-modal math reasoning (e.g., MathVista, MathVision) for larger models (7B) *did* benefit from Thinking-RFT, suggesting that complex computational problems might still necessitate explicit reasoning.
- **Finding 3: Inconsistencies Between Thinking and Answer Tags:** Thinking-RFT responses, particularly from mid-sized models (7B) on reasoning tasks, frequently showed inconsistencies between the content in the `<think>` and `<answer>` tags. The accuracy of answers within inconsistent responses was lower than the overall accuracy, suggesting that maintaining internal consistency could improve performance.
- **Finding 4: Sources of No-Thinking-RFT's Performance Gain:** The superior performance of No-Thinking-RFT primarily stems from two factors: (1) *improved learning during fine-tuning* due to direct reward signals on answers, and (2) *avoidance of inference overthinking*. When Thinking-RFT was forced into a "no-thinking" inference mode (by appending empty thinking tags), performance improved on perception tasks but still lagged behind No-Thinking-RFT, confirming the dual source of its benefits.

#### 4.3. Further Experiments and Hypothesis Verification

- **Finding 5: Explicit Thinking Before Answers Can Hinder Convergence:** The proposed Think-After-Answer method, where reasoning follows the answer, outperformed Thinking-RFT in perception and puzzle tasks, exhibiting faster reward convergence and higher accuracy. This supports the hypothesis that explicit thinking *before* verifiable answers can impede the learning process and slow reward convergence in certain scenarios. However, it still underperformed No-Thinking-RFT in some cases, indicating that even post-answer reasoning can sometimes be detrimental during RFT.
- **Finding 6: Adaptive Learning of Thinking Strategy:** The Adaptive-Thinking method showed that MLLMs can learn to decide whether to employ a thinking process during RFT. Models initially explored both thinking and non-thinking responses but consistently converged to a single strategy that aligned with their capability and task complexity (e.g., 2B models gravitated towards non-thinking for math, while 7B models adopted thinking for math). This suggests MLLMs possess the capacity to adaptively choose their reasoning approach.

#### 4.4. Parameter Change During RL

Analysis of L1 norm changes in Qwen2VL-2B models showed that language weights drifted more than visual weights. MLP components underwent the most changes. Thinking-RFT induced weight drift towards deeper layers, consistent with back-propagating rewards through a reasoning chain. In contrast, No-Thinking-RFT showed peak drift in early-mid layers, suggesting a reshaping of low-level features for a more direct path to reward tokens.

Overall, the results robustly demonstrate that explicit thinking, while beneficial for complex reasoning in larger models, is not universally required and can even be counterproductive for visual perception and puzzle tasks, especially for smaller MLLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant implications for the development and fine-tuning of multi-modal large language models, challenging a prevalent paradigm and opening new avenues for more efficient and intelligent AI.

#### 5.1. Rethinking the Role of Explicit Thinking in RFT

The most profound significance of this work is its direct challenge to the widely held belief that explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) thinking is a universal prerequisite for successful rule-based RFT in large models. By demonstrating that for many MLLM tasks, particularly visual perception and certain puzzles, "no thinking" or "think-after-answer" strategies can be equally or more effective, the paper mandates a re-evaluation of RFT design principles. This shift in perspective could lead to more nuanced and task-specific RFT implementations.

#### 5.2. Enhancing Computational Efficiency

The substantial reduction in fine-tuning and inference time, coupled with lower GPU memory requirements, achieved by No-Thinking-RFT, has a direct and positive impact on the accessibility and sustainability of MLLM research and deployment. For smaller models and resource-constrained environments, this efficiency gain is critical, enabling faster experimentation cycles and more practical application of advanced MLLM capabilities. It allows for broader participation in MLLM development beyond institutions with vast computational resources.

#### 5.3. Deeper Understanding of MLLM Learning Dynamics

The study provides valuable empirical insights into how MLLMs learn and process information under different reward structures.
- **Model Capability and Task Complexity:** The findings highlight that the optimal thinking strategy is contingent on both the model's inherent capacity and the nature of the task. Weaker models benefit from directness, while stronger models might leverage reasoning for truly complex problems. This understanding is crucial for designing more intelligent and adaptive MLLMs.
- **Reward Convergence and Overthinking:** The "Think-After-Answer" and "No-Thinking-RFT" experiments illuminate how the placement and presence of explicit reasoning steps can affect reward signal propagation and model convergence during training. This insight into "fine-tuning overthinking" complements existing research on "inference overthinking."
- **Cross-Dataset Generalization:** The discovery of a "free-lunch" phenomenon, where RFT on one dataset improves performance on others, is significant. It suggests that RFT, especially with verifiable rewards, encourages MLLMs to learn fundamental, transferable knowledge rather than mere memorization, a key step towards truly generalizable AI.

#### 5.4. Towards Adaptive and Intelligent Reasoning

The pilot study on Adaptive-Thinking, demonstrating MLLMs' ability to learn *when* to think, is a forward-looking contribution. This suggests a future where MLLMs can dynamically assess problem complexity and their own capabilities to choose the most efficient and effective reasoning strategy. Such self-aware reasoning could lead to MLLMs that are not only powerful but also optimally efficient, reducing unnecessary computations and improving reliability.

#### 5.5. Potential for Broader Application

The comprehensive evaluation across diverse visual reasoning tasks, including classification, spatial understanding, math, and puzzles, indicates that these findings are not task-specific but reflect a more general principle in MLLM behavior. This broadened understanding can inform RFT strategies for other multi-modal and complex reasoning tasks.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust empirical foundation for optimizing RFT strategies for MLLMs. By questioning established norms and proposing efficient alternatives, it pushes the frontier of MLLM development towards models that are not only capable but also highly efficient and adaptively intelligent in their reasoning processes. Future work focusing on problem-level adaptation and consistency enhancement will build upon these foundational insights, paving the way for more sophisticated and practical MLLM applications.
