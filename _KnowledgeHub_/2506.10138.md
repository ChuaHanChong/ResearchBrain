# Path Channels and Plan Extension Kernels: a Mechanistic Description of Planning in a Sokoban RNN

> [!summary] Summary
> An investigation into a model-free reinforcement learning agent for Sokoban mechanistically elucidated its planning process, identifying internal "path channels" that represent future movements and a bidirectional planning algorithm built from specialized convolutional kernels. This work revealed mechanisms for plan extension, stopping, backtracking, and a Winner-Takes-All selection system, which consistently emerged across multiple trained networks.

## Problem

- Lack of transparency in deep neural networks, especially agentic models, hindering understanding of their complex decision-making and preventing predictability.
- The challenge of understanding how model-free reinforcement learning agents achieve long-horizon planning without explicitly programmed planning components.
- Difficulty in conducting detailed, weight-level mechanistic interpretability for complex, high-performing networks beyond simpler "toy" models.

## Method

- Conducted a mechanistic ==reverse-engineering== of a DRC(3,3) convolutional recurrent neural network trained on Sokoban using qualitative inspection, quantitative validation, and causal interventions.
- Identified specific "path channels" in the hidden state that directly represent future agent and box movements, distinguishing between short-term and long-term planning horizons.
- Analyzed convolutional kernels to uncover a learned bidirectional planning algorithm, detailing mechanisms for plan extension, stopping, backtracking, and path selection.

## Results

- Fifty-nine "path channels" were identified, directly representing future agent and box movements; ablating these channels reduced the solve rate by **57.6%**.
- A bidirectional planning algorithm was characterized, comprising encoder kernels for initialization, plan extension kernels for propagation, obstacle-based stopping mechanisms, and a Winner-Takes-All mechanism for path selection.
- These planning mechanisms consistently emerged early in training and were replicated across five independently trained networks, demonstrating their robust and common learning trajectory.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Model-free reinforcement learning agents can implicitly learn sophisticated planning algorithms, including bidirectional search, through specialized internal circuits and recurrent computations.
> - The network develops an internal, value-like representation within path channels that implicitly prioritizes path length, suggesting characteristics of a learned "mesa-optimizer."
> - Detailed, weight-level mechanistic interpretability for complex, high-performing neural networks is feasible, moving beyond probe-based or localized feature analyses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The study operates within the field of mechanistic interpretability, aiming to understand the internal workings of complex deep neural networks. A fundamental challenge in modern artificial intelligence is the "black box" nature of deep learning models, particularly when applied to agentic tasks involving sequences of actions to achieve a goal. Unforeseen behaviors in such agents can have significant consequences, motivating research into understanding their internal algorithms.

Sokoban, a grid-based box-pushing puzzle, serves as a popular benchmark for planning due to its PSPACE-complete complexity and requirement for long-term strategic thought. Prior work has established the Deep Repeating ConvLSTM (DRC) architecture as a high-performing model-free reinforcement learning approach for Sokoban, rivaling some model-based agents. Guez et al. (2019) introduced the DRC family, observing that its performance on Sokoban improved with additional "thinking time" (repeated observations), suggesting the emergence of internal planning. Subsequent research by Bush et al. (2025) and Taufeeque et al. (2024) employed linear probes to extract causal representations of plans within the DRC, qualitatively proposing that the network performs a form of bidirectional search.

This paper extends these investigations by moving beyond probe-based analyses to provide a direct, weight-level mechanistic explanation of the DRC's planning process. It aims to substantially reverse-engineer the neural mechanisms, a level of detail often achieved for simpler, toy models (e.g., modular addition, binary addition, small language transformers, pathfinding in binary trees) or for specific, localized features in larger models (e.g., vision models, large language models). The current work endeavors to characterize a more complex network, the DRC(3,3), in terms of its complete planning algorithm, thereby pushing the "Pareto frontier between complexity of a network and the detail of its characterization." It also relates to the concept of "mesa-optimizers," where an AI learns to pursue internal goals, potentially distinct from its training objective, a topic of interest for AI safety research concerning "goal misgeneralization."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to partially reverse-engineer a convolutional recurrent neural network (RNN) (specifically, the DRC(3,3) architecture) trained with model-free reinforcement learning to play Sokoban. This reverse-engineering aims to provide a mechanistic description of how the network constructs and executes plans.

The key objectives can be broken down as follows:
- **Identify and characterize the plan representation:** To determine how the RNN internally stores and represents anticipated future moves (plans) and states. This includes identifying specific internal components (e.g., channels) responsible for this representation and distinguishing between different temporal horizons of planning (short-term vs. long-term).
- **Mechanistically understand plan construction:** To analyze the neural mechanisms, specifically the convolutional kernels and their interactions, that are responsible for initializing, extending, updating, and refining these internal plans. This involves understanding how plans propagate, how obstacles are handled, and how alternative plans are evaluated and selected.
- **Explain complex behaviors in familiar terms:** To translate the observed neural computations into a human-understandable algorithmic description, such as a bidirectional planning-like algorithm with components resembling aspects of a transition model, value function, and search heuristics.
- **Contribute to the understanding of agentic AI:** By elucidating the internal planning process of a high-performing agent, the work seeks to address concerns about the unpredictability and inscrutability of advanced AI systems. This understanding is motivated by the need to foresee the consequences of agent actions, debug failures, and ensure alignment with specified goals, particularly in the context of learned mesa-optimizers.

The overarching motivation stems from the broader challenge of transparency in deep learning. For agentic AI systems that perform complex action sequences, understanding their internal decision-making is critical for trustworthiness and safety. This study aims to demonstrate that such understanding is attainable, even for model-free reinforcement learning agents which are not explicitly programmed with planning algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers employed a combination of qualitative inspection, quantitative validation, and causal intervention to reverse-engineer the DRC(3,3) network.

#### 3.1. Model Under Study

The subject of analysis is an open-source DRC(3,3) network, a convolutional recurrent neural network architecture. It was trained using model-free reinforcement learning with the IMPALA V-trace actor-critic algorithm for 2 billion environment steps on 900,000 levels from the unfiltered train set of the Boxoban Sokoban dataset. The architecture comprises a convolutional encoder, a stack of three ConvLSTM layers (each performing three recurrent computations per environment timestep, totaling nine sequential computation layers), and an MLP head for policy and value function prediction.

#### 3.2. Identifying Plan Representation (Path Channels)

- **Manual Channel Inspection:** The initial phase involved manual inspection of activations across all hidden state channels (h0, h1, h2) to identify patterns correlating with future agent or box movements. This led to the classification of channels into groups: Box-movement, Agent-movement, Combined Path, Grid Next Action (GNA), Pooled Next Action (PNA), Entity, and ‘no label’ (Table 1). The Box-movement, Agent-movement, and Combined Path channels were collectively termed "path channels."
- **Quantitative Validation:**
 - **Single-Step Cache Ablation:** To assess the causal role of path channels in long-term planning, the previous values of specific hidden state channels were set to zero, forcing the network to recompute activations based only on the current observation and non-ablated channels. The impact on the solve rate was measured.
 - **Area Under the Curve (AUC) Analysis:** Each path channel's ability to predict future box or agent movements was quantified using AUC scores, evaluated for varying numbers of future timesteps. This helped differentiate between "short-term" (predictive up to ~10 steps) and "long-term" (predictive beyond ~10 steps) channels.
 - **Causal Intervention:** Channel activations were programmatically modified based on their hypothesized labels to observe if this intervention successfully altered the agent's subsequent actions, thereby confirming the causal link between channel activity and planned movement.
 - **Linear Regression for Label Verification:** Linear regression was performed using base features (agent, box, target locations) and future movement features to predict channel activations. This aided in validating channel labels, computing spatial offsets, and estimating correlation.
 - **Cross-Seed Replication:** An automated method based on AUC scores was developed to label channels, then applied to four additional independently trained DRC(3,3) networks to confirm the generality of the identified path channels and mechanisms.

#### 3.3. Analyzing Planning Algorithm Mechanisms

- **Encoder Kernel Analysis:** The convolutional operations of the encoder and the first gates of ConvLSTMs were combined into single kernels (W^d_oe) to visualize how raw RGB input observations initialize path channel activations (Figure 6).
- **Plan Extension Kernels:** The recurrent weight matrices (W^d_ch1 and W^d_ch2) within the ConvLSTM layers were analyzed to identify "plan extension kernels." These kernels were found to propagate plan activations linearly (linear plan extension kernels) or enable turns (turn plan extension kernels) (Figure 7).
- **Stopping Mechanism Analysis:** The sources of negative contributions to path channels (from encoder or static entity channels) were identified to understand how plan extension is halted at obstacles or targets (Figure 8).
- **Backtracking Mechanism:** The propagation of negative activations along paths via backward plan extension kernels was studied to explain how the network prunes unpromising paths and enables exploration of alternatives (Figure 16).
- **Winner-Takes-All (WTA) Mechanism:** The inhibitory connections between short-term path channels were investigated to understand how the network selects a single path when multiple options exist, ensuring a dominant direction for immediate action (Figure 10).
- **Activation Transfer Mechanism:** The role of the j-gate in transferring plan activations from long-term to short-term channels after preceding actions are executed was analyzed (Figure 15).
- **Weight Steering:** As an interventional experiment, the weights of the plan extension kernels (W^d_ch1 and W^d_ch2) were scaled to observe the effect on the agent's ability to solve larger, more complex levels, testing the hypothesis about their role in path length (Appendix L).
- **Emergence during Training:** The evolution of the WTA mechanism (self-connection and cross-inhibition strengths) was tracked across training checkpoints to understand its developmental trajectory (Appendix M).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Direct Plan Representation in Path Channels

The study identified specific channels within the DRC(3,3) network's hidden state (h0, h1, h2) that directly represent future agent and box movements, termed "path channels." This eliminates the need for external linear probes used in previous work.
- **Channel Grouping:** Channels were categorized into Box-movement, Agent-movement, and Combined Path channels, which collectively form the plan. Other channels (Grid Next Action, Pooled Next Action, Entity, and No Label) handle immediate action selection or represent environmental features (Table 1).
- **Temporal Horizons:** Path channels decompose into short-term (predictive for ~0-10 steps) and long-term (predictive for ~10-50 steps) channels, allowing the network to manage spatially overlapping plans for different times (Figure 4, 5).
- **Quantitative Validation:**
 - Ablating the 59 identified path channels resulted in a substantial 57.6% drop in solve rate, significantly higher than the 10.5% drop from ablating 37 non-path channels.
 - Path channels exhibited high AUC scores for predicting future movements, confirming their predictive power (Figure 4).
 - Causal interventions demonstrated high success rates in altering agent actions by manipulating path channel activations, particularly for Pooled Next Action (99.7%) and Grid Next Action (98.9%) channels (Table 2).
- **Plan-to-Action Mapping:** The plan from path channels is transferred to agent-movement channels, then filtered through Grid Next Action (GNA) channels (extracting the next move at the agent's square) and finally Pooled Next Action (PNA) channels (pooling the GNA to the entire grid) before being fed to the MLP for action prediction (Figure 14).

#### 4.2. Bidirectional Planning Algorithm

The research uncovered a bidirectional planning-like algorithm constructed through the interaction of these path channels and specialized convolutional kernels.
- **Initialization:** Combined encoder kernels (mapping raw observation to ConvLSTM gates) detect key features (agent, box, target) and initialize path channel activations near these objects, serving as starting points for plan segments (Figure 6).
- **Plan Extension Kernels:** Within the recurrent weight matrices of ConvLSTMs, "plan extension kernels" propagate these path segments. Linear kernels extend plans one square at a time along a given direction, while "turn plan extension kernels" facilitate changes in direction. These kernels enable both forward chaining from boxes and backward chaining from targets (Figure 7, 10).
- **Plan Stopping:** Plan extension is not indefinite. Negative contributions from the encoder and "entity" channels (representing static features like walls) create stopping signals at appropriate boundaries (e.g., walls, targets, squares adjacent to boxes), preventing impossible moves (Figure 8).
- **Backtracking Mechanism:** The same plan extension kernels facilitate backtracking. Negative activations, generated when a path encounters an obstacle, propagate backward along the path via the kernels. This prunes unpromising path fragments, allowing the network to discard failed plans and explore alternative routes (Figure 16).
- **Winner-Takes-All (WTA) Mechanism:** To resolve conflicts when multiple viable paths exist for a box, a WTA mechanism operates among short-term path channels. Specific kernels connecting channels of different directions cause mutual inhibition, allowing the path with stronger activation to dominate and suppress alternatives, thereby stabilizing a chosen plan (Figure 9, 10).
- **State Transition Update:** After an action, the network updates the plan representation. Encoder kernels contribute negative activations to the previously executed path segment based on the new observation, effectively "deleting" the completed move from the plan.
- **Long-to-Short Term Transfer:** A mechanism primarily mediated through the j-gate allows plan information to transfer from long-term channels to short-term channels only after preceding actions, which previously inhibited the short-term channel, are completed (Figure 15).

#### 4.3. Emergence and Stability of Mechanisms

- **Developmental Trajectory:** Analysis of training checkpoints revealed that the WTA mechanism emerges rapidly and distinctly early in training (around 19M steps, less than 1% of total training), transitioning from no structure to a fully functional WTA pattern (Figure 19).
- **Cross-Seed Consistency:** The identified path channels, plan extension kernels, and WTA mechanism were consistently found and corroborated across four additional independently trained DRC(3,3) networks, suggesting that these mechanisms represent a stable and commonly learned solution for Sokoban (Table 4, Figure 20, 21).

#### 4.4. Implications for Value Functions and Mesa-Optimization

The path channel activations exhibit characteristics of an internal value function or Q-function. They propagate positive and negative information (from targets and obstacles, respectively) along path segments. However, this internal value function differs from a typical discounted reward function; it implicitly prefers shorter paths by prioritizing paths that connect to the target faster, rather than explicitly encoding discounted reward. This observation supports the hypothesis that the DRC agent acts as a mesa-optimizer, learning an internal goal-seeking process where the learned "value" for path length differs in its exact computation from the training reward.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of mechanistic interpretability by providing a detailed, weight-level explanation of planning in a complex, high-performing recurrent neural network agent. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Advancing Mechanistic Interpretability:** The work demonstrates that detailed mechanistic understanding is achievable for neural networks beyond toy models or localized features, pushing the boundaries of what can be reverse-engineered in deep learning. By directly identifying and analyzing channels and kernels, it offers a more fundamental explanation than probe-based methods.
- **Illuminating Planning in Model-Free RL:** It provides concrete evidence that model-free reinforcement learning, without explicit model-based planning components, can implicitly learn sophisticated planning algorithms. The discovery of learned transition model components (plan extension, stopping signals) and value-like functions within a model-free agent challenges traditional distinctions between model-free and model-based approaches.
- **Empirical Evidence for Mesa-Optimization:** The study offers an empirical "model organism" for mesa-optimization. By showing that the agent learns an internal planning process with a value function that implicitly prioritizes path length rather than explicitly computing discounted reward, it illustrates how learned internal goals might diverge from the direct training signal. This finding has profound implications for AI safety research, particularly in understanding and mitigating "goal misgeneralization" in advanced AI systems.
- **Enhancing Trustworthiness and Predictability of AI:** A precise mechanistic understanding of an agent's planning allows for greater predictability of its actions, facilitating debugging, and enabling the verification of whether an agent truly conforms to its intended specifications. This transparency is crucial for the responsible development of agentic AI.
- **Guiding Future Research and Design:** The robust and consistent emergence of these planning mechanisms across multiple independently trained networks suggests that similar circuits might be common in other planning agents. This can inform the design of more interpretable or aligned AI architectures and algorithms, potentially leading to systems that are inherently easier to understand and control.
- **Opening New Avenues for Intervention:** The ability to identify specific channels and kernels responsible for planning, combined with techniques like weight steering, offers potential pathways for targeted interventions to modify or improve agent behavior, for instance, extending planning horizons for larger problems.
