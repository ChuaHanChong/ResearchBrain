# Foundation-Model-Guided Topology-Aware Semantic Risk Fields for Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A planner-ready 3D semantic risk field for manipulation. For each pair of carried object and scene object, a foundation model gives six directional risk weights and a decay scale. These are combined with voxelized scene geometry through geodesic decay and obstacle shielding. CHOMP trajectories using the field cut mean semantic exposure from 0.654 to 0.014 versus collision-only planning, with 20-object fields built at 18 Hz.

## Problem

- Collision-only planners like PRM, CHOMP and TrajOpt treat geometrically valid paths as safe, ignoring semantic hazards such as carrying water directly above a laptop.
- Semantic-Metric Bayesian Risk Fields output pixel-dense image risk, not a planner-ready field over the robot's 3D workspace that can be queried anywhere.
- Prior semantic-safety methods use predefined relations like on or near, encode no direction-dependent severity, and ignore how intervening obstacles shield objects from risk.

## Method

- ==Directional semantic priors==: an LLM assigns each ordered manipulated/scene object pair six signed-axis risk weights in [0,1) plus a Gaussian decay scale, cached in a reusable database.
- ==Topology-aware shielding==: an attenuation factor equal to Euclidean over geodesic distance (==Eikonal solver== on voxels) suppresses risk behind barriers, while ==geodesic Gaussian decay== sets absolute spread.
- Pairwise fields pass through a smooth nonlinear map phi(u)=alpha*u+(Lambda-alpha)*u^kappa so near-one weights act as finite near-no-go costs, are summed per scene, and are built in batch on GPU.

## Results

- On OOPSIEVERSE scenes, risk-aware CHOMP lowered mean reference-field path risk from **0.654** to **0.014** (fragile cluster), **0.580** to **0.408** (laptop) and **0.275** to **0.014** (hot stove).
- In the laptop ablation, geodesic decay alone leaves **0.101** of the Euclidean-baseline risk, while the full formulation with attenuation leaves **2.9e-6**. Shelf shielding gives A=**0.271**.
- Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4-mini and Qwen3-235B matched human risky/safe labels on **27**, **28** and **29** of **30** pairs. A 20-object field takes **56.9 ms** at 2 cm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Collision-free does not mean safe: hazards that depend on what is carried belong in the cost as a soft field on top of hard geometric constraints.
> - Risk should spread along free-space geodesics, not straight lines; the ratio of Euclidean to geodesic distance gives a cheap, continuous, partial-shielding-aware attenuation signal.
> - Splitting the system into foundation-model priors for what is risky and geometry for how risk spreads lets priors be cached once, keeping LLM latency out of the planning loop.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot is carrying a cup of water from a start position to a goal near a laptop. Collision avoidance prevents contact, but it does not tell a planner that carrying water above a laptop is dangerous, or that a barrier between them may reduce that danger. The authors give the planner a spatial risk field. The field combines *what* is being carried and *what* is nearby with *where* the robot is relative to those objects and intervening geometry. It adds a cost for hazards that are not physical obstacles without replacing hard collision constraints. In household simulations, adding that cost lowered exposure on the authors' reference fields. The laptop detour was longer and still had some exposure.

A UR5 carries a cup of water along a start-to-goal route. (a) The route passes above a laptop and is labeled higher risk because a spill could damage it. (b) An intervening shelf shields the laptop, and the route is labeled lower risk.

### 2. A field shaped by context

The method starts with each **ordered** pair of a manipulated object and a scene object. For each pair, a foundation model supplies six directional risk weights for the fixed workspace axes (±x, ±y, ±z), along with a pair-specific spatial decay scale. The weights are semantic severity scores, not probabilities. A larger scale means the risk extends farther.

The system then blends the six weights according to the query point's offset from the scene object's boundary. As a result, a point above an object can inherit a different risk weight from a point beside it.

Next, the system compares straight-line Euclidean distance with **geodesic distance**, which is the shortest path through collision-free workspace. The ratio between the two attenuates risk when a barrier makes the free-space route indirect. Separately, geodesic distance controls spatial decay. It therefore represents both relative detour and absolute free-space separation.

The pairwise risks are combined into a dense 3D scene field, which can be queried along a robot path as an added soft cost. Near-no-go weights produce a finite, strongly discouraging cost, not a mathematical exclusion rule.

Pipeline: object inputs; pair-specific directional priors and spatial scale; location-dependent blending of directional weights; comparison of Euclidean and geodesic paths around intervening geometry; and pairwise risk construction followed by summation over scene objects.

### 3. Does the detour term matter?

A component ablation asks whether geodesic distance alone explains the shielding effect, or whether the additional detour-based attenuation matters too. The ablation uses a single laptop query, with the directional weight fixed at 0.99999 and the spatial decay scale at 0.15 m. The residual ratio is the variant's risk-field value divided by the Euclidean-baseline value, so lower means more suppression. Geodesic-distance decay alone leaves a residual ratio of 0.101. The full formulation leaves 2.949 × 10⁻⁶.

These results support the authors' interpretation that relative-detour attenuation adds suppression beyond absolute geodesic-distance decay. They do not show that every obstacle, layout, or risk source behaves identically.

Component ablation at the laptop query. Euclidean-distance decay only: 3.080 × 10⁵ (ratio 1.000). Geodesic-distance decay only: 3.114 × 10⁴ (0.101). Full topology-aware formulation: 9.085 × 10⁻¹ (2.949 × 10⁻⁶).

### 4. A planner pays for exposure

The simulated tasks were carrying a wine glass near fragile objects, carrying water near a laptop, and carrying a banana near a hot stove. Human reference fields were hidden from both the method and the planner and used only for evaluation. Both planner variants shared the same start and goal states, initialization trajectories, occupancy information, and collision constraints. The risk-aware objective added the semantic cost.

In all three scenarios, the risk-aware trajectories had lower mean and maximum reference risk than collision-only planning, and they took longer paths. Mean path risk is the average reference-field value at sampled trajectory points. In the laptop scenario, the CHOMP-style planner's mean path risk fell from 0.580 to 0.408. Maximum risk, the highest sampled value, fell from 0.999 to 0.691. The path was 0.91 m rather than 0.68 m. The laptop's mean-risk reduction is smaller because the reference-risk region extends broadly around the laptop, so the detour still incurs some exposure.

### 5. Safety beyond contact

The contribution is a spatial semantic cost that lets a planner trade a longer feasible route for lower exposure, with topology shaping how that cost spreads. These are simulation results evaluated against human-defined fields, with oracle object poses and manually assigned labels. They are not measurements of real-world damage or spill probability. The method is a soft semantic cost, not a safety certificate or a hard no-go constraint. The evaluated setting is static and near-field.
