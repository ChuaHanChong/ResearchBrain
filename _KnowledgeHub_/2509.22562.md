# Activation Function Design Sustains Plasticity in Continual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Vermont show that careful activation function design is essential for sustaining neural network plasticity in continual learning, introducing Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky functions that enhance adaptation and achieve higher plasticity scores across various supervised and reinforcement learning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Neural networks progressively lose the ability to adapt to new information (plasticity loss) in continual learning settings, particularly in deep reinforcement learning.
- The specific role of activation functions in mitigating this plasticity loss within non-stationary data streams remained largely unaddressed.
- Current continual learning approaches mainly focus on architectural modifications or regularization, not fully exploring the potential of fundamental components like activation functions.

## Method

- The study performs a property-level analysis of activation functions, investigating how ==negative-branch== shape and saturation behavior impact plasticity.
- A stress protocol involving scaling shocks is used to analyze desaturation dynamics and recovery capabilities of different activations.
- Novel activation functions, Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky, are designed based on principles like a non-zero derivative floor and moderate negative-side responsiveness.
- These newly designed activations are empirically evaluated in both supervised class-incremental learning and continual reinforcement learning tasks.

## Results

- The proposed Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky activations consistently outperformed standard functions across five supervised continual learning benchmarks, with Randomized Smooth-Leaky often achieving the highest accuracy.
- Randomized Smooth-Leaky also demonstrated the highest 'Plasticity Score' in continual reinforcement learning MuJoCo tasks, indicating superior adaptation in later cycles.
- Empirical analysis revealed an optimal negative-side slope range (**0.6** 		≲ ¯s 		≲ **0.9**) for piecewise-linear activations, balancing dead-unit prevention and optimization stability.
- Activations with a strict non-zero derivative floor showed significantly better desaturation properties and recovery (<**5%** non-recovery rate) from pre-activation shocks compared to zero-floor activations, which had high failure rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Activation function choice significantly impacts a network's ability to sustain plasticity in continual learning, with differences becoming far more critical than in i.i.d. settings.
> - An optimal 'Goldilocks zone' for negative-side responsiveness (effective slope between 0.6 and 0.9) exists for maintaining plasticity and avoiding both dead units and optimization instability.
> - Activations with a strict non-zero derivative floor demonstrate superior desaturation and recovery capabilities from pre-activation shocks, while two-sided saturation severely impedes recovery.
