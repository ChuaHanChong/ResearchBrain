# Activation Function Design Sustains Plasticity in Continual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Vermont show that careful activation function design is essential for sustaining neural network plasticity in continual learning, introducing Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky functions that enhance adaptation and achieve higher plasticity scores across various supervised and reinforcement learning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Neural networks progressively lose the ability to adapt to new information (plasticity loss) in continual learning settings, particularly in deep reinforcement learning.
- The specific role of activation functions in mitigating this plasticity loss within non-stationary data streams remained largely unaddressed.
- Current continual learning approaches mainly focus on architectural modifications or regularization, not fully exploring the potential of fundamental components like activation functions.

## Method

- The study performs a property-level analysis of activation functions, investigating how ==negative-branch== shape and saturation behavior impact plasticity.
- A stress protocol involving scaling shocks is used to analyze desaturation dynamics and recovery capabilities of different activations.
- Novel activation functions, Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky, are designed based on principles like a non-zero derivative floor and moderate negative-side responsiveness.
- These newly designed activations are empirically evaluated in both supervised class-incremental learning and continual reinforcement learning tasks.

## Results

- The proposed Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky activations consistently outperformed standard functions across five supervised continual learning benchmarks, with Randomized Smooth-Leaky often achieving the highest accuracy.
- Randomized Smooth-Leaky also demonstrated the highest 'Plasticity Score' in continual reinforcement learning MuJoCo tasks, indicating superior adaptation in later cycles.
- Empirical analysis revealed an optimal negative-side slope range (**0.6** 		≲ ¯s 		≲ **0.9**) for piecewise-linear activations, balancing dead-unit prevention and optimization stability.
- Activations with a strict non-zero derivative floor showed significantly better desaturation properties and recovery (<**5%** non-recovery rate) from pre-activation shocks compared to zero-floor activations, which had high failure rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Activation function choice significantly impacts a network's ability to sustain plasticity in continual learning, with differences becoming far more critical than in i.i.d. settings.
> - An optimal 'Goldilocks zone' for negative-side responsiveness (effective slope between 0.6 and 0.9) exists for maintaining plasticity and avoiding both dead units and optimization instability.
> - Activations with a strict non-zero derivative floor demonstrate superior desaturation and recovery capabilities from pre-activation shocks, while two-sided saturation severely impedes recovery.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of continual learning (CL), which addresses the challenge of enabling neural networks to acquire new knowledge over time without forgetting previously learned information. Within CL, two primary issues are often discussed: catastrophic forgetting, where performance on prior tasks degrades when they are no longer explicitly trained, and loss of plasticity, where networks progressively lose the ability to adapt to new information, even if they retain past capabilities. While catastrophic forgetting has received substantial attention, the phenomenon of plasticity loss, particularly in deep reinforcement learning (RL) settings, is less understood and underexplored.

Prior research has documented symptoms associated with plasticity loss in deep RL, including reduced gradient magnitudes, increasing parameter norms, rank-deficient curvature, and declining representation diversity. Proposed mitigation strategies include architectural adjustments (e.g., replacing low-utility units) and various regularization techniques aimed at retaining plasticity.

The current study positions itself by arguing that activation functions, a fundamental component of neural networks, represent an underexplored mechanism for addressing plasticity loss. While activation functions have been extensively benchmarked in independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) training regimes, where their performance differences often diminish with careful tuning, their role in continual, non-stationary data environments is posited to be significantly more critical. This perspective contrasts with existing approaches that primarily focus on architectural modifications or regularization, instead proposing a fundamental "knob" for plasticity maintenance. Previous work has indicated that certain alternative activation functions, such as CReLU or rational functions, can offer performance improvements under non-stationary conditions, providing a foundation for the property-level investigation undertaken in this paper.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observation that while activation functions are a core element of neural network architecture, their specific role in mitigating plasticity loss in continual learning environments remains largely unexplored. In i.i.d. learning settings, differences among activation functions tend to become negligible once model size and optimization are properly tuned. However, the authors hypothesize that under continual, non-stationary data streams, these differences could become substantial, affecting a model's sustained ability to adapt.

The key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

- **Investigate the role of activation functions in plasticity loss:** To demonstrate that the choice of activation function is a primary, architecture-agnostic factor in mitigating the progressive loss of a network's ability to adapt to new information in continual learning settings.
- **Conduct a property-level analysis:** To systematically analyze how specific properties of activation functions, particularly their negative-branch shape and saturation behavior, influence plasticity. This involves identifying which characteristics of activation functions are conducive to sustained adaptation under changing data distributions.
- **Identify a "Goldilocks zone" for negative-side responsiveness:** To determine if there exists an optimal range of negative-side slope (or effective slope for smooth activations) that consistently promotes plasticity across different activation function families and continual learning scenarios.
- **Analyze desaturation dynamics under "shocks":** To understand how quickly different activation functions allow a network to recover gradient flow and performance after sudden, controlled shifts in pre-activation distributions, linking this to properties like derivative floor, sidedness of saturation, and dead-band width.
- **Develop novel, plasticity-sustaining activation functions:** To introduce new drop-in nonlinearities (Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky) informed by the property-level analysis. These functions are designed to maintain a non-zero derivative floor, moderate negative-side responsiveness, and a continuous first derivative (C1 transition) to efficiently improve continual adaptation.
- **Evaluate proposed activations in diverse continual learning settings:** To empirically assess the performance of the newly designed activation functions across both supervised class-incremental benchmarks and reinforcement learning tasks involving non-stationary environments, demonstrating their domain-general efficacy.
- **Provide diagnostic tools:** To offer a simple stress protocol and diagnostics that link the shape of the activation function to a network's ability to adapt under change, facilitating future research and design.

The overarching motivation is to identify a lightweight, domain-general approach to sustain plasticity in continual learning by leveraging thoughtful activation design, without requiring additional model capacity or task-specific tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology, combining a property-level analysis of activation functions with empirical evaluation across diverse continual learning benchmarks.

**Activation Function Characterization:**
The study begins by providing a comparative overview of common activation functions, categorizing them by properties relevant to plasticity. These include:
- **Rectifiers (ReLU, Leaky-ReLU, PReLU, RReLU):** Discussed for their efficiency but susceptibility to dead units (ReLU) and how learnable/randomized negative slopes mitigate this.
- **Saturating Sigmoids (Sigmoid, Tanh):** Noted for vanishing gradients when units saturate, which impedes sustained adaptation.
- **Smooth Non-monotonic (Swish, GeLU):** Highlighted for preserving small non-zero gradients near and below zero, potentially mitigating "dying ReLU" behavior.
- **Exponential Variants (ELU, CELU, SELU):** Examined for their negative branches and self-normalization properties, which can stabilize scales under non-i.i.d. data.

**Standardized Comparative Setup:**
To isolate the effect of activation functions, all comparative studies use a consistent architecture (4-layer CNN backbone), optimizer (Adam), and training budget on the Split-CIFAR-100 benchmark. This controlled environment aims to minimize confounding factors from architectural or optimization choices. An initial comparison on Split-CIFAR-100 demonstrates that activation function performance differences are more pronounced in class-incremental learning (C-IL) than in i.i.d. training.

**Case Study 1: Negative-Slope 'Goldilocks Zone'**
- **Method:** A systematic sweep of negative-branch slopes was performed across three families: piece-wise linear (Leaky-ReLU, RReLU), smooth-tailed (Swish, GeLU, ELU/CELU/SELU), and adaptive (PReLU at global, layer, and neuron scopes).
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed using final accuracy, dead-unit fraction, and optimization stability metrics, specifically principal curvature (λ_max) and effective rank of the gradient Gram matrix.
- **Effective Slope (¯s):** For smooth-tailed functions, an "effective slope" (¯s), defined as the average derivative for negative inputs E_x<0[φ′(x)], was introduced to allow comparison on a common scale with linear-leak activations.
- **Failure Mode Analysis:** The study identified two mechanisms: a "dead-unit regime" (correlated with low ¯s) and "optimization instability" (correlated with high ¯s), manifesting as sharp spikes in principal curvature and effective rank.

**Case Study 2: Desaturation Dynamics Under Shocks**
- **Stress Protocol:** Networks were subjected to a scaling shock protocol. Every C_ℓ=10 epochs, pre-activations were multiplied by a scaling factor γ ∈ {1.5, 0.5, 0.25, 2.0} for one epoch before reverting to γ=1. This aimed to push pre-activations into activation tails and observe recovery.
- **Metrics:** Three metrics quantified desaturation dynamics:
 - **Peak Saturated Fraction (Peak SF):** Maximum fraction of units saturated (where |φ′(x)| < 10^-3) immediately after a shock.
 - **Area Under the Saturation Curve (AUSC):** Integral of the saturated fraction over the recovery window. Lower AUSC indicates better desaturation.
 - **Recovery time (τ_95):** Steps needed to regain 95% of pre-shock performance.
 - **SF Non-recovery Rate:** Percentage of runs where units failed to desaturate.
- **Property-based Grouping:** Activations were grouped by "derivative-floor rule" (zero-floor, non-zero-floor, effective non-zero-floor) and "sidedness" (one-sided kink, one-sided smooth, two-sided saturating).
- **Dead-Band Width (DBW):** An analytical metric, DBW was defined as the fraction of a typical pre-activation range where |φ′(x)| < 10^-3. Its correlation with empirical desaturation outcomes was investigated.

**Implications for Activation Function Design and Novel Activations:**
Based on the findings from the case studies, three design principles for plasticity-friendly nonlinearities were proposed: (i) maintain a non-zero derivative floor, (ii) keep negative-side responsiveness in a moderate, activation-specific 'Goldilocks zone', and (iii) prefer a C1 (smooth) transition at the origin when (i) and (ii) are held fixed, while prioritizing recovery success.
- **Smooth-Leaky:** Introduced as a C1, drop-in replacement for Leaky ReLU, preserving the negative-side floor and positive identity but replacing the kink with a smooth transition. Defined as f(x) = αx + (1-α)x ⋅ σ(cx/p).
- **Randomized Smooth-Leaky:** A variant where the leak parameter α is randomized (r ~ U(l, u)) during training to encourage robustness, with r fixed to its mean during inference.

**Empirical Evaluation of Novel Activations:**
- **Supervised Continual Learning:** Evaluated on five benchmarks: Permuted MNIST, Random Label MNIST, Random Label CIFAR, CIFAR 5+1, and Continual ImageNet. These cover input distribution shifts and concept shifts. The primary metric was Total Average Online Task Accuracy, representing the lifetime aggregate of per-task average online accuracy. Budget-matched robustness tests were performed for fairness.
- **Continual Reinforcement Learning:** A single PPO agent was trained on a repeating sequence of four MuJoCo locomotion tasks (HalfCheetah-v4 → Hopper-v4 → Walker2d-v4 → Ant-v4, cycled three times). The policy and value networks shared a two-layer MLP backbone with task-specific input adapters and output heads.
 - **Plasticity Score:** A robust, normalized metric (Interquartile Mean (IQM) of min-max normalized late-cycle steady-state episodic returns) was used to aggregate performance across environments with distinct reward scales.
 - **Generalization Gap (∆GAP):** Measured the change in the train-test gap (R_train - R_test) from the first to the final cycle, aggregated using IQM across environments, to distinguish trainability from transferability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the impact of activation function design on plasticity in continual learning:

**General Observations:**
- Activation function performance differences, which often diminish in i.i.d. training, significantly widen in class-incremental learning settings, underscoring their critical role in non-stationary environments.

**Case Study 1: Negative-Slope 'Goldilocks Zone'**
- **Optimal Negative Slope:** For piece-wise linear activations (Leaky-ReLU, RReLU), performance reliably peaks within a moderate negative-side slope range (0.6 ≲ ¯s ≲ 0.9). Performance degrades if ¯s approaches 0 (dead-unit regime) or 1 (optimization instability).
- **Smooth-Tailed Activations:** When matched by effective slope (¯s), smooth-tailed functions (e.g., Swish, GeLU) generally underperform linear leaks within the 'Goldilocks zone' and show higher dead-unit fractions. They only approach linear-leak peaks at ¯s > 1, suggesting concentrated near-zero responsiveness and vanishing tails are less effective.
- **Adaptive Slopes:** Unconstrained adaptive learnable slopes (PReLU) tend to drift outside the identified 'Goldilocks zone' under non-stationarity, leading to suboptimal but still functional accuracy compared to fixed slopes.
- **Failure Mechanisms:** Low negative slopes (¯s → 0) correlate strongly with increasing dead-unit fractions (Pearson r = −0.51). High negative slopes (¯s → 1) coincide with optimization instability, characterized by sharp spikes in principal curvature (λ_max) and effective rank. Sustaining plasticity requires balancing gradient starvation prevention and landscape stiffness avoidance.

**Case Study 2: Desaturation Dynamics Under Shocks**
- **Derivative-Floor Rule:** Activations with a strict non-zero derivative floor (Leaky-ReLU, RReLU, PReLU) demonstrate superior desaturation properties, achieving the lowest Area Under Saturation Curve (AUSC) and near-zero non-recovery rates (<5%) even under strong scaling shocks. In contrast, zero-floor types (ReLU, Sigmoid, Tanh) exhibit the largest AUSC and very high non-recovery rates across all shock conditions.
- **Two-Sided Saturation Penalty:** Activations that saturate on both sides (Sigmoid, Tanh) suffer the most severe saturation, showing the largest peak saturated fraction and AUSC, and failing to desaturate in approximately 49.8% of runs. One-sided non-zero-floor rectifiers (Leaky-ReLU, PReLU, RReLU) recover much more reliably (non-recovery ≈ 13.3%). One-sided smooth activations (ELU, CELU, SELU) are intermediate, recovering quickly when successful but failing frequently at strong shocks.
- **Dead-Band Width (DBW) as Predictor:** The analytically computed Dead-Band Width (DBW), defined by regions where the derivative magnitude is below a threshold (ϵ < 10^-3), strongly correlates with adverse saturation outcomes. A higher DBW is associated with greater overall saturation impact (Avg. AUSC, r = 0.81) and increased non-recovery rates (r = 0.84). DBW does not, however, predict the speed of successful recovery, which is consistently fast (around one epoch).

**Novel Activation Functions (Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky):**
- **Supervised Continual Learning:** Both Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky consistently outperform ReLU and other standard activations across five diverse supervised continual learning benchmarks (Permuted MNIST, Random Label MNIST/CIFAR, CIFAR 5+1, Continual ImageNet). They particularly excel in harder settings. Randomized Smooth-Leaky often achieves the highest total average online task accuracy. Their strong performance is attributed to adherence to the identified design principles, particularly maintaining a moderate, non-zero negative slope and a C1 transition.
- **Continual Reinforcement Learning:** Randomized Smooth-Leaky achieves the highest "Plasticity Score" (IQM of normalized returns) among all tested activations on the MuJoCo locomotion tasks, indicating superior late-cycle train-side adaptation. While it can suffer from instability in highly volatile environments (e.g., Humanoid), in stable environments like Ant and HalfCheetah, its higher plasticity does not come at the cost of a widening generalization gap, suggesting solutions that transfer well. Sigmoid, despite its bounded nature offering stability in highly unstable scenarios, yields a moderate Plasticity Score due to saturation limiting peak learning capacity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research significantly impacts the field of continual learning by highlighting the often-underestimated role of activation functions in sustaining network plasticity. The findings suggest that activation choice is not merely an architectural detail to be tuned, but rather a "primary, architecture-agnostic lever" for mitigating a critical failure mode in continual learning.

The key significance points include:

- **Fundamental Mechanism for Plasticity:** The study establishes activation function design as a fundamental mechanism to sustain plasticity, distinct from and complementary to existing architectural or regularization-based continual learning strategies. This shifts focus to a core neural network component, suggesting that revisiting basics can yield substantial improvements.
- **Principled Design Rules:** By performing a property-level analysis, the research derives clear, actionable design principles for "plasticity-friendly" nonlinearities: maintaining a non-zero derivative floor, ensuring moderate negative-side responsiveness (the "Goldilocks zone"), preferring C1 (smooth) transitions at the origin, and avoiding wide dead-bands or two-sided saturation. These rules provide a theoretical framework for future activation function development.
- **Novel and Effective Drop-in Activations:** The introduction of Smooth-Leaky and Randomized Smooth-Leaky provides practical, immediately deployable solutions. These activations are demonstrated to consistently outperform a range of existing activations in diverse supervised and reinforcement learning benchmarks, offering a lightweight and domain-general way to improve continual adaptation without requiring extra model capacity or task-specific tuning.
- **Enhanced Diagnostics and Understanding:** The developed stress protocol and diagnostics (e.g., Dead-Band Width, saturation metrics) provide valuable tools for researchers to analyze and understand plasticity loss. They offer a systematic way to link an activation's intrinsic properties to a network's adaptive behavior under changing data distributions, aiding in the diagnosis and design of more robust models.
- **Broad Applicability:** The consistent performance benefits observed across both supervised image classification and complex reinforcement learning tasks suggest that the proposed activation functions and design principles are domain-general, applicable to a wide array of continual learning challenges.
- **Directions for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for further investigation, including the interaction of activation design with other continual learning interventions (e.g., experience replay, regularization), a deeper exploration of the trainability-generalizability trade-off in RL, the development of adaptive per-neuron self-tuning mechanisms for slopes, and a more rigorous theoretical understanding of curvature and desaturation bounds. This could culminate in principle-guided automated search for novel robust activations.

In summary, this research underscores that thoughtful activation function design can offer a powerful, yet often overlooked, strategy for improving the robustness and adaptability of neural networks in continually changing environments, laying a foundation for more plastic and stable continual learning systems.
