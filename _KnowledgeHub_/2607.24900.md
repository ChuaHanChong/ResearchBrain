# Inverse RL Helps Align AI by Imitating Humans

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced Projected Alignment Reward Estimated from Demonstrations (PARED), an inverse reinforcement learning framework that infers an explicit, inspectable reward function from expert demonstrations for language model alignment. PARED-aligned models demonstrated an 84.6% win-rate against an instruction-tuned baseline, outperforming supervised fine-tuning and enabling contextual alignment for different audiences.

## Problem

- Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) implicitly encodes desired behaviors, limiting inspectability and underutilizing information in expert demonstrations.
- Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and direct preference optimization (DPO) require expensive and time-consuming explicit preference annotations.
- Existing alignment methods often result in opaque objectives, hindering debugging, auditing, and reuse of learned behaviors.

## Method

- **PARED** infers an explicit reward function by training a lightweight ==discriminator== to differentiate between projected expert demonstrations and policy samples in a low-dimensional, practitioner-chosen feature space (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, topic distribution).
- The ==expert-likeness score== from the discriminator serves as an inspectable alignment reward, used for inference-time best-of-N reranking or on-policy reinforcement learning.
- The framework supports ==contextual alignment== by learning separate audience-conditioned discriminators and rewards, enabling a single policy to adapt to diverse target groups.

## Results

- PARED achieved an **84.6%** aggregate win-rate against an instruction-tuned base model, surpassing supervised fine-tuning (**81.1%** win-rate) on the same dataset in ab-initio alignment.
- When initialized from an SFT checkpoint, PARED further improved alignment, achieving an **88.4%** win-rate against its SFT initialization, demonstrating additional optimization signal extraction.
- The recovered PARED reward successfully improved inference-time response selection, with best-of-16 candidates winning **63.4%** of non-tied comparisons against random alternatives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Expert demonstrations contain implicit optimization signals that extend beyond what standard supervised fine-tuning can extract, which PARED can leverage through on-policy optimization.
> - Constraining reward inference to a small, interpretable feature space allows for transparency and auditing of the alignment objective, helping to identify and mitigate 'shortcut' behaviors.
> - An explicit, interpretable reward enables flexible application in both inference-time selection and reinforcement learning, reducing the reliance on costly explicit preference annotations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of language model (LM) alignment, which aims to ensure LMs consistently exhibit desirable properties such as helpfulness, safety, and adherence to instructions. Current predominant alignment methodologies include supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on expert demonstrations and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or direct preference optimization (DPO) based on preference comparisons. While effective, these approaches have been noted to underutilize the information embedded within expert demonstrations. SFT primarily teaches models to reproduce expert outputs without explicitly recovering the underlying objective, which limits its inspectability, reusability, and potential for on-policy optimization. RLHF and DPO, conversely, require explicit preference annotations, which can be resource-intensive to collect.

This paper positions itself by exploring whether demonstrations alone can implicitly yield an explicit reward function that can be inspected, audited, reused for inference-time selection, and optimized through policy gradients. It draws inspiration from inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), which infers a reward function from demonstrated behavior. Classical and adversarial IRL methods often recover opaque rewards, making their objectives as difficult to interpret as the policies they train.

The proposed method, Projected Alignment Reward Estimated from Demonstrations (PARED), distinguishes itself by retaining the IRL framework but constraining reward inference to a small, practitioner-chosen feature space. This design aims to produce a scalar reward that is both optimizable like a learned reward model and interpretable in terms of predefined response-level features. The paper notes concurrent work, such as VARL by Damani et al. (2026), which also explores adversarial rewards from demonstrations, but focuses on settings with an independent verifier. PARED specifically investigates scenarios where demonstrations are the primary source of supervision, without relying on additional task-specific preference annotations or external verifiers, and emphasizes the creation of a compact, inspectable, and contextually reusable alignment reward.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a method for recovering an explicit, inspectable reward function from expert demonstrations for language model alignment, addressing the limitations of existing approaches. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

1. **Underutilization of Expert Demonstrations:** Current supervised fine-tuning (SFT) methods treat expert demonstrations as direct behavioral targets for imitation. While this trains models to reproduce expert outputs, it does not extract an explicit objective or reward function. Consequently, properties like helpfulness, safety, or instruction following remain implicitly encoded within the policy's weights, making them difficult to inspect, audit, or reuse for other tasks. The authors are motivated to unlock this implicit information.
2. **Lack of Inspectable and Reusable Objectives:** Existing alignment techniques often result in objectives that are either implicit (as in SFT) or opaque (as with many classical and adversarial inverse reinforcement learning methods). The inability to directly inspect and understand the reward function hinders debugging, auditing, and ensuring that the model is optimizing for truly desirable properties, rather than exploiting unforeseen shortcuts. The authors aim to create a reward that is transparent and interpretable in terms of specific, named features.
3. **Efficiency and Reduced Annotation Burden:** Traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related preference optimization methods require explicit preference comparisons, which are expensive and time-consuming to collect. The motivation is to leverage readily available expert demonstrations—which already provide task-specific supervision—to derive a reward model, thereby reducing the need for additional, task-specific preference annotations.
4. **Enhancing Alignment Beyond Imitation:** The research seeks to determine whether demonstrations contain an optimization signal that extends beyond what supervised fine-tuning can extract. By inferring a reward, the model can then be optimized on-policy, potentially leading to further improvements in alignment even after initial SFT. This allows for continuous refinement and adaptation of the policy towards the demonstrated objective.
5. **Enabling Contextual and Personalized Alignment:** A key motivation is to facilitate contextual alignment, where a single language model can adapt its behavior based on specific conditioning variables, such as different target audiences. The authors aim to demonstrate that the inferred reward can support tailoring model responses to the preferences of diverse groups, without requiring separate models or complex reward aggregation mechanisms.
6. **Support for Diverse Downstream Uses:** The explicit and inspectable nature of the recovered reward is intended to support multiple downstream applications. This includes inference-time reranking (selecting the best response from a pool of candidates based on the reward score) and serving as a training signal for reinforcement learning, offering flexibility in how the alignment objective is applied.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is Projected Alignment Reward Estimated from Demonstrations (PARED), an inverse reinforcement learning-inspired framework. PARED aims to recover an explicit and inspectable reward by discriminating between expert demonstrations and policy samples in a low-dimensional, practitioner-chosen feature space.

**Problem Setup:**
The framework considers language model alignment using expert demonstrations, denoted as trajectories τ = (x, y), where x is the context (e.g., user prompt, audience specification) and y is the expert response. Expert demonstrations are assumed to be drawn from an empirical distribution μ, representing desired behaviors. The goal is to align a policy π(y|x) to this desirable behavior.

**Trajectory Representation in Projected Space:**
A key component is the feature map ϕ(τ), which projects each response-level trajectory τ into a low-dimensional semantic feature space. The choice of features is critical as the reward's scope is deliberately constrained by this projection, ensuring interpretability.
The candidate features considered are:
- **Zero-Shot Judgements:** Helpfulness and harmlessness scores (1-10 scale, normalized to) derived from prompting a separate frontier model (google/gemma-3-27b-it) to evaluate the response.
- **LDA Topic Distribution:** Five document-topic proportions (normalized to) obtained by applying a pre-trained Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) model to the TF-IDF representation of the response text.
- **Response Length:** Normalized completion length (initially considered, but later excluded from the main experimental recipe due to its potential to become a "controllable shortcut" that could lead to reward hacking).

The primary experimental feature vector used is a seven-dimensional length-excluded subvector: ϕ−ℓ(τ) = (h(τ)/10, s(τ)/10, q1(y)..., q5(y)), where h and s are helpfulness/harmlessness scores, and q1-q5 are topic proportions. The TF-IDF and LDA models are fitted once on a union of initial policy completions and expert demonstrations, then frozen.

**Implicit Reward Estimation:**
PARED trains a lightweight discriminator, Dψ(ϕ(τ)), to differentiate between projected expert demonstrations and projected samples from the current policy. The discriminator's objective is to maximize the likelihood of correctly classifying expert samples as positive and policy samples as negative:
maxψ Eτ∼μ [log Dψ(ϕ(τ))] + Eτ∼π [log (1 − Dψ(ϕ(τ)))]

The "expert-likeness score," defined as rϕ(τ) = log Dψ(ϕ(τ)), then serves as the explicit alignment reward. The discriminator used is a logistic model that is linear in a degree-2 polynomial expansion of the selected features (including raw features, their squares, and pairwise products). This adversarial game framework has a feature-matching interpretation, where the policy aims to match the expert's feature expectations.

**Reward-Guided Downstream Use:**
The recovered PARED reward can be utilized in two primary ways:

1. **Inference-Time Reranking (Best-of-N):** Without modifying model weights, the reward is used to select the highest-scoring completion from a set of N candidates sampled from the policy for a given context x. The selected response is y⋆(x) = arg maxy∈C(x) rϕ((x, y)).
2. **Adversarial On-Policy Reinforcement Learning (RL):** The reward drives KL-regularized policy optimization. Starting from a reference policy πref, the trainable policy πθ is optimized using a policy-gradient algorithm (specifically GRPO):
 maxθ Eτ∼πθ [rϕ(τ)] − βE x∼p(x) [KL(πθ(·|x) ∥ πref(·|x))]
 During RL, the discriminator is either held fixed (frozen schedule) or periodically refitted against a mix of initial and recent on-policy samples (online schedule). Gradients are not propagated through the discriminator refitting step. Two initialization regimes for πref are explored: an off-the-shelf instruction-tuned model (ab-initio alignment) or an SFT checkpoint (post-hoc alignment).

**Contextual Alignment:**
The framework extends to audience-conditioned alignment, where the context x includes a user prompt u and an audience g (e.g., adult, child). Separate discriminators Dψg are learned for each audience, yielding audience-conditioned rewards rg(τ). The shared policy πθ is optimized using a combined reward across audiences, allowing it to learn distinct behaviors appropriate for each target group.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Dataset:** Prompts from Anthropic/hh-rlhf, with expert demonstrations generated by GPT-5.1 prompted for adult or child audiences. Two training splits: HH-Large-Train (4,000 demonstrations) and HH-Small-Train (500 demonstrations). A disjoint test set (HH-Large-Test) is used for all evaluations.
- **Evaluation:** Automated side-by-side evaluation using google/gemini-2.5-flash as the judge with an audience-conditioned rubric. Win-rates and 95% Wilson binomial confidence intervals are reported. Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD²) is used to quantify feature-space distance between policy and expert distributions.
- **Models:** Qwen/Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct fine-tuned with LoRA adapters for policy optimization. GPT-OSS-20B for reranking candidate generation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings across different experimental settings:

**Reward Recovery and Inference-Time Selection:**
- **Discriminator Performance:** The learned discriminators effectively distinguished expert demonstrations from policy completions (GPT-OSS-20B) on held-out prompts, achieving a pooled held-out AUC of 0.849. This indicates that the chosen feature map is sufficient to capture a meaningful signal of "expert-likeness."
- **Best-of-N Reranking:** When used as an inference-time selector without any policy training, the PARED reward successfully improved response selection. From a pool of 16 GPT-OSS-20B candidates, the highest-scoring completion won 63.4% of aggregate non-tied comparisons against a random distinct candidate from the same pool. This suggests the recovered reward is immediately useful for improving response quality.

**On-Policy RL with the Recovered Reward (4,000 Demonstrations):**
- **Ab-initio Alignment (from Instruct model):** PARED, starting from an off-the-shelf instruction-tuned model, achieved an 84.6% aggregate win-rate against the base model. This numerically surpassed the 81.1% win-rate of a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) baseline (SFT-HH-Large) trained directly on the same demonstrations, despite PARED never directly applying a supervised loss.
- **Post-hoc Alignment (after SFT):** When initialized from an SFT-HH-Large checkpoint, PARED demonstrated further gains. It won 88.4% of comparisons against its own SFT initialization (using a frozen discriminator) and 86.2% (using an online discriminator). This finding indicates that expert demonstrations contain additional optimization signal beyond what is extracted through supervised fine-tuning alone, which PARED can exploit through on-policy optimization of the inferred reward.
- **Training Dynamics:** Across five RL epochs, the mean discriminator reward increased for Instruct-initialized runs, mean KL divergence to the reference policy remained low (below 0.04), and discriminator AUC generally decreased. Held-out Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD²) to the expert-demonstration feature distribution consistently decreased, showing the policy was moving towards the expert's feature distribution. For post-hoc PARED, online refitting kept AUC near chance, while the frozen discriminator's AUC fell below chance, suggesting policy optimization reversed the discriminator's original expert–policy ranking, consistent with adapting to a fixed reward.

**On-Policy RL with the Recovered Reward (500 Demonstrations):**
- **Lower Data Regimes:** With a smaller dataset of 500 demonstrations, Instruct-initialized PARED reached a 70.7% win-rate against the base Instruct model after three RL epochs. This compared favorably to SFT-HH-Small, which achieved 58.0% against the same reference. This suggests PARED can be effective in scenarios with limited demonstrations.
- **Post-hoc (500 Demos):** A selected post-hoc PARED checkpoint, initialized from SFT-HH-Small, won 70.0% directly against its SFT-HH-Small initialization (after 51.2 RL epochs), showing improvement over a weak SFT starting point.

**Audience and Feature-Space Diagnostics:**
- **Per-Audience Gains:** The improvements from PARED were observed for both adult and child audiences. For the 4,000-demonstration conditions and the 500-demonstration ab-initio condition, both audiences showed gains (e.g., 85.9% for adult and 83.3% for child with Instruct + online discriminator), indicating the method successfully facilitates contextual alignment without introducing trade-offs between audiences.
- **Feature-Space Auditing:** The interpretability of the feature space allowed for direct observation of potential "shortcut" behaviors. For instance, when "response length" was included as a feature, the discriminator's strongest weights were often related to length, and score-length correlations reached 0.92–0.98. This led to the exclusion of length from the final feature set, demonstrating the benefit of transparent feature-space auditing in controlling alignment objectives.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces PARED, a framework that leverages inverse reinforcement learning principles to derive an explicit and inspectable reward function directly from expert demonstrations. This work presents several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Enabling Explicit and Inspectable Alignment Objectives:** PARED provides a method to move beyond implicit behavioral imitation (as in SFT) by recovering an explicit reward. This reward, being defined over a practitioner-chosen, low-dimensional feature space, offers interpretability. This is significant because it allows developers and auditors to understand precisely *what* the model is optimizing for, rather than relying on opaque learned objectives. This transparency can facilitate debugging, bias detection, and verification of alignment goals.

2. **Efficient Utilization of Demonstrations:** The study demonstrates that demonstrations alone can yield a valuable alignment signal for both inference-time selection and on-policy training, without requiring additional, costly preference annotations. This reduces the annotation burden compared to RLHF and DPO, potentially making advanced alignment techniques more accessible and scalable, especially for tasks where demonstrations are easier to collect than explicit preference rankings.

3. **Enhancing Alignment Beyond Supervised Fine-Tuning:** A notable finding is that PARED can yield further alignment gains even *after* a model has undergone standard supervised fine-tuning on the same demonstrations. This indicates that demonstrations contain implicit optimization signals that SFT alone does not fully extract. By providing a mechanism to access and optimize these signals through reinforcement learning, PARED offers a complementary approach that can potentially lead to more robust and deeply aligned models.

4. **Flexible Application in Inference and Training:** The recovered PARED reward is versatile, proving effective for both inference-time best-of-N reranking and as a training signal for on-policy reinforcement learning. This flexibility allows practitioners to apply the alignment objective in ways best suited to their computational resources and specific needs, from lightweight response selection to more intensive model training.

5. **Facilitating Contextual and Personalized Alignment:** The successful demonstration of audience-conditioned alignment, where a single policy adapts its behavior for different audiences (adult vs. child) based on separate, audience-specific rewards, highlights PARED's potential for personalization. This capability is crucial for developing AI systems that can appropriately tailor their responses to diverse user groups or contexts, without the need to train multiple specialized models.

6. **Mitigating Reward Hacking Risks through Interpretability:** The explicit feature-space definition allows for direct auditing of the reward function. The paper's observation that "response length" could become a shortcut feature, leading to its exclusion, exemplifies how PARED's transparency helps identify and mitigate potential reward hacking issues. This control over the alignment target contributes to building more reliable and trustworthy AI systems.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
The work is presented within specific boundaries. The expert demonstrations used are prompted frontier-model completions rather than human-generated data, which might differ in subtle ways. The discriminator signal inherently weakens as the policy's feature distribution approaches that of the expert, which can affect training dynamics. Furthermore, the downstream quality is assessed using a single LLM judge (google/gemini-2.5-flash), raising the possibility of judge-preferred style or length effects influencing results. The study covers one base model, one specific feature instantiation, and two audience conditions; its generalization to other models, features, or broader contextual variables remains to be fully explored.
