# COvolve: Adversarial Co-Evolution of Large-Language-Model-Generated Policies and Environments via Two-Player Zero-Sum Game

> [!summary] Summary
> COvolve introduces a framework for adversarial co-evolution, employing Large Language Models to generate both training environments and agent policies within a two-player zero-sum game. This approach produces progressively complex environments and robust, generalizable policies across diverse domains, effectively mitigating catastrophic forgetting through Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium computation.

## Problem

- AI agents trained in static or manually designed environments often exhibit poor generalization to dynamic and unpredictable settings.
- Existing Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods and Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) typically rely on fixed environments or limited heuristics, restricting task diversity and relevance.
- Previous LLM-based efforts to generate environments or policies have often decoupled these processes, hindering robust agent learning and continuous adaptation.

## Method

- **COvolve** establishes a closed-loop ==adversarial co-evolutionary framework== where LLMs generate both training environments and agent policies as executable Python code.
- It models this interaction as a ==two-player zero-sum game==, adapting the ==PSRO framework== for ==Unsupervised Environment Design==.
- The system iteratively designs policies and environments, computing a ==Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium (MSNE)== to ensure robust policy performance and prevent ==catastrophic forgetting==.

## Results

- The framework consistently generated progressively complex environments across MiniGrid (mazes with doors/keys), PyGame (cluttered navigation), and CARLA (dense urban driving) domains, demonstrating an automated curriculum.
- Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium (MSNE) policies maintained high performance across the entire archive of evolved environments, preventing catastrophic forgetting and outperforming greedy or uniform policy selection.
- Policies developed through co-evolution generalized effectively to unseen benchmark environments, achieving **100%** success rates on MiniGrid-MultiRoom-N6-v0 and a **0.71** success rate on the CARLA Town02 map.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial co-evolution, driven by Large Language Models, can automatically induce progressively complex curricula for training AI agents.
> - The use of Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium (MSNE) is crucial for building robust meta-policies that effectively prevent catastrophic forgetting across a growing archive of tasks.
> - Large Language Models are capable of synthesizing complex executable code for both environments and agent policies, offering advantages in generalizability and interpretability over traditional neural encodings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of artificial intelligence agents capable of continuous learning and generalization in dynamic and unpredictable environments represents a significant challenge in AI research. Traditional methods for training these agents often rely on static or manually constructed environments and large volumes of human-curated data. This approach frequently results in agents that exhibit poor generalization beyond their specific training distribution.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a paradigm for agents to learn through extensive interaction in simulated environments. However, RL methods typically inherit the limitation of their training environments being either fixed or manually designed. Constructing an environment distribution that encompasses the diversity and variability required for robust real-world performance is inherently difficult, leading to RL agents that struggle to generalize to novel conditions. Domain randomization (DR) attempts to address this by exposing agents to a broad distribution of environments, but it often lacks adaptivity and can generate tasks that are either trivial or overly difficult to solve.

Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) represents an advancement by automatically generating curricula of environments that adapt in difficulty based on an agent's performance. UED dynamically tailors environments to challenge agent weaknesses, thereby promoting continual learning. However, conventional UED approaches commonly rely on randomization or simple heuristics for environment generation, which can restrict the diversity and relevance of the resulting tasks.

Concurrently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in generating code-based outputs, including environment specifications and agent policies. Previous research has explored LLM-driven environment generation and policy design separately. While programmatic representations offer benefits such as improved generalizability, modularity, reusability, verifiability, and interpretability compared to neural encodings, existing LLM-based approaches often decouple environment and agent learning or focus on one aspect without fostering robust agent learning or continuous adaptation. The self-play paradigm, where LLMs improve themselves by taking dual roles, also exists but can be less efficient for domains where solutions are better represented by compact, specialized policies rather than monolithic models.

COvolve integrates LLM-driven environment generation and policy design into a unified, closed-loop co-evolutionary framework. This work specifically positions UED within a two-player zero-sum game, leveraging LLMs to generate programmatic environments and policies. By applying game-theoretic principles and computing mixed-strategy Nash equilibria, COvolve aims to overcome the limitations of static environments and manual curriculum design, fostering open-ended learning, and ensuring robust policy adaptation and resistance to catastrophic forgetting in continually evolving challenges.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research is to address the fundamental limitation in AI development where agents are typically trained in static or manually constructed environments. These environments restrict an agent's capacity for continual learning and often lead to poor generalization beyond the specific training distribution. The authors aim to enable agents to continually acquire new skills in dynamic and unpredictable settings, which is a core challenge in current AI research.

The key objectives of COvolve are detailed as follows:

- **Automate Environment and Policy Generation:** A primary objective is to move beyond labor-intensive human-curated data and manually designed environments. COvolve seeks to leverage the generative capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) to automatically produce both training environments and corresponding agent policies, expressed as executable Python code. This programmatic representation is motivated by its advantages in generalizability, modularity, reusability, verifiability, and interpretability.
- **Establish Adversarial Co-evolution:** The research aims to frame the interaction between environment and policy design as a two-player zero-sum game. In this game, an LLM-powered environment designer and an LLM-powered policy designer compete adversarially. The environment designer's goal is to create increasingly challenging environments that expose policy weaknesses, while the policy designer's goal is to develop more capable policies that can overcome these challenges. This adversarial dynamic is intended to induce an automated curriculum where both environments and policies co-evolve towards increasing complexity.
- **Prevent Catastrophic Forgetting and Ensure Robustness:** A significant challenge in continual learning is catastrophic forgetting, where an agent, while learning new tasks, loses the ability to solve previously mastered ones. COvolve addresses this by computing the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE) of the two-player zero-sum game. The objective is to yield a robust meta-policy—a probability distribution over a population of policies—that ensures the agent does not forget how to solve previously seen environments while simultaneously learning to solve new, more complex ones. This MSNE meta-policy is designed to provide robust performance under adversarial conditions.
- **Achieve Open-Ended Learning:** The overarching goal is to achieve open-ended learning, a state where agents can continually discover and master new skills without requiring predefined task distributions or manual intervention. By enabling a closed-loop system where environments and policies co-evolve, the framework aims to demonstrate the potential for agents to adapt to and generate an ever-expanding range of challenges.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to advance the capabilities of AI agents in terms of continuous skill acquisition, adaptability, and resilience in diverse and evolving task landscapes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

COvolve's methodology adapts the Policy Space Response Oracles (PSRO) framework to Unsupervised Environment Design (UED), conceptualizing environment and policy generation as a co-evolutionary process. This process involves an iterative interaction between an LLM-powered Environment Designer and an LLM-powered Policy Designer, structured as a two-player zero-sum game. Both environments and policies are represented as executable Python code.

The overall algorithm, COvolve, operates in a loop over a series of iterations:

1. **Initialization:** The process begins with an initial environment (𝜃₀) and an empty policy sequence (P). A payoff matrix (M) is also initialized.

2. **Iterative Co-evolution Loop:** For each iteration (𝑡 from 0 to 𝑇):

 - **Policy Design (Structural Mutation + Selection):**
 - The Policy Designer (Ψ) generates *K* candidate policies (˜𝜋₁..., ˜𝜋_K). These candidates are produced by applying LLM-guided structural program transformations to the current "best" policy (typically the policy with the highest weight in the previous MSNE, or the latest policy for 𝑡=0). The LLM is conditioned on the level-specific observation and action spaces (O_𝜃_𝑡-1, A_𝜃_𝑡-1).
 - Each candidate policy is evaluated on the *latest* environment (𝜃_𝑡-1) (or 𝜃₀ for the first iteration).
 - The highest-performing candidate (𝜋_𝑡) based on the utility function (𝑈_𝜃_𝑡-1(˜𝜋_j)) is selected and appended to the growing policy sequence (P).

 - **Update Payoff Matrix (M):**
 - The system then updates the empirical payoff matrix M. For every policy (𝜋_i) in the current policy sequence P and every environment (𝜃_j) in the current environment set L, the expected return (𝑚_ij = 𝑈_𝜃_j(𝜋_i)) is computed. This constitutes a cross-population fitness computation. The utility 𝑈_𝜃(𝜋) is defined as the expected discounted return of policy 𝜋 in environment 𝜃, averaged over 100 episodes.

 - **Recompute Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium (MSNE):**
 - To ensure robustness and prevent catastrophic forgetting, COvolve computes the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium (𝑝*) over the current policy population. This is achieved by solving a minimax optimization problem (Equation 1), which finds a probability distribution over the policies in P that maximizes the worst-case expected payoff against the current set of environments. This linear program is formulated using PuLP and solved with the CBC backend solver.
 - The resulting 𝑝* represents the meta-policy. During evaluation, an agent samples a policy (𝜋_i) from this distribution (𝜋_i ~ 𝑝*) at the start of each episode and executes actions accordingly.

 - **Best Response Environment Design (Structural Mutation + Selection):**
 - The Environment Designer (Λ) generates *K* candidate environments (˜𝜃₁..., ˜𝜃_K) by applying LLM-guided structural program mutations to the previous environment (𝜃_𝑡-1).
 - Each candidate environment (˜𝜃_j) is evaluated based on how much it minimizes the *expected return of the MSNE policy* (E_𝜋_i~𝑝* [𝑈_˜𝜃_j(𝜋_i)]). This incentivizes the environment to exploit weaknesses in the overall MSNE policy mixture.
 - The candidate environment (𝜃_𝑡+1) that minimizes this expected return is selected.
 - This new environment is added to the growing environment set (L).

3. **Return:** After *T* iterations, the framework returns the final MSNE policy distribution (𝑝*) and the evolved set of environment levels (L).

**LLM Implementation:** For the main experiments, GPT-5.2 is utilized as the generative model for both policy and environment synthesis. The LLM receives specific prompts (detailed in the appendix) that dynamically reflect task parameters and environment configurations. All generated code is dynamically validated and executed. Crucially, environment generation includes predefined helper functions and solvability checks (e.g., BFS-based) to ensure feasibility and avoid generating unsolvable tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates COvolve across three distinct domains: MiniGrid symbolic maze-solving, PyGame geometric 2D navigation, and CARLA urban driving. The primary findings revolve around the emergent curriculum, robustness provided by the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium (MSNE), generalization capabilities, and insights from ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Emergent Curriculum and Environment Complexity

COvolve consistently produced progressively more complex environments across all three domains.
- **MiniGrid:** Evolved environments transitioned from simple grids to larger mazes with dense walls, multiple locked doors, and requiring sequential key retrieval and use.
- **PyGame:** Tasks evolved from open arenas to cluttered maps with numerous obstacles, narrow corridors, and long detours, demanding sophisticated geometric planning.
- **CARLA:** Environments advanced from basic urban driving on empty roads to crowded streets with increased traffic density, pedestrian activity, and adversarial driver behaviors (e.g., abrupt braking, traffic-light violations).

The environment designer effectively generated tasks that escalated in structural complexity and diversity over time, inducing an automated curriculum without explicit predefined difficulty levels.

#### 4.2. Robustness and Catastrophic Forgetting (MSNE vs. Greedy/Uniform)

The core benefit of computing the MSNE was demonstrated in preventing catastrophic forgetting and ensuring robust policy performance.
- **UED-Greedy:** Strategies that retained only the latest best policy (UED-Greedy) showed a significant reduction in performance on earlier environments as the curriculum progressed. This indicated that these policies overfit to the most recent challenge and forgot how to solve previous ones.
- **COvolve (MSNE):** In contrast, the MSNE mixture consistently maintained high performance across the full archive of environments as it grew over iterations. The MSNE assigns optimal mixture weights to policies in the population, effectively leveraging older, specialized policies to preserve performance on simpler tasks while newer policies address emergent complexities. When evaluated across all environments, COvolve's MSNE selection yielded higher average performance compared to the UED-Greedy strategy.
- **UED-Uniform:** A uniform mixture of all policies performed better than UED-Greedy but underperformed the MSNE, confirming that optimized mixture weights are crucial.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Unseen Environments

The policies generated by COvolve demonstrated generalization capabilities to unseen, standardized benchmark environments.
- **MiniGrid:** MSNE policies achieved 100% success rates on MiniGrid-MultiRoom-N6-v0, MiniGrid-DoorKey-16x16-v0, and MiniGrid-LockedRoom-v0, all of which are simpler than the evolved environments (e.g., involving fewer key-door dependencies or less constrained geometry).
- **CARLA:** MSNE policies showed strong performance (0.71 ± 0.05 success rate) on CARLA Town02, a different and more complex map not encountered during co-evolution.
These results suggest that the learned policies did not merely overfit to the specific evolved environments but acquired generalizable skills. The fact that evolved environments were often strictly harder than these benchmarks underscores the effectiveness of the environment designer in creating a challenging curriculum.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Curriculum Necessity:** A zero-shot ablation, where LLMs directly attempted to generate policies for the hardest environment in each domain without intermediate curricula, consistently failed. This highlights that the progressive curriculum generated through co-evolution is a necessary component for effective policy synthesis.
- **Comparison to Neural Network-based RL:** Standard RL algorithms (PPO, SAC for continuous; PPO, QR-DQN for discrete) trained on the fully observable evaluation tasks exhibited sharp performance degradation with increasing task complexity. They achieved near-zero rewards and success rates in the harder evolved environments, indicating that LLM-generated code-as-policies provided a more effective approach for these complex, programmatic tasks.
- **Weaker Language Models (GPT-4.1):** When COvolve was run with GPT-4.1, a weaker generative model, overall performance was lower, and simpler environments and policies were produced. However, even with this weaker LLM, the MSNE strategy consistently mitigated forgetting and maintained robustness across the environment archive, outperforming UED-Greedy, thereby reinforcing the value of the game-theoretic approach.

In summary, COvolve demonstrates that an adversarial co-evolutionary framework, driven by LLMs and grounded in game theory (MSNE), can successfully generate complex environments and robust, generalizable policies while effectively addressing catastrophic forgetting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The COvolve framework presents several significant contributions and holds notable potential impact on the fields of artificial intelligence, reinforcement learning, and automated system design.

Firstly, the work demonstrates a viable pathway toward **open-ended learning** without relying on predefined task distributions or extensive manual intervention. By enabling a closed-loop system where Large Language Models (LLMs) act as both environment designers and policy generators, COvolve moves beyond static training paradigms. This capacity for self-sustaining learning and generation of challenges is a crucial step towards developing more autonomous and adaptive AI systems.

Secondly, the introduction of a **game-theoretic framework (two-player zero-sum game) for adversarial co-evolution** represents a principled approach to Unsupervised Environment Design (UED). By framing the interaction as a competition where the environment designer seeks to expose policy weaknesses and the policy designer adapts, COvolve naturally induces an **automated curriculum** of increasing complexity. This method contrasts with heuristic-based UEDs and offers a robust mechanism for driving agents towards higher capabilities.

Thirdly, the adoption of **Mixed-Strategy Nash Equilibrium (MSNE)** computation is critical for achieving **robust policy design and preventing catastrophic forgetting**. Unlike approaches that might only retain the latest best policy, COvolve's meta-policy, derived from the MSNE, ensures that the agent maintains proficiency across the entire historical archive of evolved environments. This is vital for continual learning systems, enabling them to build upon past knowledge without degrading performance on previously mastered tasks.

Fourthly, the use of **LLM-generated executable Python code for both environments and policies** highlights the power and flexibility of LLMs as intelligent designers rather than just language processors. This programmatic representation confers advantages such as improved generalizability, modularity, reusability, verifiability, and interpretability compared to traditional neural network policies. The ability of LLMs to synthesize complex logical structures, including symbolic planning for maze-solving and nuanced control for urban driving, extends their application beyond natural language tasks.

The empirical results across diverse domains (MiniGrid, PyGame, CARLA) underscore the framework's versatility. The emergent curricula in these domains, ranging from symbolic planning challenges to continuous geometric navigation and high-fidelity urban driving, indicate that COvolve can effectively scale to various types of agent learning problems. The demonstrated generalization to unseen, simpler benchmarks further suggests that the co-evolution process does not lead to overfitting but rather to the acquisition of transferable skills.

Potential impacts include:
- **Accelerated AI Development:** By automating the generation of training environments and policies, COvolve could significantly reduce the human effort and cost associated with developing and refining AI agents, particularly for complex, multi-faceted tasks.
- **More Capable and Resilient Agents:** Agents trained within such evolving curricula are likely to be more robust, adaptable, and less susceptible to brittle performance in novel situations, leading to more reliable AI systems.
- **Advancements in Foundational AI Research:** The framework provides a concrete testbed for exploring fundamental questions related to emergent complexity, curriculum learning, and the role of generative models in creating self-improving AI.

While the current work relies on helper functions to ensure environment feasibility and may benefit from more principled control over difficulty (e.g., minimax regret), it establishes a foundational approach for LLM-driven co-evolution to foster open-ended and robust learning.
