# A Humanoid Visual-Tactile-Action Dataset for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Kwon et al. introduce the first humanoid visual-tactile-action dataset for manipulating deformable soft objects under diverse pressure conditions, collected using dexterous hands with 2,124 high-resolution tactile sensors. The work demonstrates that dense tactile signals provide superior discriminative power for task conditions compared to sparse representations, although current policy learning optimization methods face challenges in fully leveraging this high-dimensional data.

## Problem

- Existing robot learning datasets primarily focus on rigid objects, neglecting the complexities of contact-rich manipulation with deformable soft objects.
- Prior datasets lack adequate representation of diverse pressure conditions and often utilize basic robotic grippers without anthropomorphic hands or high-resolution, spatially distributed tactile sensing.
- Current multi-modal datasets for manipulation often lack action labels or humanoid embodiments, limiting their applicability to complex real-world tasks requiring sensitive interactive control.

## Method

- **HumanoidVTA** — a novel humanoid ==visual-tactile-action dataset== using a teleoperated robot equipped with ==Inspire Hands==, featuring **2,124 high-resolution tactile sensors** across fingers and palm.
- Collected multi-modal data (==proprioception==, ==egocentric vision==, ==dense tactile signals==) from the humanoid robot manipulating soft objects (towel, sponge) under distinct 'strong' and 'weak' pressure conditions across four tasks.
- Employed a state-of-the-art imitation learning baseline (==Action Chunking Transformer (ACT)==) to evaluate the utility of the dataset, comparing policy learning performance with dense versus sparse tactile inputs.

## Results

- Dense tactile signals, visualized through **t-SNE**, clearly separated distinct task conditions and pressure levels, whereas sparse signals failed to do so, highlighting the discriminative power of high-resolution sensing.
- Policy learning experiments using ACT showed stable training loss reduction, but a relatively **small MAE gap** between models trained with dense vs. sparse tactile signals, suggesting current optimization limitations.
- Real-world deployment demonstrated the robot's ability to adapt actions to task conditions, though tasks requiring precise contact control (e.g., sponge grasping) presented challenges and would benefit from richer data and extended training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Manipulation of soft objects induces dynamic, complex, and time-varying tactile patterns across diverse contact regions, contrasting with the stable patterns observed during rigid object interaction.
> - Dense tactile signals provide a significantly more discriminative representation for distinguishing different tasks and pressure conditions compared to low-resolution sparse tactile signals.
> - Effectively leveraging the high dimensionality and potential noise of dense tactile signals for policy learning remains a challenge for current optimization strategies, indicating a need for more efficient processing methods.
