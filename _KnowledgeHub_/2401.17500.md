# LeTO: Learning Constrained Visuomotor Policy with Differentiable Trajectory Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> LeTO integrates differentiable trajectory optimization into visuomotor imitation learning, enabling policies to intrinsically generate safe, constraint-satisfying, and smooth robotic movements. The framework achieves high success rates while significantly reducing accelerations and contact forces compared to existing methods across simulated and real-world manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing visuomotor imitation learning methods often produce uncertain actions and lack explicit safety guarantees, leading to system instability and reduced robustness in robotic applications.
- Current imitation learning approaches frequently overlook critical requirements for trajectory smoothness and adherence to physical safety constraints (e.g., position, velocity, acceleration limits).
- The 'black box' nature of neural network policies in robot learning hinders interpretability and makes it difficult to intrinsically incorporate and enforce explicit physical constraints.

## Method

- **LeTO** integrates a ==differentiable trajectory optimization (DTO)== layer directly into the neural network architecture, enabling end-to-end learning of constrained actions.
- The DTO layer formulates action generation as a ==Quadratic Program (QP)== that optimizes for an action sequence while explicitly enforcing position, velocity, and acceleration constraints, alongside promoting trajectory smoothness.
- An ==LSTM-based observation encoder== maps raw visual and robot state inputs to a latent embedding, which then serves as a ==linear cost term== for the DTO layer, blending data-driven representation with model-based optimization.

## Results

- LeTO achieved success rates comparable to DiffusionPolicy (e.g., **100%** in 'Can' tasks) in simulations while significantly reducing maximum linear acceleration (e.g., **2 m/s²** for LeTO vs. **15.5 m/s²** for LSTM-GMM).
- In real-world tasks, LeTO demonstrated improved robustness to out-of-distribution observations and higher success rates (e.g., **13/20** for 'Move-the-stack') with significantly reduced peak acceleration and contact forces (e.g., **16.1 N** peak contact force vs. **27.3 N** for DiffusionPolicy).
- An ablation study confirmed that tighter acceleration constraints directly lead to smoother trajectories and LeTO maintains task success, showcasing its direct control over output characteristics and interpretability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating differentiable optimization into imitation learning creates a 'gray box' model that combines neural network's representational power with optimization's safety, interpretability, and constraint guarantees.
> - Embedding explicit physical constraints directly within the policy's learning process intrinsically generates safe and smoother trajectories, outperforming post-hoc clipping or external safety modules.
> - The framework offers interpretability and controllability, as output trajectory characteristics (e.g., smoothness) directly correlate with specified constraint values, a feature absent in purely black-box neural networks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot learning, specifically focusing on visuomotor imitation learning (IL) for robotic manipulation. Imitation learning is a prevalent method for deriving robot policies from demonstrations, enabling robots to perform complex tasks by learning a mapping from observations to actions. While imitation learning, particularly with neural networks, has demonstrated success in acquiring diverse manipulation skills, it inherently presents challenges related to safety, uncertainty, and trajectory quality in real-world robotic applications.

Existing imitation learning research has addressed various aspects, such as mitigating compounding errors to enhance action accuracy or representing multi-modal action distributions using methods like implicit policies, Mixture Density Networks (MDNs), or diffusion models. However, the authors identify a significant gap in these approaches: they often do not explicitly account for critical safety constraints or the smoothness of generated trajectories, which are paramount for stable and reliable robotic operation.

In contrast, trajectory optimization (TO) methods, such as CHOMP, TrajOpt, and minimum-snap trajectory generation, are well-established for producing safe and smooth trajectories by explicitly optimizing for these criteria. However, traditional TO methods typically rely on model-based planning and may lack the generalization and representational power of neural networks, especially when dealing with high-dimensional observations like camera images.

The paper aims to bridge the gap between these two paradigms by integrating differentiable optimization into neural network-based imitation learning. Previous work on differentiable optimization in robot learning has explored its use in modeling discontinuous dynamics, differentiable Model Predictive Control (MPC), or Koopman operator theory for control. Some approaches have combined image encoders with differentiable MPC for specific tasks like tactile grasping, or embedded differentiable optimization for navigation and obstacle avoidance. However, the authors contend that these methods are often not suitable for general policy learning in high-dimensional observation spaces or for dexterous manipulation tasks that require both skill learning and constraint satisfaction.

The work builds upon the concept of differentiable trajectory optimization, which has been explored in methods like DiffTOP, but differentiates itself by focusing specifically on generating *safe and constrained* trajectories rather than primarily capturing multi-modal distributions. The authors also distinguish their work from Riemannian Motion Policies (RMP) by emphasizing constrained motion at the task space trajectory level with differentiable optimization, which they argue is better suited for visuomotor policies and real-world tasks.

By proposing LeTO, the authors introduce a framework that is positioned as the first visuomotor imitation learning method to leverage differentiable optimization and demonstrate its effectiveness in producing safe, constraint-controlled actions in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks without requiring additional modules for safety. This approach attempts to create a "gray box" model that benefits from both the data-driven capabilities of neural networks and the theoretical guarantees and interpretability of optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an imitation learning algorithm, named LeTO (Learning Constrained Visuomotor Policy with Differentiable Trajectory Optimization), that can generate safe and constraint-satisfying robotic behaviors from human demonstrations.

The motivation for this work stems from several critical limitations observed in existing imitation learning methods when applied to robotic systems:
- **Uncertainty and Lack of Safety Guarantees:** Neural networks, while powerful in learning complex mappings, often produce actions with inherent uncertainty. For robotic systems that interact physically with the environment and potentially humans, this uncertainty translates into a lack of theoretical safety guarantees, which can lead to system instability, reduced robustness, and unsafe operation.
- **Overlooking Safety and Smoothness:** Many state-of-the-art imitation learning algorithms prioritize accuracy in fitting human demonstrations or capturing multi-modal action distributions. However, they frequently overlook the crucial requirements of safety constraints (e.g., limits on position, velocity, acceleration) and the smoothness of generated trajectories. Unsmooth or jerky movements can increase wear and tear on hardware, lead to task failure (e.g., knocking over objects), or create hazardous situations.
- **"Black Box" Nature of Neural Networks:** Traditional neural networks used in imitation learning are often considered "black boxes," meaning their decision-making process lacks interpretability and it is difficult to explicitly incorporate and enforce physical constraints. This makes it challenging to ensure that robot behaviors comply with specific operational limits.
- **Need for Integrated Constraint Management:** Unlike optimization-based methods that naturally incorporate constraints, integrating such constraints into neural network policies typically involves post-hoc clipping or external modules, which can negatively impact performance or complicate the learning process. The authors hypothesize that an end-to-end integration of constraints directly within the learning framework would be more effective.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of LeTO are:
- **Integrate Differentiable Optimization:** To embed a differentiable optimization layer directly into the neural network architecture, allowing for end-to-end training.
- **Formulate as Trajectory Optimization:** To conceptualize this embedded layer as a trajectory optimization problem, enabling the policy to intrinsically generate actions that adhere to safety and smoothness constraints.
- **Balance Training Objectives:** To develop a learning framework that can balance the objectives of minimizing errors with human demonstrations, ensuring trajectory smoothness, and satisfying explicit constraints (position, velocity, acceleration) during the training process itself.
- **Enhance Interpretability and Safety:** To evolve beyond the "black box" nature of pure neural networks by introducing a "gray box" approach that combines the powerful representational capabilities of neural networks with the safety, interpretability, and constraint guarantees typically associated with optimization-based methods.
- **Empirical Validation:** To quantitatively evaluate LeTO's performance in terms of success rates, trajectory quality (smoothness), and robustness to out-of-distribution (OOD) data in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks.
- **Provide a Practical Example:** To demonstrate a practical and effective method for integrating neural networks with trajectory optimization for real-time policy generation in robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LeTO (Learning Constrained Visuomotor Policy with Differentiable Trajectory Optimization) is designed as an end-to-end learning framework that integrates a differentiable trajectory optimization (DTO) layer into a neural network. The approach assumes access to an offline dataset of human demonstrations, comprising observations and corresponding robot actions.

**Training Data Sampling:**
The policy is trained to predict a sequence of actions rather than a single action at each timestep, drawing inspiration from Model Predictive Control (MPC) and diffusion policies. The dataset is sampled into fixed-length sequences, where each sequence includes an observation at time `t_0` and a corresponding demonstrated action sequence of length `T_p`. The learning objective is to minimize the error (e.g., Mean Squared Error, MSE) between the predicted action sequence and the demonstrated action sequence. During online policy rollout, the robot executes the first `T_a` steps of the predicted action sequence, then repeats the process with new observations (receding horizon planning).
Actions are categorized into discrete (e.g., grasping) and continuous (e.g., end-effector velocity/increment). The differentiable optimization primarily applies to the continuous actions. A selection matrix `S` is used to extract the continuous action vector `v` from the full action vector.

**Observation Encoder:**
The observation encoder, denoted `E_θ`, processes raw observations (e.g., RGB images from multiple cameras and robot states) and maps them to a latent embedding `e`. To capture temporal correlations within a task, a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) architecture, specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), is employed. At each time step `t`, the encoder outputs `e_t` and an updated hidden state `h_t+1`. The dimension of `e_t` is set to `T_p * D_y` to align with the expected input of the DTO layer, where `T_p` is the predicted sequence length and `D_y` is the action dimension.

**Differentiable Trajectory Optimization (DTO) Layer:**
The core of LeTO is the DTO layer, which maps the latent embedding `e_t` from the observation encoder to a predicted action sequence `y_hat_t`. This mapping is achieved by solving a quadratic program (QP).
The predicted velocity sequence `v_hat_t` is derived from `y_hat_t` using the selection matrix `S`. Subsequently, the predicted acceleration sequence `a_hat_t` is computed as the finite difference of `v_hat_t`, and the predicted position sequence `p_hat_t` is computed by integrating `v_hat_t` from the current robot end-effector position `p_t`.

The forward pass of the DTO layer is formulated as the following optimization problem:
`y*_t = argmin_{y_hat_t} (1/2 * y_hat_t^T * Q * y_hat_t + e_t^T * y_hat_t + alpha/2 * a_hat_t^T * a_hat_t)`

Subject to a set of convex constraints:
- **Position Constraints:** `b_min <= A_pos * p_hat_t+i <= b_max` for each step `i` in the predicted sequence.
- **Velocity Constraints:** `v_min <= v_hat_t+j <= v_max` for each step `j`.
- **Acceleration Constraints:** `a_min <= a_hat_t+k <= a_max` for each step `k`.

In this formulation:
- `Q` is a learned parameter matrix that determines the quadratic cost associated with the action sequence.
- `e_t` represents the linear cost term, derived from the latent embedding.
- `alpha` is a constant coefficient that controls the smoothness of the trajectory by penalizing high accelerations. A larger `alpha` promotes smoother trajectories.
- `A_pos`, `b_max,min`, `v_max,min`, and `a_max,min` are interpretable, model-based parameters that are *not learned*. Instead, they are predefined based on task-specific requirements and physical limits.

The optimization problem is rewritten by substituting the definitions of `a_hat_t` and `p_hat_t` in terms of `y_hat_t`, transforming it into a standard Quadratic Program (QP). To ensure the feasibility of the QP and stable training, `Q` is parameterized using a Cholesky factorization `Q = L * L^T + epsilon * I`, where `L` is a learned lower triangular matrix and `epsilon` is a small scalar. The authors state that the QP is always feasible during training if the initial position `p_t,0` for each demonstration satisfies the position constraints, regardless of the learned `L` or `e_t`. The DTO layer, optimizing a linear transformation under motion constraints, is argued to retain the representational power necessary for fitting human demonstrations when combined with the LSTM encoder.

**Policy Deployment:**
During real-time deployment on a robot, the RNN encoder is unrolled step-by-step, with its hidden state refreshed every `T_s` steps. To account for the continuous nature of online execution versus discrete training clips, an additional constraint is introduced into the DTO layer's optimization problem: `a_min * Delta_t <= v_hat_t - v_hat_t-1 <= a_max * Delta_t`. This constraint ensures that the predicted velocity `v_hat_t` is consistent with the previously executed action `v_hat_t-1`, effectively linking consecutive actions in the continuous rollout.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates LeTO in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against several imitation learning baselines, including IBC, LSTM-GMM, and DiffusionPolicy.

**Simulation Evaluation:**
- **Tasks:** "Can" (pick and place) and "Square" (grasping and assembling) from the RoboMimic benchmark.
- **Success Rates (Table III):** LeTO demonstrates success rates comparable to DiffusionPolicy, achieving 100% in both proficient-human (ph) and multi-human (mh) "Can" tasks, and 94.7% (ph) / 88.0% (mh) in "Square" tasks. These rates significantly surpass IBC (e.g., 8%/0% for "Can") and LSTM-GMM (e.g., 94.6%/78.7% for "Can"/"Square"). Notably, applying post-hoc clipping to enforce constraints on DiffusionPolicy and LSTM-GMM *reduced* their success rates, indicating that end-to-end constraint integration is more effective than naive constraint application.
- **Trajectory Quality (Tables IV-VII):** LeTO consistently generates trajectories with superior smoothness. For instance, in the "Can" ph task, the maximum linear acceleration for LSTM-GMM was reported as 15.50 m/s², while LeTO achieved 2 m/s², demonstrating a substantial reduction in jerkiness. LeTO exhibited lower average mean, maximum, and standard deviation for both linear and rotational accelerations across all simulation tasks. The number of "optimal indicators" (metrics within 100-110% of the minimum value) was consistently higher for LeTO, highlighting its ability to produce high-quality, smoother movements.
- **Ablation Study on Acceleration Constraints (Table VIII):** This study on the "Square" ph task investigated the impact of varying acceleration constraints on LeTO's performance. The results showed a parabolic relationship between constraint tightness and success rate: both excessively tight (e.g., 0.05) and overly lenient (e.g., 0.5, 1) constraints led to reduced success rates compared to a balanced constraint (e.g., 0.1 or 0.2). Crucially, the study demonstrated that tighter acceleration constraints directly resulted in smoother trajectories, with metrics like maximum linear acceleration decreasing proportionally to the constraint value. This finding underscores LeTO's interpretability and controllability, as its output trajectory characteristics directly correspond to the specified constraints, a feature absent in black-box neural networks.

**Real-world Evaluation:**
- **Tasks:** "Move-the-stack" (smoothly transport stacked objects without falling) and "Arrange-chopsticks" (pick and arrange chopsticks with minimal contact force).
- **Move-the-stack (Table IX):** LeTO achieved a higher success rate (13/20) compared to LSTM-GMM and DiffusionPolicy (both 4/20). LeTO also significantly reduced the peak acceleration (0.250) compared to DiffusionPolicy (0.721) and LSTM-GMM (0.647). This highlights LeTO's ability to generate smooth trajectories critical for delicate manipulation and its robustness against out-of-distribution (OOD) observations (e.g., a falling chopstick), where baselines often exhibited erratic behaviors.
- **Arrange-chopsticks (Table X):** LeTO achieved a high success rate (17/20), comparable to DiffusionPolicy (18/20). However, LeTO significantly reduced contact forces with the tabletop. Its average maximum contact force was 11.7 N (compared to 13.2 N and 13.9 N for baselines), and its peak contact force was 16.1 N (compared to 27.3 N and 24.8 N for baselines). This reduction in impact force, facilitated by explicit position constraints, is crucial for hardware safety and task robustness in sensitive manipulation scenarios.

**Training Time (Table XI):** Acknowledging a limitation, LeTO exhibited a longer training time (7.25 hours for square-ph) compared to DiffusionPolicy (0.92 hours) and LSTM-GMM (2.90 hours) on a single GeForce RTX 3080. This is attributed to the cubic complexity of solving the QP in the differentiable optimization layer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LeTO framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning by addressing a critical challenge: integrating theoretical safety and constraint guarantees into visuomotor imitation learning. Its "gray box" approach combines the powerful representational capabilities of neural networks with the interpretability and constraint-satisfying properties of optimization, offering a balanced methodology for robotic control.

The potential impacts and significance of this work are multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Safety and Reliability in Robotics:** By intrinsically generating constrained and smooth trajectories, LeTO directly improves the safety and reliability of robotic systems. This is crucial for real-world deployment, where robots interact with dynamic environments, delicate objects, and potentially humans. The empirical results demonstrate that LeTO can achieve high task success rates while simultaneously adhering to explicit physical constraints, which is often not possible with purely data-driven, black-box approaches.
- **Improved Hardware Longevity and Robustness:** The generation of smoother trajectories reduces wear and tear on robot hardware. This translates to extended lifespan for robotic components and enhances the overall robustness and stability of the system over prolonged operations, mitigating the cumulative burden that jerky or unconstrained movements can impose.
- **Robustness to Out-of-Distribution Data:** LeTO demonstrated robustness in the face of out-of-distribution observations in real-world tasks (e.g., a falling chopstick). The inherent constraint satisfaction through the differentiable optimization layer helps maintain stable and safe behavior even when the perceptual input deviates from the training distribution, which is a common challenge for neural network policies.
- **Increased Interpretability and Controllability:** The ability to explicitly define and enforce physical constraints (position, velocity, acceleration) within the learning process makes LeTO more interpretable than purely neural network-based policies. The ablation study on acceleration constraints illustrated that the trajectory characteristics directly respond to the specified constraints, offering a level of control and predictability that is vital for validation and deployment in critical applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** LeTO provides a practical and effective example of integrating differentiable optimization into an end-to-end visuomotor imitation learning framework. This opens several promising avenues for future research:
 - **Computational Efficiency:** Addressing the acknowledged limitation of increased training time by exploring more efficient QP solvers or learned approximations of the optimization layer.
 - **Scalability to Large Datasets:** Designing LeTO variants with transformer architectures to leverage larger datasets, while continuing to improve computational efficiency.
 - **Low-Data Regimes:** Investigating how differentiable optimization layers, structured with prior knowledge, can enable more effective policy learning from limited demonstration data.
 - **Reinforcement Learning Integration:** Extending LeTO to reinforcement learning to develop safe and interpretable policies in scenarios where direct human demonstrations might be scarce or complex.
 - **High-Safety Scenarios:** Applying LeTO in highly sensitive domains such as human-robot interaction, surgical robotics, or tasks requiring high dynamics or heavy loads, where guaranteed constraint compliance is non-negotiable.
 - **Complex Obstacle Avoidance:** While LeTO currently relies on convex constraints, extending the framework to handle non-convex constraints for collision-free motion generation in complex, obstacle-rich environments represents an important future direction.

In summary, LeTO offers a practical and empirically validated approach to imbue imitation learning policies with critical safety and quality guarantees, representing a step towards more reliable, interpretable, and deployable robotic autonomy.
