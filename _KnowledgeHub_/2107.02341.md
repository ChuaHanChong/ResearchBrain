# Feature Fusion Vision Transformer for Fine-Grained Visual Categorization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Warwick and Griffith University developed the Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT) to enhance fine-grained visual categorization by fusing discriminative tokens from multiple transformer layers. This method achieved 91.5% accuracy on Stanford Dogs and demonstrated superior performance on challenging small-scale, ultra-fine-grained datasets like CottonCultivar80 with 57.92% accuracy.

## Problem

- Existing FGVC methods, including CNN-based and prior ViT adaptations, struggle to efficiently capture subtle, multi-level discriminative features required for differentiating visually similar subordinate categories.
- Standard Vision Transformers (ViTs) tend to focus on global information in deeper layers, often neglecting the crucial local and low-level features vital for fine-grained distinctions.
- Prior ViT-based FGVC approaches introduce computational overhead, such as increased patch numbers, and may not fully integrate features from different network hierarchies.

## Method

- A **Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT)** framework is proposed, extending the standard ViT architecture to explicitly incorporate multi-level feature information.
- A ==Feature Fusion Module== aggregates important tokens from shallow to deep transformer layers, enriching the final classification token with both local and global discriminative features.
- The parameter-free ==Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS)== module efficiently selects discriminative tokens from each layer by considering mutual attention weights between the class token and patch tokens.

## Results

- FFVT achieved **91.5%** accuracy on Stanford Dogs, surpassing previous state-of-the-art ViT methods, and reached **91.6%** on CUB-200-2011 with comparable performance but improved computational efficiency.
- The model demonstrated superior accuracy on challenging small-scale, ultra-fine-grained datasets, attaining **57.92%** on CottonCultivar80 and **44.17%** on SoyCultivarLocal, outperforming all other listed methods.
- Ablation studies confirmed the effectiveness of the Feature Fusion Module and MAWS, yielding accuracy improvements of up to **+6.67%** on CottonCultivar80 over the baseline Vision Transformer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing multi-level local and low-level features significantly enhances the discriminative power of Vision Transformers for fine-grained tasks, as deep layers often prioritize global context.
> - Bidirectional attention-based token selection (MAWS) effectively identifies crucial discriminative features without additional parameters, outperforming simpler attention-based selection methods.
> - Pure transformer architectures, when adapted with feature fusion, can achieve state-of-the-art performance in fine-grained visual categorization, even on challenging small-scale and ultra-fine-grained datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Fine-Grained Visual Categorization (FGVC) is a specialized area within computer vision focused on differentiating subordinate categories that share high inter-class similarity and often exhibit large intra-class variance. Examples include distinguishing between different species of birds or cultivars of plants. Historically, FGVC methods have largely relied on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). These CNN-based approaches typically fall into two main categories: localization-based methods and attention-based methods.

Localization-based methods aim to explicitly identify and utilize discriminative parts of an image. Early versions required manual part annotations, which are costly and time-consuming. More recent methods attempt to alleviate this by integrating Region Proposal Networks (RPNs) or Weakly Supervised Object Detection (WSOD) techniques to automatically suggest potential discriminative regions. However, these methods face challenges such as generating large bounding boxes that might include background noise, ignoring relationships between selected regions, or failing to accurately delineate subtle discriminative features that are not rectangular (e.g., leaf veins).

Attention-based CNN methods, conversely, utilize self-attention mechanisms to automatically highlight discriminative regions without requiring explicit annotations. While these methods have achieved notable results, they often incur significant computational costs.

The landscape of general image recognition has recently seen a paradigm shift with the emergence of Vision Transformers (ViTs). Originally developed for Natural Language Processing (NLP), transformers, with their self-attention mechanisms, have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in various computer vision tasks, including general image classification, image retrieval, and semantic segmentation. The inherent self-attention mechanism in ViTs, which aggregates and weights information from all image patches to a classification token, suggests their potential suitability for FGVC by automatically identifying important parts.

Despite their success in general vision tasks, the application of pure transformer architectures to FGVC remains relatively underexplored. One notable prior work, TransFG, introduced a method to adapt ViT for FGVC by selecting discriminative tokens and feeding them to the final transformer layer. However, this approach often entails increased computational complexity due to strategies like overlapping patches, and it has been observed that the deep layers of a standard ViT tend to focus more on global information, potentially overlooking the crucial local and low-level features essential for distinguishing fine-grained categories.

This current work, Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT), positions itself within this evolving landscape by specifically addressing the limitations of existing ViT adaptations for FGVC. It aims to harness the power of transformers for FGVC while explicitly tackling the issue of insufficient local and low-level feature utilization in deep transformer layers, and doing so in a computationally efficient manner, particularly on challenging small-scale and ultra-fine-grained datasets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a pure transformer-based framework, named Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT), that effectively addresses the challenges of Fine-Grained Visual Categorization (FGVC). The motivation stems from several observations regarding current approaches:

1. **Requirement for Subtle Feature Learning**: The fundamental challenge in FGVC is the accurate identification and learning of subtle yet discriminative features that differentiate visually similar subordinate categories. Traditional CNN-based methods, whether localization-based or attention-based, often struggle with computational complexity, reliance on costly annotations, or an inability to precisely capture minute, non-rectangular discriminative elements.
2. **Limitations of Standard Vision Transformers (ViT) for FGVC**: While Vision Transformers have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in general image recognition due to their powerful self-attention mechanism, their direct application to FGVC presents specific challenges. The classification token in deeper transformer layers primarily aggregates global information. For FGVC, however, local and low-level features are critical for distinguishing fine details (e.g., differences in leaf venation or subtle bird plumage variations). A standard ViT architecture may thus lack the necessary fine-grained local context in its final representation.
3. **Inefficiencies in Existing ViT-based FGVC Approaches**: Prior efforts to adapt ViTs for FGVC, such as TransFG, involve selecting discriminative tokens. However, these methods can introduce additional computational overhead, for instance, by significantly increasing the number of input patches through overlapping strategies, which consumes more GPU memory and processing time. Furthermore, these methods might not fully integrate features from different levels of the network hierarchy, potentially missing out on a richer representation.
4. **Performance on Challenging Datasets**: There is a recognized gap in understanding the capability of vision transformers on small-scale and ultra-fine-grained visual categorization (ultra-FGVC) settings, which often feature very limited training data and exceptionally subtle inter-class differences (e.g., distinguishing between plant cultivars). Addressing these challenging scenarios is crucial for broader real-world applications in domains like intelligent agriculture.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

- **To propose a novel pure transformer-based framework (FFVT)** for FGVC that can automatically detect discriminative regions and leverage multi-level information.
- **To address the deficiency of local and low-level features** in deep ViT layers by designing a feature fusion module that aggregates important tokens from various transformer layers (low, middle, and high-level) to enrich the classification token's representation.
- **To develop an efficient and parameter-free token selection mechanism** called Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS). This module aims to guide the network in effectively selecting the most informative and discriminative tokens for fusion, without introducing additional trainable parameters or significant computational overhead.
- **To comprehensively evaluate the FFVT's performance** on a diverse set of FGVC benchmarks, including both widely used large-scale datasets (CUB-200-2011, Stanford Dogs) and particularly challenging small-scale, ultra-fine-grained datasets (SoyCultivarLocal, CottonCultivar80) to verify its capability in difficult scenarios with limited data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT) is a pure transformer-based framework designed to enhance fine-grained visual categorization by specifically addressing the limitation of standard Vision Transformers (ViT) in capturing sufficient local and low-level features for subtle distinctions. The methodology builds upon the standard ViT architecture and introduces two core components: a Feature Fusion Module and a Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS) Module.

#### 3.1. Vision Transformer (ViT) Fundamentals

The FFVT framework operates on the principles of the Vision Transformer (ViT). An input image of resolution H × W is initially divided into a sequence of N fixed-size patches (P × P). These patches are then linearly projected into a D-dimensional latent embedding space. To incorporate positional information, a learnable position embedding is added to each patch embedding. An additional learnable "class token" is prepended to this sequence of patch embeddings. This combined sequence is then fed into a Transformer Encoder. The encoder consists of multiple identical layers, each comprising a Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA) module and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) block, with Layer Normalization applied before each module and residual connections. The output class token from the final transformer layer is then typically passed through a classification head (an MLP) to predict the image category.

#### 3.2. FFVT Architecture

FFVT modifies the standard ViT by introducing a feature fusion mechanism before the final transformer layer. The overall architecture involves processing image patches through initial transformer layers, selectively aggregating important tokens from these layers, and then feeding these aggregated tokens, along with the class token, into the final transformer layer for classification.

#### 3.3. Feature Fusion Module

The motivation for the Feature Fusion Module is to compensate for the observed tendency of the class token in deep ViT layers to focus predominantly on global information, potentially neglecting the local and low-level features critical for FGVC. This module aims to enrich the feature representation by incorporating discriminative tokens from earlier, shallower layers that are richer in local and middle-level information.

Specifically, the Feature Fusion Module operates as follows:
- For each transformer layer `l` (from the first to the second-to-last layer), a set of `K` "important tokens" are selected from its output. These selected tokens, denoted as `z_local_l = [z_1^l, z_2^l, ..., z_K^l]`, represent localized and discriminative features at that specific hierarchical level.
- Before the final transformer layer (`L`), instead of feeding the standard output of layer `L-1` (i.e., `z_L-1`), the inputs are reconfigured. The original class token from layer `L-1` (`z_0^L-1`) is retained. However, all the patch tokens from `L-1` are replaced with the concatenated sequence of selected local tokens from *all* preceding layers (`z_local_1`, `z_local_2`..., `z_local_L-1`).
- This fused sequence, `z_ff = [z_0^L-1; z_local_1; z_local_2; ...; z_local_L-1]`, then serves as the input to the final transformer layer. This mechanism allows the classification token in the last layer to interact directly with a richer set of features, encompassing global information (from `z_0^L-1`) as well as selected low-level, middle-level, and high-level local discriminative features.
- Finally, the classification token of the output from the last transformer layer is passed to a classification head to perform the categorization.

#### 3.4. Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS) Module

A crucial aspect of the Feature Fusion Module is the selection of "important tokens." The MAWS module is designed to achieve this effectively and efficiently, without introducing any extra learnable parameters. It leverages the attention scores generated by the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA) module itself.

The process for selecting tokens from a given transformer layer `l` is as follows:
- **Attention Score Matrix**: For each MSA head, an attention score matrix `A ∈ R^(N+1)×(N+1)` is generated, where `N` is the number of image patches. Each element `a_i,j` represents the attention score between token `i` (query) and token `j` (key). For multi-head attention, the attention scores are first averaged across all heads to obtain a single attention score matrix.
- **Single Attention Weight Selection (SAWS) (Baseline Concept)**: A naive approach, referred to as SAWS, would be to select tokens based solely on their attention scores with the class token (i.e., sorting `a_0` – the first row of `A` – and picking the `K` tokens with the highest values). However, this might select tokens that are highly attentive to the class token but themselves aggregate noisy information from other patches.
- **Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS)**: To address the potential for noise, MAWS introduces a "mutual" criterion. It selects tokens that are important not only from the perspective of the class token but also in terms of how much they attend to the class token.
 - Let `a_0,i` be the attention score of the class token (index 0) to a patch token `i`.
 - Let `b_i,0` be the attention score of a patch token `i` to the class token (index 0).
 - These scores are first normalized using the softmax function:
 `a'_0,i = e^(a_0,i) / Σ_j e^(a_0,j)`
 `b'_i,0 = e^(b_i,0) / Σ_j e^(b_j,0)`
 - The mutual attention weight `ma_i` for each token `i` is then calculated as the Hadamard product (element-wise multiplication) of these normalized scores: `ma_i = a'_0,i * b'_i,0`.
 - Finally, the `K` tokens with the highest `ma_i` values are selected to form `z_local_l`.

This MAWS strategy ensures that selected tokens are strongly related to the classification task from a bidirectional attention perspective, making the selection more robust and discriminative. The absence of additional parameters contributes to the efficiency of the module.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Pretraining**: The ViT backbone is pretrained on the ImageNet21K dataset, a common practice for leveraging large-scale data.
- **Data Augmentation**: Input images are resized (500x500 for Soy.Loc/Cotton, 600x600 for CUB/Stanford Dogs) and then cropped (384x384 for Soy.Loc/Cotton, 448x448 for CUB/Stanford Dogs) with random cropping during training and center cropping during testing. Random horizontal flipping and color augmentation (for CUB) are also applied.
- **Hyperparameters**: The number of selected tokens `K` in the Feature Fusion Module is set to 12 for CUB, Soy.Loc, and Cotton datasets, and 24 for Stanford Dogs.
- **Optimizer**: SGD optimizer with a momentum of 0.9 is used.
- **Learning Rate**: Initial learning rate is 0.02 for CUB, Soy.Loc, and Cotton datasets, and 0.003 for Stanford Dogs, with a cosine annealing scheduler.
- **Batch Size**: Batch size of 8 for most datasets, and 4 for Stanford Dogs.
- **Hardware**: Experiments were conducted on four Nvidia 2080Ti GPUs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT) was evaluated on four fine-grained visual categorization benchmarks: CUB-200-2011, Stanford Dogs, SoyCultivarLocal, and CottonCultivar80. The findings indicate competitive and, in several cases, superior performance compared to existing state-of-the-art methods, particularly on challenging ultra-fine-grained datasets.

#### 4.1. Performance on Widely Used FGVC Datasets

- **CUB-200-2011**: FFVT achieved an accuracy of 91.6%. This positions it as the second-best performing method, closely matching TransFG (91.7%), which is considered the current state-of-the-art for ViT-based FGVC on this dataset. The paper notes that FFVT achieves this comparable performance with reduced computational cost and GPU memory consumption, primarily because TransFG utilizes an overlapping patch strategy that increases the number of input patches significantly (from 784 to 1296). It is also mentioned that TransFG's reported results on CUB were obtained with a batch size twice as large as FFVT's.
- **Stanford Dogs**: FFVT achieved an accuracy of 91.5%, establishing it as the top-performing method on this benchmark among those listed. This result surpassed the second-best method, TransFG (90.6%), by a margin of 0.9 percentage points. This indicates FFVT's robust performance on another common FGVC dataset.

#### 4.2. Performance on Small-Scale Ultra-Fine-Grained Datasets

The performance of FFVT on SoyCultivarLocal and CottonCultivar80 datasets highlights its capability in extremely challenging scenarios characterized by subtle inter-class differences and very limited training data (three images per category).

- **CottonCultivar80**: FFVT obtained the highest accuracy of 57.92%, outperforming the second-best method by a substantial margin of 4.17 percentage points.
- **SoyCultivarLocal**: FFVT achieved an accuracy of 44.17%, also outperforming all other listed methods on this dataset.

These results suggest that FFVT is particularly effective at locating and leveraging discriminative regions even with minimal training data and highly subtle distinctions, which is crucial for real-world applications in domains like agriculture.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies were conducted on CottonCultivar80, SoyCultivarLocal, and CUB datasets to validate the contribution of the proposed modules.

- **Effectiveness of Feature Fusion with SAWS**: When the simple Single Attention Weight Selection (SAWS) strategy (selecting tokens based on high attention scores with the class token) was combined with feature fusion, a notable improvement was observed over the baseline ViT backbone:
 - CottonCultivar80: +4.58% accuracy increase.
 - SoyCultivarLocal: +3.50% accuracy increase.
 - CUB: +0.64% accuracy increase.
 These results confirm the importance of aggregating local and multi-level information for enhancing ViT performance in FGVC.

- **Effectiveness of MAWS vs. SAWS**: Replacing SAWS with the Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS) strategy yielded further performance gains:
 - CottonCultivar80: Total +6.67% accuracy increase over baseline ViT.
 - SoyCultivarLocal: Total +4.84% accuracy increase over baseline ViT.
 - CUB: Total +0.80% accuracy increase over baseline ViT.
 The superior performance of MAWS indicates that its bidirectional attention-based token selection mechanism is more effective at identifying truly discriminative tokens, preventing the inclusion of noisy information that might arise from simpler selection strategies.

- **Influence of Hyperparameter K**: An investigation into the number of selected tokens `K` per layer was performed on SoyCultivarLocal. FFVT achieved its best performance at `K=12` (44.17% accuracy). Performance steadily improved from `K=10` to `K=12` and then slightly decreased when `K` was further increased to 13 and 14. This suggests an optimal balance, where too few tokens might provide insufficient discriminative information, and too many might introduce noise. However, all tested `K` values showed significant improvements over the baseline ViT (39.33%), indicating that FFVT's performance is not overly sensitive to the exact value of `K` within a reasonable range.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, focusing on the Feature Fusion Vision Transformer (FFVT) for Fine-Grained Visual Categorization (FGVC), carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of computer vision.

#### 5.1. Advancement in FGVC with Pure Transformers

The FFVT framework demonstrates that pure transformer-based models can achieve state-of-the-art performance in FGVC, providing a robust alternative to the long-dominant CNN-based approaches. This is significant because transformers, with their global receptive fields and self-attention mechanisms, offer a different inductive bias compared to CNNs, which might be particularly beneficial for capturing subtle, non-local discriminative features characteristic of FGVC tasks.

#### 5.2. Addressing ViT Limitations for Fine-Grained Tasks

A key contribution of this work is the successful mitigation of a perceived limitation of standard Vision Transformers for FGVC: their tendency for deep layers to prioritize global information over crucial local and low-level features. The proposed Feature Fusion Module explicitly addresses this by aggregating discriminative tokens from various hierarchical levels. This method enhances the class token's representational capacity by incorporating fine-grained local context, which is vital for distinguishing highly similar subordinate categories.

#### 5.3. Novelty and Efficiency of Token Selection

The Mutual Attention Weight Selection (MAWS) module represents a novel and efficient approach to identifying informative tokens. By leveraging existing attention scores in a "mutual" fashion (considering attention from class token to patch and vice-versa), MAWS selects tokens that are highly relevant to the classification task without introducing additional trainable parameters or significant computational overhead. This efficiency is a practical advantage, making the model more deployable.

#### 5.4. Robustness to Data Scarcity and Ultra-Fine-Grained Differences

The strong performance of FFVT on small-scale and ultra-fine-grained datasets (SoyCultivarLocal, CottonCultivar80) is particularly impactful. These datasets pose immense challenges due to extremely subtle inter-class differences and very limited training data. FFVT's ability to achieve high accuracy in these settings suggests its potential for applications where large annotated datasets are unavailable, and distinctions are highly granular, such as in specialized scientific classification, rare species identification, or quality control in manufacturing.

#### 5.5. Broader Implications for Transformer Research

The success of FFVT reinforces the idea that pure transformer models hold immense potential across diverse computer vision settings, even those traditionally thought to require the specific inductive biases of convolutional networks. This work contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the versatility and power of transformer architectures, potentially inspiring further research into their adaptation for other specialized visual recognition tasks.

#### 5.6. Real-World Applications

The improved accuracy in FGVC has direct implications for various real-world applications. For instance, in autonomous driving, precise classification of vehicle models or road signs under various conditions is critical. In intelligent agriculture, differentiating between plant cultivars or detecting specific plant diseases often relies on fine-grained visual cues. The robust performance of FFVT, especially on ultra-fine-grained classification, could lead to more accurate and reliable systems in these domains.
