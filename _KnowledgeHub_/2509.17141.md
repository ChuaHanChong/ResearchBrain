# History-Aware Visuomotor Policy Learning via Point Tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> History-aware visuomotor policies that encode manipulation history as object-centric 3D point tracks instead of raw frames, giving policies unbounded temporal memory at near-constant cost. A lightweight track encoder aggregates tracked points per object and injects them into ACT, Diffusion Policy, and RISE backbones, lifting decision accuracy above 90% on repeated-state and long-horizon tasks where Markovian and fixed-window baselines fail.

## Problem

- Visuomotor policies assume the Markov property, so they cannot disambiguate repeated or visually similar states that depend on unobserved past actions, like whether salt was already added.
- Fixed-window history extensions still fail to distinguish repeated long-horizon states and cannot adapt their horizon to each task's differing memory requirements.
- Video-encoder and keyframe-motion history representations either incur heavy compute over full frame sequences or discard subtle transitions critical for precise manipulation.

## Method

- ==Object-centric point-track history representation==: tracked 3D points across time are encoded and aggregated per object into a compact structured memory of unbounded length.
- **HistACT**, **HistDP**, **HistRISE**: the point-track history tokens are injected into Transformer backbones of ACT and RISE, or fused with observation tokens before diffusion-based action denoising in DP.
- ==Asynchronous tracking== with ==random-dropping augmentation== decouples slow 3D point tracking from policy inference frequency, trading a small performance cost for higher throughput.

## Results

- Across five manipulation tasks HistRISE reaches up to **90-95%** success rate versus **0-65%** for RISE, ACT, TraceACT, TraceDP, and LongDP baselines, with decision accuracy above **90%** on nearly all tasks.
- 3D tracker TAPIP3D beats 2D tracker CoTracker under occlusion, **85%** vs **55%** success rate on the Guess-Hard task, with tracking accuracy **93%** vs **56%**.
- Asynchronous tracking with augmentation preserves Swap-Hard performance (**80%** success rate, **6.50** average length) while raising inference frequency from **0.93** Hz to **5.44** Hz.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compressing history to object-level abstractions rather than raw frames or keyframes can give effectively infinite temporal context without the compute blowup of video encoders.
> - 3D point tracking is markedly more robust to occlusion than 2D tracking for history representations, a gap that widens on spatially demanding tasks.
> - Decoupling perception latency from control latency via asynchronous computation plus train-time augmentation is a general recipe for adding expensive history modules to real-time policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in visuomotor policies have enabled robots to perform increasingly complex manipulation tasks, primarily leveraging behavior cloning paradigms that map visual observations directly to robot actions. A foundational assumption in many of these policies is the Markov property, which posits that the current observation contains all necessary information for optimal decision-making. However, this assumption frequently breaks down in practical robotic manipulation scenarios.

Many real-world tasks involve repeated states, long-horizon dependencies, or require memory of past actions, such as avoiding over-salting a dish after salt has already been added, remembering object positions in a swap task, or tracking the state of an object after a button press where visual changes are minimal. In these situations, relying solely on the current visual input is insufficient.

Existing approaches to address this limitation have explored extending the observation horizon to a fixed temporal window. While these methods provide some history awareness, they face two key challenges: (1) their fixed window limits their ability to distinguish repeated states or track long-horizon dependencies, and (2) they lack adaptability to the diverse and potentially unbounded memory requirements of different tasks.

More comprehensive history utilization, such as processing all historical frames, incurs substantial computational costs. Methods employing video encoders, often based on a keyframe-motion scheme, can overlook subtle yet critical information essential for precise manipulation. Other techniques like visual prompting via trace overlays have issues with scalability and information density for long temporal horizons.

This work addresses the critical gap between the need for robust historical information in visuomotor policies and the limitations of current representations. It draws inspiration from human cognitive processes, where past experiences are abstracted into compact, object-centric memories rather than storing every visual detail. The paper positions itself as a method to provide efficient and full history-awareness, enabling visuomotor policies to move beyond the Markov assumption and handle tasks requiring sophisticated temporal reasoning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind this research are detailed as follows:

1. **Overcome Limitations of the Markov Assumption**: The central motivation is to address the inherent struggle of most visuomotor policies with tasks that violate the Markov assumption. This includes scenarios with repeated states (e.g., a dish looking the same before and after salt is added), long-horizon dependencies requiring recall of previous actions (e.g., counting scoops), and tasks demanding memorization of spatial locations over time (e.g., object swapping). The goal is to enable policies to make informed decisions by effectively leveraging past information that is not available in the current observation alone.

2. **Develop a Compact and Structured History Representation**: Current methods for incorporating history are often computationally inefficient (e.g., processing all past frames) or informationally limited (e.g., fixed-length windows, keyframe-based approaches that miss subtle cues, or cluttered trace overlays). The authors aim to design a history representation that is both compact and structured, encoding only essential, task-relevant information. This is motivated by the observation that humans do not store every visual detail but rather abstract experiences into high-level, object-centric memories.

3. **Achieve Full History-Awareness with High Computational Efficiency**: A significant challenge in utilizing full historical information is the associated computational burden. The objective is to enable policies to access and process arbitrary lengths of historical data (full history-awareness) without incurring excessive computational or memory costs during training and inference. This involves developing mechanisms for compressing unbounded historical sequences into manageable features.

4. **Improve Overall Task Performance and Decision Accuracy in Non-Markovian Tasks**: The ultimate goal is to enhance the capabilities of robotic systems in complex manipulation tasks. This includes improving the success rate and, specifically, the decision accuracy in critical task phases that depend on historical context. The work aims to validate its approach across diverse memory requirements, such as task stage identification, spatial memorization, action counting, and tasks requiring pre-loaded or continuous memory.

5. **Seamless Integration with Existing Visuomotor Policy Architectures**: To ensure broad applicability and avoid re-designing entire policy frameworks, a key objective is to create a history representation that can be readily integrated into various existing visuomotor policy backbones. This allows for augmenting established policies with history-awareness without fundamental architectural changes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces an object-centric history representation based on point tracking, designed to be compact, structured, and efficiently integrated into existing visuomotor policy architectures. The approach can be broken down into several key stages:

**A. History Representation via Object Point Tracking**
The core of the method is to convert the raw observation history into an object-centric point tracking representation. This is performed as follows:
1. **Object Identification and Segmentation**: In the initial observation (`o_0`), N task-related objects are identified and segmented using SAM-2 (Segment Anything Model 2). This step ensures that only relevant objects are considered for history tracking, aligning with the concept of object-centric memory.
2. **Point Sampling and Tracking**: For each identified object, K points are randomly sampled within its segmented mask. These K points per object are then tracked across all subsequent frames, from the initial pre-loaded memory (`o_{-h_p:0}`) up to the current observation (`o_t`), using an off-the-shelf 3D point tracker, TAPIP3D. The output is a set of `N × K` point tracks, denoted as `τ(o_{-h_p:t-1}) ∈ R^(N × K × (h_p+t) × 3)`. Each track represents the spatio-temporal trajectory of a specific point on a task-relevant object, capturing its motion and dynamics. The use of a 3D tracker is justified by its robustness to occlusions and depth ambiguities prevalent in robotic manipulation.

**B. Compressing Unbounded Tracks**
Since the point tracks can be of arbitrary and unbounded length, a compression module `ϕ(·)` is introduced to generate a compact, informative feature representation.
1. **Patch Encoding for Individual Tracks**: For a single point track `T ∈ R^((h_p+t) × 3)`, it is first divided into overlapping or non-overlapping consecutive patches of a fixed size P. Each patch is then encoded using a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to capture local spatio-temporal patterns within that segment of the trajectory. Temporal positional encodings are added to these patch embeddings to inject information about the order of points within the trajectory.
2. **Track Token Generation via Cross-Attention**: A learnable token is introduced that directly performs cross-attention with the patch encodings. This mechanism condenses the sequence of patch encodings into a single, compact point track token, effectively summarizing the entire trajectory regardless of its original length.
3. **Object-Level Aggregation**: After each individual point track is compressed into a feature, the features corresponding to all K points of a single object are aggregated (e.g., by averaging) into a single "object track token." This aggregation consolidates object-level features and reduces variability introduced by random point sampling, leading to a more stable representation.
The output of this compression and aggregation process is a set of N object track tokens, `ϕ(τ(o_{-h_p:t-1})) ∈ R^(N × D)`, where D is the feature dimension, forming the compact history representation.

**C. History-Aware Policy Learning**
The compact history representation is then integrated into existing visuomotor policies:
1. **Integration into Policy Backbone**: The N object track tokens are appended alongside the current observation tokens and any other task-specific tokens. Token type encodings may be added to distinguish different types of tokens (e.g., observation, history, other).
2. **Transformer Backbone**: These combined tokens are fed into the Transformer backbone of the chosen visuomotor policy (e.g., ACT, Diffusion Policy, RISE). This allows the Transformer's attention mechanism to jointly process and attend to both current observations and historical context, enabling history-aware action prediction. Formally, `π_H(o_{-h_p:t}) = π_M(o_t, ϕ(τ(o_{-h_p:t-1})))`.
3. **Asynchronous Tracking and Robustness**: To address the computational latency of 3D trackers compared to policy inference, tracking is performed asynchronously during deployment. To maintain policy robustness under these conditions, a "random dropping augmentation" is applied during training. This augmentation simulates potential discrepancies between the policy's real-time observations and the tracker's slightly delayed or incomplete outputs, mitigating observation mismatch and enhancing robustness.

**D. Experimental Setup and Evaluation**
The methodology is evaluated on seven real-world manipulation tasks categorized by five aspects of history-awareness: "counting," "spatial memorization," "task stage identification," "pre-loaded memory," and "continuous memory." Three representative base policies—2D image-based ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), and 3D point-cloud-based RISE—are augmented with the proposed history representation (HistACT, HistDP, HistRISE). Baselines include Markovian policies, fixed-window approaches (TraceACT, TraceDP), and specialized history methods (LongDP). Performance is measured by Success Rate (SR), Decision Accuracy (DA), and Average Length (AL) for multi-stage tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address five research questions (Q1-Q5), demonstrating the efficacy and characteristics of the proposed history-aware visuomotor policies.

**Q1: Effectiveness of object-centric point track history representation.**
The object-centric point track history representation significantly improves policy performance across diverse manipulation tasks. Policies augmented with this representation, such as HistDP and HistRISE, consistently achieved decision accuracies exceeding 90% in most tasks (Table I). This indicates a substantial improvement in the ability of policies to make correct decisions in scenarios requiring historical context. Notably, HistRISE demonstrated superior performance in challenging long-horizon tasks like Swap-Easy and Swap-Hard. It is noted that the method primarily enhances decision accuracy, while action accuracy (e.g., successful grasping) largely depends on the underlying base policy.

**Q2: Leveraging historical information better than existing methods.**
The proposed history representation demonstrated strong effectiveness across all five evaluated history-awareness aspects (counting, spatial memorization, task stage identification, pre-loaded memory, continuous memory). HistDP consistently outperformed other history-aware variants built upon the Diffusion Policy (DP) baseline, including TraceDP and LongDP. HistRISE further surpassed HistDP, achieving state-of-the-art performance across all five aspects (Fig. 5). Fixed-window approaches (TraceDP, LongDP) showed limitations in long-sequence tasks (e.g., Three-Scoop, Swap-Hard) due to their inability to retain critical early-stage information over extended durations. The object-centric design was found to be crucial for enabling continuous memory, a capability discrete snapshot-based methods lack.

**Q3: Integration with pre-loaded memory.**
The history representation seamlessly integrates with pre-loaded memory, allowing policies to utilize prior observations or human actions before manipulation begins. In the Guess-Easy and Guess-Hard tasks, HistRISE effectively leveraged pre-loaded memory to assist the robot in identifying the correct box containing a toy (Table I, right). This demonstrates the framework's capacity to incorporate valuable contextual information about the environment and task dynamics beyond the robot's own interactions. While HistRISE performed well, HistDP exhibited lower accuracy in these specific Guess tasks, with a hypothesis suggesting potential overfitting of the DP base policy to implicit behavioral patterns or an overemphasis on visual observation over history point track modalities.

**Q4: Tracking quality and efficiency.**
Ablation studies on tracking quality revealed that 3D trackers significantly outperform 2D trackers for robust history awareness. HistRISE using a 3D tracker (TAPIP3D) consistently outperformed its variant with a 2D tracker (CoTracker) across all tasks (Table I). Specifically, TAPIP3D achieved 93% tracking accuracy compared to CoTracker's 56%, leading to much higher policy performance in complex tasks like Guess-Hard (85% SR vs. 55% SR for 2D tracker variant). This difference is attributed to 3D trackers' ability to maintain accurate spatial relationships and handle occlusions, which are frequent in manipulation (Fig. 6, Table II).
Regarding efficiency, asynchronous tracking substantially reduced latency compared to synchronous tracking. By employing random dropping augmentation during training, the policy could maintain comparable performance (e.g., 80% SR, 97% DA for Swap-Hard) while achieving a 5.8x improvement in efficiency (Table III), demonstrating a successful performance-efficiency trade-off.

**Q5: Point selection strategy.**
The choice of points for tracking had a notable impact on policy performance. Selecting object-centric points consistently improved history-aware policy performance. In contrast, incorporating end-effector (EE) points into the history representation consistently degraded performance across all tasks (Table I). The analysis suggests that policies might over-rely on EE point tracks, neglecting crucial visual cues. This can lead to difficulties in recovery if initial actions, such as grasping, fail, as the policy might continue based on incomplete EE information. Object-centric points, being more directly related to the task's success conditions, enabled better recovery and more reliable task completion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a robust and generalizable framework for equipping visuomotor policies with history-awareness, addressing a fundamental limitation in robotic manipulation. The significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Robotic Capabilities in Complex Tasks**: By moving beyond the Markov assumption, the proposed method enables robots to tackle a broader spectrum of real-world manipulation tasks that inherently require memory, reasoning about past actions, and understanding state changes over extended periods. This includes tasks involving counting, spatial memory, and complex task stage identification, which are critical for increasing autonomy and versatility in unstructured environments.

2. **Generalizability Across Policy Architectures**: The design allows for seamless integration into various existing visuomotor policy backbones (e.g., ACT, Diffusion Policy, RISE). This modularity means that the benefits of history-awareness can be extended to a wide range of current and future robot control systems without requiring extensive re-engineering, accelerating the development of more intelligent robotic agents.

3. **Computational Efficiency for Long-Horizon Memory**: The method effectively compresses unbounded historical information into a compact, object-centric representation. Combined with techniques like asynchronous tracking and training-time augmentation, it achieves full history-awareness without prohibitive computational costs. This efficiency is crucial for practical deployment in real-time robotic systems, making long-term memory a viable feature for complex operations.

4. **Human-Inspired Abstraction for Robotic Cognition**: Drawing inspiration from how humans abstract past experiences into essential memories, this work provides a practical mechanism for robots to form similar "object-centric memories." This approach aligns with cognitive science principles and could inform future designs for more intuitive and intelligent robotic reasoning systems.

5. **Support for Pre-Loaded and Continuous Memory**: The demonstrated ability to integrate pre-loaded memory (e.g., from human demonstrations) opens avenues for in-context learning, faster task adaptation, and more informed decision-making based on prior knowledge. The continuous nature of the point track representation also ensures that critical temporal details are preserved, unlike discrete keyframe methods.

6. **Improved Robustness and Decision-Making**: The enhanced decision accuracy, particularly in complex and ambiguous scenarios, suggests that robots can perform tasks more reliably and with fewer errors. This contributes to safer and more dependable human-robot collaboration and autonomous operation.

In summary, this work represents a significant step towards developing more intelligent and adaptive robotic systems capable of sophisticated temporal reasoning. The framework's generalizability and efficiency suggest a broad impact on the future development of visuomotor policies for real-world applications. Future work on failure recovery and extended applications of pre-loaded memory will further solidify its utility.
