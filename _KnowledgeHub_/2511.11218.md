# Humanoid Whole-Body Badminton via Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Beijing Phybot Technology Co., Ltd developed a unified whole-body reinforcement learning policy enabling a humanoid robot to play badminton autonomously. This system, trained with a multi-stage curriculum, achieves sustained rallies in simulation and high-speed returns on real hardware, demonstrating robust dynamic interaction with fast-moving objects.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face challenges in dynamic, real-world interactions with fast-moving objects, particularly in tasks requiring precise timing and coordination like badminton.
- Badminton introduces unique complexities such as uncertain aerodynamics, limited decision time, and large swing amplitudes that demand robust whole-body balance recovery.
- Existing robotic racket sport systems often rely on modular or hierarchical approaches, which limit the joint optimization of lower-body footwork and upper-body striking motions for humanoids.

## Method

- A multi-stage reinforcement learning (RL) training pipeline, utilizing ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== in IsaacGym, was developed for a 21-degrees-of-freedom (DoF) humanoid robot.
- A three-stage curriculum (Footwork Acquisition, Precision-Guided Swing Generation, Task-Focused Refinement) progressively trains the whole-body policy by adjusting reward terms and weights.
- Two control variants were explored: a target-known policy relying on an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) for shuttlecock trajectory prediction and a prediction-free policy that observes short-horizon shuttle positions.

## Results

- Two simulated humanoids running the target-known policy successfully sustained a rally of 21 consecutive returns, demonstrating robust performance over extended exchanges.
- The EKF-based target-known policy on real hardware achieved a **91.3%** success rate (42 out of 46 valid returns) for machine-fed shuttlecocks, producing outgoing shuttle speeds up to **19.1** m/s (mean **11.1** m/s).
- The prediction-free policy achieved a **71.7%** success rate on hardware, with outgoing shuttle speeds up to **18.1** m/s (mean **8.2** m/s), indicating robustness to aerodynamic variations despite a modest performance drop compared to the target-known policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The multi-stage RL curriculum effectively enables the humanoid to learn complex, coordinated whole-body skills for dynamic tasks like badminton without requiring motion priors or expert demonstrations.
> - The learned policy demonstrates emergent adaptive coordination patterns, including simultaneous stepping and backswings for distant targets, tiptoe reaching for higher interceptions, and active lower-limb push-off contributing to whole-body impulse generation during strikes.
> - A prediction-free policy, which infers hitting pose and timing implicitly from short histories of shuttlecock observations, is a viable control strategy with comparable performance to prediction-based methods, simplifying deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots have exhibited increasing capabilities in locomotion and manipulation within static environments. However, their ability to engage in dynamic, real-world interactions, particularly with fast-moving objects requiring precise timing and coordination, remains an area of active research with notable challenges. This work addresses this gap by focusing on humanoid badminton, a task that demands complex whole-body control within sub-second reaction windows.

The field of robotic racket sports, including table tennis and badminton, serves as a benchmark for assessing advancements in dynamic whole-body loco-manipulation. Prior work in robotic table tennis, such as HITTER and other systems, has achieved success, but badminton introduces additional complexities. These include highly uncertain aerodynamics, which lead to less usable decision time despite longer flight durations, and larger swing amplitudes that necessitate robust whole-body balance recovery. Crucially, in badminton, footwork and striking motions are tightly coupled and must co-evolve, rather than the lower body merely repositioning the base. Existing humanoid table tennis systems often rely on hierarchical planners, reference motions, or separate commands for base movement, which can limit the joint optimization of lower-body footwork and upper-body striking.

Regarding robotic badminton specifically, previous research has explored legged manipulators learning coordinated skills with onboard vision. However, deploying such capabilities on humanoid hardware has been elusive, and simulated humanoid policies in prior studies did not consistently exhibit realistic badminton-style footwork, suggesting a lack of effective training signals for lower-body coordination.

This research aims to advance the state of the art by developing a unified whole-body controller for humanoids that can effectively coordinate footwork and striking, scale to real-world hardware, and operate without requiring motion priors or expert demonstrations. It positions itself as a step toward addressing the challenges of dynamic, precise goal striking in scenarios involving fast-moving objects, specifically on a humanoid platform.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a humanoid robot to autonomously play badminton in the real world, a task characterized by its highly dynamic and precision-demanding nature. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives:

1. **Develop a unified whole-body controller:** The aim is to create a single controller capable of coordinating all degrees of freedom across the humanoid's body, encompassing both footwork and striking actions. A key aspect is to achieve this coordination without reliance on motion priors or expert demonstrations, allowing the policy to discover energy-efficient swings through reinforcement learning.
2. **Achieve dynamic yet precise goal striking:** The controller must be able to react within sub-second windows, perform large-amplitude swings, maintain whole-body balance, and intercept fast-moving shuttlecocks with high accuracy in a 3D interception volume.
3. **Demonstrate sustained performance in simulation:** A quantitative objective is to achieve long rallies between two simulated robots, indicating the controller's ability for repeated repositioning, high-quality returns, and posture recovery over extended exchanges.
4. **Validate on real-world hardware:** The system must demonstrate its capabilities on a physical humanoid robot. This includes returning machine-fed shuttles reliably and participating in human-robot rallies. Specific metrics include achieving high outgoing shuttle speeds and accurate return landing distances.
5. **Explore a prediction-free control variant:** An additional objective is to investigate a more end-to-end control strategy that removes the reliance on an explicit shuttlecock trajectory predictor. This variant aims to simplify deployment and potentially improve robustness to aerodynamic variability by implicitly inferring hitting targets from short-horizon shuttle observations.

The motivation for pursuing this research stems from several factors:

- **Advancing humanoid capabilities:** Humanoids are envisioned as general-purpose embodied agents for human-compatible skills. Successfully tackling a complex task like badminton demonstrates significant progress towards this vision, particularly in dynamic, interactive scenarios.
- **Badminton as a challenging benchmark:** Badminton serves as an ideal testbed due to its stringent requirements: sub-second perception-action loops, precise racket-shuttle contact timing and orientation, large 3D interception volumes, and the necessity for coordinated rapid arm swings with stable, agile leg motions. The complex, uncertain aerodynamics of a shuttlecock and the significant whole-body disturbances induced by large swings further amplify the challenge compared to other racket sports like table tennis.
- **Addressing limitations of prior work:** Existing approaches in robotic racket sports often involve highly modular or hierarchical systems, rely on expert demonstrations, or are developed for platforms with simpler balance requirements (e.g., quadrupeds). This work is motivated by the need for a more integrated, learning-based approach that specifically addresses the unique challenges of humanoid morphology and the inherent coupling of locomotion and manipulation in badminton.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-stage reinforcement learning (RL) training pipeline to develop a unified whole-body controller for a 21-degrees-of-freedom (DoF) humanoid robot.

**Robot Platform and System Architecture:**
The humanoid robot used is 1.28 m tall, weighs 30 kg, and has 21 DoF distributed across its legs, waist, and arms. A standard 100 g badminton racket is rigidly attached to the distal forearm. For real-world deployment, the robot operates within a motion-capture (MoCap) arena, which provides base position, orientation, linear velocity, and shuttlecock position data at 210 Hz. The policy operates at 50 Hz, outputting joint position targets, which are then tracked by a low-level joint-space PD controller running at 500 Hz. The system is designed for zero-shot transfer from simulation to hardware.

**Reinforcement Learning Framework:**
Policy learning is cast as a partially observable Markov Decision Process (MDP) and trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in the IsaacGym simulation environment.
- **Observation Space:** The actor's observation is an 87-dimensional vector concatenating proprioceptive information and external sensing from MoCap. This is augmented with short and long histories of all joint positions, velocities, and actions (adding 1470 features) to mitigate partial observability. The critic receives a 98-dimensional observation, which includes privileged information such as noise-free base and joint states, racket speed, and preemptive knowledge like subsequent hitting times, next target position and orientation, and the number of remaining targets. This asymmetric actor-critic design is intended to stabilize value estimation.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs 21 joint position targets for all DoF, scaled by 0.25, to be tracked by the low-level PD controller.
- **Training Settings:** Training utilizes 4096 parallel environments on a single Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU, with a total wall-clock time of 12.4 hours across all stages. Domain randomization and observation noise are applied during training to enhance sim-to-real transfer robustness.

**Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning Curriculum:**
A three-stage curriculum is employed, where each stage continues from the previous checkpoint, and only reward terms and their weights change.
1. **Stage 1 (S1) – Footwork Acquisition:** The objective is for the robot to efficiently approach target regions with a stable, forward-facing posture and reasonable lower-limb gait. The primary reward term `r_track` exponentially encourages reaching the target region. This stage includes standard locomotion-style shaping rewards (e.g., base height, orientation, acceleration regularization, contact-aware footstep terms, gait symmetry, face alignment).
2. **Stage 2 (S2) – Precision-Guided Swing Generation:** Building upon S1, this stage introduces a sparse hit reward (`r_swing`) active only at the scheduled hit instant (`t*`). `r_swing` combines position and orientation accuracy errors with a racket swinging speed component. Initial loose pose accuracy tolerances are progressively tightened. Additional regularization terms include `r_y-align` to encourage human-like hitting postures and `r_hold` for a default holding pose, along with strengthened energy, torque, and collision constraints.
3. **Stage 3 (S3) – Task-Focused Refinement:** In this final stage, the target approach reward and several gait-shaping regularization terms from `r_loco` (e.g., foot distance, step/contact symmetry, step height) are removed to prevent gradient interference with the hitting objective. Global regularization terms and `r_hit` rewards are retained. Domain randomization and observation noise are fully enabled to consolidate robustness. This stage aims to maximize hitting performance and further reduce action rates, joint velocities, energy consumption, and torque usage.

**Shuttlecock Trajectory Management:**
- **Trajectory Generation for Training:** A physics-based simulation approach, adhering to the badminton dynamics model from, is used to generate approximately 20,000 diverse shuttlecock flight trajectories. These trajectories are filtered to ensure interception within a designated hitting zone (x ∈ [−0.8, 0.8], y ∈ [−1, 0.2], z ∈ [1.5, 1.6] m) and a traversal time of at least 0.8 seconds. For each valid trajectory, the interception position, orientation (tangent direction defining racket normal), and timing are stored.
- **Trajectory Prediction for Deployment (Target-Known Policy):** During real-world deployment, an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) is used to estimate the shuttlecock's state online and predict the interception target using the same badminton dynamics model. The predictor is activated 0.36 seconds after launch, providing a rolling update of the hitting target (`p*ee`, `q*ee`, `t*`) to the controller.

**Prediction-Free Variant:**
A prediction-free policy variant is also trained, differing from the main pipeline only in its actor's observation space. Instead of receiving explicit hitting targets, the actor observes a sliding window of the current and previous five world-frame shuttlecock positions (sampled at 50 Hz). The critic and reward function, however, retain privileged access to ground-truth interception points. This approach aims to reduce reliance on explicit predictors, enhancing robustness to aerodynamic variability, and simplifying deployment. During training, aerodynamic parameters are randomized per shot to expose the policy to varying conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from five sets of experiments conducted in both simulation and on real hardware, evaluating accuracy, agility, robustness, and deployability.

**Simulation Results:**
1. **Two-Robot Rally:** In a simulated scaled badminton court, two identical humanoids, both running the target-known policy, successfully sustained a rally of 21 consecutive returns. A hit was defined as successful if the impact position error was below 0.10 m and orientation error below 0.2 rad. This demonstrates the controller's ability for repeated repositioning, high-quality returns, and robust posture recovery over extended exchanges.
2. **Target-Known vs. Prediction-Free Comparison:** This experiment contrasted the two observation designs under identical training and simulation conditions across twenty randomly sampled hits. The prediction-free policy demonstrated comparable performance to the target-known policy. While the target-known policy typically achieved mean position errors around 0.02 m and orientation errors around 0.02 rad with mean swing speeds of approximately 5.5 m/s, the prediction-free policy showed only a modest performance drop, with mean position errors around 0.04 m, orientation errors around 0.04 rad, and mean swing speeds around 5.0 m/s. This indicates that the policy can implicitly infer hitting pose and timing from short shuttle history.

**Sim2Real Transfer:**
The controller, trained exclusively in simulation, was deployed to real hardware in a zero-shot manner without specific transfer tricks like additional delays, actuator network models, or system identification. Domain randomization and observation noise during training contributed to this direct transfer capability.

**Real Robot Deployment:**
1. **EKF Prediction Accuracy Study:** Evaluation of the EKF in real-world conditions using 20 shuttle trajectories showed that at 0.6 seconds before impact, the mean position error was below 100 mm, converging to 10 mm by 0.3 seconds prior to contact. Interception timing prediction achieved a mean error of approximately 20 ms at 0.6 seconds before impact. Perturbing the aerodynamic characteristic length by ±20% resulted in reduced but still usable prediction accuracy.
2. **Virtual-Target Swinging:** In 71 virtual-target swing trials without a real shuttle, the target-known policy achieved a mean hit position error of 2.46 cm, while the prediction-free variant had a mean error of 6.71 cm. The measured rigid-body racket speed at impact typically ranged from 5 to 8 m/s. Repeatability tests with a fixed target showed mean Euclidean distance errors of 23.21 mm (std 10.55 mm) for the target-known policy and 54.00 mm (std 18.76 mm) for the prediction-free policy over 20 repeated swings. These results confirm the robot's ability to control the contact point within the racket face.
3. **Real-World Shuttle Hitting:** When returning machine-fed shuttles (46 trials per policy), the EKF-based target-known policy achieved a success rate of 91.3% (42 valid returns), with the robot intercepting shuttles at heights between 1.4-1.7 m within a 98 cm × 50 cm region. This policy produced outgoing shuttle speeds up to 19.1 m/s, with a mean of 11.1 m/s. The returned shots were observed to follow sharp, steep trajectories with peak heights exceeding 3 m, and had an average landing distance of approximately 4 m from the interception area. The prediction-free policy achieved a 71.7% success rate (33 valid returns) and outgoing shuttle speeds up to 18.1 m/s, with a mean of 8.2 m/s. The target-known policy was subsequently used for human-robot rally validation, successfully sustaining a few rallies with human players, indicating repeatable performance.

**Emergent Behaviors:**
The controller demonstrated adaptive coordination patterns, taking short corrective steps for nearby targets and initiating long strides and backswings in parallel for distant or time-critical targets. Without explicit incentives, behaviors such as brief tiptoe reaching for higher interceptions and re-centering between hits towards the middle-right court region emerged. Analysis of supporting-leg joint torques revealed a significant pre-strike torque peak, indicating that the learned policy leverages a push-off behavior to coordinate lower-limb actuation with arm swing, contributing to whole-body impulse generation and rapid racket acceleration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents the development and real-world deployment of a humanoid robot capable of playing badminton, powered by a unified whole-body reinforcement learning (RL) policy. This achievement represents an advancement in dynamic, interactive whole-body response tasks on humanoid platforms.

The introduction of a three-stage curriculum (footwork acquisition, precision-guided swing generation, and task-focused refinement) demonstrates an effective methodology for training complex, coordinated skills without relying on expert demonstrations or motion priors. This curriculum structure enables the policy to progressively acquire locomotion, striking, and refined task-oriented behaviors, ultimately leading to stable and precise hitting. The successful zero-shot transfer from simulation to real hardware, aided by domain randomization, indicates the robustness and generalizability of the learned policy.

The exploration of a prediction-free control variant is significant as it suggests a path toward more end-to-end robotic systems. While this variant exhibited a slight performance reduction compared to the EKF-based target-known policy, its ability to implicitly infer hitting targets from short shuttle history and its robustness to moderate aerodynamic variations simplify deployment by removing the need for a manually tuned explicit predictor.

The demonstrated capabilities, including sustained simulated rallies (21 consecutive returns), high-speed real-world shuttle returns (up to 19.1 m/s), and initial human-robot rallies, establish a benchmark for humanoid performance in agile, contact-rich sports. The emergent coordination patterns, such as adaptive stepping and active lower-limb contribution to striking, highlight the effectiveness of the whole-body control approach.

Potential impacts and future directions for this work include:
- **Advancement towards uninstrumented environments:** Moving beyond motion-capture arenas to pure vision systems would require robust shuttle tracking, reliable visual odometry, and policies capable of maintaining shuttle visibility during aggressive movements.
- **Expansion of stroke repertoire:** The current system primarily exhibits a forehand-like hitting style. Future work could focus on diversifying the robot's capabilities to include a wider range of human strokes, such as backhand hits, lunges, and jump smashes.
- **Enlarging effective interception workspace:** Expanding the feasible interception area would enhance the robot's ability to handle diverse incoming shots and contribute to longer, more complex multi-ball rallies.
- **Bridging the performance gap:** Further research on the prediction-free variant could aim to close the performance gap with prediction-based approaches, demonstrating full autonomy from explicit hierarchical perception.
- **Higher-level decision making:** Integrating multi-agent training for a high-level policy could enable more strategic decision-making regarding interception and landing-point control.
- **Extension to other racket sports:** The core pipeline, with adaptations to ball dynamics and interception styles, could potentially be extended to other racket sports like tennis or squash, demonstrating its versatility.

Overall, this research contributes to the development of highly dynamic and interactive humanoid robots, pushing the boundaries of what these platforms can achieve in complex, real-world scenarios.
