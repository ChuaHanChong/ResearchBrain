# RIDE: Relocalization-Informed Depth Estimation with 3D Gaussian Splatting

> [!summary] Summary
> RIDE estimates dense metric depth from a robot's RGB stream by reusing PnP-RANSAC inlier correspondences from 3D Gaussian Splatting relocalization as sparse metric anchors. These calibrate a frozen video-depth prior through robust global-scale memory, bounded spatial correction, and flow-guided temporal memory. Trained on RGB-D videos, it transfers without fine tuning, reaching 0.0927 AbsRel and 0.9361 delta-1 on real robot routes.

## Problem

- Render-match-PnP relocalization pipelines use retained inlier correspondences only for pose recovery, discarding the metric depth information those geometrically verified matches already encode.
- Rendering dense depth directly from the Gaussian map misrepresents the current scene: dynamic or unseen objects are absent, and incomplete geometry plus rendering artifacts inject errors.
- Prior depth completion assumes externally supplied, regularly patterned sparse depth; a single global scale cannot fix spatially varying prior error, and calibration fails when anchors vanish.

## Method

- ==Metric anchor extraction== lifts matched rendered pixels to map points via rendered depth, then keeps ==PnP-RANSAC== inliers as at most 256 pixel-depth pairs in the query camera frame.
- **Global Scale Memory** pairs ==Cauchy-weighted robust log-scale observation== with a learned reliability head and a ==gated recurrent drift-plus-gain update==, holding scale through invalid frames.
- ==Bounded spatial decoder== emits a 0.3-tanh log-scale residual field from rasterized anchor prompts, while a ==SEA-RAFT== flow-confidence-warped ==ConvGRU== adds ==gated temporal correction==.

## Results

- On the LingBot public split RIDE reaches the highest delta-1 at **0.9322** and lowest held-out error **0.0675**, cutting temporal geometric error from **0.0674** to **0.0394**, a **41.5%** reduction.
- On **3,012** frames over **27** real robot routes with no fine tuning, AbsRel drops **0.1056** to **0.0927**, delta-1 rises **0.8919** to **0.9361**, and TGE falls **0.0530** to **0.0298**.
- Ablations show spatial correction cuts AbsRel **0.2173** to **0.1178**; shuffling recurrent history degrades every metric worst, and removing flow-confidence weighting raises held-out error **0.0576** to **0.0693**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Geometric byproducts of one task can seed another: correspondences already validated for pose recovery supply sparse metric supervision that needs no extra deployment sensor.
> - Bounding learned residuals to a small multiplicative range keeps a frozen foundation prior responsible for structure while calibration only handles scale, which aids cross-domain transfer.
> - Temporal consistency of a relative prior does not survive per-frame metric calibration; recurrent scale memory is required so sparse-anchor fluctuation does not cause visible scale jitter.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

RIDE addresses the intersection of three research areas: visual relocalization, monocular or video-based depth estimation, and sparse-to-dense depth completion for robotics.

Visual relocalization systems estimate a camera’s six-degree-of-freedom pose relative to a known scene. Recent render–match–PnP pipelines use a reconstructed scene representation, including 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), to render an image from an approximate camera pose. Image correspondences between the rendered view and the query image are then lifted into 2D-to-3D correspondences and filtered using PnP-RANSAC. These systems generally treat the retained inliers as a means of improving pose estimation. RIDE instead observes that every geometrically consistent correspondence also provides a metric depth measurement at a query-image pixel.

The work also builds on recent video-depth models, particularly Video Depth Anything Small (VDA-S). Such models estimate temporally coherent scene structure from RGB video, but their predictions are generally relative rather than metrically calibrated to a specific robot or map. A global scale can align the predictions to metric depth, but a single scale cannot correct spatially varying errors caused by scene content, occlusions, or model biases.

A third relevant area is sparse-guided depth completion. Existing methods commonly use sparse measurements from LiDAR, time-of-flight sensors, structured-light cameras, or visual-inertial reconstruction. RIDE differs in that its sparse metric inputs originate from image correspondences retained during relocalization. These observations are irregularly distributed, potentially noisy, and intermittently unavailable. The paper therefore focuses not only on spatial completion, but also on robust calibration and temporal memory under changing observation support.

The proposed contribution is consequently an interface between localization and perception: the same 3DGS-based localization process produces both a camera pose and sparse metric depth anchors, which are then used to calibrate a dense video-depth prior.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to estimate dense metric depth from a robot’s RGB stream using information already generated by a relocalization system, without adding a depth sensor during deployment.

The motivation has several components. First, pose estimation and metric depth are complementary but are often implemented as separate systems. Relocalization determines where the robot is in a known reference frame, while dense depth is useful for navigation, obstacle avoidance, manipulation, mapping, and interaction. Reusing localization correspondences could connect these tasks with limited additional computation or sensing.

Second, directly rendering depth from the 3DGS map is insufficient for current-scene perception. A map may omit dynamic objects, newly introduced objects, or portions of the environment that were not visible during reconstruction. Rendering artifacts and incomplete geometry can also produce incorrect depths. PnP-RANSAC inliers are sparse, but they are geometrically verified against the current image and map. The authors therefore treat these inlier-derived observations as more trustworthy metric anchors than a dense map rendering in regions where the map and current scene differ.

Third, a pretrained video-depth model supplies dense scene structure but lacks the metric scale of the robot’s environment. The system must estimate a scale from sparse anchors. Since anchor quality and distribution vary across frames, independently estimating a scale in every frame can cause temporal flicker. The system therefore needs a persistent global scale state.

Finally, a global scale alone cannot correct local errors. The paper aims to estimate a spatially varying correction field while preventing that correction from overriding the structural information supplied by the video-depth prior. It also seeks to maintain predictions during short periods when too few valid relocalization anchors are available.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Extraction of Metric Depth Anchors

At time \(t\), RIDE receives the RGB frames available so far, a metrically scaled 3DGS model, camera intrinsics, and a coarse camera pose. The 3DGS model is rendered to produce a virtual RGB image and metric depth map.

Image matching establishes pairs between query-image pixels and rendered-image pixels. The rendered depth at each matched pixel is back-projected through the camera intrinsics and transformed into the world coordinate system, producing a 3D map point. These 2D-to-3D correspondences are processed by PnP-RANSAC. The refined pose and the retained inlier set are then used to compute the optical-axis depth of each inlier map point in the query camera frame.

The resulting set of pixel–depth pairs is the metric anchor set. The implementation uses at most 256 anchors per frame. Because only PnP-RANSAC inliers are retained, the anchors are intended to exclude geometrically inconsistent matches, although they may still be sparse or affected by map imperfections.

#### 3.2. Relative Video-Depth Prior

RIDE uses a frozen VDA-S model to produce dense relative depth and multiscale image features. The raw VDA-S output is processed to remove nonfinite or extremely small values. The remaining prediction is converted into a positive prior \(P_t(u)\), where \(u\) denotes an image pixel. Pixels replaced by a numerical floor are marked separately because they are less reliable for scale estimation and spatial correction.

For each valid anchor, the system compares the metric anchor depth \(z_{t,i}\) with the corresponding prior depth \(p_{t,i}\) using a logarithmic residual:

\[
r_{t,i}=\log z_{t,i}-\log p_{t,i}.
\]

A common residual across anchors represents an approximate global log-scale correction.

#### 3.3. Reliability-Aware Global Scale Estimation

A learned multilayer perceptron predicts a reliability value for each anchor. Its inputs include pixel coordinates, metric and prior depths, residual statistics, local support, and validity information. Masked mean and maximum pooling provide order-independent context about the anchor set.

The residuals are then combined with Cauchy robust weights. The Cauchy term reduces the influence of anchors whose residuals deviate strongly from the median residual, while the learned reliability score further reduces the influence of potentially unreliable observations. Their weighted average gives an instantaneous scale observation.

RIDE does not necessarily replace its previous scale estimate with this observation. Instead, a recurrent global-scale memory predicts a small drift from image features and compares the current observation with the predicted scale. A learned gain determines how much the new evidence should update the memory. If fewer than eight valid anchors are available, the scale observation is treated as invalid and the previous state is retained. Metric output remains unavailable until the first valid scale observation initializes the state.

#### 3.4. Local Spatial Correction

The authors introduce a second correction to address spatially varying errors in the VDA-S prior. Anchor information is rasterized into spatial prompts containing residuals, reliability, occupancy, neighborhood support, and the prior’s floor mask. A decoder combines these prompts with the VDA-S prior and multiscale features to produce a dense spatial log-scale residual.

This residual is bounded using a hyperbolic tangent and a maximum magnitude of 0.3 in log space. The bound restricts the local branch to moderate multiplicative adjustments, leaving the pretrained prior responsible for most of the scene structure.

#### 3.5. Flow-Guided Temporal Memory

To preserve local calibration information over time, RIDE uses SEA-RAFT optical flow between consecutive frames. Flow is computed in both directions. Forward–backward consistency and the flow model’s uncertainty are converted into a confidence mask. The previous latent state is warped into the current frame using the estimated motion and is suppressed in regions with poor flow confidence or out-of-bounds coordinates.

A ConvGRU combines the aligned historical state with current VDA-S features, the prior, anchor context, global scale, spatial correction, and flow-validity information. The temporal branch predicts another bounded log-scale residual and a utility gate. The gate controls whether temporal correction is applied at each location. The final prediction is:

\[
D_t(u)=P_t(u)\exp\left(\alpha_t^{global}
+\delta_t^{spatial}(u)+\delta_t^{temporal}(u)\right).
\]

The inference process is causal, using only current and earlier frames and the recurrent states carried from those frames.

#### 3.6. Training and Evaluation Design

The model is trained in three stages on the RobbyReal subset of the LingBot-Depth dataset. The dataset contains 183 indoor scenes divided into scene-disjoint training, validation, and test splits. Training inputs are generated by sampling registered RGB-D depth at detected feature locations, thereby approximating the pixel–depth interface produced by deployment-time relocalization.

The authors hold out approximately 20% of sampled anchors for supervision, apply dropout to vary anchor density, limit the input to 32, 64, 128, or 256 observations, and introduce up to 30% structured outliers. These perturbations simulate irregular correspondences, incorrect associations, clustered errors, and missing observations.

Stage 1 trains the anchor-reliability module. Stage 2 trains the spatial correction decoder while using direct robust scale estimates. Stage 3 adds recurrent global and temporal modules using five-frame chunks and truncated backpropagation through time. VDA-S and SEA-RAFT remain frozen, and no test-time parameter updates are used.

Evaluation uses absolute relative error, the \(\delta_1\) accuracy threshold, held-out anchor log-depth error, and temporal geometric error. In addition to public RGB-D data, the model is tested on 3,012 frames from 27 real robot routes across five scenes. Deployment anchors are derived from PnP-RANSAC inliers, and the model is used without robot-data training or fine tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

On the public LingBot benchmark, RIDE obtains an absolute relative error of **0.1115**, a \(\delta_1\) accuracy of **0.9322**, held-out error of **0.0675**, and temporal geometric error of **0.0394**. PriorDA achieves a lower absolute relative error of **0.0917**, but RIDE has higher threshold accuracy and lower held-out and temporal errors among the compared methods. Relative to the VDA-S spatial calibration baseline, RIDE reduces temporal geometric error from **0.0674 to 0.0394**, a reduction of approximately **41.5%**.

On the real robot sequences, RIDE records an absolute relative error of **0.0927**, \(\delta_1\) of **0.9361**, and temporal geometric error of **0.0298**. The VDA-S spatial variant obtains corresponding values of **0.1056**, **0.8919**, and **0.0538**. The results indicate that a calibration model trained with RGB-D-derived anchors can transfer to relocalization-derived anchors without fine tuning.

The ablation studies show distinct contributions from the system components. A global-only version has an absolute relative error of **0.2173** and held-out error of **0.1515**. Adding spatial correction reduces these values to **0.1178** and **0.0676**, respectively, indicating that local calibration is important beyond a single scale factor.

Adding state carryover and temporal adaptation improves the full set of metrics, particularly temporal geometric error, which decreases from **0.0513** for the spatial-only model to **0.0354** for the complete system. Resetting the recurrent state every frame increases temporal error to **0.0438**, while shuffling historical states produces a larger degradation to **0.0754**. Shuffling optical flow raises temporal error to **0.0492**, supporting the role of motion-based alignment. Removing the flow-confidence mask also degrades frame-wise and held-out performance.

A qualitative example illustrates the reliability of anchor extraction. In one robot frame, PnP-RANSAC retains 50 of 63 correspondences, with a mean reprojection error of 1.56 pixels. Among 47 anchors with valid RGB-D references, the median and 90th-percentile absolute depth differences are 0.138 and 0.352 meters. The paper also reports that the temporal memory maintains predictions during short anchor outages after scale initialization and follows moving foreground boundaries more continuously than frame-wise methods.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

RIDE’s main significance is conceptual and system-level. It demonstrates that localization correspondences can provide useful metric supervision for dense perception rather than serving only as intermediate data for pose estimation. This can reduce dependence on additional depth sensors at deployment and may benefit robots operating with an existing metric scene map.

The method is particularly relevant to environments where a 3DGS map is available but is incomplete or contains outdated scene content. The VDA-S prior supplies dense image-based structure, while map-derived anchors establish metric scale and constrain the prediction. The recurrent design further addresses a practical property of relocalization systems: reliable correspondences may appear intermittently rather than in every frame.

However, the approach depends on several conditions. It requires a metrically scaled 3DGS model, a functioning render–match–PnP relocalization front end, and sufficient overlap between the current view and the map. The system does not guarantee metric output before scale initialization, and prolonged loss of map overlap or reliable optical flow may cause failure. The deployment evaluation covers five scenes and 27 routes, so broader environmental diversity remains to be tested. In addition, the reported robot metrics use each method’s valid-output mask intersected with the reference-validity mask; therefore, accuracy results should be interpreted alongside output availability. The authors identify initialization with very sparse anchors and recovery from prolonged loss of map overlap or flow as areas for future work.
