# PA-LOCO: Learning Perturbation-Adaptive Locomotion for Quadruped Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Sun Yat-sen University developed PA-LOCO, a reinforcement learning framework that enables quadruped robots to robustly adapt to diverse terrains and actively compensate for unforeseen external force perturbations using only onboard sensor measurements. The framework, featuring a multi-encoder architecture and a residual policy network, achieved higher success rates and faster recovery times in real-world impact tests compared to baseline methods.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots face challenges in maintaining safe and reliable locomotion when subjected to unforeseen external disturbances like collisions, which can lead to instability or falls.
- Existing robust locomotion methods, such as domain randomization, often result in passive and conservative behaviors, creating a trade-off between robustness and performance.
- Current privileged learning frameworks for quadruped locomotion exhibit performance degradation in student policies and their single-encoder architectures are insufficient for effectively handling external force perturbations, leading to entangled feature representations.

## Method

- **PA-LOCO** integrates a ==teacher-student architecture== with a ==multi-encoder structure== to infer and decouple various types of privileged information (external forces, terrain, robot states) into distinct latent features.
- A ==residual policy network== is introduced to enhance the student policy's performance and mitigate degradation often observed during the transfer from the teacher policy.
- ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== is used for training, combined with ==domain randomization== and a ==curriculum learning== strategy that gradually increases perturbation intensity and terrain difficulty.

## Results

- In real-world lateral impact tests, PA-LOCO achieved a **90%** success rate with a **4.6 kg** impact and an average recovery time of **0.75 seconds**, significantly outperforming a robust baseline (**43%** success rate, **1.98 seconds** recovery time).
- The multi-encoder force adaptation (MEFA) mechanism consistently outperformed a single-encoder approach (SEFA), which frequently led to unstable locomotion, confirming the importance of decoupled feature learning.
- The Unitree GO1 robot, controlled by PA-LOCO, demonstrated robust locomotion and effective recovery from external impulses across diverse real-world outdoor terrains including grassy surfaces, staircases, and slopes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A multi-encoder structure is crucial for effectively handling external force perturbations, as it decouples force-related privileged information from other environmental and state signals, allowing for more precise adaptation.
> - The residual policy network successfully alleviates the performance degradation typically observed when transferring a privileged teacher policy to a student policy with limited observations.
> - Learned latent representations generated by the multi-encoder can distinctly differentiate external forces based on their magnitudes and directions, enabling targeted adaptive responses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of quadrupedal robot locomotion has seen advancements through model-free reinforcement learning (RL) methods, enabling robots to traverse various challenging terrains such as steps, slopes, grass, mud, snow, and sand. The objective of much of this research is to enhance blind locomotion capabilities for outdoor operations.

A significant challenge in this domain is ensuring safe and reliable locomotion in the presence of unforeseen disturbances, such as collisions with dynamic obstacles or sudden external forces. Without adequate mechanisms to address these perturbations, robots can be propelled away from stable locomotion, deviate from their intended trajectories, or even topple. Existing methods to achieve robust locomotion include domain randomization, which involves training policies across varied environments with randomized properties. While domain randomization can yield robust policies, it often results in passive and conservative behaviors, requiring a trade-off between robustness and performance.

An alternative approach is privileged learning, which employs a teacher-student architecture. In this framework, a teacher policy is trained with "privileged information" (data not available during deployment, such as terrain profiles, ground friction, or various robot states) to learn robust behaviors. A student policy then learns to imitate the teacher's actions and inferred latent features using only readily available onboard sensor measurements (e.g., IMU, joint encoders). Privileged learning has been successfully applied to enable stable locomotion over challenging terrains.

This paper addresses specific limitations within the privileged learning paradigm for quadruped locomotion. Firstly, the potential of privileged learning for adapting to *external force perturbations* has been less explored compared to its application for terrain adaptation. Secondly, existing privileged learning frameworks often suffer from performance degradation in the student policy compared to the teacher, largely due to discrepancies in feature embedding when the student attempts to clone the teacher's behaviors. Thirdly, the common single-encoder architecture in privileged learning is noted as insufficient for effectively handling external force perturbations, as it can intertwine perturbation-related privileged information with other environmental or state signals, making it difficult to isolate and adapt to force changes. This work aims to contribute to the robustness and adaptability of quadrupedal locomotion by mitigating these identified limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:
1. Enable quadrupedal robots to achieve robust blind locomotion across diverse terrains.
2. Develop a mechanism that allows the robot to actively compensate for external perturbations using only onboard sensor measurements.

These objectives are motivated by several factors:
- **Enhanced Robustness to Perturbations**: Quadruped robots are susceptible to unexpected external forces in real-world environments. The ability to actively adapt to such perturbations, rather than merely relying on passively robust policies, is crucial for safe and reliable operation. This includes recovering from impacts and maintaining stability.
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Privileged Learning**: Current privileged learning methods, while effective for terrain adaptation, exhibit shortcomings when faced with external forces.
 - **Performance Degradation**: The transfer from a teacher policy (with privileged information) to a student policy (with limited observations) often results in a performance gap. Mitigating this degradation is essential for practical deployment.
 - **Inadequate Single-Encoder Architecture**: The use of a single encoder for all privileged information can lead to features related to external forces being entangled with other state or environmental features. This entanglement hinders the policy's ability to precisely identify and react to specific force perturbations, as it becomes challenging to distinguish changes due to external forces from those caused by other variables like velocity or heading.
- **Decoupling Latent Features**: By decoupling latent features from different types of privileged information (e.g., external forces, terrain, robot states) through a multi-encoder structure, the policy can potentially gain a more nuanced understanding of the environment, leading to more targeted and effective adaptive behaviors.
- **Improved Recovery and Stability**: The goal is for the learned policy to exhibit enhanced robustness and stability against sudden force disturbances, enabling the robot to recover its intended locomotion more rapidly after an impact.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, PA-LOCO, integrates a teacher-student architecture with a multi-encoder structure and a residual policy network. The locomotion control problem is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), addressed by inferring unavailable privileged information into a latent feature space.

**Observations:**
- **Teacher Policy Observations**: Comprise proprioceptive sensor measurements (angular velocity, gravity vector, joint positions and velocities, high-level commands, previous actions – 45 dimensions), robot state information (linear velocity, trunk mass, COM, ground friction, foot contact forces and states – 28 dimensions), time-series external force history (last 10 timesteps – 30 dimensions), and terrain profile (187 height values).
- **Student Policy Observations**: Only proprioceptive sensor measurements, specifically the history of these measurements over the last second (50 timesteps), are available to the student.

**Actions:** The action space consists of 12-dimensional reference joint angles. These are combined with default joint positions and sent as command signals to low-level PD controllers (proportional gain $K_p=20$, derivative gain $K_d=0.5$).

**Reward Functions:** The total reward is a weighted sum of 12 terms, including task-specific rewards (linear and angular velocity tracking) and auxiliary rewards (penalties for trunk height/orientation deviation, joint acceleration/torque, action rate, self-collision, and rewards for foot air time and foot end position).

**Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is utilized for training the feedback body controller, with specific hyperparameters detailed in the paper (Table II).

**Domain Randomization:** To facilitate sim-to-real transfer and enhance robustness, domain randomization is applied during training. This includes randomizing dynamic parameters (trunk mass, COM displacement, ground friction coefficient), applying random forces and wrenches to the robot, and introducing noise to sensor feedback (Table III). Training is conducted in the Isaac Gym simulator with 4096 parallel agents, running at 200 Hz simulation frequency with a 50 Hz policy update rate.

**Teacher-Student Architecture with Multi-Encoders and Residual Network:** The training proceeds in three phases:
1. **Phase 1: Teacher Policy Training**: The teacher policy $\pi_T$ consists of a base policy network and multiple encoder networks. A three-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with ELU activation functions parameterizes the base policy and critic network. The multi-encoder structure includes:
 - **External Force Encoder ($E_F$)**: Encodes the external force history into a latent feature $\hat{l}_{F_t}$.
 - **Terrain Encoder ($E_T$)**: Processes terrain height samples into $\hat{l}_{T_t}$.
 - **State Encoder ($E_S$)**: Encodes privileged robot state information into $\hat{l}_{S_t}$.
 The teacher policy's input comprises proprioceptive observations and the concatenated latent features from these encoders.
2. **Phase 2: Student Policy Training**: The student policy $\pi_S$ consists of a base policy network and a proprioception history encoder ($E_H$). The student's base policy is initialized by copying the parameters of the teacher's base policy. The proprioception history encoder $E_H$ processes the last 50 timesteps of proprioceptive sensor data into a latent feature $l_t$. The student policy is trained via supervised learning to imitate the teacher’s actions $\hat{a}_t$ and its concatenated latent features $\hat{l}_t$, minimizing a loss function defined as the sum of squared differences between student and teacher actions and latent features.
3. **Phase 3: Residual Network Training**: A residual policy network ($R$) is introduced to further enhance the student's performance, particularly against perturbations. This network is a smaller MLP that takes proprioceptive data and the student's latent features as input. During this phase, the parameters of the encoders and the student's base policy are frozen. An asymmetric actor-critic architecture is employed, with the critic initialized from the teacher’s critic. The final RL signal is a weighted sum of the outputs from the student policy and the residual network.

**Curriculum Learning:** A curriculum learning strategy is implemented for both perturbation and terrain adaptation. Perturbation intensity (force magnitude) is gradually increased as the robot achieves a predefined mean tracking reward threshold. Similarly, terrain difficulty progresses from flat to more complex terrains (rough flats, slopes, stairs, discretized terrains) based on the robot's velocity tracking performance. If performance drops, the robot is returned to an easier terrain.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of PA-LOCO was evaluated through both real-world experiments on a Unitree GO1 robot and simulation studies.

**A. Adaptive Locomotion Under External Perturbations (Real-world)**
Experiments involved applying external force impulses to the robot. When subjected to a front kick while tracking a constant forward velocity of 1 m/s, the robot initially decelerated significantly (to approximately -1.5 m/s) and lowered its trunk height (from 0.29 m to 0.22 m). The external force encoder successfully registered these changes. Subsequently, the robot recovered its commanded velocity, followed by a gradual return to its initial trunk height. This demonstrates the policy's ability to adapt its posture for stability and prioritize velocity tracking during recovery from perturbations.

**B. Effects of Force Adaptation and Residual Network (Real-world)**
The proposed PA-LOCO framework was compared against three benchmark algorithms on a flat surface under a sudden lateral impact:
- **Robust**: Policy trained with domain randomization but without force adaptation or a residual network.
- **SEFA (Single-Encoder Force Adaptation)**: Policy with force adaptation using a single-encoder structure.
- **MEFA (Multi-Encoder Force Adaptation)**: Policy with force adaptation using the multi-encoder structure, but without the residual network.

A pendulum system (2.65 m length, 1.75 m horizontal offset, impacting at 0.3 m height) was used to deliver lateral impacts with weights of 2.2 kg and 4.6 kg, generating instantaneous lateral velocity transitions of approximately 1.3 m/s and 2 m/s, respectively. Performance was measured using success rate (SR), average lateral COM offset (LO), average recovery time (RT), and average trunk height offset (HO).

The results (Table V) showed:
- **SEFA** exhibited highly unstable locomotion and could not be quantitatively evaluated. This indicates the deficiency of a single encoder in handling external force perturbations and highlights the benefit of the multi-encoder structure.
- **Robust** policy showed limited adaptability, resulting in larger lateral offsets (LO: 0.33 m for 2.2 kg, 1.46 m for 4.6 kg) and longer recovery times (RT: 1.06 s for 2.2 kg, 1.98 s for 4.6 kg). Its success rate was 43% for the 4.6 kg impact. It showed minimal trunk height adjustment.
- **MEFA** demonstrated improved performance over Robust, with lower LO (0.17 m for 2.2 kg, 0.64 m for 4.6 kg) and RT (0.50 s for 2.2 kg, 0.89 s for 4.6 kg). Its success rate was 100% for 2.2 kg and 80% for 4.6 kg. It also showed trunk height adjustment (-0.01 m for 2.2 kg, -0.06 m for 4.6 kg).
- **PA-LOCO** (MEFA with residual network) achieved the best performance. It recorded the lowest LO (0.16 m for 2.2 kg, 0.59 m for 4.6 kg) and RT (0.45 s for 2.2 kg, 0.75 s for 4.6 kg), and high success rates (100% for 2.2 kg, 90% for 4.6 kg). PA-LOCO also exhibited slightly greater trunk height adaptation (-0.02 m for 2.2 kg, -0.05 m for 4.6 kg). These results confirm the effectiveness of both the multi-encoder force adaptation mechanism and the residual network in enhancing robustness and recovery capabilities.

**C. Analysis of the Latent Representation (Simulation)**
Simulations were performed to analyze the multi-encoder's ability to discern different perturbations.
- **Varying Force Magnitude**: When subjected to constant backward forces of varying magnitudes (0 N, 10 N, 20 N, 30 N, 40 N), t-SNE visualization (Fig. 5) of the learned latent representations showed distinct clusters for each force magnitude. This indicates that the student policy, through the force encoder, is capable of distinguishing external forces of different magnitudes.
- **Varying Force Direction**: A correlation heatmap (Fig. 6) analyzed latent features when constant 20 N forces were applied from four directions (forward, backward, left, right). The components of the external force latent feature ($l_F$) exhibited distinct positive or negative correlations based on the force direction, demonstrating the multi-encoder's capacity to differentiate forces from various directions. Some components of the state latent feature ($l_S$) also showed minor correlations, attributed to changes in trunk velocity and foot contact states induced by external forces. This analysis supports the effectiveness of the multi-encoder structure in decoupling and interpreting distinct types of privileged information.

**D. Locomotion Control in Various Outdoor Environments (Real-world)**
The PA-LOCO algorithm was deployed on the Unitree GO1 robot in outdoor settings, including grassy surfaces, staircases, and slopes (Fig. 7), with lateral kicks applied during movement. The robot demonstrated adaptability to unforeseen impulses and successfully recovered its normal motion across these diverse terrains. On a 20-degree uphill slope, the robot exhibited a noticeably reduced trunk height after impact, further indicating adaptive posture control. In contrast, a controller trained with a single-encoder architecture and force adaptation showed excessive sensitivity and struggled to maintain stable locomotion in these real-world conditions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of quadrupedal robot locomotion by proposing a novel reinforcement learning framework that enhances robustness and adaptability to external perturbations. The key advancements include:
- **Improved Perturbation Adaptability**: The PA-LOCO framework enables quadruped robots to actively compensate for sudden external forces, which is a critical capability for operating in unpredictable and dynamic real-world environments.
- **Enhanced Privileged Learning**: The introduction of a multi-encoder structure addresses a limitation of previous privileged learning approaches by decoupling different types of privileged information. This allows the policy to generate more specific and effective responses to external forces without interference from other environmental or state cues.
- **Mitigation of Student Policy Degradation**: The residual policy network is demonstrated to effectively alleviate the performance degradation typically observed when transferring a teacher policy to a student policy. This improvement makes the learned controllers more reliable for real-world deployment.
- **Real-world Validation**: The extensive real-world experiments on a Unitree GO1 robot across diverse terrains and with varying perturbation magnitudes provide empirical validation of the framework's effectiveness, which is crucial for robotics research.

The potential impact of this work is significant for the broader development and deployment of autonomous legged robots. By increasing the robustness, stability, and reliability of quadrupedal locomotion in the face of unforeseen disturbances, PA-LOCO could facilitate wider adoption of these robots in applications such as search and rescue, logistics, environmental monitoring, and hazardous inspection, where encounters with dynamic obstacles and unexpected impacts are common. Future work may explore the integration of attention mechanisms to dynamically weigh the importance of different encoder outputs, potentially leading to even more nuanced and efficient adaptive behaviors.
