# Co-Evolution in Agentic Systems: Toward Self-Directed Evolution Beyond Human Design

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of co-evolution in agentic systems: settings where two or more units, agents, peers, environments, or the evolution mechanism itself, jointly adapt and reshape each other's later evolution. It formalizes an agentic system as agents plus environment driven by an evolution mechanism, organizes work into a three-stage taxonomy (agent-agent, agent-environment, meta co-evolution), and aggregates cross-paper evidence showing evolving counterparts beat frozen ones but plateau.

## Problem

- Single-entity self-evolution updates one agent against fixed tasks, feedback, and counterparts, so progress plateaus once the static learning context stops supplying new adaptive pressure.
- Prior surveys organize the literature around multi-agent systems, harness engineering, or self-evolution, leaving co-evolution as scattered examples, a future direction, or one branch.
- Existing benchmarks score an agent's final capability on fixed tasks, giving no way to check whether all coupled components improve or transfer to unseen partners.

## Method

- Formal criterion separating ==co-evolution== from interaction: a state transition driven by an evolution mechanism must change at least two units that exert mutual evolutionary pressure, with each agent defined as backbone plus harness.
- **Progressive Three-Stage Taxonomy** ordered by expanding evolutionary freedom: ==Agent-Agent== (adversarial, collaborative, evolving organizations), ==Agent-Environment== (task, feedback, interaction spaces), and ==Meta Co-Evolution== over the mechanism itself.
- Decomposition of the evolution mechanism into five adaptive decisions, what, when, how, and where to evolve plus how to evaluate, and a within-paper protocol for aggregating cross-paper evidence.

## Results

- Matched evidence panels admit a paper only when it reports both frozen and evolving counterparts under one backbone and setting; enabling counterpart evolution gives positive mean paired improvement in most matched settings.
- Normalized trajectories, averaged to one equally weighted curve per paper with a one-standard-deviation band, show gains concentrated early and flattening as evolution approaches a plateau.
- Stage 3 is nearly empty: **1** qualifying work co-evolves task agents, evaluators, and a meta-agent, while **4** named precursors evolve mechanisms without any lower-level co-evolving system.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mutual adaptation, not interaction, is the load-bearing distinction: it counts as co-evolution only when both units persist changes that reshape the other's later learning.
> - Making counterparts adaptive buys headroom but not open-endedness; plateaus return unless the evolution mechanism itself becomes revisable.
> - Higher task success can hide evaluator exploitation, partner overfitting, or diversity collapse, so pair fixed benchmarks with held-out evaluators, historical cross-play, and component ablations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the evolving field of artificial intelligence, specifically addressing the transition from static, isolated AI models to dynamic, autonomous agentic systems. Agentic systems are characterized by their ability to interact with other agents and external environments and are expected to continuously improve after deployment.

Existing approaches to continuous improvement often involve single-entity self-evolution, where an agent updates itself based on experience. However, the paper argues that such self-evolution can be constrained by static learning contexts, such as fixed tasks or feedback mechanisms. This limitation aligns with the "Red Queen effect" in evolutionary biology, suggesting that sustained progress requires mutual adaptation between components.

Co-evolution, defined as a multi-component form of self-evolution where multiple adaptive units jointly adapt and continually reshape each other, is presented as a paradigm to overcome the limitations of single-entity evolution. This concept extends to scenarios where agents evolve with adaptive peers (e.g., opponents or collaborators) or with their environments (e.g., tasks, feedback, interaction spaces). The paper also considers a meta-level extension where the evolution mechanism itself can adapt.

The authors distinguish their work from existing surveys that primarily focus on specific aspects of agentic AI, such as LLM-based agents, multi-agent systems, agent components and harness engineering, environment scaling, or self-evolving agents in general. While these surveys may touch upon co-evolution, they typically treat it as a subtheme rather than a central organizing principle. This paper aims to address this gap by providing the first dedicated survey that centers on co-evolution in agentic systems, focusing on *which* components co-evolve, *how* evolutionary freedom expands, and the potential for open-ended improvement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this survey stems from the increasing complexity and deployment expectations of agentic AI systems. As these systems become more autonomous and interactive, the ability to improve continuously post-deployment becomes crucial. The authors identify a fundamental limitation in current self-evolutionary paradigms: the tendency for single-entity self-evolution to plateau when confronted with a static learning context. This constraint prevents agents from achieving sustained, open-ended improvement.

To address this, the paper highlights co-evolution as a potential solution, where the adaptive pressure for an evolving unit is generated by another evolving unit. This mutual adaptation is posited to enable more robust and open-ended improvement trajectories. The absence of a dedicated, comprehensive survey on co-evolutionary agentic systems motivates this work.

The key objectives of this research are:

1. **To provide a focused survey on co-evolution in agentic systems:** This involves explicitly distinguishing mutual adaptation from mere interaction or information exchange among components. The aim is to establish co-evolution as a distinct and critical paradigm for agentic system development.
2. **To propose a progressive three-stage taxonomy:** This taxonomy is designed to organize the existing literature by tracing the expanding scope of evolutionary freedom within agentic systems. It illustrates how systems gradually shed human-engineered constraints across three stages: Agent–Agent Co-Evolution, Agent–Environment Co-Evolution, and Meta Co-Evolution.
3. **To discuss open challenges and future directions:** The survey aims to identify critical areas requiring further research, particularly concerning the evaluation, scaling, and governance of increasingly autonomous co-evolutionary systems. This includes addressing potential pitfalls like evaluator exploitation, partner overfitting, and diversity collapse.
4. **To lay a unified foundation:** By systematizing the concept and manifestations of co-evolution, the authors seek to provide a conceptual framework that can guide the development of agentic systems capable of robust and open-ended improvement beyond fixed, human-designed paths.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper is a survey, and its methodology primarily involves a systematic review, categorization, and synthesis of existing literature related to co-evolution in agentic systems. The authors define core concepts and then construct a hierarchical taxonomy to structure their analysis.

**Formal Foundations:**
The paper begins by formally defining an agentic system `S = (A, E)` as comprising an agent collective `A` and an environment `E`. An agent `a_i` is defined as a unit with a `model backbone (m_i)` and a `harness (h_i)` (e.g., memory, tools, skills). The agent collective `A` is structured by `Π`, encoding roles and communication topology. Evolution (`Δa_i ≠ 0`) occurs when either the backbone or harness changes. Co-evolution is then precisely defined as a coupled form of self-evolution involving at least two evolving units (`x`, `y`) within `S_t` that jointly adapt (`x_{t+1} ≠ x_t`, `y_{t+1} ≠ y_t`) and exert mutual evolutionary pressure (`x <-> y`).

**Progressive Three-Stage Taxonomy:**
The central methodological contribution is the introduction of a three-stage taxonomy, designed to reflect an expanding boundary of evolutionary freedom and a gradual reduction of human-engineered constraints (Figure 2):

1. **Stage 1: Agent–Agent Co-Evolution:** This stage focuses on mutual adaptation among agents (`A_{t+1} = Ω(A_t, E, τ_t)`) while the environment `E` remains fixed.
 - **Adversarial Agents:** Examines co-evolution through opposing objectives, where agents improve by defeating rivals. Sub-categorized into pairwise pressure (e.g., GANs, LLM red-teaming) and multi-source pressure (e.g., league training, diverse red-team populations).
 - **Collaborative Agents:** Explores co-evolution through shared goals. Distinguished by parallel collaboration (equivalent agents, shared rewards) and role-differentiated collaboration (fixed, distinct roles, produce-and-revise loops).
 - **Evolving Agent Organizations:** Addresses systems where agent roles, assignments, and interaction structures (`Π`) co-evolve with individual agents.

2. **Stage 2: Agent–Environment Co-Evolution:** This stage extends the adaptive process to the environment itself (`(A_{t+1}, E_{t+1}) = Ω(A_t, E_t, τ_t)`), allowing agent behavior to reshape environmental conditions that subsequently influence the agents.
 - **Task-Space Co-Evolution:** Environment adapts `what problem` the agent solves next. Includes exposure and selection of tasks from a pool (curriculum learning, replay-based selection) and adaptive generation of new tasks (goal generation, LLM-driven task calibration).
 - **Feedback-Space Co-Evolution:** Environment adapts `how it evaluates` agent behavior. Includes preference-driven feedback (learning reward models from comparisons) and outcome-driven feedback (revising rewards or verifiers based on task results). Consistency-augmented feedback further refines evaluator learning.
 - **Interaction-Space Co-Evolution:** Environment adapts `where` the agent acts. Includes executable world construction (generating challenging worlds, simulating robotics, open-ended games) and model-based world construction (co-evolving world models to replace real environments).

3. **Stage 3: Meta Co-Evolution:** This is an emerging stage where the evolution mechanism `Ω` itself becomes adaptive (`Ω_{t+1} = Γ_t(S_t, Ω_t, τ_t)`), allowing the system to revise `what`, `when`, `how`, `where` to evolve, and `how to evaluate`. This stage is presented as a pathway toward open-endedness. The paper decomposes the evolution mechanism into five adaptive decisions: adaptation target, trigger conditions, variant generation/update methods, evolution domain/setting, and evaluation criteria.

**Evidence Aggregation (Figure 4, Appendix C):**
To provide quantitative support for the benefits of co-evolution, the authors synthesize results from numerous papers. They detail how they derive cross-paper evidence:
- **Effect and Consistency (Panels A and B):** They aggregate within-paper comparisons between static/frozen and evolving counterparts, ensuring consistent metrics (higher values indicating better performance). They report mean performance scores and the proportion of settings showing improvement.
- **Convergence (Panel C):** Performance trajectories over successive evolution rounds are normalized to show progress from initial to maximum observed improvement. These normalized trajectories are averaged across papers to depict general trends of improvement and potential plateaus.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper's main findings are derived from its comprehensive review and categorization of the literature, supported by an aggregation of performance data from the surveyed works.

**Effective Organization of Literature:**
The proposed progressive three-stage taxonomy (Agent–Agent, Agent–Environment, and Meta Co-Evolution) successfully categorizes a wide range of research papers, illustrating a coherent progression in the autonomy and scope of co-evolutionary systems. This framework effectively highlights the expanding "evolutionary freedom" as systems shed human-engineered constraints.

**Prevalence and Evolution within Stages:**

- **Stage 1: Agent–Agent Co-Evolution:**
 - **Adversarial Agents:** Research demonstrates a clear progression from pairwise adversarial training (e.g., GANs, Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (RARL) for embodied control, competitive self-play in games) to more complex multi-source adversarial pressure (e.g., AlphaStar's league training, red-teaming LLMs against diverse attack styles, tri-role self-play with an attacker, defender, and evaluator). These systems show agents developing tool use, counter-strategies, and robust safety mechanisms through continuous competitive adaptation.
 - **Collaborative Agents:** Work in this area spans parallel collaboration (e.g., Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) with shared rewards, LLM agents co-adapting with group-relative advantages) and role-differentiated collaboration (e.g., produce-and-revise loops, agents specializing in distinct tasks like research and engineering). Methods often derive co-evolutionary pressure from interaction traces, shared memory, or explicit credit assignment.
 - **Evolving Agent Organizations:** Emerging research illustrates that systems can co-evolve not only individual agents but also their collective structures, including role assignments and interaction topologies, leading to adaptive organizational designs.

- **Stage 2: Agent–Environment Co-Evolution:**
 - **Task-Space Co-Evolution:** This involves environments adapting the challenges agents face. Methods include selecting tasks from a fixed pool based on learning progress, competence, or regret (curriculum learning, replay-based selection) and generating entirely new tasks. Adaptive task generation is seen in embodied control (goal generation), LLM tool use (calibrating difficulty, converting failures to new tasks), search, GUI interaction, and software engineering (building repair tasks from solving traces).
 - **Feedback-Space Co-Evolution:** Here, the environment adapts how it evaluates or rewards agents. This includes preference-driven feedback (evolving reward models from trajectory comparisons) and outcome-driven feedback (revising reward functions from task returns or failures, evolving unit tests). Consistency constraints are also used to augment evaluator learning.
 - **Interaction-Space Co-Evolution:** This involves the environment adapting the space in which agents act. Examples include executable world construction (e.g., POET for evolving agent-environment pairs, generating levels based on agent weaknesses in games, expanding tool combinations in tool-use agents) and model-based world construction (e.g., co-evolving world models with agent policies for virtual web servers, textual world models for feedback prediction, video world models for training policies).

**Quantitative Evidence of Co-evolution's Impact (Figure 4):**
The aggregated cross-paper evidence indicates that co-evolution generally leads to performance improvements compared to static baselines:
- **Effect and Consistency:** Stage 1 (Agent–Agent Co-Evolution) systems demonstrate an average performance gain of 11.2% over static baselines, with 81.6% of matched evaluation settings showing positive improvement. Stage 2 (Agent–Environment Co-Evolution) systems show an even higher average gain of 18.9%, with 89.1% of settings improving. This suggests that enabling multi-component adaptation consistently enhances system performance.
- **Convergence:** Analysis of normalized performance trajectories reveals that while Stage 1 and Stage 2 systems achieve significant improvements, their gains often plateau over time. This observed convergence underscores the inherent limitations of fixed evolutionary mechanisms and motivates the exploration of higher-level adaptation.

**Emergence of Meta Co-Evolution:**
While still an early-stage concept with limited full realizations, the survey identifies precursors in single-entity meta-evolution (e.g., evolving prompts for self-modification, memory architectures, or self-modification machinery). The conceptual framework for Meta Co-Evolution, where the evolution mechanism itself adapts, is presented as a critical step toward open-endedness, potentially allowing systems to move beyond the observed plateaus by continuously generating novelty in their evolutionary processes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey makes several contributions to the field of agentic AI, with potential impacts on future research and development:

**Unified Conceptual Framework:** The proposed three-stage taxonomy offers a systematic and unified lens through which to understand, categorize, and analyze the diverse and rapidly growing body of research on co-evolution in agentic systems. This framework helps to clarify the distinctions between various forms of mutual adaptation and provides a common language for researchers.

**Guidance for Open-Ended AI:** By delineating a progressive pathway of "evolutionary freedom," from agents adapting to each other to the evolution mechanism adapting itself, the paper provides a conceptual roadmap toward achieving open-endedness in AI. This aligns with the long-term goal of developing systems that can continually generate meaningful novelty and adapt indefinitely without human intervention. The identification of Meta Co-Evolution as a critical, albeit nascent, stage emphasizes the importance of making the very rules of evolution adaptive to overcome inherent performance plateaus.

**Identification of Critical Research Gaps and Challenges:**
The survey highlights several crucial areas for future research and development:

- **Dynamic Evaluation:** Current benchmarks are often insufficient for assessing co-evolutionary systems. The paper underscores the need for new evaluation methodologies that can measure improvement across multiple evolving components, assess transferability to unseen contexts, quantify individual component contributions, and detect specific failure modes such as evaluator exploitation, partner overfitting, or diversity collapse. Process-level testing, historical cross-play, and component ablations are suggested as necessary additions.
- **Scaling Co-evolution:** Moving beyond localized co-evolutionary loops (e.g., attacker-defender) to integrated systems where agents, their harnesses, and environments co-evolve synergistically presents a significant challenge. The survey points to the need for understanding which components should adapt, how their interactions influence stability, and how to maintain productive co-evolutionary pressure.
- **Safety and Governance:** As co-evolutionary systems become more autonomous, the potential for human loss of control increases. The paper raises concerns about agents developing uninterpretable or harmful behaviors, particularly in meta co-evolution where the reward structures themselves might evolve. This necessitates the development of robust governance frameworks, including sandboxed deployment, continuous monitoring, rollback capabilities, and clearly defined human intervention points, to ensure auditable and controllable development.

**Advancement of Robust Agentic Systems:** By focusing on the principles of mutual adaptation, the survey contributes to the fundamental understanding required to build more robust, adaptive, and self-improving AI agents. Such agents would be better equipped to operate in complex, dynamic, and unpredictable real-world environments, learning and evolving beyond their initial human-designed capabilities. This comprehensive overview serves as a foundational reference for researchers aiming to push the boundaries of agent intelligence towards greater autonomy and sustained improvement.
