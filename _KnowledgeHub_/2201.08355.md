# Sim-to-Lab-to-Real: Safe Reinforcement Learning with Shielding and Generalization Guarantees

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Princeton University developed a "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" framework for safe reinforcement learning, which integrates Hamilton-Jacobi reachability-based shielding with PAC-Bayes generalization guarantees. The framework enables the training of policies that demonstrate superior empirical safety and performance, along with probabilistic certificates for deployment in unseen environments, validated on a quadrupedal robot.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation often fail catastrophically in real-world deployment due to the "reality gap" and lack formal safety or generalization guarantees.
- Existing safe RL methods struggle to enforce hard safety constraints or rely on sparse violation signals, while model-based safety methods are often intractable for complex, high-dimensional, and unknown real-world scenarios with vision-based inputs.
- A lack of formal, probabilistic guarantees for generalization performance and safety hinders the trusted deployment of learning-based autonomous systems in safety-critical applications.

## Method

- A two-stage "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" training pipeline is introduced, learning a safety-aware policy distribution through a dual-policy architecture (performance and backup) conditioned on latent variables.
- Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis is incorporated to train the backup policy with dense safety signals, enabling a "least-restrictive" shielding mechanism to enforce hard safety constraints.
- The PAC-Bayes framework is applied during the "Lab" stage to fine-tune the policy distribution, providing probabilistic generalization bounds for both performance and safety in unseen real-world environments.

## Results

- The framework demonstrated the lowest safety violation rates during Lab training across all environment settings, reducing violations by 4%-77% compared to previous safe RL methods.
- It achieved significantly tighter PAC-Bayes generalization bounds for task success and safety satisfaction, improving success bounds from 0.366 to 0.786 and safety bounds from 0.367 to 0.794 in challenging environments.
- Policies trained with the framework were successfully deployed on a Ghost Spirit quadrupedal robot in real indoor environments, achieving a 0.767 success rate and a 0.867 safety rate, outperforming baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining HJ reachability for dense, proactive safety signals with a value-based shielding mechanism effectively prevents safety violations during both training and deployment in complex, vision-based tasks.
> - Training a diverse distribution of policies by maximizing mutual information between latent variables and trajectories is crucial for achieving tighter PAC-Bayes generalization bounds and improved adaptation to diverse real-world conditions.
> - The "Lab" stage provides a critical intermediate step for safely fine-tuning policies and certifying their performance and safety, mirroring established industrial engineering practices for robust system deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the critical and long-standing challenges of **safety** and **generalization** in reinforcement learning (RL) for autonomous systems, particularly in the context of **Sim-to-Real transfer**. The current landscape sees RL excelling in simulations but often failing catastrophically when deployed in the real world due to the "reality gap" – discrepancies between simulated and physical environments (e.g., lighting, noise, dynamics).

Traditional approaches to Sim-to-Real, such as **domain randomization**, randomize simulation properties to create robust policies. While effective in improving generalization, these methods typically do not explicitly guarantee safety or provide formal certificates of performance in unseen real-world conditions. On the safety front, existing safe RL techniques, such as **Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDP)** or **reward shaping**, often struggle with a delicate balance between performance and safety, requiring extensive hyperparameter tuning and rarely enforcing *hard* safety constraints. Furthermore, many model-based safety methods, like **Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis** or **Control Barrier Functions (CBF)**, offer strong guarantees but assume known system dynamics and environment models, making them intractable for complex, high-dimensional, and unknown real-world scenarios, especially with vision-based inputs.

More recent learning-based safe RL methods, like SQRL and Recovery RL, learn safety critics but often rely on sparse, binary safety violation signals, which can be sample-inefficient and less proactive in preventing failures. In parallel, the **Probably Approximately Correct (PAC)-Bayes framework** has emerged as a powerful tool for providing generalization guarantees for learning-based policies in unseen environments. However, prior PAC-Bayes Control work has not explicitly integrated safety-aware policy architectures or considered hard safety constraints during training.

Hsu et al.'s "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" framework strategically bridges these gaps by synergistically combining these disparate but complementary approaches. It integrates HJ reachability analysis to enforce hard safety constraints with dense learning signals during training, and the PAC-Bayes framework to provide robust generalization guarantees. The novel "Lab" stage, positioned between simulation and real-world deployment, provides a structured environment for fine-tuning policies and certifying their performance and safety, mirroring established industrial practices in engineering design. This work thus represents a significant advancement by offering a holistic solution that tackles both the empirical and theoretical challenges of safe and generalizable autonomous system deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and certifiable framework for deploying reinforcement learning-based autonomous systems in the real world with **probabilistically guaranteed safety and performance**. This overarching goal is motivated by several critical challenges inherent in current RL applications:

1. **Ensuring Safety in Real-World Deployment:** Policies trained purely in simulation often exhibit unsafe behaviors (e.g., collisions) when deployed in the real world. Even existing "safe" RL methods often fail to enforce hard safety constraints or rely on reward penalties that create a fragile balance between task performance and safety. The paper aims to move beyond soft constraints to enforce *hard safety* guarantees.

2. **Bridging the Reality Gap and Generalization:** A significant performance degradation occurs when policies trained in simulation are transferred to physical robots and real environments. While domain randomization helps, it doesn't offer formal guarantees that a policy will perform reliably in *unseen* real-world conditions. The authors seek to provide such guarantees.

3. **Lack of Formal Guarantees for Learning-Based Policies:** Before deploying autonomous systems in safety-critical applications (e.g., healthcare, human interaction, or public spaces), a "certificate" of their generalization performance and safety is essential. Traditional control methods offer such guarantees but are often incompatible with high-dimensional, perception-driven learning-based policies. The motivation is to provide rigorous, probabilistic bounds for learned policies.

4. **Systematic and Practical Validation:** The paper is motivated by conventional engineering practice, where systems undergo rigorous testing in controlled, realistic environments (e.g., test tracks, dedicated labs) before full deployment. The proposed "Lab" stage formalizes this intermediate validation, allowing for systematic fine-tuning and certification in more realistic settings than pure simulation.

To address these motivations, the paper sets out to:
- Develop a dual-policy architecture (performance and backup) that prioritizes safety while optimizing task rewards.
- Incorporate Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis for the backup policy to learn robust, worst-case safety behavior from dense signals, enabling "least-restrictive" shielding.
- Utilize the PAC-Bayes framework to train a distribution of policies, providing lower bounds on expected performance and safety when deployed in unseen, real-world environments.
- Introduce diversity into the policy distribution during simulation training to facilitate better generalization during fine-tuning.
- Empirically validate the framework through extensive simulation and real-world experiments with a quadrupedal robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" framework is a two-stage training pipeline designed to learn a **safety-aware policy distribution** with generalization guarantees. The core components are a dual-policy architecture, a shielding mechanism based on HJ reachability, and PAC-Bayes control for generalization.

#### 3.1. Dual Policy Architecture with Latent Variables

The system employs two policies:
- **Performance Policy ($\pi_p$):** Optimized for the cumulative task reward.
- **Backup (Safety) Policy ($\pi_b$):** Designed to prevent safety violations.
Both policies are conditioned on a **latent variable $z$** sampled from a multivariate Gaussian distribution $N(\mu, \Sigma)$. This parameterization allows the learning of a *distribution* of policies rather than a single deterministic one, which is crucial for PAC-Bayes generalization and inducing diverse behaviors.

#### 3.2. Safety Through Hamilton-Jacobi Reachability and Shielding

- **Safety Bellman Equation (DSBE):** The backup policy $\pi_b$ is trained by minimizing the discounted safety Bellman equation (Equation 5), which captures the worst-case safety by evaluating the maximum future cost (e.g., inverse of distance to obstacles) along a trajectory. This approach learns from "near-failure" states with dense cost signals, unlike methods relying on sparse binary failure indicators. The discount factor $\gamma$ is annealed from 0.8 to 1, progressively increasing attention to long-term safety.
- **Value-Based Shielding:** A "least-restrictive" control law is applied: the performance policy's proposed action $a_p$ is overridden by the backup policy's action $a_b$ *only if* $Q_b(o, a_p) > v_{thr}$, where $Q_b$ is the safety state-action value function and $v_{thr}$ is a predefined safety threshold (e.g., a minimum safe distance to obstacles). This minimizes intervention while ensuring safety.

#### 3.3. Inducing Diversity via Mutual Information Maximization

During the Sim stage, the framework explicitly encourages diverse behaviors within the policy distribution. This is achieved by maximizing the mutual information between the latent variables $z$ and the observed trajectories. The performance policy's reward function is augmented with a weighted mutual information term (Equation 8), and the backup policy's objective is also modified (Equation 10) to promote distinct safety strategies based on $z$. This diversity is crucial for effective fine-tuning and generalization.

#### 3.4. Two-Stage Training Pipeline

- **Sim Stage (Pre-training a Diverse Dual Policy):**
 - Policies are trained in a wide variety of simulated environments ($M'$) with randomized properties (domain randomization).
 - The latent distribution $N_{\psi_0}$ (prior) is fixed, and policies learn to generate diverse behaviors conditioned on $z$.
 - A joint training algorithm (Algorithm 1) concurrently updates both $\pi_p$ and $\pi_b$ using modifications of the off-policy Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm.
 - Two annealing parameters, $\rho$ (probability of sampling actions from $\pi_b$) and $\epsilon$ (probability of activating shielding), are introduced. $\rho$ starts high (1) and anneals to 0, ensuring early safety exploration, while $\epsilon$ starts low (0) and anneals to 1, gradually integrating shielding.
 - Action re-labeling is used: $\pi_p$ is trained on its *proposed* action, even if it was overridden, to ensure it learns from its intentions.

- **Lab Stage (Safely Fine-Tuning Policies with Generalization Guarantees):**
 - The pre-trained policy distribution from Sim ($P_0$) acts as the prior.
 - The policy distribution is fine-tuned in a new set of environments ($M$) sampled from a target distribution $D$, which is closer to real-world conditions (e.g., realistic room layouts).
 - The objective is to maximize the PAC-Bayes generalization lower bound $R_{PAC}$ (Equation 1), which involves maximizing empirical reward $R_M(P)$ while regularizing with the KL divergence between the posterior $P$ and prior $P_0$ (Equation 14).
 - Value-based shielding is continuously applied to prevent safety violations during Lab training, ensuring safe exploration.
 - After training, the final posterior distribution $P$ is used to compute the generalization bound, providing a certificate for deployment.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Environments:** Two types: "Vanilla-Env" (simple rooms with geometric obstacles) and "Advanced-Env" (realistic 3D-FRONT dataset rooms with complex furniture, matching real robot camera).
- **Task:** Ego-vision navigation to a target location, avoiding collisions.
- **Robot:** Quadrupedal robot (Ghost Spirit with ZED 2 camera) for real-world validation, mimicking Advanced-Env settings.
- **Observations:** RGB images (48x48 or 90x160 pixels), stacked for Advanced-Env, plus auxiliary distance/bearing to goal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experiments, spanning simulation and real-world deployment, yielded several key findings:

1. **Superior Safety During Lab Training:** The proposed Sim-to-Lab-to-Real framework (PAC Shield Perf and PAC Shield Both variants) demonstrated the **lowest safety violation rates** during Lab training across all environment settings compared to all baselines (Base, Reward Penalty (RP), PAC-Bayes Control variants, SQRL, and Recovery RL). This highlights the effectiveness of the HJ reachability-based safety critic and shielding mechanism in preventing unsafe exploration. Specifically, safety violations were reduced by 4%-77% compared to previous safe RL methods.

2. **Effectiveness of Reachability-Based Safety Critic:** Visualizations of the safety state-action value functions (Fig. 6) showed that the HJ reachability critic provides a **more gradual and thicker unsafe region** around obstacles, allowing the robot more time and space to react. In contrast, risk-based critics (SQRL, Recovery RL) produced more binary, thinner unsafe regions, making proactive safety challenging and leading to more failures.

3. **Stronger Generalization Guarantees:** The framework achieved **significantly tighter and stronger PAC-Bayes generalization bounds** for both task success and safety satisfaction compared to baseline PAC-Bayes Control methods (e.g., PAC Base). For instance, in the challenging Advanced-Realistic environment, the success bound improved from 0.366 to 0.786, and the safety bound from 0.367 to 0.794. This demonstrates that explicitly integrating hard safety constraints into the policy architecture and learning process improves not only empirical outcomes but also the theoretical certifiability.

4. **Improved Empirical Performance and Safety at Deployment:** In unseen test environments, Sim-to-Lab-to-Real methods exhibited the **lowest unsuccessful trajectory fraction** (including both failures and unfinished trials) and the **fewest safety violations**. This indicates that training a diverse and safe policy distribution, combined with shielding, leads to superior generalization to novel environments.

5. **Successful Real-World Deployment:** Policies trained with PAC Shield Perf were successfully deployed on a **Ghost Spirit quadrupedal robot** in real indoor environments. This real-world validation demonstrated the framework's practical applicability, achieving a success rate of 0.767 and a safety rate of 0.867, outperforming PAC Base (0.433 success/safety) and SQRL (0.667 success/safety) baselines. This empirical performance strongly validates the theoretical generalization guarantees.

6. **Ablation Studies and Sensitivity Analyses:**
 - **Importance of Two-Stage Training:** Both Sim and Lab stages were found to be crucial. Omitting Lab training increased deployment failures by 8-16%, while omitting Sim training significantly increased safety violations during Lab training (60-65%), confirming the necessity of pre-training for safe exploration.
 - **Shielding Threshold ($v_{thr}$):** A higher (more negative) $v_{thr}$ leads to more conservative, safer maneuvers but can sometimes prevent reaching the goal or inadvertently steer the robot into tight spaces in complex environments. Finding an appropriate balance is critical.
 - **Diversity (Mutual Information and Latent Dimension):** Maximizing diversity (e.g., with augmented reward coefficient $\beta = 2$) significantly improved generalization performance. Without diversity, policies converged to a single trajectory, leading to higher test-time failures, even if training safety was good. The framework was robust to different latent dimensions.
 - **Annealing Parameters ($\rho, \epsilon$):** Proper annealing of the probability of sampling from the backup policy ($\rho$) and activating shielding ($\epsilon$) during Sim training was crucial for balancing early safe exploration with later performance learning. Constant parameters led to either excessive violations or poor task completion.
 - **KL Divergence Regularization Weight ($\alpha$):** This parameter in the Lab stage influences the balance between empirical reward maximization and deviation from the prior policy. An appropriately tuned $\alpha$ (e.g., $\alpha=2$ for Advanced-Env) was important for achieving optimal bounds and test performance; too strong regularization hindered fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" framework represents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous systems and reinforcement learning, with far-reaching implications for the safe and reliable deployment of robots in real-world, safety-critical applications.

1. **Bridging the Theoretical and Practical Gaps:** The most profound impact lies in its ability to bridge the long-standing gaps between theoretical safety guarantees (via HJ reachability) and statistical generalization guarantees (via PAC-Bayes) with practical, high-dimensional, vision-based reinforcement learning. This is a crucial step towards making RL-trained autonomous systems truly deployable outside of highly controlled environments.

2. **Enhanced Safety for Autonomous Systems:** By explicitly integrating hard safety constraints using HJ reachability and a least-restrictive shielding mechanism, the framework significantly reduces safety violations both during training and deployment. This is paramount for applications where human-robot interaction or damage to property is a concern (e.g., home robotics, autonomous driving, industrial automation). The dense learning signal from near-failure states makes the safety learning process more robust and proactive.

3. **Certifiable Generalization:** Providing probabilistic "certificates" of performance and safety for policies operating in *unseen* environments is a game-changer. This offers a level of confidence currently lacking in most deep RL approaches, making it feasible to justify the deployment of such systems in regulated or high-stakes contexts.

4. **Systematic Development Pipeline:** The proposed "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" pipeline introduces a structured and methodical approach to autonomous system development, mirroring established engineering practices. The "Lab" stage serves as a crucial intermediate validation step, enabling fine-tuning and certification in more realistic, yet still controlled, settings before full real-world deployment. This systematic approach can accelerate development cycles while improving reliability.

5. **Applicability to Complex, Vision-Based Tasks:** The demonstration of the framework on ego-vision navigation with high-dimensional RGB inputs, without reliance on pre-trained vision encoders or explicit mapping, broadens its applicability to a wide range of perception-driven robotic tasks where state estimation or explicit dynamics models are challenging.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The work opens several avenues for future research. Discussions on online adaptation of shielding thresholds, exploration of more sample-efficient PAC-Bayes bounds, identification of representative environments for Lab training (e.g., using coresets), and leveraging large-scale distributed training or crowdsourcing for Lab data collection are important next steps. These advancements could further enhance the practicality and scalability of the framework.

In conclusion, "Sim-to-Lab-to-Real" offers a principled and robust methodology for developing autonomous agents that are not only high-performing but also demonstrably safe and generalizable. Its impact is poised to be significant in enabling the trusted adoption of advanced reinforcement learning techniques in real-world robotic applications.
