# ReCAT: Remember, Count, and Time: Structured Recurrent Memory for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ReCAT is a language-conditioned manipulation policy with structured recurrent memory: an instruction-conditioned Mamba-attention frame encoder produces per-step features that both feed a flow-matching Transformer decoder directly and drive a recurrent memory of Mamba-2 layers plus one causal attention layer. The decoder reads current observation and memory via separate cross-attention in every block, reaching 95.3% on LIBERO, 62.4% on RMBench, and 66.7% on real-robot memory tasks.

## Problem

- Fixed-window and memory-bank policies give direct access to past frames, but their attention cost grows with retained tokens unless they select or compress history.
- Prior recurrent-memory policies each fix one recurrent backbone and one route from memory to action, leaving open how the update rule shapes what gets remembered.
- Pure fixed-size recurrent states struggle with exact recall of earlier tokens, while precise manipulation still needs direct access to the current spatial and proprioceptive observation.

## Method

- ==Instruction-conditioned observation encoder==: frozen LoRA-adapted DynaFLIP and T5 feed a ==FiLM==-modulated global/local query compressor and a six-layer ==Mamba-2== encoder with one bidirectional attention layer, yielding one 768-d feature per step.
- ==Hybrid recurrent memory==: six width-768 blocks, five Mamba-2 plus one causal attention layer, trained as a ==parallel scan== over full episodes, with a swappable update rule (Mamba-3, ==Gated DeltaNet-2==).
- ==Flow-matching Transformer decoder== in **ReCAT** predicting 16-step ==action chunks==, with separate cross-attention to the current-frame feature and to the memory readout in every one of its four blocks.

## Results

- Real robot: best ReCAT variant reaches **66.7%** average across spatial recall, counting, and timing tasks versus **8.3%** for history-augmented X-VLA; baselines fail exactly at the history-dependent decision stage.
- Benchmarks: **95.3%** on LIBERO (X-VLA **98.1%**), **64.2%** on LIBERO-Plus, **62.4%** on RMBench with best or tied-best on **six of nine** tasks, at **374M** parameters and **59 ms** latency.
- Ablations: full-Transformer encoder drops average to **25.0%**, removing memory gives **0%**, late fusion **38.3%**; Mamba-2/3 reach **85-90%** on counting versus **20%** for Gated DeltaNet-2, which leads spatial recall at **40%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Recurrent update-rule semantics matter for robot memory: additive accumulation appears to suit counting and timing, while delta-rule replacement suits spatial recall, so match the rule to the memory demand.
> - How memory enters the action generator matters as much as the memory itself; every-block cross-attention far outperformed late fusion, AdaLN, scaling, or gating.
> - Stage-wise evaluation exposes failures that final success hides: short-history policies handle manipulation fine but fail at decisions needing information absent from the current observation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

The camera can show the same scene at two different points in a task, even when the robot should do something different at each. In the authors' PLANT task, a shovel held over a pot after one scoop looks like the shovel after two. At that moment the robot must either scoop again or put down the shovel and proceed to planting.

ReCAT's approach is to keep a running account of earlier observations while still consulting the live camera view to choose its next movement. In real-robot tests, it often reaches history-dependent decisions that a short-history policy misses.

### 2. Keep the present; summarize the past

The practical bottleneck is that a robot must act on task-relevant information after that information has left view or is no longer apparent in the current image. ReCAT changes the policy's route to that information rather than relying only on recent frames. It has three parts:

1. Images, the task instruction, and proprioception are encoded into one feature per observation.
1. That feature goes directly to the decoder and also updates a temporal memory.
1. A flow-matching Transformer generates a chunk of actions. Each decoder block reads the current features and the history through separate cross-attention.

"Recurrent" here means updating a retained representation as each observation arrives, rather than passing the whole image history to the action generator. The six-block memory has five Mamba-2 layers and one causal attention layer.

The recurrent states are fixed-size. The attention layer's key-value cache, however, keeps one 768-dimensional entry per time step and grows linearly with episode length. It stores neither images nor patch tokens.

The authors describe accumulation as an intended property of the update rule, but they do not measure an internal count. That interpretation remains a hypothesis.

Visual, language, and proprioceptive inputs feed a visual compressor and frame encoder, which produces a per-step readout. That feature branches to the decoder and to recurrent memory. The memory is drawn as three Mamba-2 layers, one causal-attention layer, and two more Mamba-2 layers, and it returns a readout to the decoder. Noisy action tokens enter a Transformer decoder that produces an action chunk.

### 3. The decision is the test

Each real-robot policy was trained with a single seed. It was then tested on 20 rollouts per task with randomized initial object positions, so one rollout equals five percentage points of success rate. Each of the three tasks probes a different demand:

- **PLANT** requires exactly two scoops before planting.
- **POT TIMER** requires waiting 29–31 seconds while the scene is nearly unchanged.
- **SPONGE** requires returning a sponge to its unmarked starting position after it has left view.

ReCAT-Mamba-2 averages **66.7%** success, versus **8.3%** for the strongest short-history baseline, X-VLA-H. These figures are means of the three task-level success rates. The reported 95% Wilson intervals are [54.1%, 77.3%] and [3.6%, 18.1%], and they do not overlap.

The stage-wise results show where the baseline breaks down. X-VLA-H puts the sponge on the plate in 15 of 20 rollouts but returns it correctly in only two. It scoops at least once in 19 of 20 PLANT rollouts but completes the task in two, with 17 over-scooping.

ReCAT's results are uneven across the three demands. It succeeds on all POT TIMER waits by the paper's timing criterion. Its SPONGE success is only 20%, although all four rollouts that reach the plate return the sponge correctly.

A second comparison asks where the decoder reads memory. With the encoder and memory held fixed, every-block cross-attention averages 66.7% real-robot success. Late fusion, which uses cross-attention only in the last two decoder blocks, averages 38.3%.

SPONGE is the exception: late fusion scores 25% versus 20%, a difference of one rollout. Late fusion reaches the plate in 14/20 rollouts and returns correctly in five. The main method reaches the plate in 4/20 and returns correctly in all four.

Stage-wise real-robot success for ReCAT cross-attention compared with late fusion, AdaLN, Scale, Dropout, and Gate. Values are percentages of 20 rollouts, and the final column is average success across the three tasks. The main ReCAT row averages 66.7%; late fusion averages 38.3%.

Removing the memory module yields 0% on these tasks. That ablation, however, removes the recurrent and attention components together and does not identify their individual contributions.

### 4. Memory must meet action

The same visible scene can call for different actions. ReCAT therefore gives the action decoder both a path to task history and a path to the live observation. In the decoder-conditioning ablation, reading memory in every block outperformed late fusion on average.

This evidence comes from a small, specific evaluation. It covers one real-robot task per memory demand, with 20 rollouts each and single-seed training, and the authors call for broader studies across tasks and embodiments.
