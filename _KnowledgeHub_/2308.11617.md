# GRIP: Generating Interaction Poses Using Spatial Cues and Latent Consistency

> [!summary] Summary
> GRIP takes noisy body and object motion without finger articulation and synthesizes realistic two-hand interaction motion before, during, and after grasping. It denoises arms with ANet, extracts distance-based Ambient and Proximity hand-sensor cues, generates temporally consistent poses with CNet under latent temporal consistency, then refines penetrations with RNet. Feed-forward only, it beats ManipNet, GrabNet, and TOCH and nearly matches ground-truth perceived realism.

## Problem

- Motion-capture and reconstruction pipelines track body and object reliably but rarely capture fingers, leaving large interaction datasets with rigid, unarticulated, unrealistic hands.
- Prior grasp models produce only static poses, need an initial hand pose to refine, or handle a single hand, so bimanual dynamic interaction stays unmodeled.
- Existing dynamic-grasp methods depend on costly iterative optimization or physics-based reinforcement learning, and generalize poorly to unseen object geometries and shapes.

## Method

- Two virtual hand sensors: an ==Ambient Sensor== of 1024 hemisphere points sensing object shape and reach distance, and a ==Proximity Sensor== giving fine per-vertex hand-to-object distance fields, both passed through interaction-aware exponential attention weighting.
- **Consistency Network (CNet)** encodes two successive frames into a global plus residual latent code and decodes both through a shared decoder, enforcing ==Latent Temporal Consistency== in latent space rather than on output poses.
- An arm denoiser (**ANet**) cleans noisy arm motion upstream, and a refinement network (**RNet**) recomputes Proximity Sensor features from CNet poses to remove penetrations, keeping the whole **GRIP** pipeline feed-forward.

## Results

- Full GRIP reaches **7.88/6.17** mm MPVPE and **10.56/6.25** mm contact consistency for right/left hands, versus **8.19/6.58** and **11.44/7.03** without RNet.
- Penetration volume drops to **2.38** cm3 with contact ratio **1.00**, beating ManipNet (**2.68**) and GrabNet (**2.65**) and approaching ground truth (**1.95**); ManipNet penetrates **13%** more.
- Perceptual study rates GRIP **4.02** average realism versus **3.65** for ManipNet and **4.06** for ground truth; against TOCH on rotation-perturbed GRAB, GRIP cuts MPVPE **6.21** to **4.62** while TOCH worsens to **11.5**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Applying temporal smoothing in latent space beats smoothing output poses, which injects high-frequency noise into contact regions and degrades both accuracy and contact consistency.
> - Continuous distance-field sensing generalizes better than binary voxel grids and, because it senses only local interaction regions, transfers from small training objects to large unseen ones.
> - Coarse-then-refine feed-forward staging replaces iterative optimization for penetration removal, but short future-horizon conditioning buys accuracy at the cost of fixed interactive latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The modeling of realistic human-object interactions in 3D is a fundamental challenge in computer graphics, computer vision, and mixed reality applications. Human hands are highly versatile manipulators, and generating their detailed, natural motion, including individual finger articulation, is critical for achieving perceived realism in digital avatars. Previous research efforts have addressed various aspects of this problem, ranging from static grasp generation to dynamic interaction synthesis and hand-object interaction tracking.

Early work in static grasp generation, particularly in robotics and computer graphics, utilized physics-based control, optimization techniques, or reference poses to synthesize plausible hand configurations for stationary objects. More recent approaches leverage data-driven methods, training models on large datasets of hand-object interactions to estimate hand poses or refine initial grasps based on predicted contact maps.

Dynamic grasp generation, which involves continuous hand motion throughout an interaction, presents additional complexities. Many methods in this area have relied on generating contact constraints and resolving them through optimization-based techniques. While these methods can achieve physical plausibility, they often struggle with realism and can introduce interaction artifacts. Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, applied to both hand-only and full-body scenarios, sometimes incorporating physics simulations. However, generalization to novel object geometries and hand configurations remains a challenge for many RL-based methods. Some approaches utilize distance-based spatial representations or object-centric spatio-temporal representations, often followed by optimization steps, but frequently treat hands separately, limiting the realism of bi-manual interactions.

The broader field of object and scene interaction extends beyond hands to encompass full-body engagement with environments. This includes using contact annotations with optimization, deep reinforcement learning for body-scene or hand-object interactions, and geometric descriptors or Laplacian deformations for interaction modeling. Data-driven methods have also been employed to predict action sequences or generate key frames.

A significant hurdle across these areas is the acquisition of high-quality hand motion data. Motion capture (MoCap) systems often struggle to accurately track hands due to small markers, frequent occlusions, and the need for high-resolution cameras. Consequently, existing datasets may either lack detailed hand information (e.g., MoGaze, KIT, BEHAVE) or contain noisy hand poses (e.g., InterCap). While some datasets provide accurate hand-object interactions (e.g., GRAB), these are resource-intensive to capture and do not scale easily.

The current work, GRIP, positions itself within this landscape by specifically addressing the generation of dynamic, realistic hand motions for both single-hand and bi-manual interactions, given only the motion of the body and object. It aims to overcome several limitations of prior art, including requirements for initial hand poses, computational expense of iterative optimization, lack of generalization to novel objects, and the inability to handle bi-manual scenarios realistically. By focusing on a feed-forward, learning-based approach, GRIP seeks to provide a generalizable and efficient solution for synthesizing detailed hand articulation.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a learning-based method, GRIP, capable of synthesizing realistic and temporally consistent 3D motion for both hands before, during, and after interaction with an object. This synthesis is performed by taking as input only the 3D motion of the body and the object, which are typically easier to acquire or animate than detailed hand movements.

The motivation for GRIP stems from several observations within the field:

- **Difficulty of Hand Motion Capture:** Capturing accurate, high-fidelity 3D hand motion, especially finger articulation, is technically challenging. Motion capture systems often produce noisy or incomplete hand data due to marker occlusion or the inherent complexity of hand kinematics. This limits the availability of comprehensive datasets for training robust models.
- **Limitations of Existing Data:** Many existing motion capture datasets, while providing body and object motion, either lack detailed hand information or contain noisy hand poses. This restricts their utility for applications requiring fine-grained hand-object interaction. GRIP aims to "upgrade" such datasets by automatically adding realistic hand motions.
- **Need for Dynamic and Generalizable Interaction:** Prior work often focuses on static grasp generation or requires an initial hand pose to refine. Moreover, many methods are limited to single-hand interactions, struggle to generalize to novel object shapes or sizes, or involve computationally expensive iterative optimization steps, preventing real-time application.
- **Demand for Realism in Digital Humans:** Applications in computer graphics, virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), games, and telepresence critically depend on highly realistic digital humans. Subtle errors in hand posture or object interaction significantly diminish perceived realism, making accurate hand motion synthesis a priority.

To address these motivations, GRIP sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:

- **Synthesize Full Interaction Dynamics:** Generate hand motions that cover the entire interaction sequence, including pre-grasp opening, the actual grasping phase, and post-grasp release, rather than just static grasp poses or contact frames.
- **Support Bi-manual and Single-hand Interactions:** Develop a unified model capable of simultaneously predicting poses for both the left and right hands, enabling realistic modeling of both bi-manual and single-hand object manipulations.
- **Generalize to Unseen Objects and Motions:** Ensure the model can adapt to a variety of previously unseen object shapes, sizes, and interaction motions, moving beyond the specific objects or interaction types seen during training.
- **Robustness to Noisy Inputs:** Account for potential noise in input body and arm motions, often present in real-world tracking or reconstruction data, by incorporating an arm denoising component.
- **Achieve Temporal Consistency:** Generate hand motions that are smooth and consistent across successive frames, avoiding jitter or sudden, unrealistic changes, particularly in contact areas.
- **Minimize Hand-Object Penetrations:** Produce physically plausible interactions by minimizing intersections between the hand and the object surfaces.
- **Operate in a Feed-forward Manner:** Design a non-iterative, learning-based pipeline that can generate hand motions efficiently, aiming for near real-time performance suitable for interactive applications.
- **Eliminate Initial Hand Pose Requirement:** Infer dynamic hand motion directly without needing an initial hand pose, simplifying the input requirements.

By achieving these objectives, GRIP aims to provide a robust and practical solution for enhancing the realism of digital human-object interactions across various domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GRIP employs a learning-based, two-stage inference pipeline to generate realistic hand-object interaction poses, preceded by an arm denoising step. The method is designed to be feed-forward, avoiding iterative optimization.

#### 3.1. Body and Hand Representations

The SMPL-X model is utilized to represent the human body and hands. SMPL-X allows for detailed motion and accurate physical interactions by modeling the body surface through linear blend skinning with a rigged skeleton. GRIP specifically focuses on predicting the parameters for the right and left hand poses (θ_r ∈ ℝ^(15×6) and θ_l ∈ ℝ^(15×6)). To efficiently represent the hand surface, 99 vertices are sampled on each hand.

#### 3.2. Hand Sensors

To extract rich spatio-temporal interaction cues between the hands and the object, GRIP introduces two types of virtual "hand sensors":

- **Ambient Sensor:** This sensor is designed to capture the object's geometry and its spatial relationship to the hands within a broader reaching region. For each hand, 1024 points are uniformly sampled within a hemisphere rigidly attached to the hand's middle-finger base joint. For each point, the distance to the closest vertex on the object surface is computed. This distance-based representation is continuous, differing from discrete voxel grids. To emphasize points closer to the object, these distances are transformed using an interaction-aware attention function (I_w(d) = exp(-w × d) with w=5).
- **Proximity Sensor:** This sensor provides fine-grained geometric details about the distance of specific hand regions to the object surface, crucial for identifying contact areas. It computes the closest distance from 99 sampled hand vertices to the object surface. For CNet's initial prediction, a mean hand pose is used to compute these features. For RNet's refinement stage, the hand poses generated by CNet are used to recompute more accurate Proximity Sensor values. A higher weight (w=50) is applied in the interaction-aware attention transformation for the Proximity Sensor to emphasize very close distances.

#### 3.3. Arm Denoising Network (ANet)

As input arm motion can be noisy, potentially affecting the accuracy of the hand sensors, an arm denoising network, ANet, is employed as a preliminary step. ANet refines noisy arm motions while enforcing temporal motion consistency using the Latent Temporal Consistency (LTC) algorithm. It takes the current and noisy future arm poses as input and outputs denoised arm poses.

#### 3.4. Consistency Network (CNet)

CNet is the first stage of the hand prediction framework. It is an encoder-decoder neural network designed to generate smooth and consistent hand interaction motions.

- **Inputs:** CNet takes a comprehensive set of features for two consecutive frames (time t and t+1):
 - SMPL-X body pose parameters (excluding global root).
 - Ambient Sensor values for both hands.
 - Proximity Sensor values for both hands (initialized with mean hand pose).
 - Average hand-to-object distance and its rate of change over the next `n` future frames (default `n=10`), which helps disambiguate grasp and release moments.
- **Latent Temporal Consistency (LTC):** This is a key innovation to ensure temporal and contact consistency without introducing high-frequency noise in contact areas. The encoder maps the input to two latent codes: a global latent code for the current frame (z_t) and a relative latent code for the next frame (z_t_t+1). The global latent code for the next frame is computed as z_t+1 = z_t + z_t_t+1. Both global latent codes (z_t and z_t+1) are then passed individually to a *shared* decoder. This shared decoder helps regulate inconsistencies by penalizing discrepancies in the final decoded hand poses.
- **Outputs:** CNet outputs the predicted right and left hand poses (θ_r^(t:t+1), θ_l^(t:t+1)) and inferred Proximity Sensor values (h_P^(t:t+1)) for both current and next frames.
- **Loss Function:** The training loss includes terms for hand vertices (L_v), joint rotations of both hands (L_θ), and the hand-to-object distances (L_hP), which are both directly estimated by the network and derived from the estimated hand poses.

#### 3.5. Refinement Network (RNet)

The second stage, RNet, refines the hand poses generated by CNet to improve realism and reduce hand-object penetrations.

- **Inputs:** RNet takes the hand poses generated by CNet and recomputed Proximity Sensor features (h_Pθ), which are now more accurate due to the initial CNet prediction.
- **Functionality:** RNet is trained to add subtle refinements, primarily focusing on interaction frames where hands are close to the object surface, to maintain the motion dynamics established by CNet.
- **Training:** RNet is trained on both CNet's output and synthetically perturbed ground-truth data to simulate noisy inputs, making it robust to interaction artifacts. The loss functions are similar to CNet.

#### 3.6. Training Data

GRIP is trained on the GRAB dataset, a motion capture dataset with accurate whole-body human grasping of 3D objects. A subset of 5 objects is withheld for testing, with the rest used for training and validation. Synthetic data with Gaussian noise is also used to train ANet (for arm noise) and RNet (for hand pose perturbations to simulate CNet's errors and penetrations). The model supports simultaneous generation of both left and right hand motion, enabling realistic modeling of single-hand and bi-manual interactions.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents both quantitative and qualitative evaluations demonstrating GRIP's capabilities in generating realistic hand-object interaction motions.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Evaluation

- **Refinement Effect:** Initial poses from CNet are described as reasonable but may exhibit artifacts such as hand-object interpenetrations. The subsequent application of RNet effectively refines these poses, resulting in more realistic and physically plausible grasps by reducing penetrations and "non-contact" issues.
- **Versatility:** GRIP generates diverse hand motions, including pre-grasp hand opening, grasping, post-grasp release, and both single-hand and bi-manual interactions for a variety of unseen object shapes and sizes. The generated motions are reported to be smooth and consistent.
- **Generalization to Unseen Data:** Despite being trained solely on the GRAB dataset (which features primarily small, hand-held objects), GRIP demonstrates generalization to larger, unseen objects from other datasets like InterCap and MoGaze. This generalization is attributed to the local nature of the virtual hand sensors, which focus on interaction areas rather than the entire object.
- **Cross-Object Grasp Transfer:** The method can transfer grasping motions from a source object to a target object of similar size, facilitating synthetic data generation and applications in visual effects.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Evaluation

Standard metrics were used, including Mean Per-Joint Position Error (MPJPE) and Mean Per-Vertex Position Error (MPVPE) to measure the Euclidean distance between generated and ground-truth hand joints and vertices. Additionally, two custom metrics were introduced:
- **Intersection Volume (IV):** Quantifies the volume of intersection between the hand and object, indicating physical plausibility.
- **Contact Consistency (CC):** Measures the consistency of contacts during grasping, assessing finger sliding or deviation from contact areas.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Latent Temporal Consistency (LTC):** Experiments comparing GRIP with LTC against a network without enforced consistency and one with consistency applied in the output space revealed that LTC significantly reduces errors across MPVPE, MPJPE, and CC. This supports the effectiveness of smoothing motion in the latent space for maintaining both temporal and contact consistency.
- **Hand Sensors:** Ablations showed that the proposed distance-based Ambient and Proximity Sensors contribute to lower MPVPE, MPJPE, and CC errors compared to variants without these features or with voxel-grid representations. This indicates that the continuous, distance-based representation, combined with interaction-aware attention, captures richer interaction information.
- **Refinement Network (RNet):** The RNet component was shown to reduce hand MPJPE and MPVPE errors and enhance motion consistency, validating its role in improving the quality of generated grasps.
- **Number of Future Frames:** An analysis of using different numbers of future frames (n) for the hand-to-object distance input demonstrated a trade-off between real-time performance and accuracy. Using 10 future frames yielded higher accuracy, with performance saturating beyond this point.

#### 4.4. Perceptual Study

A perceptual study conducted on Amazon Mechanical Turk compared GRIP's output (with and without RNet) to ground truth (GRAB) and a baseline (ManipNet). Participants rated realism based on hand-object grasp, hand motion smoothness, contact consistency, and in-hand manipulations on a 5-level Likert scale.
- GRIP-generated hand motions were rated as very realistic and close to ground-truth motions.
- RNet refinement improved scores, particularly for Contact Consistency, demonstrating the efficacy of the refinement step.
- GRIP received higher realism ratings than ManipNet, addressing several known limitations of ManipNet, such as its focus on single-hand inference and potentially poorer generalization.

#### 4.5. Comparison to Baselines

- **TOCH:** When evaluated on refining perturbed test-sets from GRAB, the combination of ANet and RNet in GRIP demonstrated superior performance in denoising noisy hand interactions (both pose and translation perturbations) compared to TOCH.
- **Penetration and Contact Ratio:** GRIP (full model) outperformed GrabNet variants and ManipNet in terms of lower penetration volume and higher contact ratio, indicating more physically plausible and realistic grasps, especially considering GRIP's capability to generate dynamic, bi-manual motions.

#### 4.6. Runtime and Physics Simulation

- **Runtime:** GRIP achieves a rapid inference time of 0.022 seconds (approximately 45 fps) on a V100 GPU, making it suitable for interactive applications, though a 10-frame latency is introduced by relying on future frame information for accuracy.
- **Physics Simulation:** In Bullet physics simulations, GRIP-generated grasps (after RNet) achieved 97% stability (object displacement <1 mm after 5 steps), suggesting physical realism in addition to visual plausibility.

#### 4.7. Grasp Analysis

Aggregated contact heatmaps comparing GRIP-generated contacts with GRAB ground truth showed similar patterns, with high contact likelihood on fingertips, suggesting that GRIP produces realistic contact behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GRIP represents an advancement in the field of 3D human-object interaction modeling by providing a data-driven, feed-forward method for generating realistic, dynamic, and temporally consistent hand poses for both single-hand and bi-manual interactions. The work addresses several persistent challenges, particularly the difficulty of acquiring high-quality hand motion data and the limitations of previous methods regarding generalization, computational efficiency, and realism.

The significance of GRIP lies in its novel technical contributions and its potential for broad impact across various applications:

- **Overcoming Data Acquisition Limitations:** By demonstrating the ability to synthesize detailed hand motions from readily available body and object motion, GRIP offers a solution to the scarcity and noise inherent in direct hand motion capture. This implies that existing datasets that lack fine-grained finger movements can be "upgraded" to include realistic hand interactions, and new datasets can be captured with reduced reliance on explicit finger tracking.
- **Enhanced Realism for Digital Avatars:** The generation of physically plausible, smooth, and temporally consistent hand poses, along with the mitigation of hand-object penetrations, directly contributes to more realistic digital humans. This is crucial for improving user immersion and experience in applications such as virtual reality, augmented reality, video games, and telepresence systems.
- **Generalization and Adaptability:** GRIP's design, particularly its distance-based hand sensors and robust training on diverse interactions, enables it to generalize to unseen objects of varying shapes and sizes, as well as to different motion sequences. This broadens its applicability beyond specific training scenarios, allowing for more flexible content creation.
- **Efficient and Practical Solution:** The feed-forward nature of GRIP's neural network pipeline, coupled with its near real-time inference speed, makes it a practical tool for interactive applications. Unlike optimization-based methods, it does not require iterative computations, offering a more streamlined workflow for content generation and live avatar animation.
- **Facilitating Content Creation:** The ability to transfer grasping motions between objects can significantly accelerate content creation workflows in animation, visual effects, and game development. Animators can repurpose a single body-object interaction sequence to generate numerous variations with different target objects, reducing manual effort.
- **Advancing Human-Computer Interaction Research:** By providing a robust method for synthesizing human hand interactions, GRIP can serve as a foundation for further research into more complex human-computer and human-robot interaction scenarios. It can help bridge the gap between abstract body-object motions and the detailed finger movements required for truly dexterous manipulation.

In summary, GRIP offers a comprehensive and effective solution for a complex problem in digital human modeling. Its innovations in spatial cue extraction, latent space temporal consistency, and a multi-stage refinement process contribute to generating highly realistic and versatile hand interactions, with significant implications for both research and practical applications in computer graphics and interactive media.
