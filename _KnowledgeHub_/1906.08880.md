# Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space: An Action Space for Reinforcement Learning in Contact-Rich Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford AI Lab performed a systematic study of action spaces for deep reinforcement learning in robotic manipulation, introducing Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space (VICES). This method demonstrated improved sample efficiency, safety, and zero-shot transferability across diverse contact-rich tasks in simulation and on a real robot.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning research in robotics has often overlooked the systematic evaluation and design of action spaces, focusing more on observation spaces and reward models.
- Existing action spaces (e.g., joint-space control, fixed end-effector impedance) are often ill-suited for contact-rich and constrained manipulation tasks, leading to inefficient learning, instability, or inability to perform dynamic force modulation.
- Policies learned in joint space lack transferability across different robot embodiments, and manually specifying time-varying compliance for complex tasks is not scalable.

## Method

- A comprehensive empirical study was conducted comparing seven common action spaces, including joint torques, velocities, positions, fixed end-effector impedance, and variable impedance in joint space, within a PPO-based deep reinforcement learning framework.
- The paper introduces Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space (VICES), an action space where the policy learns to output desired 6-D end-effector poses and dynamically adapts the 6-D impedance gains (stiffness and damping) along each axis.
- A hierarchical control structure was used, where the RL policy generates high-level references in the chosen action space, which are then translated into low-level joint torque commands by an analytical controller.

## Results

- VICES policies achieved high task success rates across free-space, constrained (e.g., door opening, near 100% success), and contact-rich (e.g., surface wiping, highest wiped unit ratio) tasks with improved sample efficiency.
- Superior zero-shot transferability was demonstrated for VICES, both between simulated robots (Panda to Sawyer) and from simulation to a real Panda robot for a wiping task (successfully wiped over 75% of the line in 8 out of 10 trials) while maintaining controlled forces (mean 28 N).
- VICES enabled learning of more energy-efficient behaviors and safer interactions by dynamically adjusting stiffness and maintaining applied forces within operational limits, outperforming fixed-impedance and joint-space methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The choice of action space is a critical design decision in deep RL for robotics, significantly influencing sample efficiency, safety, physical effort, and generalization capabilities.
> - Defining the action space in end-effector (operational) space, as in VICES, inherently abstracts away robot-specific kinematics and dynamics, which is crucial for transferability across different robot platforms.
> - Dynamic adaptation of impedance parameters is essential for safely and efficiently performing contact-rich tasks, enabling robots to dynamically modulate forces and compliance to interact with uncertain environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in learning complex robotic manipulation tasks, particularly those involving contact. However, much of the research in deep RL for robotics has primarily concentrated on optimizing observation spaces and reward models, often treating the action space as a secondary consideration. Traditional robot control literature, in contrast, emphasizes that the selection of an appropriate action space, one that aligns with the intrinsic characteristics of a task, can substantially simplify exploration, enhance learning efficiency, and improve robustness against disturbances.

Existing approaches in robot manipulation employ various action spaces, including direct joint torques, joint velocities, joint positions, or end-effector poses (often with fixed impedance control). While these methods have shown success in certain contexts, they often fall short in contact-rich tasks where the robot needs to dynamically modulate forces and compliance. For instance, tasks like wiping a surface or operating a constrained mechanism require the robot to vary its stiffness and damping parameters in specific directions over time, a capability not inherently provided by fixed impedance or joint-space control. Manually specifying these time-varying constraints is often impractical and not scalable across diverse tasks.

Within compliant control, impedance control allows robots to safely interact with uncertain environments by explicitly regulating the force exerted when deviating from a kinematic goal. However, many variable impedance control strategies either assume a pre-defined kinematic trajectory or operate in joint space, which can limit the transferability of learned policies across different robot embodiments. Learning from Demonstration (LfD) has also explored variable impedance, but typically to reflect variability in demonstrations rather than adapting to underlying task constraints or force profiles.

Model-based RL approaches have investigated learning variable impedance parameters in end-effector space, but frequently require initial trajectories to bootstrap the learning process. Other RL methods have focused on refining initial trajectories with force profiles or optimizing joint impedance parameters. This paper distinguishes itself by conducting the first empirical study to systematically compare a broad range of common action spaces within a deep RL framework, specifically for contact-rich manipulation, with an emphasis on learning policies from scratch without reliance on demonstrations or initial trajectories. The work proposes and evaluates an action space that enables dynamic adaptation of both motion and compliance in end-effector space, aiming to address the limitations of current action space choices in contact-rich and constrained robotic tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were twofold:
1. **To conduct a comprehensive empirical study** comparing the impact of various action spaces on the learning process and performance of deep reinforcement learning policies for robot manipulation tasks. This included evaluating common action spaces such as joint torques, joint velocities, joint positions, and fixed end-effector impedance, alongside variable impedance control in joint space.
2. **To introduce and advocate for Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space (VICES)** as a particularly advantageous action space for learning constrained and contact-rich manipulation tasks, and to empirically demonstrate its benefits across multiple metrics.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several observations within the robotics and RL communities:

- **Underserved Area in RL:** Despite impressive results in RL for manipulation, there has been limited systematic investigation into the effect of the action space choice. Most RL research prioritizes observation space and reward design, overlooking that the action space defines the interface with the robot's physical capabilities and the environment.
- **Impact on Learning and Robustness:** Studies in robot motion control indicate that a task-conforming action space can simplify exploration for RL agents and improve the robustness of learned policies to environmental disturbances. An ill-suited action space can lead to inefficient learning, instability, or inability to perform certain tasks effectively.
- **Challenges of Contact-Rich Tasks:** Many real-world manipulation tasks involve contact with the environment (e.g., opening a door, wiping a surface). These tasks often require dynamic modulation of forces and compliance. Current action spaces, especially those with fixed impedance or operating solely in joint space, struggle to naturally express and learn these dynamic behaviors.
- **Scalability of Manual Specification:** Manually specifying time-varying task constraints or impedance profiles for a wide range of contact-rich tasks is labor-intensive and not a scalable solution. A learning-based approach that discovers these dynamically is desirable.
- **Transferability:** Policies learned in joint space are inherently tied to a specific robot's kinematics and dynamics, limiting their transferability to different robot platforms or even different instances of the same robot. An action space defined in end-effector space offers the potential for greater generalization.
- **Learning from Scratch:** Many existing variable impedance learning methods require initial trajectories or demonstrations. This work seeks to understand how policies converge and perform when learned from scratch, providing a more fundamental evaluation of action space suitability.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aimed to provide guidance on selecting appropriate action spaces for deep RL in robotics, thereby improving sample efficiency, safety, and transferability for contact-rich manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a deep reinforcement learning framework, specifically using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), to evaluate various action spaces on a set of three distinct robot manipulation tasks. The core idea involved learning a policy $\pi(o) = a$ that maps observations to abstract reference signals (`a`) in a defined action space `A`. These reference signals are then translated into low-level joint torque commands (`u`) by an analytical controller $f_{\kappa}(a, s_{robot})$, where $\kappa$ represents controller parameters that can be fixed or learned by the policy. This hierarchical control structure separates the learning of high-level task strategy from low-level robot execution.

**Action Spaces Evaluated:** The study compared seven distinct action spaces, categorized by the control domain (joint vs. end-effector) and control type (position, velocity, torque, fixed/variable impedance):

1. **Joint Torques:** The policy directly outputs desired joint torques (`a = $\tau_{des}$`), with $f_{\kappa}$ being a direct mapping.
2. **Joint Velocities:** The policy outputs desired joint velocities (`a = $\dot{q}_{des}$`), which are then tracked by a proportional (P) controller mapping to joint torques: $u = k_v (\dot{q}_{des} - \dot{q})$.
3. **Joint Positions:** The policy outputs desired joint positions (`a = $q_{des}$`), tracked by a proportional-derivative (PD) controller mapping to joint torques, compensated by the robot's inertia matrix: $u = M [k_p (q_{des} - q) - k_v \dot{q}]$.
4. **End-Effector Pose (Fixed Impedance):** The policy outputs desired 6-D end-effector poses ($p_{des}, R_{des}$), which are then translated to joint torques using an impedance-based PD controller in operational space. This was evaluated with **low, medium, and high fixed impedance gains** ($k_{pos,p}, k_{pos,v}, k_{ori,p}, k_{ori,v}$). The operational space formulation ensures dynamic decoupling of end-effector motions.
5. **Variable Impedance Control in Joint Space:** Similar to joint positions, but the policy additionally learns to output the proportional and derivative gains ($k_p, k_v$) alongside desired joint positions.
6. **Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space (VICES) (Proposed):** The policy outputs desired 6-D end-effector poses ($p_{des}, R_{des}$) and dynamically adapts the impedance gains ($k_{pos,p}, k_{pos,v}, k_{ori,p}, k_{ori,v}$) along each of the six axes. This allows simultaneous learning of trajectory references and compliant behavior.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was used for policy optimization.
- **Control Frequencies:** Policies generated actions at 20 Hz, while the underlying analytical controllers executed at 500 Hz. Linear interpolation was applied between policy actions to ensure smoother high-frequency controller signals.
- **Robot Models:** A simulated Panda robot was primarily used for training, with a simulated Sawyer robot used for zero-shot transfer evaluation. A real Panda robot was used for sim-to-real transfer.

**Experimental Tasks:** The action spaces were evaluated on three prototypical manipulation tasks designed to represent different levels of contact and constraint:

1. **Path Following (Free-Space Motion):** The robot had to follow a trajectory passing through four fixed via-points without contacting the environment.
 - **Reward:** Task completion (hitting via-points, inverse distance to next), time bonus, and subsequently, an energy consumption penalty.
 - **Observations:** End-effector pose and velocity, via-point locations and status.
2. **Door Opening (Manipulation of Constrained Mechanisms):** The robot had to open a door to a target angle (60 degrees) using a two-finger gripper. The task involved interacting with a 1-DoF kinematic constraint.
 - **Reward:** Progress towards target door angle, constant reward for being close, penalties for excessive forces/torques (over 40 N), collisions with non-gripper parts, and joint limit violations.
 - **Observations:** End-effector pose and velocity, door angle and angular velocity.
3. **Surface Wiping (Contact-Rich Manipulation):** The robot, equipped with a wiping tool, had to wipe randomly placed "stains" on a table with unknown surface location. This required continuous contact and force modulation.
 - **Reward:** Wiping off elements, bonus for wiping all elements, small reward for maintaining contact, penalties for excessive forces (over 40 N), joint limits, or non-tool collisions.
 - **Observations:** 48x48x3 RGB images of the scene (visual observations), end-effector pose and velocity. For sim-to-real, real images were converted to fake simulated images by color segmentation.

**Evaluation Metrics:** For each task and action space, the following were measured:

1. **Sample Efficiency and Task Completion:** Rate of reward increase during training and the ultimate task success rate (e.g., via-points crossed, door angle achieved, elements wiped).
2. **Physical Efficiency:** Total energy consumed by the robot, estimated as the time integral of joint torques (assuming proportionality to electric power).
3. **Physical Effort:** Wrenches applied to the environment during contact-rich tasks.
4. **Transferability Between Robots:** Zero-shot transfer of policies trained on the simulated Panda robot to a simulated Sawyer robot, evaluated by task performance.
5. **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Zero-shot transfer of policies trained on the simulated Panda robot to a real Panda robot for the surface wiping task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provided empirical evidence regarding the suitability of different action spaces for various manipulation tasks, consistently highlighting the advantages of Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space (VICES).

**A. Free-Space Motion - Path Following:**
- **Sample Efficiency and Task Completion:** Policies utilizing end-effector space actions (fixed medium, fixed high, and variable impedance) along with joint velocity and joint position control were able to achieve the task of passing through all four via-points. Joint torque and fixed low end-effector impedance policies were unsuccessful. VICES policies exploited impedance by dynamically adjusting stiffness to navigate between via-points, increasing stiffness for faster motion and avoiding overshooting.
- **Physical Efficiency:** When an energy penalty was introduced, VICES policies in end-effector space demonstrated improved energy efficiency while maintaining task performance. Fixed impedance end-effector policies became unstable, indicating their inability to optimize for energy due to limited degrees of freedom in their action space. Joint-space variable impedance also reduced energy significantly, often more than VICES, which was attributed to their initially faster (and higher energy) task completion, leaving more room for energy optimization.
- **Transferability:** End-effector space policies (fixed and variable impedance) exhibited successful zero-shot transfer from the simulated Panda to the simulated Sawyer robot. In contrast, joint-space policies failed to transfer due to significant differences in robot kinematics and dynamics. This indicates that operational space control effectively abstracts away robot-specific embodiment details.

**B. Manipulation of Constrained Mechanisms - Door Opening:**
- **Sample Efficiency and Task Completion:** End-effector space policies (medium, high, and variable impedance) consistently outperformed joint-space policies, achieving close to 100% task success rates. VICES and medium fixed impedance policies learned the task efficiently and attained similar high rewards, with VICES learning an appropriate impedance for the task. High fixed impedance policies achieved task success but incurred higher penalties due to excessive forces. Low fixed impedance policies failed to generate sufficient force to open the door. Joint velocity policies reached up to 75% success but with lower rewards, often encountering joint limits. Other joint-space actions were unable to open the door without violating joint limits.
- **Transferability:** End-effector space policies demonstrated significantly better transferability from the Panda to the Sawyer robot compared to joint-space policies. However, a performance drop was observed even for end-effector space policies, attributed to the robots' differing kinematic constraints (e.g., joint limits) that the policies did not fully account for during transfer.

**C. Contact-Rich Manipulation - Surface Wiping:**
- **Sample Efficiency and Task Completion:** VICES agents converged faster and achieved higher rewards, along with the highest ratio of wiped units. This was attributed to their ability to dynamically adapt stiffness, resulting in lower applied forces (mean 28 N, within payload limits) and thus fewer penalties compared to other action spaces that either applied too much force or insufficient force for effective wiping.
- **Transferability:** VICES policies exhibited superior zero-shot transfer between the simulated Panda and Sawyer robots compared to other action spaces, though some performance degradation was still observed due to the unique force generation characteristics of different robot embodiments.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A policy trained with VICES in simulation on the Panda robot was directly applied to a real Panda robot for a whiteboard wiping task without any retraining. The robot successfully wiped more than 75% of the painted line in 8 out of 10 trials. The successes were attributed to VICES leveraging knowledge of the robot's dynamic model. Failure cases included abrupt motion triggering safety mechanisms or incomplete wiping. Notably, the robot operated without direct force sensing, relying solely on visual observations and end-effector pose/velocity.

In summary, VICES consistently proved to be a robust, sample-efficient, and safe action space across tasks with varying contact properties. It enabled dynamic impedance adaptation, leading to better physical efficiency, lower applied forces, and notably improved transferability between robot models and from simulation to real-world deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides significant contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly at the intersection of reinforcement learning and compliant control.

**Significance of Findings:**
- **Empirical Validation of Action Space Importance:** The study systematically demonstrates that the choice of action space is a critical design decision in deep RL for robotics, influencing sample efficiency, safety, physical effort, and transferability. This provides concrete evidence for a principle often acknowledged in classical control but less frequently explored in deep RL.
- **Introduction and Validation of VICES:** Variable Impedance Control in End-Effector Space (VICES) is presented and empirically validated as a superior action space for learning contact-rich and constrained manipulation tasks. Its ability to dynamically adapt impedance parameters alongside desired motion in operational space addresses key limitations of fixed-impedance or joint-space approaches.
- **Improved Sample and Energy Efficiency:** VICES policies consistently showed improved sample efficiency for exploration and learning. Furthermore, they demonstrated the capability to learn energy-efficient behaviors while maintaining task performance, which is crucial for practical robot operation.
- **Enhanced Safety and Controlled Interaction:** By allowing dynamic force modulation, VICES enables safer robot interactions with the environment, maintaining applied forces within specified payloads and preventing unstable behaviors often seen with rigid or high-impedance controllers in contact situations.
- **Superior Generalization and Transferability:** A key finding is the significantly improved transferability of VICES policies across different simulated robot embodiments (Panda to Sawyer) and from simulation to a real robot. This is attributed to the operational space formulation decoupling the policy from robot-specific kinematics and dynamics, enabling more abstract and generalizable skill learning. This zero-shot transfer capability is a substantial step towards reducing the sim-to-real gap, a major challenge in robotics.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Guiding RL Research:** This work can serve as a guide for future RL research in robotics, prompting a more deliberate consideration of action space design based on task characteristics. Researchers may prioritize developing policies within operational space or variable impedance frameworks for tasks involving physical interaction.
- **Enabling Robust and Adaptive Robot Skills:** By providing a more suitable action representation, VICES can facilitate the development of more robust, adaptive, and versatile robot skills for complex real-world tasks that inherently involve uncertain contacts, such as assembly, dexterous manipulation, or human-robot collaboration.
- **Reducing Deployment Costs:** The demonstrated sim-to-real transfer capability of VICES policies has the potential to significantly reduce the time, effort, and cost associated with deploying RL-trained robots in real-world environments, as extensive real-world retraining or fine-tuning may become less necessary.
- **Advancing Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability of VICES to enable compliant and dynamically adaptable behavior can lead to safer and more natural human-robot interactions, as robots can learn to adjust their stiffness and force in response to human actions or unexpected contact.
- **Foundation for Complex Behaviors:** VICES can form a foundational component for learning more complex, multi-phase manipulation tasks where different stages require varying degrees of stiffness and compliance, such as picking and placing fragile objects, disassembling components, or performing medical procedures.
