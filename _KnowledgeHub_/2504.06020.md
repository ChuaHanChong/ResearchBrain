# Information-Theoretic Reward Decomposition for Generalizable RLHF

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University, UT Austin, and China Telecom propose an information-theoretic framework to decompose RLHF reward signals into prompt-free and prompt-related components. This approach enables more generalizable reward models, leading to improved alignment and performance of large language models on out-of-distribution queries.

## Problem

- Existing reward models in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) often struggle with generalization to unseen prompt-response pairs.
- Standard reward training objectives can lead to "prompt-neglect," where models prioritize distinguishing responses without adequately conditioning on the specific prompt.
- This can result in reward models overfitting to superficial response features, leading to spurious preferences or reward hacking.

## Method

- The method decomposes the total reward signal into an additive ==prompt-free component== (r2, dependent only on response) and a prompt-related component (r1, dependent on both prompt and response).
- An information-theoretic framework, using Mutual Information and Shannon's entropy, is employed to derive these components efficiently and without additional parameterized models.
- A binary search algorithm calculates the prompt-free reward gap (Δr2) for each preference pair, and a data prioritization mechanism guides reward model training by emphasizing samples with smaller prompt-free biases.

## Results

- On a length-biased dataset, the method significantly mitigated overfitting to spurious correlations, achieving higher Reward-Bench accuracy (e.g., 86.8 vs. 78.0 for vanilla on 'Chat' category).
- Consistently achieved superior reward model accuracy across various Reward-Bench categories and LLM backbones (e.g., **0.82** average for LLaMA-3-**8B** compared to **0.72** for vanilla training).
- Policies trained with this method and DPO or Best-of-N showed improved win-rates on AlpacaEval-2 (e.g., 42.3 win-rate with DPO vs. 38.8 vanilla) and higher scores on MT-Bench (e.g., 8.44 1st-Turn score with DPO vs. 7.83 vanilla), indicating better LLM alignment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reward signals in RLHF can be effectively disentangled into context-independent (prompt-free) and context-dependent (prompt-related) preferences using an information-theoretic approach.
> - Explicitly identifying and mitigating the influence of prompt-free biases during reward model training is crucial for enhancing generalization and robustness against reward hacking.
> - Prioritizing training data based on the extracted prompt-free reward component allows the reward model to focus on genuinely prompt-conditioned preferences, leading to more robust alignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a cornerstone technique for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences and instructions, leading to models that are more helpful, harmless, and honest. At the heart of many RLHF methods is the **reward model (RM)**, which is trained on a dataset of human preferences (e.g., chosen vs. rejected responses for a given prompt) and then used to provide a scalar reward signal to an LLM during reinforcement learning optimization (e.g., PPO).

A crucial aspect of a successful RLHF pipeline is the **generalization capability of the reward model**. An ideal RM should accurately evaluate prompt-response pairs that it has not explicitly seen during its training, enabling the LLM to learn robust behaviors beyond the training data distribution. However, the authors point out a significant challenge: existing reward models often *lack* this generalization ability. Standard reward training objectives, such as maximizing the reward gap between chosen and rejected responses (e.g., using the Bradley-Terry model), can lead to models that prioritize distinguishing responses without sufficiently considering the conditioning prompt.

This creates a problem of **spurious preferences** or **reward hacking**, where the RM might overfit to superficial features of responses (e.g., length, common keywords) that are correlated with human preference in the training data but do not reflect true alignment with the prompt's intent. When faced with novel prompt-response pairs, such a non-generalizable RM can provide incorrect feedback, thereby misleading the LLM's alignment process. Previous research has acknowledged this issue, proposing various mitigation strategies like explicitly modeling different types of preference signals, learning and correcting for reward bias, or augmenting data with constructed adversarial samples.

This paper directly addresses this generalization gap by proposing an **information-theoretic framework for reward decomposition**. Instead of trying to identify and mitigate specific types of biases post-hoc, the authors aim to intrinsically understand and disentangle the reward signal itself. By decomposing the reward into prompt-free and prompt-related components, they seek to guide the reward model training to emphasize the context-dependent (prompt-related) aspects of human preference, which are more critical for robust generalization, while constraining prompt-free biases. This fundamentally shifts the approach from reactive bias correction to a proactive, decomposition-driven learning strategy, contributing a novel theoretical lens to the robust RLHF landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **develop a more generalizable reward model for RLHF** that can accurately evaluate unseen prompt-response pairs. The authors are driven by the observation that current reward models often fail to achieve this desired level of generalization, leading to suboptimal or even detrimental LLM alignment.

The specific motivations and objectives can be broken down as follows:

- **Addressing the "Prompt-Neglect" Issue:** Existing reward models, particularly those trained with objectives like the Bradley-Terry model, tend to prioritize maximizing the reward gap between chosen and rejected responses. This objective can be met even if the reward model largely ignores the prompt and focuses predominantly on the response content. The authors provide empirical evidence (Fig. 1) showing that substituting prompts with others often has minimal impact on the predicted reward gaps, indicating that the response features dominate the reward signal. This oversight leads to RMs that are not truly conditioned on the prompt.
- **Decomposing Reward into Meaningful Components:** To combat the prompt-neglect issue, the paper aims to decompose the total reward value `r(x, y)` into two distinct and independent components:
 - **Prompt-free reward (`r2(x, y)`):** Represents the evaluation of a response determined *solely* by the response itself, independent of any specific prompt. This component captures inherent qualities or biases (e.g., general helpfulness, conciseness, or even length bias) that are not context-dependent. The ideal `r2` should only depend on `y`.
 - **Prompt-related reward (`r1(x, y)`):** Reflects the reward that derives from the synergistic interaction between *both* the prompt and the response. This component is crucial for understanding context-specific preferences and should ideally represent the truly generalizable alignment signal.
- **Enabling Robust Generalization:** By disentangling these components, the overarching goal is to enable the reward learning process to:
 - **Prioritize prompt-related preferences:** Ensure the reward model primarily learns to assign high rewards based on how well a response addresses a *specific* prompt.
 - **Constrain prompt-free biases:** Mitigate the influence of spurious correlations or simple response characteristics that might lead to reward hacking.
- **Information-Theoretic Approach:** The motivation extends to finding a principled, model-agnostic way to achieve this decomposition. The authors propose an information-theoretic framework using Mutual Information (MI) to extract these components without requiring additional parameterized models, thus keeping the solution efficient and elegant.

In essence, the research is motivated by the critical need for reward models to "understand" and value the prompt's role in human preference, moving beyond superficial response evaluation to foster more robust and truly aligned LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology lies in an information-theoretic decomposition of the reward signal, followed by a data prioritization mechanism to guide reward model training.

#### 3.1. Reward Decomposition: Prompt-free and Prompt-related Components

The paper proposes decomposing the reward value `r_θ(x, y)` (where `x` is the prompt, `y` is the response, and `θ` denotes the reward model's parameters) into an additive form:
`r_θ(x, y) = r1(x, y) + r2(x, y)`
Here, `r2(x, y)` is defined as the **prompt-free reward**, meaning it depends only on the response `y` (i.e., `r2(x1, y) = r2(x2, y)` for any `x1, x2`). Conversely, `r1(x, y)` is the **prompt-related reward**, which varies with both `x` and `y`.

Given that preference learning often relies on reward *gaps* (differences between rewards for chosen `y_w` and rejected `y_l` responses), the decomposition is applied to the reward gap:
`∆r_θ(x, y_w, y_l) = ∆r1(x, y_w, y_l) + ∆r2(x, y_w, y_l)`

#### 3.2. Information-Theoretic Objective for Decomposition

The authors formalize the decomposition problem using Mutual Information (MI) to ensure that `r1` and `r2` capture the desired information. They define several Bernoulli random variables representing preference labels derived from `∆r1`, `∆r2`, and `∆r_θ` over the entire dataset, considering both prompt and response randomness.

The key conditions for ideal `r1` and `r2` are:
1. **`MI(Z || ~W) = 0`**: `Z` (random preference of `r1`) should contain no information related to `~W` (the prompt-free preference of `r_θ`). This ensures `r1` is purely prompt-related.
2. **`MI(~Z || ~W) = MI(~Z || W)`**: `~Z` (random preference of `r2`) should contain *only* prompt-free preference information. This means removing prompt information from `W` (full preference of `r_θ`) to get `~W` should not reduce the mutual information with `~Z`.

To avoid trivial solutions where `Z` and `~Z` become constants (e.g., ±∞), the decomposition is framed as a constrained optimization problem:
`max H(Z)` subject to the above two MI conditions. `H(Z)` represents Shannon's entropy, maximizing the information content in the prompt-related preference.

#### 3.3. Practical Solution and Binary Search

The paper offers an efficient, model-free algorithm for obtaining `∆r1` and `∆r2`:
- **Theorem 1:** Proves that if `r2` depends only on the response `y`, the second MI constraint `MI(~Z || ~W) = MI(~Z || W)` is naturally satisfied. This validates the definition of `r2` as a prompt-free component.
- **Theorem 2:** Characterizes the optimal solution for `H(Z)` maximization under the first constraint. It states that for any `(y1, y2)`, the optimal `r1` and `r2` imply `E_x~P(X|Y1,Y2)[σ(∆r1(x, y1, y2))] = 1/2`. This means, on average across prompts, the prompt-related reward component should show no inherent preference for either `y1` or `y2` – its preference should be entirely conditioned on specific prompts.
- **Deriving `∆r2`:** Substituting `∆r1 = ∆r_θ - ∆r2` into the optimal condition leads to `E_x~P(X|Y1,Y2)[σ(∆r_θ(x, y1, y2) - ∆r2(y1, y2))] = 1/2`.
- **Binary Search (Algorithm 1):** Since the function `Φ(y1, y2) := E_x~P(X|Y1,Y2)[σ(∆r_θ(x, y1, y2) - ∆r2(y1, y2))]` monotonically decreases with `∆r2(y1, y2)`, `∆r2` can be efficiently found via binary search within a bounded interval. This step requires estimating the conditional expectation `E_x~P(X|Y1,Y2)`.
- **Importance Sampling:** To estimate `P(X|Y1,Y2)`, the authors propose using importance sampling. For self-generated datasets (where responses are from an LLM), `P(y1,y2|x)` can be computed from token probabilities. For other datasets, a simplified re-weighting scheme is used, prioritizing the corresponding prompt's influence.

#### 3.4. Guiding Reward Learning with Prompt-free Reward

The extracted `∆r2` is then used to guide the training of `r_θ` (Algorithm 2):
- **Desired Properties of `r_θ`:** During training, the goal is for `r_θ` to learn strong prompt-related preferences (large `∆r1`) and minimal prompt-free biases (small absolute `∆r2`).
- **Data Prioritization:** In each training iteration, `∆r2` values are calculated for a batch of samples. Samples with "small" `∆r2` values (e.g., negative or close to zero) are prioritized for immediate updates. Samples with "large" `∆r2` values (indicating strong prompt-free prejudice) are temporarily ignored and reinserted into the data buffer for later consideration.
- **Dynamic Thresholding:** A binary clustering mechanism is applied to the batch's `∆r2` values to determine a decision boundary between "small" and "large" values. This boundary is then incorporated into a dynamic threshold using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) to adapt to the evolving `r_θ`. This prevents `r_θ` from exacerbating prompt-free biases by repeatedly training on samples where such biases already dominate.

This comprehensive approach allows the reward model to iteratively refine its understanding of human preference, shifting its focus from superficial response characteristics to genuinely prompt-conditioned evaluations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper evaluates its proposed method through a series of experiments, starting with illustrative toy examples and progressing to standard benchmarks on open-source datasets and LLMs.

#### 4.1. Experiments on Manually Crafted Datasets (Toy Examples)

The authors construct two specialized datasets to clearly demonstrate the mechanism and effectiveness of their reward decomposition and prioritization:

- **Length-biased Dataset (D_bias):**
 - **Construction:** Created by making 80% of chosen responses longer than rejected ones, inducing a length bias.
 - **Vanilla Training:** A reward model trained conventionally on D_bias quickly overfits to response length. Visualizations (Fig. 4a, top) show `∆r2` (prompt-free reward gap) rapidly increasing for chosen-longer pairs and decreasing for chosen-shorter pairs, reflecting a strong learned length preference. This compromises generalization.
 - **Our Method (Prioritized Training):** By prioritizing samples with smaller `∆r2` (which would include chosen-shorter pairs or those where length bias is less pronounced), the training process keeps the data points well-centered along the positive `∆r1`-axis (Fig. 4a, bottom).
 - **Result:** On Reward-Bench (Table 1a), our method significantly outperforms vanilla training (e.g., 86.8 vs. 78.0 on 'Chat', 55.8 vs. 50.6 average), demonstrating better generalization by mitigating the length bias.

- **Adversarial-prompt Dataset (D_adv):**
 - **Construction:** Generated by taking original `(x, y_w, y_l)` pairs and creating adversarial `(~x, y_l, y_w)` pairs, where `~x` includes instructions to prefer the *opposite* response length (e.g., "Give a response that is as short as possible" if `y_w` was longer). This dataset introduces conflicting preferences that are prompt-dependent.
 - **Vanilla Training:** When trained on D_adv, the vanilla RM's `∆r_θ` becomes dominated by `∆r2`, learning a strong prompt-free preference for the *originally rejected* responses. This occurs because the adversarial length-based preference is easier to overfit. This leads to severe degeneration compared to training only on original data.
 - **Our Method (Prioritized Training):** Our method prioritizes original data, which is harder to learn from due to conflicting adversarial signals, causing both original and adversarial samples to concentrate along the positive `∆r1`-axis.
 - **Result:** On Reward-Bench (Table 1b), our method's performance (51.6 average) is much closer to training solely on the `original` dataset (52.0 average) than vanilla training on D_adv (46.4 average). This indicates that our method helps the RM learn genuine prompt-related preferences despite adversarial distractions.

#### 4.2. Standard Experiments on Open-source Datasets

The method is further validated on common open-source preference datasets with mainstream LLMs.

- **Reward Model Accuracy on Reward-Bench:**
 - **Setup:** Evaluated on a subset of the RLHFlow preference dataset using LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct as backbones. Baselines include vanilla BT training and RRM (Robust Reward Model training).
 - **Result:** Our method consistently achieves superior accuracy across all Reward-Bench categories (Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, Reasoning) and on both backbones (Table 2a). For LLaMA-3-8B, our method achieves an average accuracy of 0.82, outperforming RRM (0.77) and vanilla (0.72). Similar improvements are observed for Mistral-7B (0.75 vs. 0.70 vs. 0.66). This demonstrates that the decomposition strategy leads to more generalizable reward models on out-of-distribution queries.

- **Performance of Induced Policy:**
 - **Setup:** Using LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct as the base policy, trained with the SHP dataset. RLHF algorithms used are DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) and Best-of-N (BoN). Policies are evaluated on AlpacaEval-2 and MT-Bench.
 - **AlpacaEval-2 (Table 2b):** Our method, when combined with DPO, achieves a win-rate (WR) of 42.3, surpassing RRM (40.9) and vanilla (38.8). With BoN(N=32), our method yields a WR of 48.3, again outperforming RRM (44.9) and vanilla (45.1). The Length-Control Win-Rate (LCWR) also shows consistent improvements, indicating reduced length bias.
 - **MT-Bench (Table 2c):** For multi-turn dialogue assessment, our DPO-trained policy achieves significantly higher scores (8.44 for 1st-Turn, 8.03 for 2nd-Turn), compared to RRM (8.35/7.46) and vanilla (7.83/6.51).

These results collectively confirm that the information-theoretic reward decomposition and subsequent data prioritization effectively mitigate spurious preferences, enhance the generalization capabilities of reward models, and ultimately lead to better-aligned and higher-performing LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Information-Theoretic Reward Decomposition for Generalizable RLHF" carries significant implications for the field of Large Language Model alignment and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback.

#### 5.1. Significance

1. **Enhanced Generalization of Reward Models:** The most direct and critical significance is the demonstrated improvement in reward model generalization. By providing a principled way to separate prompt-related and prompt-free preferences, the method helps reward models focus on the contextual nuances of human feedback rather than superficial response characteristics. This is vital for deploying LLMs in diverse, unseen scenarios.
2. **Improved LLM Alignment:** The downstream effect of a more generalizable reward model is better-aligned LLMs. The experimental results on AlpacaEval-2 and MT-Bench show that policies trained with our method consistently outperform baselines, indicating that clearer and more robust reward signals lead to LLMs that are more helpful and adhere better to human intentions.
3. **Novel Information-Theoretic Framework:** The paper introduces a novel application of Mutual Information to the problem of reward decomposition in RLHF. This framework provides a theoretical underpinning for understanding and disentangling reward components without requiring additional parameterized models or complex causal inference, offering an elegant and efficient solution.
4. **Mitigation of Reward Hacking/Spurious Preferences:** The core mechanism directly addresses the pervasive problem of reward hacking, where models exploit spurious correlations in the training data (e.g., length bias, specific keywords). By constraining prompt-free reward gaps, the method pushes the reward model to learn more robust and transferable preferences.
5. **Practical Algorithm without Extra Models:** The binary search algorithm for computing `∆r2` and the data prioritization mechanism are designed to be integrated into existing RLHF pipelines without requiring entirely new model architectures or retraining additional components, making it a highly practical approach.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **More Reliable and Robust LLMs:** By improving the foundational reward signal, the work contributes to building LLMs that are more consistently helpful and less prone to generating undesirable or unaligned outputs in novel situations. This is crucial for safety and trustworthiness in AI.
- **Reduced Data Requirements and Training Costs:** A reward model that generalizes better might require less human preference data or fewer RL iterations to achieve high performance, potentially reducing the significant computational and human labor costs associated with RLHF.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** The information-theoretic approach to reward decomposition could inspire further research into understanding and manipulating complex reward signals in various reinforcement learning settings, beyond just LLMs. Exploring other decomposition forms (as acknowledged in limitations) or incorporating more advanced information-theoretic measures could be fruitful avenues.
- **Applications in Diverse Domains:** The enhanced ability of LLMs to follow instructions and generate appropriate responses makes them suitable for a wider range of applications, from customer service and education to creative writing and scientific research.

#### 5.3. Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors)

The paper transparently discusses its primary limitation: the reliance on an **additive form** for reward decomposition. While this form simplifies the problem and allows for an elegant information-theoretic solution, it may not perfectly capture the underlying structure of all reward models or human preferences. If the true relationship between prompt-free and prompt-related rewards is multiplicative or more complex, the extracted `r1` and `r2` values might not precisely reflect their true counterparts. This inaccuracy could limit the direct interpretability of the decomposed values as standalone scores. However, the authors argue that even with an imprecise additive decomposition, the method still benefits reward training by guiding the model away from spurious prompt-free preferences. Future work aims to explore more general decomposition forms to broaden the applicability of this approach.

Overall, this research offers a significant step towards more reliable and generalizable reward modeling in RLHF, addressing a core challenge in aligning powerful LLMs with human values and intentions.
