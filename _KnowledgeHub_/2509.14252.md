# LLM-JEPA: Large Language Models Meet Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures

> [!summary] Summary
> LLM-JEPA introduces a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)-based training objective for Large Language Models, enhancing fine-tuning and pretraining by improving accuracy, robustness against overfitting, and fostering more structured abstract representations. This method achieved superior performance across models like Llama, Gemma, and OpenELM on datasets including NL-RX-SYNTH, GSM8K, and Spider, while mitigating computational costs with a novel 'loss dropout' mechanism.

## Problem

- A methodological gap exists between vision and language model training, where vision tasks benefit from JEPAs for robust representation learning, but language tasks predominantly rely on reconstruction-based generative objectives.
- Current LLM training, focused on generative capabilities like next-token prediction, may not optimally foster the abstract and robust representations needed for complex reasoning and perception tasks.
- Generative models can be computationally intensive due to full input-space reconstruction and may inadvertently learn biases from surface-level features.

## Method

- Introduced ==LLM-JEPA==, a novel training objective that combines the standard autoregressive language modeling loss with a ==Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) loss==.
- Designed the JEPA component to leverage =='multi-view' data== (e.g., natural language descriptions and corresponding code or paraphrases) by predicting embeddings of one view from another.
- Implemented the JEPA objective with minimal architectural overhead by reusing the LLM's internal transformer layers as both encoder and predictor, facilitated by a special `[PRED]` token and custom attention mask, and introduced =='loss dropout'== to reduce computational cost.

## Results

- Achieved consistent and statistically significant accuracy improvements across various models (e.g., Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, Gemma-2-2b-it) and datasets (e.g., NL-RX-SYNTH, GSM8K, Spider) in fine-tuning, with enhanced robustness against overfitting.
- Demonstrated encouraging results in pretraining, leading to improved representation quality and better downstream task performance (e.g., statistically significant improvements on Rotten Tomatoes and Yelp sentiment classification) while maintaining generative abilities.
- The 'loss dropout' technique effectively reduced computational overhead by up to **50%** for LD=0.5 and enabled faster convergence and higher accuracy under equivalent compute budgets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Minimizing the standard autoregressive LLM loss does not implicitly minimize the JEPA objective, underscoring the explicit need for a JEPA term to learn robust and abstract representations.
> - LLM-JEPA induces a clearer, more structured organization in the latent space, making mappings between different views (e.g., Text and Code) approximately linear and confined to a narrow subspace.
> - The JEPA objective functions as a powerful regularization and representation-structuring mechanism, complementing LLMs' generative capabilities without diminishing them.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of representation learning in machine learning has broadly diverged into two main approaches: generative/reconstruction-based methods and reconstruction-free Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs).

**Generative/Reconstruction-Based Methods**: This category includes the vast majority of current Large Language Models (LLMs) and many foundational vision models. These models, like GPT-series, Llama, and BERT, are primarily trained with objectives that involve reconstructing their input (e.g., predicting the next token in a sequence, filling in masked tokens, or generating images from latent codes). This approach has been incredibly successful, leading to unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and multimodal tasks. However, its effectiveness, particularly for perception tasks, has been questioned in certain contexts, with criticisms pointing to potential biases and the computational intensity of full input-space reconstruction.

**Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)**: JEPAs represent an alternative paradigm, particularly prominent in computer vision. Instead of reconstructing pixel-perfect images, JEPAs learn representations by predicting embeddings of different "views" of the same input from each other, while simultaneously preventing a collapse of the embedding space (where all inputs map to the same representation). Examples include I-JEPA (Image-JEPA) and Data2vec. This approach has shown provable benefits in vision tasks, offering more robust, less biased, and potentially more abstract representations for perception and knowledge discovery. The core idea is to learn *what* features are important, rather than *how* to perfectly regenerate the input.

**The Mismatch and the Gap**: This paper highlights a critical mismatch: while JEPAs have demonstrated superiority in vision for learning robust representations, the language domain overwhelmingly relies on reconstruction-based methods (e.g., autoregressive next-token prediction). LLMs are primarily evaluated on their generative capabilities, making it challenging to directly apply JEPA-style objectives which are inherently non-generative in their core loss. This creates a significant gap, as LLMs also perform tasks involving perception and reasoning where JEPA-like representations might be beneficial.

**Bridging the Gap**: This research directly addresses this disparity by proposing LLM-JEPA, a first step towards adapting JEPA objectives for LLMs. It aims to harness the benefits of JEPAs—such as learning richer, less biased, and more structured abstract representations—without sacrificing the LLMs' crucial generative capabilities. By focusing on scenarios where "multiple views" of the same underlying knowledge exist (e.g., natural language description and corresponding code), the paper seeks to demonstrate that language models can also benefit from embedding-space predictive learning. This work represents a significant step towards a more unified approach to representation learning across different modalities, potentially paving the way for more efficient and robust self-supervised learning for language. It aligns with the broader goal of developing more general and capable AI systems by integrating successful paradigms from different subfields.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to introduce and validate a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)-based training objective for Large Language Models (LLMs), dubbed **LLM-JEPA**. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Address the Mismatch between Vision and Language Representation Learning**: The authors observe a fundamental divergence in training paradigms: vision tasks increasingly leverage JEPAs for superior, less biased embedding-space learning, while language tasks remain predominantly reliant on input-space reconstruction and generative objectives (e.g., next-token prediction). The primary motivation is to bridge this gap, asking: "can language training methods learn a few tricks from the vision ones?"
2. **Improve LLM Representation Quality and Abstract Reasoning**: The core hypothesis is that while generative tasks are crucial, they might not optimally foster the abstract and robust representations needed for complex reasoning and perception tasks. By introducing a JEPA objective, the authors aim to improve the intrinsic quality of LLM embeddings, enabling them to capture richer, more structured semantic relationships. This is crucial for enhancing LLMs' ability to understand and reason beyond surface-level text generation.
3. **Enhance Robustness and Prevent Overfitting**: JEPAs in vision have shown benefits in terms of robustness and better generalization. The authors are motivated to explore if LLM-JEPA can imbue LLMs with similar properties, making them more resilient to overfitting during fine-tuning and capable of generalizing better to unseen data.
4. **Maintain Generative Capabilities**: A significant challenge in adapting JEPAs to LLMs is that JEPAs are inherently reconstruction-free, while LLMs are valued for their generative prowess. A key motivation is to design an objective that *complements* the standard generative loss, ensuring that the benefits of JEPA do not come at the cost of sacrificing the LLM's ability to generate coherent and relevant text in input space.
5. **Explore Novel Pretraining and Fine-tuning Strategies**: The paper seeks to explore LLM-JEPA's applicability both during fine-tuning, where it can enhance task-specific performance, and during pretraining, where it has the potential to learn more general and transferable representations from scratch. This aims to open new avenues for developing more powerful and versatile LLMs.
6. **Leverage "Multi-View" Data in NLP**: Many NLP tasks naturally involve multiple representations or "views" of the same underlying concept (e.g., a natural language description and its corresponding code, or paraphrases of the same sentence). The motivation is to explicitly leverage these inherent multi-view structures with a JEPA objective to learn more aligned and robust joint embeddings.

In essence, the paper is motivated by the desire to push the boundaries of LLM capabilities by integrating a proven, but previously underutilized, self-supervised learning paradigm from computer vision, aiming for more intelligent, robust, and efficient language models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the introduction of **LLM-JEPA**, a novel training objective that combines the standard autoregressive language modeling loss with a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) loss.

#### 3.1. The LLM-JEPA Objective

The proposed loss function, $L_{LLM-JEPA}$, is defined as:

$L_{LLM-JEPA} = \sum_{l=2}^{L} L_{LLM}(Text_{1:l-1}, Text_l) + \lambda \times d(Pred(Enc(Text)), Enc(Code))$

Here's a breakdown of its components:

- **Generative Capabilities (LLM Term)**: The first term, $\sum_{l=2}^{L} L_{LLM}(Text_{1:l-1}, Text_l)$, is the standard cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction. It ensures the LLM retains its autoregressive generative capabilities. This term is computed causally over the sequence. The authors emphasize that their JEPA construction is agnostic to the specific $L_{LLM}$ used, making it broadly applicable.
- **Abstraction Capabilities (JEPA Term)**: The second term, $\lambda \times d(Pred(Enc(Text)), Enc(Code))$, is the novel JEPA objective.
 - $\lambda \ge 0$: A hyperparameter balancing the contribution of the JEPA term relative to the standard LLM loss.
 - $Enc(\cdot)$: An encoder network, which in this context is the LLM itself. It extracts embeddings of the input sequences. Specifically, the hidden state of the last token from the last layer is used as the embedding, a common practice for LLM probing.
 - $Text$ and $Code$: These represent different "views" of the same underlying knowledge. The paper initially focuses on tasks where such natural pairs exist (e.g., natural language description of a regular expression and the regular expression itself; natural language issue description and corresponding code diff).
 - $Pred(\cdot)$: A predictor network that takes the embedding of one view (e.g., $Enc(Text)$) and attempts to predict the embedding of the other view (e.g., $Enc(Code)$).
 - $d(\cdot, \cdot)$: A distance metric to compare the predicted embedding with the ground-truth embedding. Cosine similarity is chosen, consistent with practices in vision JEPAs.

#### 3.2. Key Components and Implementation Details

1. **Views (Text and Code)**: The success of the JEPA objective hinges on identifying meaningful "views" of the same underlying information. The paper uses (Text, Code) pairs as concrete examples, such as:
 - Natural Language (Text) to Regular Expression (Code)
 - Natural Language (Text) to SQL Query (Code)
 - Git issue description (Text) to code diff (Code)
 The paper also extends this concept to Q&A datasets (question as Text, answer span as Code) and context-completion tasks (context as Text, correct continuation as Code), demonstrating flexibility in defining views.

2. **The Encoder**: The LLM's internal transformer layers serve as the encoder. Both $Text$ and $Code$ are packed into a single context window.

3. **The Predictor**: To minimize architectural overhead, the authors leverage the auto-regressive nature of LLMs and their internal self-attention mechanisms to define a tied-weights predictor. A special token `[PRED]` is appended to the input prompt (e.g., $Text$ before its encoding). The embedding of the last predictor token (after $k$ such tokens) serves as $Pred(Enc(Text))$. This reuses the LLM's internal weights for the prediction task, reducing new parameters. $k$ is an additional hyperparameter, with $k=0$ meaning $Pred(x)=x$.

4. **Custom Attention Mask**: A critical challenge is obtaining independent embeddings for $Text$ and $Code$ in a single forward pass while maintaining causal attention for the $L_{LLM}$ term. The authors propose a custom additive self-attention mask that ensures causal attention *within* each view but prevents tokens from one view from attending to tokens in the other view when computing the JEPA loss component. This allows the JEPA embeddings for $Text$ and $Code$ to be computed simultaneously and independently in two forward passes, rather than three, mitigating some computational overhead.

5. **Loss Dropout (for efficiency)**: To further address the computational cost (which is initially about 2x that of standard training due to the additional forward passes), the authors introduce "loss dropout." During training, the JEPA loss term is randomly dropped for a certain percentage of mini-batches. This reduces the per-epoch cost and, surprisingly, can lead to higher accuracy under the same compute budget, especially when $\lambda$ is scaled proportionally to the dropout rate.

#### 3.3. Ablation Studies and Design Choices

The methodology also includes thorough ablation studies to justify design choices, such as:
- Comparing cosine similarity with L2-norm and MSE for the distance metric $d(\cdot, \cdot)$.
- Evaluating the position of the `[PRED]` token (append vs. prepend).
- Testing prediction direction (Text $\rightarrow$ Code vs. Code $\rightarrow$ Text).
- Comparing with InfoNCE loss.
These studies empirically confirm the chosen setup (cosine similarity, appended `[PRED]`, Text $\rightarrow$ Code prediction) to be superior.

The methodology highlights a clever adaptation of JEPA principles to LLMs, carefully integrating it with existing generative capabilities and addressing practical implementation challenges like computational efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical validation section of the paper presents a comprehensive set of experiments demonstrating the effectiveness of LLM-JEPA across various models, datasets, and training scenarios.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance in Fine-tuning

- **Consistent Accuracy Gains**: LLM-JEPA consistently and significantly outperforms standard LLM fine-tuning objectives (which rely solely on next-token prediction) across a wide range of models and datasets.
 - **Models**: Improvements are observed across Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, Gemma-2-2b-it, OpenELM-1_1B-Instruct, and OLMo-2-0425-1B-Instruct.
 - **Datasets**: Performance boosts are seen on NL-RX-SYNTH (Natural Language to Regular Expression, synthetic), NL-RX-TURK (NL to Regex, human-labeled), GSM8K (math word problems), and Spider (NL to SQL).
 - **Model Sizes**: The benefits hold across different model sizes (1B, 3B, 7B, 8B), indicating scalability of the approach.
- **Robustness to Overfitting**: Unlike standard fine-tuning, which often shows signs of overfitting (accuracy plateaus or drops after a few epochs), LLM-JEPA models demonstrate greater resistance to overfitting, with accuracy often continuing to improve over more training epochs. This is observed in both full fine-tuning and LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) settings.
- **LoRA Compatibility**: LLM-JEPA also enhances LoRA fine-tuning, achieving substantially higher accuracy and faster convergence compared to LoRA with only the standard LLM loss. Notably, LLM-JEPA with LoRA rank 512 achieves accuracy comparable to full fine-tuning, whereas baseline LoRA still has a significant gap.

#### 4.2. Encouraging Results in Pretraining

- **Improved Representation Quality from Scratch**: Pretraining Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct from random weights on NL-RX-SYNTH with LLM-JEPA yields significantly better performance (60.59% accuracy vs. 54.38% for baseline) when evaluated using a "starts-with" criterion for generation termination, suggesting improved learned representations.
- **Downstream Task Benefits**: A more advanced pretraining experiment on the cestwc/paraphrase dataset (leveraging paraphrases as different views) showed that models pretrained with LLM-JEPA achieved statistically significant improvements on downstream fine-tuning tasks like Rotten Tomatoes and Yelp sentiment classification, even though JEPA loss was *not* used during fine-tuning. This highlights that LLM-JEPA pretraining can instill more transferable and useful representations.
- **Maintenance of Generative Capabilities**: Despite the embedding-space objective, pretrained models maintain their generative abilities, as shown by samples generated from the cestwc/paraphrase dataset.

#### 4.3. Structured Representations

- **Enhanced Latent Space Structure**: Visualizations using t-SNE plots reveal that LLM-JEPA induces a clearer, more structured organization in the latent space for Text and Code representations, unlike standard fine-tuning, which can disrupt this structure compared to the base model.
- **Confined Mappings and Near-Linearity**: The difference between $Enc(Text)$ and $Enc(Code)$ for LLM-JEPA models exhibits significantly smaller singular values compared to baselines. This suggests that the mapping between these two views is constrained within a narrow subspace. Furthermore, the least-squares regression error between $Enc(Text)$ and $Enc(Code)$ is remarkably low, indicating that the transformation between these representations is approximately linear, fostering the observed accuracy improvements.
- **JEPA Term is Not Implicitly Minimized**: An important finding is that minimizing the standard $L_{LLM}$ loss does *not* implicitly minimize the JEPA objective. This empirically validates the necessity of explicitly adding the JEPA term for representation learning without hindering next-token prediction capabilities.

#### 4.4. Generalizability Beyond Code-centric Views

- **Q&A and Reasoning Tasks**: LLM-JEPA's benefits extend beyond tasks with obvious "Text-Code" views. It achieves statistically significant improvements on NQ-Open (Question as Text, Answer Span as Code) and HellaSwag (Context as Text, Correct Continuation as Code) datasets.
- **Reasoning Models**: Even strong reasoning models like Qwen3-1.7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B show statistically significant accuracy improvements on GSM8K when augmented with LLM-JEPA, suggesting its applicability to Large Reasoning Models (LRMs).

#### 4.5. Efficiency Improvements via Loss Dropout

- **Computational Cost Mitigation**: Introducing random JEPA-loss dropout (LD) during training significantly reduces the computational overhead (up to 50% for LD=0.5).
- **Faster Convergence and Higher Accuracy**: Loss dropout allows LLM-JEPA to converge faster and achieve higher accuracy for the same compute budget, especially when the JEPA loss weighting hyperparameter ($\lambda$) is appropriately scaled.

#### 4.6. Ablation Studies

- **Optimal Design Choices**: Ablation studies confirm that cosine similarity as the distance metric, appending the `[PRED]` token, and predicting Code from Text are the most effective design choices for LLM-JEPA, outperforming alternatives including InfoNCE loss.
- **Essential Role of $L_{LLM}$**: The generative loss term ($L_{LLM}$) remains crucial; setting its weight ($\gamma$) to zero results in models that generate empty outputs, indicating that the JEPA component acts primarily as a powerful regularization and representation-structuring term rather than a replacement for generative capabilities.

Overall, the findings strongly support the hypothesis that integrating JEPA-style objectives can significantly enhance LLMs' performance, robustness, and internal representation quality across diverse tasks and models, while also paving the way for more efficient training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of LLM-JEPA marks a significant step towards bridging the methodological gap between vision and language model training, with profound implications for the future of Large Language Models.

1. **Enhanced LLM Capabilities and Robustness**: The most immediate impact is the demonstrated improvement in LLMs' fine-tuning and pretraining performance. By integrating a JEPA objective, LLMs exhibit higher accuracy, greater robustness against overfitting, and the ability to learn more structured and abstract representations. This translates to more reliable and effective LLMs for a myriad of applications, from code generation and natural language understanding to complex reasoning tasks.
2. **Richer and More Transferable Representations**: The evidence of LLM-JEPA inducing a "nice structure" in the latent space, confining mappings to a narrow subspace, and promoting near-linear transformations between views is highly significant. This suggests that LLM-JEPA fosters the learning of semantically richer and more disentangled representations. Such representations are inherently more transferable and generalizable, which could lead to better few-shot or zero-shot learning capabilities in downstream tasks, a long-standing goal in AI.
3. **Unified Self-Supervised Learning Paradigm**: This work contributes to the broader effort of developing a more unified and general self-supervised learning paradigm that can effectively apply across different modalities (vision, language, etc.). By showing that the benefits of JEPAs observed in vision can be adapted to language without sacrificing generative capabilities, it moves closer to the vision of "world models" and more general artificial intelligence as advocated by Yann LeCun.
4. **Novel Pretraining Opportunities**: The promising results from pretraining with LLM-JEPA on diverse datasets (like paraphrases) suggest new avenues for pretraining LLMs that go beyond pure next-token prediction. Future research could explore large-scale, multi-view pretraining datasets, leveraging the JEPA objective to create foundational models with superior reasoning and understanding abilities.
5. **Addressing Computational Efficiency**: While JEPAs initially introduce computational overhead, the proposed loss dropout mechanism offers a practical solution, enabling LLM-JEPA to achieve superior performance even with comparable computational budgets. This is crucial for real-world adoption and scaling of the method to larger models and datasets.
6. **Broader Applicability**: The successful application of LLM-JEPA to a wider range of tasks, including Q&A and reasoning benchmarks, beyond the initial "Text-Code" paradigm, underscores its generalizability. This indicates that the concept of "views" can be flexibly interpreted in various NLP contexts, opening up many more potential applications.
7. **Guidance for Future LLM Architectures**: The finding that minimizing standard LLM loss does not implicitly minimize the JEPA objective provides critical insight into the limitations of current autoregressive training. This highlights the explicit need for objectives that focus on abstract representation learning, potentially guiding the design of future LLM architectures and training methodologies.

In summary, LLM-JEPA represents a conceptual and empirical breakthrough, demonstrating that LLMs can significantly benefit from representation-centric, reconstruction-free objectives. It sets a new direction for LLM research, promising models that are not only capable generators but also superior reasoners and more robust learners.
