# Dynamic Modeling of a Welding Torch Umbilical and Its Impact on Robot Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> A constrained multibody model of a robotic welding umbilical, discretized as a serial chain of rigid bodies linked by passive joints carrying stiffness, Coulomb and viscous friction. Prescribed end-effector motion enters as holonomic velocity constraints; projecting onto the admissible-velocity subspace removes Lagrange multipliers, yielding joint accelerations plus an explicitly recovered anchor reaction wrench. A planar 3-R case study shows non-negligible torque effects.

## Problem

- Finite-element and geometrically exact beam formulations model cable dynamics accurately but produce large-scale systems too expensive for in-line robot control integration.
- Cosserat rod models are expressive yet demand implicit integration and delicate numerical treatment, especially when solving the forward dynamics problem for slender structures.
- Lightweight and collaborative robots are highly load-sensitive, yet externally routed welding umbilical weight and stiffness are typically treated as negligible in robot dynamics.

## Method

- Umbilical discretized as a ==constrained multibody== chain of N+1 rigid bodies with N passive joints; joint torque model combines a diagonal stiffness matrix with ==Coulomb== sign-based and viscous friction terms.
- ==Holonomic constraints== at the distal anchor enforced at velocity level; an admissible-velocity mapping H splits dependent from independent coordinates, and ==H-projection== eliminates Lagrange multipliers from the reduced dynamics.
- ==Newton-Euler recursion== builds the inertia matrix and generalized forces; the reaction wrench is recovered by a ==Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse== of the constraint Jacobian transpose, then injected into robot inverse dynamics.

## Results

- Planar case study: **1.54 m** umbilical, **N=10** revolute joints of **0.14 m**, **2 kg/m** density, **5 N m/rad** joint stiffness, **0.1 N m s/rad** viscous friction, catenary-initialized.
- Coupled robot-umbilical simulation over a **0.3 m** radius circular trajectory in **5 s**, GMRES-solved and integrated by predictor-corrector at **0.5 ms**, shows clearly modified joint torques.
- Torque contributions remain non-negligible despite umbilical mass being more than an **order of magnitude** below the **36 kg** planar 3-R robot; increasing N left qualitative results unchanged.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Mass ratio is a poor proxy for dynamic relevance: a light attached cable can still dominate torque error on lightweight, load-sensitive collaborative arms.
> - Projecting constrained dynamics onto admissible velocities buys multiplier-free efficiency while still exposing the reaction wrench needed to couple two subsystems.
> - Initializing a discretized cable near its catenary static equilibrium suppresses artificial startup transients, a cheap trick for any lumped flexible-structure simulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic welding is a prevalent application in industrial manufacturing, valued for its consistency, repeatability, and productivity. A common setup involves a welding torch attached to a robot end-effector, connected to a generator via an external umbilical. Historically, the dynamic influence of this umbilical on robot motion has often been considered negligible. However, with the increasing adoption of lightweight and collaborative robots, which possess reduced mass and are consequently more sensitive to external loads, the dynamic effects of the umbilical become more pronounced. These effects, stemming from the umbilical's weight, bending stiffness, and torsional stiffness, can significantly alter the robot's dynamic response and the required actuation forces.

The accurate modeling of elongated flexible structures, such as welding umbilicals, is an established area of research within robotics and mechanics. Various approaches have been developed to address this challenge, each offering different trade-offs between fidelity and computational cost.
- **Continuous Formulations:** These methods, including finite element methods (FEM) and geometrically exact beam theories, provide high-fidelity representations of cable dynamics. While accurate, they typically lead to large-scale systems that are computationally demanding, limiting their applicability in real-time control or online integration scenarios.
- **Cosserat Rod Theory:** This framework models cables and slender structures as continuous rods with distributed strain variables. Cosserat models are expressive but often require implicit integration schemes and careful numerical treatment, particularly for solving forward dynamics problems.
- **Discretized Multibody or Lumped-Parameter Models:** This approach approximates the cable as a serial chain of rigid links interconnected by passive joints, which incorporate stiffness and damping properties. This strategy aims to balance physical realism with computational efficiency, making it suitable for applications requiring faster computation.
- **Hybrid or Data-Driven Approaches:** More recent developments include combinations of physics-based modeling with learning techniques to capture complex coupling effects between robots and heavy industrial cables.

This research aligns with the category of discretized multibody models. It specifically adopts a multibody discretization of the umbilical, representing it as a serial chain of rigid bodies interconnected by passive joints. The work addresses the challenge of integrating such a model into a robot's dynamic framework while maintaining computational efficiency. By using a projection-based approach to handle kinematic constraints, the paper aims to provide a formulation that is both physically meaningful and compatible with the real-time requirements of robotic control and analysis. This positions the work as an advancement in developing practical and efficient dynamic models for external flexible components in industrial robotics, particularly for systems where these components are no longer dynamically negligible.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a dynamic model for a welding torch umbilical that accurately captures its mechanical effects on a robot manipulator, while ensuring computational efficiency suitable for integration into robotic control and dynamic analysis systems.

The motivation behind this work stems from several factors:
- **Neglected Dynamic Influence:** In traditional industrial robotic welding, the umbilical's dynamic influence has often been overlooked or simplified due to its relatively small mass compared to heavy industrial robots. This assumption becomes increasingly problematic with the growing deployment of lightweight and collaborative robots, which are inherently more susceptible to external loads.
- **Impact on Robot Performance:** The weight, bending, and torsional stiffness of the umbilical introduce additional forces and moments that can significantly affect the robot's motion and the actuation forces required. Neglecting these effects can lead to inaccuracies in trajectory tracking, force estimation, and potentially compromise the stability of robot control.
- **Demand for Accurate Models:** Applications such as advanced robot control strategies, precise collision detection, and safe physical human-robot interaction necessitate a more accurate understanding of the forces acting on the robot. For these applications, an accurate model of the umbilical's dynamics is crucial.
- **Computational Efficiency Requirement:** While high-fidelity continuous models (e.g., FEM, Cosserat rods) exist for flexible structures, their computational expense often precludes their use in real-time or in-line applications. There is a specific need for models that offer a favorable trade-off between physical realism and computational efficiency to be compatible with typical robot control loop rates.
- **Explicit Reaction Force Recovery:** The objective includes not only modeling the umbilical's internal dynamics but also explicitly recovering the reaction wrench it exerts on the robot. This reaction force is vital for dynamically coupling the umbilical model with the robot's dynamic model, providing a more comprehensive system-level simulation.

In summary, the research is motivated by the industrial shift towards lighter and more collaborative robots in welding, which accentuates the dynamic influence of the welding umbilical. The aim is to bridge the gap between detailed but computationally intensive flexible body dynamics and simplified models, by proposing an efficient, physically-based modeling framework that accurately accounts for the umbilical's interaction with the robot.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves the development of a constrained multibody dynamic model for the welding umbilical, followed by a projection-based reduction method to achieve computational efficiency.

#### 3.1. Umbilical Kinematic and Dynamic Modeling

- **Discretization:** The umbilical is modeled as a serial multibody system comprising N+1 rigid bodies interconnected by N joints. The specific type and arrangement of joints are configured to reproduce desired beam kinematics. Bodies are indexed from a fixed base body (B0) to a distal body (BN).
- **Reference Frames:** An orthonormal frame (Fj) is attached to each body, with a unit vector (aj) defining the joint axis.
- **Generalized Coordinates:** The system's configuration is described by a vector of generalized coordinates, q = (q1..., qN)T, where qj represents the relative angle around joint j.
- **Kinematic Constraints:** The umbilical is assumed to be clamped at both ends. The base body (B0) is fixed relative to a world frame (Fw). The distal body (BN), which would be attached to a robotic manipulator, is subjected to prescribed motions. These motions are introduced as holonomic kinematic constraints, specifically at the velocity level, by enforcing a prescribed velocity twist VE ∈ R6. The constraint function is defined as Ψ(q, q̇, t) = A(q) q̇ - VE = 0, where A(q) is the kinematic Jacobian matrix.
- **Equations of Motion:** The forward dynamics problem aims to compute joint accelerations (q̈) and the reaction wrench (fN) at the distal anchor point given the system state (q, q̇). The equations of motion are expressed in an assembled Lagrangian form: M0(q) q̈ + Q0(q, q̇) - τ = ∇q̇Ψ(q, q̇, t)T λ. Here, M0 is the inertia matrix, Q0 encompasses Coriolis, centrifugal, and gravitational forces, τ represents joint torques, and λ contains Lagrange multipliers corresponding to constraint forces.
- **Joint Torques (Passive Joints):** The umbilical joints are passive. Joint torques (τ) arise from the umbilical's mechanical properties, specifically elastic stiffness and internal friction. The model for these torques is τ = -Kq - Fc sign(q̇) - Fv q̇, where K is the joint stiffness matrix, and Fc and Fv are diagonal matrices for Coulomb and viscous friction coefficients, respectively.
- **Newton–Euler Formalism:** The Newton–Euler formalism is employed to compute the dynamic matrices (M0 and Q0). This recursive approach ensures computational efficiency and scalability for multibody systems by systematically calculating link velocities, accelerations, and internal forces through forward and backward passes.

#### 3.2. Constraint Reduction and Reaction Force Recovery

- **Projection Method:** To enhance computational efficiency by eliminating Lagrange multipliers from the primary system of equations, the dynamic equations are projected onto the null space of the velocity constraints (Ψ=0). This corresponds to the subspace of admissible joint velocities.
- **Velocity Partitioning:** The generalized velocity vector is partitioned into dependent (constrained, q̇d) and independent (q̇i) components. The constraint Jacobian A is similarly decomposed.
- **Admissible Velocity Mapping:** Assuming the dependent Jacobian A_d is nonsingular, dependent joint velocities can be expressed in terms of independent velocities and the prescribed velocity twist. This leads to an admissible joint velocity vector in the compact form: q̇ = H [q̇i; VE], where H is the admissible velocity mapping matrix.
- **Reduced Dynamic System:** Differentiating the velocity mapping yields joint accelerations. Substituting this into the Lagrangian equations and pre-multiplying by HT projects the system onto the admissible velocity subspace. This eliminates the Lagrange multipliers explicitly. The resulting reduced dynamic system is: Mr [q̈i; V̇E] + Qr = 0, where Mr and Qr are the reduced inertia matrix and force vector, respectively.
- **Solving for Accelerations:** The reduced system is partitioned to solve for the unknown independent joint accelerations (q̈i). The full joint acceleration vector (q̈) is then reconstructed.
- **Reaction Wrench Recovery:** The Lagrange multipliers (λ), representing the generalized reaction wrench at the anchor point, are recovered using the equation: λ = (AT(q))+ (M0(q) q̈ + Q0(q, q̇) - τ), where (+) denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse. This provides the minimum-norm solution for the reaction forces transmitted to the robot.

#### 3.3. Planar Case Study

- **Umbilical Model:** A planar serial chain of N=10 revolute joints and N+1 rigid links of equal length (l=0.14m, total length=1.54m) is used, approximating a planar Kirchhoff–Love beam. Axial, compression, and shear effects are neglected.
- **Parameters:** Linear mass density of 2 kg m⁻¹, local joint bending stiffness of 5 N m rad⁻¹, and viscous friction of 0.1 N m s rad⁻¹.
- **Initialization:** The initial configuration is approximated by a catenary curve to minimize transient effects from gravity.
- **Robot Model:** The umbilical is attached to a planar 3-R robot with 0.4 m link lengths and a total mass of 36 kg. Inverse dynamics are used for robot modeling.
- **Coupled Simulation:** The robot's end-effector trajectory (circular, radius 0.3m, constant orientation, 5s duration) is prescribed. The corresponding end-effector twist and acceleration are fed as kinematic constraints to the umbilical model. The umbilical dynamics are solved to obtain the reaction wrench, which is then input to the robot's inverse dynamics to calculate additional joint torques.
- **Numerical Solvers:** A Krylov subspace method (GMRES) is used to solve the linear system for independent joint accelerations, avoiding explicit matrix inversion for potentially large and ill-conditioned systems. A predictor-corrector scheme with a fixed time step of 0.5 ms integrates the coupled system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the dynamic modeling of welding umbilicals and their interaction with robotic systems:

- **Feasibility of Multibody Discretization:** The study successfully demonstrates the application of a constrained multibody dynamic model to represent a welding umbilical. This approach effectively captures essential elastic stiffness and dissipative (friction) effects through passive joints, while remaining compatible with standard formalisms used in robot dynamics.
- **Efficient Constraint Handling:** The proposed projection-based method for handling holonomic kinematic constraints at the distal anchor point proves effective. By projecting the equations of motion onto the subspace of admissible velocities, the Lagrange multipliers are implicitly eliminated from the reduced system, allowing for the efficient computation of joint accelerations without solving a larger, coupled system including constraint forces.
- **Explicit Reaction Wrench Recovery:** Despite the reduction of the dynamic system, the formulation allows for the explicit recovery of the reaction wrench exerted by the umbilical on the robot. This capability is crucial for accurately integrating the umbilical's dynamic effects into the robot's dynamic model. The Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse is utilized to obtain a minimum-norm solution for these forces.
- **Significant Dynamic Impact:** The planar case study provided illustrative quantitative results. Even though the umbilical's mass (approximately 3 kg for a 1.54 m length with 2 kg/m density) was an order of magnitude smaller than the robot's total mass (36 kg), its dynamic influence on the robot's joint torques was demonstrably non-negligible. The simulated robot joint torques, when accounting for the umbilical dynamics, showed marked differences compared to simulations where the umbilical was ignored.
- **Modification of Robot Dynamic Response:** The comparison of robot joint torques with and without considering umbilical dynamics indicated that the umbilical can significantly modify the robot's dynamic response. This suggests that ignoring the umbilical's properties can lead to substantial errors in torque estimation and potentially impact control performance, particularly for lightweight robots executing dynamic trajectories.
- **Computational Suitability:** The methodology, including the use of recursive Newton-Euler formalism and a Krylov subspace method (GMRES) for solving the reduced linear system, supports the claim of computational efficiency. This aspect is important for the potential integration of the model into real-time robot control systems.

In summary, the research establishes a computationally efficient framework for modeling the dynamic effects of welding umbilicals. The findings highlight that the umbilical's influence on robot dynamics, particularly for lightweight systems, is considerable and warrants explicit consideration for accurate simulation, control, and interaction tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing a practical and increasingly relevant challenge in industrial robotics: the accurate dynamic modeling of external flexible components. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood in several key areas:

- **Enhanced Robot Control Performance:** For lightweight and collaborative robots, which are inherently more sensitive to external disturbances, accurately accounting for the umbilical's dynamic interaction is critical. This model can enable more precise trajectory tracking, improved disturbance rejection, and better overall control performance by providing a realistic estimate of external forces. This is particularly relevant for high-speed or high-acceleration welding paths where dynamic effects are more pronounced.
- **Improved Safety in Collaborative Robotics:** In collaborative robot applications, where humans and robots share a workspace, accurate force estimation is paramount for safety features like collision detection and reaction. The explicit recovery of the reaction wrench from the umbilical can lead to more reliable collision detection mechanisms and safer physical human-robot interaction by providing a more complete picture of the forces acting on the robot.
- **Realistic Simulation and Design:** The proposed dynamic model offers a tool for more realistic simulation of robotic welding systems. This can be invaluable during the design phase of new robot platforms or umbilical routing strategies, allowing engineers to predict and mitigate potential dynamic issues before physical prototyping. It can also aid in optimizing robot movements to minimize umbilical stress or robot energy consumption.
- **Foundation for Advanced Applications:** The computationally efficient nature of the model makes it suitable for integration into real-time applications. This opens avenues for advanced control strategies, such as force control, impedance control, or model-predictive control, where accurate and fast dynamic models are indispensable.
- **Parameter Identification and Validation:** The framework provides a structured approach for future experimental validation. The next logical step of identifying the umbilical's mechanical parameters (stiffness, damping, friction) through experimental data would further increase the model's fidelity and practical utility.
- **Extensibility to Complex Scenarios:** The methodology's basis in multibody dynamics and constrained systems provides a robust foundation for future extensions. These include modeling umbilicals with attachments at intermediate robot links, handling mobile or sliding anchor points along the robot structure, or even incorporating more complex material behaviors. Such advancements would further broaden the applicability of the model to diverse industrial configurations.

In conclusion, this research provides a crucial step towards more accurate, robust, and safe robotic welding operations, especially with the ongoing shift towards lightweight and collaborative robotic platforms. By addressing a previously underestimated dynamic influence, the work facilitates the development of more sophisticated control systems and more reliable robotic applications in industrial settings.
