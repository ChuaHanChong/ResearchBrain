# Mixture-of-Experts RL for Fault-Tolerant Legged Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> A fault-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) reinforcement learning framework enables legged robots to achieve robust locomotion despite actuator failures by explicitly activating specialized control policies based on fault diagnosis. This architecture demonstrates improved locomotion performance across various fault conditions compared to monolithic policies, shows greater resilience to reduced network capacity, and reduces active inference computational costs.

## Problem

- Legged robots operating in remote and challenging environments face risks of hardware degradation and actuator failures, necessitating autonomous adaptation capabilities to maintain mobility.
- Existing monolithic reinforcement learning policies struggle to efficiently encode diverse and heterogeneous control strategies required for nominal locomotion and a growing number of fault conditions within a single network.
- Traditional Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, while capable of multimodal control, often incur computational overhead due to learned gating networks that infer operating modes or combine expert outputs.

## Method

- A ==fault-aware Mixture-of-Experts== (MoE) policy is developed, consisting of a shared encoder and specialized experts, each tailored to a specific actuator failure condition, generating joint-space locomotion commands.
- Expert selection is performed via a 'one-hot selection mechanism' that directly activates the appropriate expert using explicit fault diagnosis information, eliminating the need for a learned routing network.
- The control pipeline integrates a fault-detection module, trained to identify actuator failures from recent state history, and a state-estimation module for robust base velocity estimation, even under severe failures.

## Results

- The proposed MoE architecture consistently achieved higher cumulative episodic rewards across all tested fault conditions (No Fail, LF Fail, LH Fail, LH+RH Fail) compared to a monolithic PPO baseline, with performance advantages increasing with fault severity.
- The MoE architecture demonstrated greater resilience to network capacity reduction; its 'Small' variant (32x32x32) achieved performance levels comparable to or surpassing the 'Large' monolithic baseline (128x128x128) across fault scenarios.
- Experimental validation on a physical Unitree Go2 robot confirmed the practical viability of the framework, demonstrating successful three-legged locomotion on rocky terrain with a left-hind leg failure and body-dragging adaptation with simultaneous hind-leg failures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly conditioning expert policies on fault diagnosis information enables highly specialized and effective fault recovery strategies, particularly for heterogeneous locomotion behaviors required by severe failures.
> - The structured specialization within the MoE framework allows for significantly reduced individual expert network capacities while maintaining or even surpassing the performance of larger monolithic policies, critical for compute-constrained platforms.
> - Directly leveraging existing fault diagnosis information for expert selection simplifies the MoE architecture, reducing active inference computational complexity compared to learned gating mechanisms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged robots are being developed for applications in challenging and remote environments, such as planetary exploration, industrial inspection, and autonomous operations in unstructured settings. Their inherent mobility advantages over wheeled systems in traversing rough terrain and obstacles make them candidates for long-duration missions. However, extended deployments in these harsh environments expose robotic platforms to hardware degradation and actuator failures, which can severely compromise their ability to locomote reliably. Autonomous adaptation to such failures is crucial, as physical intervention is often impractical or impossible.

Recent advancements in legged locomotion control, particularly those leveraging reinforcement learning (RL), have enabled robots to achieve robust performance across various terrains. These RL-based approaches frequently employ monolithic neural policies. A limitation of these monolithic policies is their requirement to simultaneously encode multiple complex objectives within a single network. When faced with diverse and potentially competing objectives, such as handling various terrain conditions, performing different gaits, and adapting to multiple failure modes, these policies can struggle to efficiently represent the distinct control strategies needed for each scenario. This can lead to representational interference and diminished control performance.

The concept of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures has been explored to address multimodal control problems. In MoE, multiple specialized policies (experts) are coordinated by a routing mechanism, allowing each expert to specialize in a particular aspect of the task. Previous work has demonstrated the utility of MoE for multimodal locomotion, such as enabling quadrupeds to execute different gaits or adapt to diverse terrain conditions. However, these traditional MoE approaches typically involve learning the routing function, which introduces additional computational overhead and requires the network to infer the robot's operating mode.

This research positions itself within this landscape by addressing the challenge of fault-tolerant legged locomotion using a modular RL approach. It builds upon the strengths of MoE architectures but distinguishes itself by explicitly leveraging pre-existing fault diagnosis information. Instead of learning a routing mechanism, the proposed framework directly uses detected actuator failure modes to activate specialized control experts. This approach aims to reduce the computational overhead associated with learned routing, simplify the architecture, and enhance the efficiency and performance of fault adaptation, particularly for computationally constrained robotic platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient control architecture for legged robots that can maintain reliable locomotion performance even in the presence of actuator failures. The motivation stems from several key observations and challenges in the field of autonomous robotics:

**Motivation:**

1. **Demand for Robustness in Remote Environments:** Legged robots are increasingly deployed in remote, unstructured, and hazardous environments, including space exploration. In such settings, hardware degradation and actuator failures are highly probable over long mission durations. The inability to autonomously adapt to these failures can lead to mission failure, as human intervention for repairs is often infeasible. Therefore, developing robots that can autonomously adapt their locomotion despite partial hardware failures is a critical requirement.
2. **Limitations of Monolithic RL Policies:** While reinforcement learning has proven effective for achieving robust and agile legged locomotion, most existing methods rely on monolithic neural policies. These single networks must implicitly learn to manage a wide spectrum of behaviors, including terrain perception, nominal locomotion, and various fault recovery strategies. The control strategies required for different fault conditions (e.g., a single-leg failure versus a double-leg failure) can be fundamentally heterogeneous, requiring distinct compensatory actions such as gait modification, load redistribution, or even body-dragging maneuvers. Forcing a monolithic policy to encode all these diverse behaviors within a single set of parameters can lead to representational interference, reduce learning efficiency, and ultimately limit the robot's overall performance and adaptability.
3. **Inefficiencies of Learned Routing in MoE:** Mixture-of-Experts architectures offer a solution for multimodal control by allowing specialized policies. However, conventional MoE designs often require a gating network to learn how to route observations to the appropriate experts. This learned routing introduces additional computational complexity and relies on the network inferring the operational mode, which can be challenging and computationally intensive during deployment.
4. **Opportunity from Existing Fault Diagnosis:** In many robotic systems, actuator faults can be detected by onboard health-monitoring systems, dedicated diagnostic modules, or analysis of historical state signals. This readily available, explicit fault information represents an underutilized resource that can directly inform the control architecture, potentially simplifying the expert selection process and improving efficiency.

### 3. Key Objectives

Based on these motivations, the authors established the following key objectives for their work:

1. **Propose a Fault-Aware Modular Control Architecture:** To design and implement a reinforcement learning framework that explicitly leverages fault-diagnosis information to activate specialized control experts. This architecture aims to enable conditional computation, where only the relevant expert for a given fault condition is active, thereby reducing inference cost.
2. **Demonstrate Performance Superiority over Monolithic Policies:** To empirically evaluate whether explicit fault-conditioned modular policies can consistently outperform monolithic policies of comparable size in terms of locomotion performance across various actuator failure scenarios, particularly as fault severity increases.
3. **Assess Suitability for Compute-Constrained Platforms:** To investigate the impact of reduced network capacity on the performance of the proposed modular architecture compared to monolithic baselines. The goal is to demonstrate that the structured specialization offered by the modular design can maintain competitive performance even under significant computational constraints, thereby highlighting its applicability for resource-limited robotic systems like those used in space exploration.
4. **Provide a Reproducible Baseline:** To release the associated code to foster further research and provide a standardized benchmark for fault-tolerant legged locomotion.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates terrain perception, proprioceptive state estimation, and fault-aware control within a modular reinforcement learning architecture. It contrasts with conventional monolithic policies by explicitly using estimated fault information to activate specialized policy experts. The overall control pipeline comprises three primary components: a fault-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) policy, a state-estimation module, and a fault-detection module.

**A. Fault-Aware Mixture of Experts (MoE) Policy:**

The core of the control framework is the MoE policy, designed to handle the heterogeneous dynamics introduced by actuator failures.
- **Observation Input:** The policy receives proprioceptive information, $o_p = (v, \omega, \bar{g}, q, \dot{q})$, where $v$ and $\omega$ are base linear and angular velocities, $\bar{g}$ is the projected gravity vector, and $q, \dot{q}$ are joint positions and velocities. These are stacked over a temporal horizon $H$. Additionally, visual terrain information (heightmap $h$) and desired linear ($v_{des}$) and angular ($\omega_{des}$) velocities are included, forming the final observation $o = (o_{prop}, o_{terrain}, v_{des}, \omega_{des})$.
- **Architecture:** Both the actor and critic networks share a common architectural structure. A shared encoder, $z(o)$, processes the observation vector $o$ to extract a compact latent representation $o_{lat}$. This latent representation is then fed into a collection of $K$ specialized experts, $\pi_1, \pi_2, \ldots, \pi_K$, each dedicated to a specific fault condition.
- **Expert Selection:** Unlike traditional MoE architectures that use a learned gating network, this framework employs a direct, one-hot selection mechanism. The diagnosed failure mode $k$, provided by the fault-detection module, directly activates the corresponding expert. This eliminates the need for a learned routing function, simplifying the architecture.
- **Computational Efficiency:** This explicit selection provides an advantage in inference complexity. Only the shared encoder ($C_{enc}$) and one selected expert ($C_k$) are active at runtime, resulting in an active computational cost of $C_{active} = C_{enc} + C_k$. This is a reduction compared to dense-routing MoE where all experts contribute ($C_{dense} = C_{enc} + \sum_{k=1}^K C_k$).
- **Critic Network:** The critic network shares the same observation structure as the actor but is augmented with privileged information, such as PD gains, contact states, and swing/stance timings of the robot legs, to aid learning.

**B. State Estimation Module:**

Traditional model-based state estimators rely on assumptions about contact configurations and robot dynamics, which can break down under severe actuator failures (e.g., body dragging).
- **Approach:** A learning-based state-estimation approach is adopted. A neural network, $f_{est}$, is trained to estimate the robot's base linear velocity, $\hat{v}$, directly from sensor observations. The input $\bar{o}$ is similar to the policy observation but uses IMU linear acceleration instead of linear velocity.
- **Training:** The estimator is trained in simulation using ground-truth velocity measurements as supervision. It is optimized using a mean-squared-error (MSE) objective: $L_{est} = \|v - \hat{v}\|_2^2$. This method avoids explicit contact modeling, accommodating changes in robot dynamics due to failures.

**C. Fault Detection Module:**

A key component of the fault-aware MoE is the availability of information regarding the current actuator failure condition.
- **Approach:** Actuator fault identification is formulated as a supervised learning problem. A neural network, $f_{fault}$, is trained to predict the current fault condition, $\hat{f}$, from the same observation history, $\bar{o}$, used by the state estimation network. Using a temporal observation window is crucial for reliable fault identification.
- **Output:** The network outputs a categorical distribution over the predefined set of fault conditions, which is then used to determine which locomotion expert to activate.
- **Training:** During training, direct supervision from fault labels available in simulation is used. The network is optimized with a standard cross-entropy loss: $L_{fault} = - \frac{1}{N_f} \sum_{i=1}^{N_f} [f_i \log(\hat{f}_i) + (1 - f_i) \log(1 - \hat{f}_i)]$. This module is trained concurrently with the locomotion policy.

**D. Training and Evaluation Details:**

- **Simulation Environment:** Training and evaluation were conducted using the IsaacLab framework on a simulated Unitree Go2 quadruped robot.
- **Reward Function:** A comprehensive reward function was designed (detailed in Table I of the paper), combining task-oriented objectives (e.g., velocity tracking, terrain-aware pose adaptation, base height, orientation) with regularization terms (e.g., joint torque, acceleration, power, action rate/smoothness, feet air time, feet slide, feet clearance).
- **Fault-Specific Rewards:** Critically, some reward terms are activated or weighted differently based on the number of failed legs ($n_f$). For instance, base height and orientation objectives are emphasized when sufficient support is available but disabled for two-leg failures. For single-leg failures, the policy is encouraged to shift the center of mass towards the healthy support region. This adaptive reward structure facilitates the learning of diverse fault-recovery behaviors.
- **Evaluated Fault Conditions:** Four representative failure conditions were studied: "No Fail" (nominal), "LF Fail" (left-front leg failure), "LH Fail" (left-hind leg failure), and "LH+RH Fail" (simultaneous failure of both hind legs). These were selected to represent increasing degradation levels and exploit quadrupedal locomotion symmetries, reducing experimental burden.
- **Comparison Methodology:** The proposed fault-aware MoE policy was compared against a monolithic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) baseline.
- **Metrics:** Performance was assessed based on cumulative episodic reward and the degradation of performance under reduced policy network capacity.
- **Network Capacity Experiment:** The actor network capacity was varied across "Large", "Medium", and "Small" configurations, while the critic's capacity was kept constant (Large).
- **Real-World Validation:** Experiments were conducted on a physical Unitree Go2 robot to demonstrate real-world applicability, involving traversing rocky terrain under nominal conditions and flat ground/rocky terrain under specific leg failure scenarios.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research provides quantitative and qualitative results demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed fault-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture in simulation and on a physical robot.

**A. Simulation Results:**

1. **Locomotion Performance across Fault Scenarios:**
 - **Overall Superiority:** The proposed MoE architecture consistently achieved higher locomotion performance than the monolithic PPO baseline across all evaluated fault conditions.
 - **Nominal Performance:** In the "No Fail" scenario, both the MoE and monolithic approaches exhibited comparable locomotion performance, suggesting that expert specialization does not negatively impact the robot's ability to perform standard locomotion.
 - **Increasing Fault Severity:** As the severity of the fault increased, the performance advantage of the MoE architecture became progressively more pronounced.
 - **Single-Leg Failures (LF Fail, LH Fail):** The MoE policy achieved higher cumulative rewards and showed a more concentrated reward distribution compared to the monolithic baseline. This indicates that explicit fault-conditioned specialization enabled the controller to learn more effective and consistent compensation strategies for single-leg failures.
 - **Double-Leg Failure (LH+RH Fail):** The most significant performance improvement was observed in the "LH+RH Fail" condition, where both hind legs were disabled, requiring substantially different locomotion strategies, often involving body-dragging behaviors. In this severely degraded regime, the MoE architecture maintained substantially higher rewards than the monolithic policy, underscoring the benefit of associating dedicated experts with specific fault conditions when the required locomotion strategies are highly heterogeneous.

2. **Sensitivity to Network Capacity:**
 - **Performance Degradation with Size Reduction:** For both the MoE and monolithic approaches, reducing the actor network capacity (from Large to Medium to Small) led to a degradation in locomotion performance, as anticipated.
 - **MoE's Robustness to Capacity Reduction:** The proposed MoE architecture was less affected by reductions in network capacity compared to the monolithic PPO baseline. Specifically, the "Small" variant of the MoE policy achieved performance levels comparable to those of the "Large" monolithic baseline. This behavior was consistent across all fault conditions and was particularly evident in the challenging "LH+RH Fail" scenario.
 - **Implication of Structured Specialization:** These results suggest that the MoE architecture, through its expert structure, can effectively preserve fault-specific locomotion skills even when the capacity of individual expert networks is reduced. This is attributed to the MoE's ability to exploit a larger overall parameterization while ensuring that each expert specializes in a distinct behavior, whereas the monolithic policy must encode all behaviors within a single, shrinking parameter set.

**B. Experimental Validation on Real Hardware:**

- **Real-World Applicability:** Experiments were conducted on a physical Unitree Go2 robot to demonstrate the practical applicability of the proposed approach.
- **Demonstrated Scenarios:** The robot successfully performed:
 - Nominal locomotion on rocky terrain ("No Fail" scenario).
 - Locomotion on flat ground with both hind legs deactivated ("LH+RH Fail" scenario), relying on its forelegs.
 - Traversal of rocky terrain with a single left-hind leg failure ("LH Fail" scenario), operating in a three-legged configuration.
- **Qualitative Confirmation:** The successful execution of these diverse fault conditions on a real robot qualitatively supports the effectiveness and robustness of the fault-aware MoE framework in real-world settings.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic locomotion control, particularly in enhancing the robustness and adaptability of legged robots in challenging and unpredictable environments. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Fault Tolerance for Legged Robots:** The proposed fault-aware Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) framework significantly advances the state of fault-tolerant locomotion for quadrupedal robots. By explicitly leveraging fault-diagnosis information to activate specialized control experts, the architecture effectively addresses the challenge of managing diverse and heterogeneous control strategies required by different actuator failure modes. This leads to higher and more consistent locomotion performance, especially under severe fault conditions where monolithic policies struggle.
2. **Increased Autonomy and Reliability for Remote Operations:** For applications like planetary exploration or autonomous inspection in hazardous environments, the ability of a robot to autonomously adapt to hardware failures is critical. This work provides a method for robots to maintain functionality despite degradation, reducing reliance on remote intervention and enhancing mission reliability and success rates in long-duration deployments.
3. **Computational Efficiency for Resource-Constrained Platforms:** A key advantage of the proposed approach is its reduced active inference cost. By directly selecting a single expert based on diagnosed fault conditions, the framework avoids the computational overhead of learning a routing function or executing multiple experts simultaneously. This makes the architecture particularly suitable for platforms with limited onboard computational resources, such as space robotics, where processing power and energy consumption are significant design constraints. The demonstration that the MoE architecture maintains competitive performance even with significantly reduced network capacity further underscores this benefit.
4. **Foundation for Advanced Modular Control:** This work demonstrates a successful paradigm for integrating explicit state information (fault diagnosis) into a modular reinforcement learning architecture. This approach could be extended to other forms of multimodal control where environmental states or robot conditions can be reliably sensed or inferred, offering a generalizable strategy for designing more adaptable and robust robotic systems.
5. **Reproducibility and Future Research:** The release of the associated code provides a valuable resource for the robotics and reinforcement learning communities. It establishes a reproducible baseline for fault-tolerant legged locomotion, facilitating comparative studies and serving as a foundation for future research into more sophisticated fault-detection mechanisms, parameter-sharing strategies among experts, and scaling to a broader range of failure conditions.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite these contributions, the paper acknowledges a limitation regarding sample efficiency during training. Since individual expert networks do not share all parameters, each expert is updated only with trajectories from agents experiencing its corresponding fault condition. This effectively reduces the number of samples available for policy optimization, leading to noisier policy-gradient estimates compared to monolithic baselines. While this was mitigated by using a large number of parallel simulation environments (over 16,384 agents), scaling the architecture to a very large number of fault conditions might necessitate more sophisticated parameter-sharing strategies or more sample-efficient reinforcement learning algorithms to maintain training feasibility.
