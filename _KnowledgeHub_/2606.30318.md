# Chronos: A Physics-Informed Full-History Framework for Non-Markovian Long-Horizon Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Chronos is a framework for long-horizon robotic manipulation that addresses non-Markovian tasks by representing the entire observation history as an intrinsic latent state. It employs a physics-informed second-order action generation mechanism for smoother, physically consistent robot actions, achieving significant performance gains on memory-dependent benchmarks and competitive results on general manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Observation aliasing in long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks, where identical visual inputs necessitate different actions based on the robot's prior history or task phase.
- Limitations of current Markovian policies, including large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, in handling memory-dependent (non-Markovian) scenarios due to their inability to retain and utilize long-range temporal context.
- Existing generative imitation learning methods often predict first-order actions or noise scores, potentially leading to less smooth or physically inconsistent robot motion in precision tasks.

## Method

- **Chronos** models the robot's full observation history as an intrinsic latent state using a ==selective state space model (SSM)== and ==modality-agnostic state tokenization==, enabling continuous tracking of hidden task phases.
- The framework employs a ==physics-informed second-order generative action bridge==, inspired by ==Schrödinger dynamics==, that explicitly predicts ==acceleration fields== for action refinement, enhancing action smoothness and physical consistency.
- It integrates ==Implicit Maximum Likelihood Estimation (IMLE)== for generating coarse multimodal action priors, which are then refined by the acceleration bridge using a novel ==quartic bell noise schedule== tailored for second-order dynamics.

## Results

- Chronos achieved an average success rate of **73.6%** on the RMBench memory-dependent tasks, outperforming the Markovian π0.5 baseline by **+62.4 absolute percentage points** and the memory-VLA Mem-0 by **+22.8 points** with significantly fewer parameters.
- On the ALOHA bimanual insertion task, Chronos reached **90%** success, surpassing variants using diffusion (**66%**), flow matching (**72%**), and regression (**50-76%**) action heads, largely due to its second-order action bridge and quartic noise schedule.
- In real-world dual-arm experiments, Chronos achieved **72%** average success on memory-dependent tasks where π0.5 completely failed (**0%**), and qualitative observations indicated it produced smoother, more continuous robot trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating the full observation history as the policy's intrinsic latent state, rather than auxiliary context, is essential for effectively resolving observation aliasing and enabling robust non-Markovian control.
> - A physics-informed second-order action generation mechanism, predicting acceleration fields and using a carefully designed quartic bell noise schedule, can produce smoother and more precise robot trajectories than first-order generative methods.
> - End-to-end backpropagation through a full-trajectory state space model facilitates long-range temporal credit assignment, allowing late-stage imitation losses to refine early-stage historical representations for complex, phase-dependent behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is experiencing significant advancements driven by large-scale visuomotor foundation models, including vision-language-action (VLA) systems and generative imitation learning policies. Models such as OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, RDT-1B, and X-VLA have demonstrated capabilities in various manipulation tasks by leveraging extensive visual, linguistic, and robotic datasets. These systems often operate by conditioning actions on current observations or short historical windows, an approach commonly referred to as Markovian or short-horizon conditioning.

However, a fundamental challenge in robotic control, particularly for long-horizon tasks, is the issue of *observation aliasing*. This occurs when identical visual or proprioceptive observations can necessitate different actions depending on the robot's prior history or the current task phase. For example, the visual scene might appear identical before and after a critical event, yet the required subsequent action changes. Existing Markovian policies, even large-scale VLA models, struggle with such memory-dependent (non-Markovian) scenarios because they lack the capacity to retain and utilize long-range temporal context. While some approaches extend observation windows, these are often insufficient if the decisive event occurred further in the past than the window's length.

A growing area of research addresses this memory-dependent manipulation, with benchmarks like RMBench, RoboMME, MIKASA-Robo, LIBERO-Mem, and RoboMemArena specifically designed to evaluate policies requiring robust historical understanding. Correspondingly, various memory-augmented policies have emerged, such as MemoryVLA, ReMem-VLA, MemoAct, and SAM2Act, which integrate explicit memory modules or hierarchical memory designs. Previous work like MTIL explored full-history imitation learning with state-space memory, but sometimes relied on detached hidden states, limiting full end-to-end temporal credit assignment. Chronos aims to advance this by proposing that history should not be an auxiliary context, but rather the intrinsic latent state of the control policy itself, allowing for continuous evolution of task-phase information and full-trajectory credit assignment during training.

In parallel, generative imitation learning methods, including Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), Diffusion Policy (DP), and Flow Matching (FM), have been developed to handle the multimodal nature of expert demonstrations. These policies learn to predict action chunks, denoise actions, or transport actions via velocity fields. While effective, many of these primarily model direct actions or first-order velocities, potentially leading to issues with action smoothness or physical grounding. Chronos distinguishes itself by employing a second-order generative paradigm, leveraging concepts from Schrödinger dynamics to model action refinement through acceleration fields, thus aiming for smoother and more physically consistent robot motion compared to existing first-order methods.

This work positions itself at the intersection of long-horizon memory and physics-informed action generation, seeking to overcome the limitations of current large-scale policies in non-Markovian settings and enhance action quality beyond first-order generative approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind Chronos is to address a critical limitation in current robotic manipulation policies, particularly those based on vision-language-action (VLA) models and generative imitation learning. This limitation arises from the assumption that the present observation is a sufficient statistic for determining the next action. In many real-world long-horizon tasks, this assumption, termed the Markovian shortcut, does not hold. The authors identify the core problem as **observation aliasing**, where visually identical scenes can demand entirely different robot actions due to variations in the robot's operational history or the implicit task phase. For instance, a robot might encounter a cup in the same physical location at two different points in a task—once before removing it for inspection, and again after returning it. A policy solely relying on the current visual frame would receive identical input in both scenarios, but the correct action (e.g., "pick up" versus "release") would be fundamentally different based on the task's history.

To overcome this, the paper sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Elevate History to Latent State:** The central objective is to model robot policies as dynamical systems where the *entire observation history* is not merely an auxiliary context or a short window of past frames, but is explicitly represented as the latent state of the policy dynamics. This aims to allow the policy to continuously track and reconstruct the hidden task phase, enabling memory-dependent decision-making.

2. **Ensure Full Temporal Credit Assignment:** A specific goal is to design a memory mechanism that processes the full trajectory, assigning one state-representative token per physical control step. This design, coupled with end-to-end backpropagation through the entire historical sequence, is intended to enable late-stage imitation losses to influence and refine early-stage historical representations, thereby facilitating long-range temporal credit assignment for learning complex, phase-dependent behaviors.

3. **Develop a Physics-Informed Second-Order Action Generation Mechanism:** A secondary but crucial objective is to improve the physical realism and smoothness of robot actions. Existing generative policies often predict actions directly, noise scores, or first-order velocities. The authors aim to introduce a novel approach that explicitly models action generation through *acceleration fields*. This is motivated by the idea that acceleration is the quantity that mediates smooth changes in velocity and displacement, and by incorporating a "Schrödinger-inspired bridge," the policy can generate smoother, more physically grounded robot motions, particularly beneficial for precision tasks.

4. **Validate on Diverse and Challenging Tasks:** The research seeks to rigorously evaluate the proposed framework across a range of tasks. This includes:
 - **Precision Insertion:** To test the fine-grained control and smoothness of the action generation module.
 - **General Manipulation:** To assess the overall competence of the policy in visually complex and variable environments.
 - **Memory-Dependent Long-Horizon Control:** To specifically demonstrate the framework's ability to handle observation aliasing and non-Markovian task structures, contrasting its performance against both Markovian and explicit-memory baselines.

In essence, the motivation is to develop a robot policy that not only "remembers" its past to act intelligently in ambiguous situations but also moves physically realistically and precisely, overcoming limitations of both state-of-the-art memory architectures and generative action models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Chronos framework integrates full-history state representation with a physics-informed second-order action generation mechanism. The methodology is structured around three main components: state tokenization, causal state-space memory, and a Schrödinger-inspired action bridge.

**A. Full-History State Tokenization and Causal State-Space Memory**

The first principle involves representing the robot's history in a structured, temporal manner.
At each physical control step *t*, the robot's exteroceptive observation ($o_t$) and proprioceptive state ($p_t$) are fused into a single **state-representative token** ($x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$) using a modality-agnostic encoder $\phi_\eta$. This ensures that the token sequence ($X_{1:L} = (x_1, \dots, x_L)$) is directly aligned with physical time, where *L* is the full trajectory length.

- **Modality-Agnostic State Token Extraction:**
 - For **point-cloud observations**, a shared point encoder maps individual points to local features, which are then pooled into a global geometric descriptor ($g^{pc}_t$). Proprioception is encoded ($g^{prop}_t$), and both are fused and normalized to form $x_t$.
 - For **image observations**, a visual encoder (e.g., DINOv2, ResNet18) extracts dense features, which are compressed by a lightweight adapter into a global visual descriptor ($g^{img}_t$). This is then fused with proprioception and normalized to produce $x_t$. This design allows Chronos to maintain a consistent temporal interface (one state token per physical step) regardless of the sensing modality.

- **Causal Full-History Selective State-Space Encoding:**
 The generated sequence of state tokens ($X_{1:L}$) is fed into a **selective state space model (SSM)**, implemented using Mamba-style linear-time recurrence. This SSM, denoted $S_\theta$, propagates an internal recurrent state ($h_t$) and emits a causal historical context ($y_t$) at each time step. The SSM's parameters (A, B, C matrices) are dynamically determined by the current input token $x_t$, allowing for input-dependent transitions. Crucially, the policy is causal during deployment, meaning $h_t$ and $y_t$ depend only on observations up to time *t*. During training, losses from all time steps are backpropagated through the *complete* SSM computation graph (full-trajectory BPTT), which is designed to enable long-range temporal credit assignment, allowing late-stage errors to influence early-stage memory formation.

- **Condition Construction and Physical Anchoring:**
 The historical context ($y_t$) and the current state token ($x_t$) are combined using a lightweight fusion network ($\Gamma_\omega$) to form an action-generation condition ($c_t$). This condition leverages $y_t$ for task-phase memory and $x_t$ for current geometric, visual, and proprioceptive evidence. The current state token $x_t$ also serves as a *physical anchor* for the second-order acceleration refinement process.

**B. Physics-Informed Second-Order Action Bridge**

Chronos's action generation is a two-stage process: first, a coarse multimodal prior is generated, then it is refined by a second-order bridge inspired by Schrödinger dynamics.

- **Coarse Multimodal Action Prior via IMLE:**
 Robotic tasks often have multiple valid action sequences (multimodality) for a given state. Direct regression often averages these modes. To address this, Chronos employs **Implicit Maximum Likelihood Estimation (IMLE)**. A conditional implicit generator ($G_\phi$) samples a coarse multimodal action chunk ($q_0$) from a latent noise sample ($z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$) and the condition ($c_t$). During training, *K* latent samples are generated, and the one closest to the expert action chunk ($q^\star_t$) is selected for the IMLE loss. This prevents mode averaging by allowing different latent regions to correspond to different valid action modes. The selected $q_0$ then serves as the starting point for refinement.

- **Second-Order Schrödinger-inspired Bridge:**
 The core of action refinement is a novel second-order bridge. Instead of directly predicting actions or velocities, this module predicts an acceleration field.
 - **Derivation from Schrödinger Equation:** The derivation begins with the Schrödinger equation, applies the Madelung transformation, and extracts continuity and quantum Hamilton-Jacobi equations. This process introduces a "quantum potential" ($Q(q,s)$), whose gradient corresponds to a "quantum force." This motivates the use of acceleration.
 - **Newtonian Projection:** By taking the gradient of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, a Newtonian-like equation emerges: $ma = F_{classic} + F_{quantum}$, where $F_{quantum} = -\nabla Q$. This provides a theoretical basis for predicting acceleration.
 - **Cubic Reference Bridge:** To provide a stable reference path, a cubic Hermite clock reparameterization ($\alpha(s) = 3s^2 - 2s^3$) is used to construct a reference action chunk trajectory ($q_{ref}(s)$) between the coarse IMLE prior ($q_0$) and the expert action ($q^\star_t$). This reparameterization ensures zero velocity at both bridge endpoints (s=0, s=1), which is compatible with second-order dynamics. The acceleration derived from this cubic path ($a_{spline}(s)$) forms the classical force term.
 - **Gaussian Quantum Control Projection and Kostin Dissipation:** The quantum potential term is projected to a position-stabilizing force ($F_q(q,s) = K_p (q_{ref}(s) - q)$). Inspired by Kostin's dissipative Schrödinger equation, a velocity-dependent friction term is introduced, leading to a velocity-stabilizing force ($F_v(v,s) = K_d (v_{ref}(s) - v)$).
 - **Target Acceleration:** These components combine to form a target acceleration ($a_{tar}(q,v,s)$) that acts as a generalized second-order stabilizer around the cubic reference path, ensuring both position and velocity tracking.
 - **Second-Order Noise Schedule:** A critical aspect for training this bridge is the **quartic bell schedule** ($\sigma(s) = 16\sigma_{max}s^2(1-s)^2$) used to inject noise ($q_s = q_{ref}(s) + \sigma(s)\epsilon$, $v_s = v_{ref}(s) + \dot{\sigma}(s)\epsilon$). This schedule ensures that both the position and *velocity* perturbations vanish at the bridge endpoints (s=0, s=1). This is distinct from first-order diffusion schedules, which may have non-zero endpoint velocity derivatives, potentially introducing artificial boundary impulses that destabilize the acceleration-learning process.

**C. Memory-Efficient Training and Causal Inference**

- **Memory-Efficient Training:** To manage the memory demands of high-dimensional perception across long trajectories, Chronos separates perception from temporal learning. Observations are processed in chunks (size C) to generate state tokens ($X_b$). The full token sequence is then assembled and passed through the SSM. If visual backbones are frozen, their activations are not retained for full-trajectory backpropagation, significantly reducing memory. Gradients are preserved through trainable adapters, fusion layers, SSM, and action generation modules. For trainable point-cloud encoders, gradient checkpointing can be used.
- **Training Objective:** The total loss is a combination of the IMLE loss ($L_{IMLE}$), an acceleration matching loss ($L_{acc}$) for the second-order bridge, and a behavior cloning loss ($L_{BC}$) on the final action chunk.
- **Causal Inference:** During deployment, a temporally correlated latent state for IMLE ($z_t, \dot{z}_t$) is maintained to ensure smooth transitions in the coarse action prior. The SSM updates its internal state incrementally. The IMLE generator provides a coarse action prior ($q_0$), which is then refined by integrating the learned acceleration field for *N* steps using a semi-implicit Euler update, resulting in the final action chunk ($\hat{q}_t$).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Chronos was evaluated across 16 simulated tasks and 4 real-world experiments, covering precision insertion, general manipulation, and memory-dependent long-horizon control.

**A. RMBench (Memory-Dependent Manipulation)**
On RMBench, a benchmark specifically designed for memory-dependent tasks where success requires remembering the task phase, Chronos demonstrated substantial improvements:
- **Overall Performance:** Achieved a 73.6% average success rate.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** This significantly outperformed the Markovian VLA baseline $\pi_{0.5}$ by +62.4 absolute percentage points (a 6.6$\times$ relative gain), while using 10$\times$ fewer parameters. It also surpassed Mem-0, a state-of-the-art memory-VLA policy, by +22.8 points, with over 30$\times$ fewer parameters.
- **Task-Specific Results:**
 - High success rates (90-99%) were achieved on `Rearrange Blocks`, `Put Back Block`, `Swap Blocks`, `Swap T`, and `Cover Blocks`, tasks characterized by observation aliasing where historical context is critical.
 - `Battery Try` showed modest improvement (29%) over baselines, but failures were attributed to physical brittleness and precision issues rather than memory.
 - `Press Button` remained very challenging (2% success), indicating limitations in visual digit recognition rather than memory itself.
- **Representation Visualization:** PCA trajectories of the instantaneous observation token ($x_t$) and the historical context ($y_t$) showed that while $x_t$ could be visually similar across different task phases, $y_t$ distinctly separated these phases, indicating that the SSM effectively captured and evolved task-phase information.

**B. RoboTwin 2.0 (General Manipulation)**
On RoboTwin 2.0, a suite of bimanual general manipulation tasks with randomized object placements and scene configurations, Chronos demonstrated competitive performance even when memory was not the primary bottleneck:
- **Overall Performance:** Achieved the best average score of 70.0% across eight tasks.
- **Task-Specific Highlights:** Led in seven out of eight tasks, including 95% on `Place Cans Plasticbox`, 78% on `Beat Block Hammer`, and 86% on `Move Can Pot`.
- **Comparison:** Outperformed DP3 (59.5%), $\pi_0$ (48.8%), RDT-1B (35.8%), DP (35.1%), and ACT (30.6%).
- **Limitations:** Underperformed DP3 on `Put Bottles Dustbin`, a task heavily reliant on local geometry and precise grasp/placement, suggesting that compact Markovian diffusion policies with strong local geometric biases can sometimes excel in fully observable settings.

**C. ALOHA Bimanual Insertion (Fine-Grained Action Generation)**
On the ALOHA bimanual insertion task, which demands precise dual-arm coordination and millimeter-level accuracy, ablations isolated the performance contributions of the action generation module:
- **Peak Performance:** Chronos with its IMLE prior and quartic-scheduled second-order bridge achieved 90% success.
- **Action Head Comparison:** This significantly outperformed Chronos variants using diffusion (66%) or flow matching (72%) action heads, and regression-based methods like ACT (50%) and MTIL (76%).
- **Importance of Second-Order Schedule:** A crucial finding was that replacing Chronos's quartic bell noise schedule with a conventional first-order diffusion scheduler reduced success from 90% to 72%. This indicates that the performance gain is not solely from using an acceleration network but also from matching the noise process to second-order dynamics by ensuring zero endpoint velocity perturbations.
- **Efficient Inference:** Chronos achieved peak performance with only 3-5 bridge integration steps at inference, contrasting with the typically higher number of steps required by diffusion and flow-matching policies. This suggests the bridge acts as an efficient, low-latency refinement module.

**D. Real-World Experiments (Dual-Arm, Single RGB Camera)**
Real-world experiments on a dual-arm platform with a single RGB camera further validated Chronos's capabilities and transfer:
- **Overall Performance:** Achieved 78% average success across four tasks.
- **Memory-Dependent Tasks:** On the three memory-dependent tasks (`Put Back Blocks`, `Swap T-Mem`, `Cover Blocks`), Chronos achieved an average of 72% success. In contrast, $\pi_{0.5}$ achieved 0% on all these tasks.
- **Non-Memory Control Task:** On `Swap T` (a non-memory control task), Chronos achieved 96% success, while $\pi_{0.5}$ achieved 28%.
- **Observation Aliasing Validation:** The contrast between `Swap T` and `Swap T-Mem` (where the robot returns home, creating visual aliasing) clearly demonstrated Chronos's ability to leverage historical memory to resolve ambiguity, while $\pi_{0.5}$ completely failed on `Swap T-Mem`.
- **Action Smoothness:** Qualitative observations from recorded videos indicated that Chronos produced smoother, continuous trajectories, whereas $\pi_{0.5}$ often exhibited stop-and-go or jittery motion, supporting the benefits of the second-order acceleration field.
- **Real-World Challenges:** A lower success rate on real-world `Cover Blocks` (20%) compared to simulation was attributed to the change in the perception interface (single RGB camera/frozen ResNet18 in real-world vs. point cloud/trainable PointNet in simulation), which impacted precise color-location binding after occlusion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Chronos, a framework that addresses critical limitations in existing robotic manipulation policies, particularly in scenarios requiring long-horizon memory and precise, physically grounded actions. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Resolution of Observation Aliasing:** The most substantial impact is Chronos's ability to effectively handle non-Markovian control problems caused by observation aliasing. By treating the full observation history as the latent state of the policy dynamics, Chronos allows robots to infer and track hidden task phases that are not evident from the current sensory input alone. This is a fundamental advancement over conventional Markovian policies, including large-scale VLA models, which are often semantically powerful but dynamically underdetermined in such scenarios. This capability is crucial for more complex, multi-stage, and adaptable robotic tasks in unstructured environments.

2. **Efficient and Scalable Memory:** Chronos demonstrates that highly effective long-horizon memory can be achieved with a relatively compact model (0.3B parameters). Its performance on RMBench, surpassing a 10B-parameter memory-VLA model (Mem-0) with significantly fewer parameters, indicates a more efficient architectural approach to incorporating memory. This suggests that future advancements in robot learning might benefit more from structural innovations in temporal modeling (e.g., SSMs and full-history credit assignment) than from merely scaling up existing transformer-based architectures.

3. **Enhanced Action Quality through Second-Order Dynamics:** The introduction of a physics-informed second-order Schrödinger-inspired bridge for action generation represents a notable contribution to generative imitation learning. By modeling action refinement through acceleration fields and employing a carefully designed quartic noise schedule, Chronos generates smoother, more physically grounded robot motions. This is particularly advantageous for precision tasks like bimanual insertion and contact-rich manipulations, where abrupt action changes or jitter can lead to failure. This approach offers an alternative to first-order diffusion or flow-matching models, suggesting that higher-order dynamics can yield superior control quality.

4. **Broad Applicability and Robustness:** The comprehensive evaluation across 16 simulated and 4 real-world tasks, encompassing diverse challenges (precision, general manipulation, memory-dependent tasks, different sensing modalities like point clouds and images), demonstrates the versatility and robustness of the Chronos framework. Its strong performance on general manipulation benchmarks (RoboTwin 2.0) indicates that its memory mechanisms do not compromise its ability to handle visually complex, present-state-dominated tasks.

5. **Guiding Future Robot Learning Designs:** The paper's core message—that "history is not context; for many manipulation tasks, history is state"—provides a significant guiding principle for the design of future robot learning policies. It advocates for architectures that natively integrate temporal and physical structures, rather than solely focusing on increasing data volume or model parameters. This shift in perspective could lead to more intelligent, capable, and efficient general-purpose robot policies.

In summary, Chronos offers a cohesive framework that effectively addresses the non-Markovian nature of long-horizon manipulation through a principled, full-history state-space approach and enhances action quality via physics-informed second-order dynamics. Its demonstrated efficiency and performance across challenging tasks indicate a substantial step toward more autonomous and capable robotic systems.
