# SAPIEN: A SimulAted Part-based Interactive ENvironment

> [!summary] Summary
> SAPIEN is a simulated environment and dataset designed to advance robot learning for household manipulation by integrating physically accurate simulation, a large-scale collection of articulated objects, and photorealistic rendering. It facilitates the development and evaluation of robot perception and interaction algorithms, allowing for seamless transfer of learned policies to real-world robot systems.

## Problem

- Training robots in real-world environments is time-consuming, costly, unsafe, and challenging to reproduce.
- Existing simulation platforms often lack a combination of physically accurate interaction, photorealistic rendering, and diverse content for articulated objects.
- This limitation creates a significant visual and physical domain gap, hindering the development of robust perception and manipulation skills for home-assistant robots.

## Method

- Developed **SAPIEN**, a unified simulation framework integrating the ==Nvidia PhysX 4.1== engine for ==rigid body dynamics== and ==ROS interfaces== for robot control, supporting both synchronous and asynchronous modes.
- Introduced **PartNet-Mobility**, a large-scale dataset of 2,346 articulated objects with detailed ==part-level kinematic and dynamic attributes== (e.g., motion types, limits, semantic labels).
- Incorporated a robust rendering engine using ==OpenGL== and ==Nvidia OptiX== to generate real-time and photorealistic images, including RGB, depth, and ==semantic segmentation==, to minimize the visual domain gap.

## Results

- **SAPIEN** demonstrates high simulation fidelity and performance, with the PhysX engine running at approximately **5000Hz** and the OpenGL renderer at around **700Hz**.
- Heuristic manipulation approaches achieved high success rates within the simulator, with **95.3%** for drawer-pulling and **81.8%** for door-opening tasks, confirming the platform's physical accuracy.
- Learning-based methods achieved up to **32.8 degrees** of door rotation and **68%** success for drawer-pulling on unseen objects, but revealed that generalization remains a significant challenge for reinforcement learning agents in diverse interaction scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A comprehensive simulation environment for robotics requires a synergistic integration of physically accurate interactions, photorealistic rendering, and a large, diverse dataset of articulated objects to support advanced robot learning.
> - The use of ROS-compatible interfaces and support for both synchronous and asynchronous simulation modes are crucial for enabling effective transfer of learned policies from simulation to real-world robotic systems.
> - Current perception algorithms face challenges in accurately detecting small, functionally critical movable parts and precisely estimating 3D motion attributes, while reinforcement learning agents struggle with generalizing manipulation skills to unseen object instances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of home-assistant robots capable of human-level perception and interaction with 3D environments is a long-standing objective in robotics and computer vision. A significant challenge in this field is the training and evaluation of robot learning algorithms. Directly training robots in real-world environments presents several limitations, including being time-consuming, costly, potentially unsafe, and difficult to reproduce due to physical perturbations. Consequently, simulated environments have emerged as a critical tool for reproducible research in robotics, particularly for tasks such as navigation and control.

Existing simulated environments offer varying levels of physical realism, interactive capabilities, and content diversity.
- **Navigation-focused environments** like Habitat, Gibson, and MINOS typically provide photorealistic rendering suitable for semantic navigation. However, they generally offer limited or no object interaction, failing to capture the dynamic nature required for manipulation tasks.
- **Game-engine based interactive environments** such as AI2-THOR and VirtualHome integrate photorealistic rendering with interactive features. These environments often simplify physics, relying on high-level state changes or simplified force/torque models. This simplification limits their utility for learning detailed low-level robot-object interactions, as they may not accurately simulate articulated robots or objects.
- **Physics-engine integrated environments** like OpenAI Gym (using MuJoCo), PyBullet, Gazebo, and RLBench (using V-REP) are designed for continuous control and reinforcement learning tasks. While providing robust physical simulation, many of these environments face limitations in rendering capabilities, which can lead to a significant visual domain gap between simulation and the real world. Furthermore, some are task-centric, supporting only a few predefined scenarios, and many are restricted by the use of commercial software, impacting customizability. A common bottleneck across these simulators is the limited diversity and scale of their content, particularly regarding articulated objects common in household settings.

SAPIEN positions itself within this landscape by addressing the identified gaps, specifically focusing on the manipulation of diverse articulated daily objects. It aims to integrate detailed, physically realistic simulation, a large-scale dataset of articulated objects, and robust rendering capabilities, along with interfaces designed for seamless transfer to real robot systems. This approach seeks to provide a comprehensive platform that supports both advanced robotic perception and intricate interaction tasks that require a fine-grained understanding of object parts and their motions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing SAPIEN stems from the limitations of existing simulation platforms in enabling the advancement of human-level perception and interaction capabilities for home-assistant robots. Specifically, the authors identify several critical needs:

1. **Addressing Real-World Training Challenges:** Training robots directly in physical environments is economically prohibitive, time-intensive, and inherently risky. Slight environmental variations can also compromise research reproducibility. A robust simulation environment is therefore essential for scalable and safe robot learning.
2. **Enhancing Physical Realism:** Current simulators often compromise on physical accuracy. A truly effective environment needs to accurately reproduce real-world physics, including rigid body dynamics, joint constraints for articulated objects, and potentially deformable bodies or fluids, to facilitate the development of control policies that can transfer to the real world.
3. **Improving Robot Simulation Fidelity:** For seamless transfer of learned policies, the simulated environment must faithfully replicate the behaviors of real robot manipulators, sensors, and controllers. This includes providing interfaces compatible with standard robotics frameworks.
4. **Minimizing the Visual Domain Gap:** Photorealistic rendering is crucial to ensure that perception algorithms trained in simulation perform effectively in real-world scenarios. Many physics-centric simulators traditionally lack advanced rendering capabilities, contributing to a substantial visual domain gap.
5. **Providing Sufficient and Diverse Content:** Data diversity is fundamental for training generalizable learning-based algorithms. Existing simulators often have limited content, especially regarding the variety and complexity of interactive, articulated objects commonly found in households. The ability to interact with a wide range of objects, such as bottles with caps, ovens with doors, and electronics with switches, is a basic skill for household robots that requires an environment rich in such objects.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of the SAPIEN project are:
- To construct a physically realistic and interaction-rich simulated environment that integrates state-of-the-art physical simulators, modern graphics rendering engines, and user-friendly robotics interfaces.
- To host a large-scale, ready-to-simulate dataset of articulated objects (PartNet-Mobility) with detailed part-level kinematic and dynamic attributes.
- To enable a wide variety of robotic perception tasks requiring detailed part-level understanding, such as movable part detection and motion attribute estimation.
- To support diverse robotic interaction tasks, from low-level control to long-horizon logical planning, with mechanisms for evaluating both heuristic and reinforcement learning approaches.
- To facilitate the transfer of learned robot behaviors from simulation to real robots by providing ROS-compatible interfaces and supporting both synchronous and asynchronous simulation modes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SAPIEN is designed as a unified framework composed of three main components: SAPIEN Engine, SAPIEN Asset, and SAPIEN Renderer. It is primarily developed in C++ for efficiency, with a Python wrapper API provided for user accessibility.

#### 3.1. SAPIEN Engine

The SAPIEN Engine is the core simulation environment responsible for physical interactions and robot control.
- **Physical Simulation:** It utilizes the open-source Nvidia PhysX 4.1 engine for rigid body kinematics and dynamics. PhysX was chosen for its design suitability for robotics. The engine supports three types of body-joint systems for articulated objects:
 - **Kinematic joint system:** For objects so heavy that small external forces do not affect them, providing kinematic parent-child relations.
 - **Dynamic joint system:** Employs PhysX joints to constrain rigid bodies, suitable for complex objects not requiring precise control.
 - **PhysX articulation:** Specifically designed for robot simulation, offering accurate force control, P-D control, and inverse dynamics, though at a comparatively lower speed.
- **ROS Interfaces:** SAPIEN integrates with the Robot Operating System (ROS), a widely-used framework for robot applications. The ROS interface provides high-level APIs for robot interaction, supporting three levels of abstraction:
 - **Direct force control:** Applies forces and torques directly to joints, similar to OpenAI Gym.
 - **ROS controllers:** Offers joint space and Cartesian coordinate space control APIs, implementing standard interfaces based on.
 - **Motion planning interface:** Provides support for high-level motion planning using the popular MoveIt framework, enabling collision-free robot movement.
- **Simulation Modes:** SAPIEN supports both synchronous and asynchronous simulation.
 - **Synchronous mode:** The simulation step is client-controlled, typical for training reinforcement learning algorithms where the agent's action directly advances the simulation.
 - **Asynchronous mode:** The simulation runs independently, akin to the real world, while the client interacts via ROS-compatible sensors and controllers over TCP/IP. Timestamps are synchronized, allowing for direct code transfer between simulated and real robots, which is a significant advantage for robotics research.

#### 3.2. SAPIEN Asset

SAPIEN Asset encompasses the simulation content, including object models, robot models, and scene layouts.
- **PartNet-Mobility Dataset:** This is a large-scale 3D interactive model dataset specifically developed for SAPIEN. It comprises 2,346 object models from 46 common indoor object categories, containing over 14,068 annotated articulated parts. The models are sourced from 3D Warehouse and organized following ShapeNet and PartNet structures. The annotations include:
 - **Motion Types:** Hinge (rotation), slider (translation), and screw (combined rotation and translation).
 - **Motion Limits:** Specific angular or linear limits for hinge and slider joints. For screw joints, motion limits and coupling between degrees of freedom are noted.
 - **Structure:** Joints are organized into a tree structure with a single root, compatible with most physical simulators.
 - **Semantic Labels:** Each movable part is assigned a category-specific semantic label.
 Compared to existing datasets, PartNet-Mobility provides a larger number of movable part annotations and includes crucial details like textures and motion range limits necessary for physical simulation.
- **Asset Loader:** Object and robot models are represented using the Unified Robot Description Format (URDF). For accurate contact simulation, meshes are decomposed into convex parts. Physical properties such as friction, damping, and density can be randomized or manually set. C++/Python APIs are also provided for constructing robots piece-by-piece.

#### 3.3. SAPIEN Renderer

The SAPIEN Renderer handles scene visualization and sensor data generation.
- **Real-time Rendering:** It uses OpenGL 4.5 with GLSL shaders, which are exposed to the client for customization. The default pipeline employs deferred lighting, generating RGB, albedo, normal, depth, and semantic segmentation images from camera views. Lighting is computed using the OrenNayar diffuse model and GGX specular model.
- **Photorealistic Rendering:** For higher visual fidelity, SAPIEN includes a ray tracing option using Nvidia OptiX, capable of producing physically accurate images, though at a higher computational cost.

#### 3.4. Profiling Analysis

The SAPIEN engine demonstrates a simulation speed of approximately 5000Hz for manipulation tasks, while the OpenGL renderer achieves about 700Hz. These figures were obtained on a system with a 2.2 GHz Intel i7-8750 CPU and an Nvidia GeForce RTX 2070 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SAPIEN's capabilities were evaluated through a series of robotic perception and interaction tasks.

#### 4.1. Robotic Perception Tasks

Two key perception tasks were investigated: movable part detection and motion attributes estimation.

- **Movable Part Detection:**
 - **Task Definition:** Given a 2D RGB or RGB-D image, or a 3D partial point cloud, the algorithm identifies and segments individual movable parts, assigning semantic labels.
 - **Algorithms:** Mask R-CNN (2D input) and PartNet-InsSeg (3D input via PointNet++) were evaluated.
 - **Dataset and Metrics:** The full PartNet-Mobility dataset (2,346 objects) was rendered into 20 views each, creating RGB and RGB-D images (512x512 resolution). 75% of data was used for training, 25% for testing. Average Precision (AP) at an IoU threshold of 0.5 was used, with mean AP (mAP) computed across all part categories.
 - **Results (Table 4):** Mask R-CNN achieved mAP scores of 53.0% (RGB) and 52.8% (RGB-D). PartNet-InsSeg achieved mAP scores of 36.1% (XYZ) and 37.1% (XYZRGB). Both methods exhibited difficulty in detecting small movable parts such as table wheels and casters, which are often crucial for robot interaction. Performance was generally better for object categories with more balanced part sizes. The results indicate a need for more specialized algorithms to accurately detect small, functionally important parts.

- **Motion Attributes Estimation:**
 - **Task Definition:** Predict the motion type (rotation or translation), motion axis, and current part state (e.g., angle for doors, extension for drawers) for articulated parts from partial scans.
 - **Algorithms:** ResNet-50 (using 5-channel RGB-D images augmented with part masks) and PointNet++ (using camera-space RGB point clouds) were evaluated. Both networks were trained to output a 14-dimensional motion vector.
 - **Dataset:** A subset of 640 models (779 doors, 529 drawers) from 10 categories was used, due to ambiguities in defining "rest states" for all object types.
 - **Metrics (Table 5):** Metrics included classification accuracy for hinge/slider motion types, average error for pivot point and axis direction, and average position error for doors (angle) and drawers (length).
 - **Results:** Both algorithms achieved high accuracy (approximately 95.5%) in classifying motion types. Axis prediction for translational joints (sliders) showed lower error compared to rotational joints (hinges). ResNet-50 generally outperformed PointNet++, which the authors suggest might be due to its higher number of parameters, although 3D information is intuitively expected to be better processed directly from 3D data. The results suggest avenues for future research into improving 3D axis prediction directly from 3D data.

#### 4.2. Robotic Interaction Tasks

SAPIEN supports various robot interaction tasks, ranging from low-level control to complex planning. The tasks were evaluated under two modes: using ground-truth perception information, or using raw sensor inputs for end-to-end learning.

- **Door-opening and Drawer-pulling:**
 - **Setup:** A flying gripper (Kinova Gripper 3) with full dynamics simulation (excluding gravity) was used. Tasks were performed on 108 cabinet instances for drawer-pulling and 77 for door-opening.
 - **Success Criteria:** An interaction was considered successful if the gripper moved the target joint (slider for drawer, hinge for door) through 90% of its motion range.
 - **Agent Input:** Point clouds, normal maps, and segmentation masks from three fixed cameras, along with the robot's 6-DoF pose.

- **Heuristic Based Manipulation:**
 - **Drawer-pulling:** Using ground-truth segmentation for grasp pose detection and a velocity controller, a success rate of 95.3% was achieved.
 - **Door-opening:** An initial grasp was followed by Position Based Visual Servoing (PBVS) to track and clamp the door edge, then rotating it open. This method achieved an 81.8% success rate. These high success rates demonstrate the simulator's fidelity for basic manipulation.

- **Learning Based Manipulation (Reinforcement Learning):**
 - **Algorithm:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) was used to evaluate generalization to unseen objects. Agents were trained on 2, 4, 8, or 16 door or drawer instances and tested on the remaining unseen models.
 - **State Representations:** Three representations were tested: `raw-exp` (full scene state), `mobility-exp` (motion axis pose, average normal, joint angles/velocities of target part), and `visual-exp` (RGB-D images with segmentation masks from a fixed camera).
 - **Training Protocol:** The gripper was initialized on the handle using a heuristic grasp. Rewards were given for moving the target part towards its joint limit, and negative rewards for losing the grasp.
 - **Results (Table 6):**
 - **Door-opening:** Training performance generally decreased as the number of training objects increased, suggesting overfitting on fewer instances. However, test performance showed a slight improvement with more training scenarios, indicating enhanced generalization. `mobility-exp` yielded the highest test performance (32.8 degrees with 16 train objects).
 - **Drawer-pulling:** Similar patterns were observed, with `mobility-exp` achieving the best test success rate (68% with 16 train objects). Drawers were found to be comparatively easier due to more consistent movement patterns.
 - **Representation Comparison:** `mobility-exp` consistently delivered the best performance. `visual-exp` performed reasonably for doors but struggled with drawers, as the fixed camera view provided limited state change information for a linear pull action.
 - **Conclusion:** While heuristic approaches performed well, RL agents demonstrated difficulty generalizing effectively to unseen objects, even when trained on multiple instances. This highlights challenges in RL transferability and the need for improved vision methods to dynamically encode geometric information during interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SAPIEN contributes to the fields of robotics, computer vision, and reinforcement learning by providing a comprehensive and detailed simulation environment that addresses several critical limitations of prior platforms.

- **Advancement in Robotics Research:** SAPIEN offers a robust platform for developing and evaluating robot learning algorithms for complex manipulation tasks, particularly those involving diverse articulated objects found in human environments. Its integration of a physically accurate simulator with ROS interfaces and support for both synchronous and asynchronous modes facilitates a more direct transfer of learned policies from simulation to real robots. This enables researchers to explore advanced control strategies and long-horizon planning in a safe and reproducible setting.
- **Enabling New Vision Research:** The PartNet-Mobility dataset, integrated within SAPIEN, provides a large-scale, richly annotated resource for perception research. It specifically supports the development of algorithms for fine-grained part-level understanding, including movable part detection and motion attribute estimation. The evaluation of existing methods within SAPIEN highlights areas requiring further research, such as improving detection of small functional parts and enhancing 3D axis prediction.
- **Challenges for Reinforcement Learning:** By providing a diverse array of articulated objects and detailed interaction tasks, SAPIEN serves as a challenging benchmark for reinforcement learning algorithms. The results from the learning-based manipulation tasks underscore current limitations in generalization and transferability of RL agents across varied object instances, prompting future research into more robust and efficient learning methods.
- **Foundation for Future Work:** The authors propose several future research directions enabled by SAPIEN, including learning cognition through interaction, part motion discovery, and the construction of more sophisticated robotics-ready simulated game environments. The environment's customizability, through its renderer and asset loader, further allows researchers to adapt and expand its capabilities for novel applications.

In summary, SAPIEN provides a significant step towards bridging the gap between simulated and real-world robot learning, particularly for household manipulation tasks, by combining detailed physics, diverse content, and flexible interfaces.
