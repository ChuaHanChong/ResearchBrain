# Rolling-WAM: World Action Models with Rolling Imagination

> [!summary] Summary
> Rolling-WAM is a world action model that spreads joint video-action diffusion denoising across replanning cycles. It keeps a sliding window of chunks at staggered noise levels, fully denoising only the imminent action chunk while partially refining farther futures, then shifting the window on each new observation. It reaches 98.1% on LIBERO, 93.3% on RoboTwin 2.0, 85.0% real-world humanoid success, with 4.5x faster replanning.

## Problem

- Standard joint WAMs denoise the entire video-action horizon from pure noise at every replanning cycle, so inference latency delays action updates and limits closed-loop responsiveness.
- Receding-horizon chunk samplers execute only the first portion of each predicted horizon, discarding the unexecuted tail instead of reusing its denoising computation across replans.
- Faster alternatives drop future-video generation at deployment, losing explicit visual predictions; earlier rolling-noise policies denoise actions only, in small task-specific settings.

## Method

- A ==rolling noise schedule== assigns chunk j the level σ((j−1+τ)/W), so the imminent chunk finishes clean while retained chunks land exactly at the start level of their next slot.
- A separate initialization schedule starts an episode from pure noise; afterwards each steady-state cycle needs only N/W denoising steps, executing K actions then appending a fresh Gaussian chunk.
- A **Mixture-of-Transformers** pairs a pretrained Wan2.2 video DiT with a 1B action expert, trained by ==flow matching==; ==masked joint attention== lets action tokens read partially denoised video across the whole window.
- On the Unitree G1 humanoid the policy predicts **78-D** actions: a **64-D** ==SONIC== whole-body motion latent plus a **7-D** command per hand, from a 320×224 egocentric RGB image and a 43-D robot state (§IV-A.3).

## Results

- LIBERO average is **98.1%** versus **98.5%** for Joint-WAM and LingBot-VA; on RoboTwin 2.0 it leads with a **93.3%** average against **92.2%**, **91.8%** and **90.6%**.
- Steady-state replanning takes **215 ms** versus **978 ms** for Joint-WAM and **548 ms** for Fast-WAM, **4.5x** and **2.5x** speedups, beating the VLA baselines at **296 ms** and **285 ms**.
- Real-world Unitree G1 tasks average **85.0%** versus **78.3%** for Joint-WAM and **75.0%** for Fast-WAM; success peaks at window size 5 (**78.2%**), falling to **69.5%** at 8.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Denoising cost can be amortized across control cycles: reuse the unexecuted tail of a plan rather than restarting each replan from pure noise.
> - Longer imagined futures do not monotonically help; distant predictions are weakly constrained by the current observation and can misguide the imminent action chunk.
> - Passing context through a shared visual window beat direct action-to-action attention, and matching training noise profiles to the inference schedule outperformed broader noise mixtures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Closed-loop robot manipulation requires frequent replanning: the robot keeps updating its plan from the latest camera view. World Action Models (WAMs) predict both robot actions and future visuals, so each action can draw on a predicted scene. A conventional joint WAM, however, starts denoising the full video-and-action horizon from scratch at every replanning cycle, even though the robot executes only the nearest action chunk. Repeating that work delays updates based on new camera observations.

Rolling-WAM carries the unexecuted future forward instead. It finishes the nearest action chunk for execution and keeps refining the rest after the robot observes again. Under the paper's controlled RoboTwin 2.0 timing setup, a steady-state replan takes 215 ms with Rolling-WAM and 978 ms with Joint-WAM, about 4.5× faster.

Author project video illustrating Rolling-WAM.

### 2. Keep the future, finish the next action

The model divides its prediction window into aligned video-action chunks and denoises the window jointly. Noise levels are staggered, with farther-future chunks noisier. Only the nearest chunk reaches completion.

The robot then executes that chunk's actions and obtains a new camera observation and robot state. The model drops the executed chunk and retains the future chunks. It appends a new chunk initialized with Gaussian noise and refines the window again under the new observation. This distributes denoising steps across cycles and keeps visual prediction in the model.

The default setup uses five chunks, 16 actions per chunk, and 10 denoising steps per chunk. Initialization differs from later cycles. The first window begins entirely from Gaussian noise, so the initialization cycle takes 10 steps, and steady-state replans take two. Each chunk still receives all 10 denoising steps before execution.

The method stays joint across vision and action in one specific way. Each action chunk can attend to visual features across the entire window, while direct action-to-action attention is restricted to the same chunk.

Rolling-WAM framework (paper Fig. 2). Panel (b) shows joint denoising, execution of $A_{1}$, a window shift that retains later chunks, and a newly appended $V_{W+1}, A_{W+1}$ pair at full Gaussian noise. Panel (c) is the attention mask, which is used in both training and inference.

### 3. Speed and task results

The latency measurement was taken on one NVIDIA A100 in RoboTwin 2.0 at 384 × 320 resolution. Timing includes visual encoding and denoising and excludes warm-up and initialization. Both methods execute 16 actions per cycle, but Rolling-WAM maintains an 80-action prediction window. The 215 ms versus 978 ms result is therefore elapsed latency per replan. It is not evidence of fewer generated tokens, fewer attempts, or lower total computation.

Average steady-state replan latency in milliseconds (paper Fig. 4); lower is better. π₀.₅: 296, GR00T N1.7: 285, Fast-WAM: 548, Joint-WAM: 978, Rolling-WAM: 215. Initialization is excluded.

Success rates stay competitive under the stated evaluations. On LIBERO, the average over four suites of 10 tasks each is 98.1% for Rolling-WAM, versus 98.5% for Joint-WAM. On RoboTwin 2.0, the average across clean and randomized settings for 50 bimanual tasks is 93.3%, versus 90.6% for Joint-WAM. In real-world Unitree G1 trials, one policy averages 85.0% across three tasks, each evaluated in 20 trials. Success there means completion without human intervention. These are measured success rates under these evaluations, not proof of general performance beyond them.

### 4. The next action is the point

Rolling-WAM retains future visual prediction and reuses its denoising progress. This lowers measured per-replan latency against a matched Joint-WAM while keeping a longer prediction window and competitive success. The comparison does not establish that test-time imagination is necessary, or that it alone causes the task results.
