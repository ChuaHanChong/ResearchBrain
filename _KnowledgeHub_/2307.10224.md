# RL-ViGen: A Reinforcement Learning Benchmark for Visual Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> RL-ViGen introduces a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating out-of-distribution generalization in visual reinforcement learning, integrating diverse tasks and an expanded set of generalization categories. The benchmark reveals that no single existing algorithm achieves universally strong performance, with all methods demonstrating significant limitations in generalizing to novel scene structures and cross-embodiments.

## Problem

- Existing visual reinforcement learning (RL) benchmarks for generalization are limited in task diversity, realism, and the range of generalization categories they address (e.g., Procgen for non-realistic images, DMC-GB for narrow scope).
- These limitations risk promoting algorithmic overfitting to specific benchmark characteristics, hindering the development of truly robust agents capable of real-world out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization.
- A lack of a unified evaluation framework across various algorithms makes fair and reliable comparisons difficult, leading to potentially misleading conclusions about algorithmic progress.

## Method

- **RL-ViGen** constructs a novel benchmark integrating 5 diverse task categories, including dexterous manipulation, autonomous driving, indoor navigation, table-top manipulation, and locomotion, using high-fidelity simulators.
- The benchmark incorporates 5 distinct generalization categories: visual appearances, lighting conditions, camera views, scene structures, and cross embodiments, thoroughly testing OOD shifts.
- A unified algorithmic framework is established to evaluate 8 state-of-the-art visual RL algorithms under consistent optimization schemes and hyperparameters, ensuring fair and reliable comparisons.

## Results

- PIE-G, which leverages ImageNet pre-trained models, showed superior generalization for visual appearances and lighting changes in Indoor Navigation and Dexterous Hand Manipulation tasks.
- All evaluated algorithms demonstrated unsatisfactory performance when tested with novel 'scene structures' (e.g., in Autonomous Driving), indicating a significant unaddressed challenge for current methods.
- SGQN, utilizing saliency maps, exhibited advantageous generalization abilities against varying 'camera views' (in Dexterous Hand Manipulation), attributed to its enhanced awareness of object geometry.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - No single existing visual generalization algorithm demonstrates universally strong performance; their efficacy varies significantly depending on the specific task and generalization category.
> - Algorithms that perform well in terms of sample efficiency or asymptotic performance during training do not necessarily guarantee good OOD generalization without additional inductive biases.
> - Current visual RL algorithms show substantial weaknesses in generalizing to changes in 'scene structures' and 'cross embodiments', categories that impact not just observations but also action space interpretation and transition dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual Reinforcement Learning (Visual RL) has achieved considerable success across various domains, from playing video games to controlling robots. By learning directly from high-dimensional visual inputs, RL agents can operate in complex environments. However, a fundamental and persistent challenge facing Visual RL agents is **out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization**. This means that an agent trained in a specific environment often performs poorly when deployed in a slightly different, unseen environment, even if the underlying task remains conceptually similar. This lack of generalization capability is a major barrier to the deployment of RL agents in real-world scenarios, where environmental conditions are inherently variable and unpredictable.

To address this generalization problem, several benchmarks have emerged, including Procgen, Distracting Control Suite, and DMC-GB. While these benchmarks have been instrumental in advancing the field, the authors of "RL-ViGen" argue that they suffer from inherent limitations:
- **Procgen** offers diverse environment configurations and visual appearances but is restricted to video games with non-realistic images and low-dimensional discrete action spaces, creating a significant gap from real-world applicability.
- **Distracting Control Suite** incorporates two generalization types but is criticized for its limited diversity and complexity.
- **DMC-GB**, often considered a standard for state-of-the-art visual generalization algorithms, has a narrow scope of task classes (primarily locomotion) and generalization categories.

The prevailing trend has been for researchers to demonstrate the superiority of their proposed algorithms on these existing, often narrow, benchmarks. The authors contend that this practice carries a risk of promoting **overfitting** to specific benchmark characteristics, rather than fostering the development of algorithms truly capable of solving real-world generalization problems. This can lead to a "distorted perception of the situation" where algorithms appear advanced on limited tests but lack real-world robustness.

RL-ViGen positions itself as a critical evolution in this landscape. It aims to bridge the gap by providing a more comprehensive, diverse, and realistic benchmark that reflects the complexities of real-world visual generalization. By integrating a wider array of tasks and generalization types, and by offering a unified framework for fair comparisons, RL-ViGen seeks to enable more robust evaluation and ultimately catalyze the development of truly generalizable visual RL agents. It moves beyond isolated tests to offer a holistic platform, intending to provide more reliable conclusions about the true capabilities and limitations of generalization algorithms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind RL-ViGen stems from the authors' assertion that existing benchmarks for visual reinforcement learning generalization are insufficient for comprehensive evaluation. They argue that these benchmarks are "restricted to isolated tasks and generalization categories," which undermines a thorough assessment of agents' visual generalization capabilities and can lead to misleading conclusions about algorithmic progress.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the RL-ViGen project are:

1. **Introduce a Novel, Comprehensive Benchmark:** To create "RL-ViGen: A Reinforcement Learning Benchmark for Visual Generalization," designed to overcome the limitations of current benchmarks.
2. **Enhance Task Diversity and Realism:** To integrate a broad spectrum of task categories with realistic image inputs. This includes domains such as table-top manipulation, locomotion, autonomous driving, indoor navigation, and dexterous hand manipulation, all of which are highly relevant to real-world robotic and autonomous systems.
3. **Expand Generalization Categories:** To incorporate a wide array of visual generalization types. These include visual appearances, lighting changes, camera views, scene structures, and cross embodiments, providing a multi-dimensional challenge for agents.
4. **Establish a Unified Evaluation Framework:** To provide a consistent and fair platform for evaluating existing state-of-the-art visual RL and generalization algorithms. This involves implementing these algorithms within a unified codebase, using the same optimization schemes and hyperparameters, which is crucial for drawing reliable comparisons.
5. **Facilitate Deeper Algorithmic Analysis:** To enable researchers to conduct comprehensive experiments that reveal the distinct performance characteristics, benefits, and limitations of current generalizable visual RL algorithms across diverse tasks and generalization categories.
6. **Catalyze Future Research:** To serve as a catalyst for future advancements in visual RL generalization, ultimately laying a foundation for the creation of truly universal visual generalization RL agents that are robust and adaptable enough for real-world scenarios.

In essence, the authors are motivated by the need for a more rigorous and realistic testing ground for visual RL generalization algorithms, believing that a better benchmark will accelerate the development of more effective and deployable AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of RL-ViGen revolves around constructing a multifaceted benchmark that combines diverse tasks with a wide array of generalization challenges, all within a unified evaluation framework.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Design: RL-ViGen Environments

RL-ViGen incorporates 5 distinct task categories, leveraging existing high-fidelity simulators and extending them with novel generalization scenarios:

1. **Dexterous Manipulation (Adroit):** Enriched with diverse visual appearances, camera perspectives, hand types, lighting changes, and object shapes. These tasks require fine-grained feature capturing and deal with sparse rewards and high-dimensional action spaces.
2. **Autonomous Driving (CARLA):** Provides enhanced ranges of dynamic weather, more complex road conditions in different scene structures, and flexible camera angle adjustments, building on prior visual RL deployments.
3. **Indoor Navigation (Habitat):** Builds upon the `skokloster-castle` scene by proposing additional scenarios with varied visual and lighting settings, and adjustable camera views and scene structures, focusing on scene understanding.
4. **Table-top Manipulation (Robosuite):** Leverages Robosuite's modularity to incorporate dynamic backgrounds, adaptive lighting conditions, and options for embodiment variation, extending prior work on visual background changes.
5. **Locomotion (DeepMind Control + DMC-GB):** Enhances DMC-GB by introducing objects and tasks from sophisticated real-world locomotion and manipulation applications (e.g., Unitree, Anymal quadrupedal robots, Franka Arm), and offers a variety of additional generalization categories.

#### 3.2. Generalization Categories

RL-ViGen defines and implements five primary generalization categories, designed to thoroughly test an agent's robustness to OOD shifts (Table 1):

1. **Visual Appearances:** Modifying colors, textures, materials of objects and scenes, and introducing dynamic video backgrounds.
2. **Lighting Conditions:** Varying light intensity, colors, and dynamic shadow changes.
3. **Camera Views:** Adjusting camera configurations, angles, distances, Field of Views (FOVs), and even the number of cameras.
4. **Scene Structures:** Modifying spatial arrangements, maps, patterns, or introducing additional objects within the environment. This includes changes in road layouts for autonomous driving.
5. **Cross Embodiments:** Adapting learned skills to different physical morphologies or robot embodiments (e.g., different robot arm types or hand configurations), which impacts both visual input and potentially kinematic properties.

#### 3.3. Unified Algorithmic Framework and Evaluation Protocol

A key methodological contribution is the implementation of a **unified codebase** for comparing various visual RL algorithms. This addresses a critical issue in prior research where different algorithms often used distinct optimization schemes, RL baselines, and hyperparameters, making fair comparisons difficult. By standardizing these aspects, RL-ViGen ensures more persuasive and reliable conclusions.

- **Algorithms Evaluated:** Eight leading visual RL algorithms are included: DrQ-v2, DrQ, CURL, VRL3, SVEA, SRM, PIE-G, and SGQN. These algorithms cover various approaches, from data augmentation to contrastive learning and pre-trained models.
- **Evaluation Procedure (Figure 2):**
 1. **Stage 1: Training:** Agents are trained in a *fixed, single training scenario* within each task category.
 2. **Stage 2: Zero-Shot Generalization:** The trained agents are then evaluated across *various unseen scenarios* (incorporating the different generalization categories) in a zero-shot manner. No further training or fine-tuning is performed in the test environments.
- **Metrics and Statistical Rigor:** Experiments are conducted over 5 random seeds, reporting mean scores and 95% confidence intervals. Aggregated scores are used for tasks with multiple subtasks.
- **Implementation Details:** The appendix provides extensive details on specific modifications to each simulator, hyperparameter settings, and computational resources used, ensuring reproducibility.

This comprehensive approach allows for a rigorous investigation into how different generalization techniques perform under varied and challenging out-of-distribution conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on RL-ViGen yield several critical insights, challenging previous perceptions of algorithmic superiority and highlighting specific areas for future research. The overarching finding is that **no single existing algorithm demonstrates universally strong generalization abilities across all tasks and generalization categories.**

Here are the specific findings across different generalization categories:

- **Visual Appearances and Lighting Changes:**
 - **Indoor Navigation (Habitat):** PIE-G, which integrates an ImageNet pre-trained model, shows superior generalization. Its exposure to a wealth of real-world images enables it to handle novel visual and lighting scenarios more effectively. SGQN, designed to segment the agent from the background, proves ineffective in object-rich, first-person view tasks where the agent is not a central visual element.
 - **Autonomous Driving (CARLA):** SRM, which applies data augmentation in the frequency domain, performs best. This is attributed to its ability to adapt to varying input image frequencies (e.g., extreme darkness/brightness). PIE-G struggles with low-frequency scenarios despite ImageNet pre-training, as its training data primarily contains higher-frequency features. SGQN again shows reduced performance in visually rich scenes without the ego-agent in the frame. DrQ surprisingly shows some generalization, hypothesized to be due to entropy collapse leading to a consistent action distribution.
 - **Dexterous Hand Manipulation (Adroit):** PIE-G again leads in generalization, particularly in harder settings. Adroit tasks require capturing fine-grained features, where PIE-G's pre-trained models provide an advantage. VRL3 and CURL, without additional objectives to mitigate visual changes, struggle in these demanding tasks. Interestingly, strong data augmentation, when combined with VRL3's safe Q mechanism, does not negatively affect sample efficiency.

- **Scene Structures (CARLA):** This category proves to be a significant challenge for all algorithms. When agents trained on highways are tested in complex structures like narrow roads, tunnels, and roundabouts, **all algorithms demonstrate unsatisfactory performance**. This indicates a substantial gap in current visual RL algorithms' ability to generalize to changes in the fundamental layout and spatial organization of environments.

- **Camera Views (Adroit):** SGQN exhibits advantageous generalization ability in the face of varying camera views, especially in hard settings with substantial changes in camera position, orientation, and FOV. This is attributed to its reliance on saliency maps, which enhance the agent's self-awareness of object geometry and relative positioning, making it more robust to viewpoint alterations. Most other algorithms lose generalization ability under hard camera view changes.

- **Cross Embodiments (Robosuite):** Testing generalization from one robot arm (Panda) to different morphologies (KUKA IIWA, Kinova3), the overall performance of all algorithms is **suboptimal**. Generalization-based methods show a slight advantage over sample-efficiency focused ones, but significant improvement is needed. This highlights that embodiment mismatch, which affects not only observation space but also action space interpretation and transition dynamics, is a profoundly difficult challenge.

**Key Takeaways from the Discussion Section:**

1. **No Universal Algorithm:** There is no single "best" generalization algorithm; performance varies significantly by task and generalization type.
2. **Training Performance ≠ Generalization:** Algorithms with high sample efficiency or asymptotic performance during training (like DrQ(v2) and CURL) do not automatically guarantee good generalization. Additional inductive biases are crucial for OOD generalization.
3. **Unaddressed Generalization Categories:** Existing algorithms show considerable shortcomings in **cross embodiments** and **scene structures**. These categories are particularly challenging because they involve changes beyond just the observation space, affecting action space and transition probabilities within the Markov Decision Process (MDP).
4. **Algorithmic Strengths are Unique:** Each generalization algorithm possesses distinct strengths (e.g., PIE-G for visual appearances, SRM for frequency variations, SGQN for camera views, SVEA for minimal modification effectiveness). This suggests that future advancements might involve fusing different algorithms to leverage their complementary strengths.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RL-ViGen represents a significant step forward in the field of visual reinforcement learning and its pursuit of robust generalization. Its potential impact is multi-faceted:

1. **Driving Research Towards Real-World Applicability:** By incorporating diverse, realistic tasks and a wide spectrum of generalization types, RL-ViGen directly addresses the critical need to bridge the gap between simulated RL success and real-world deployment. The benchmark's challenges closely mirror the variability encountered in real-world robotics, autonomous driving, and embodied AI, making progress on RL-ViGen highly indicative of an agent's potential for practical application.

2. **Enabling Reliable and Fair Evaluation:** The unified framework is a crucial contribution. By standardizing optimization schemes and hyperparameters, RL-ViGen ensures that algorithmic comparisons are fair and meaningful. This eliminates inconsistencies that often plague benchmark results, allowing researchers to draw more robust and trustworthy conclusions about the true efficacy of different generalization strategies. This can prevent the "overfitting" of algorithms to narrow, non-representative benchmarks.

3. **Highlighting Critical Research Gaps:** The comprehensive experimental analysis reveals that existing algorithms fall short in specific, complex generalization categories, particularly **scene structures** and **cross embodiments**. This explicit identification of weaknesses provides a clear roadmap for future research directions, signaling where concentrated efforts are most needed to advance the state of the art. These findings prompt researchers to move beyond simpler visual distractions and tackle more fundamental changes to the environment and agent morphology.

4. **Catalyzing Algorithmic Innovation:** The finding that no single algorithm is universally superior encourages the development of novel, multi-modal, or ensemble approaches. Researchers might explore combining the strengths of different techniques – for instance, integrating pre-trained models with frequency-based augmentations – to build more comprehensively robust agents. It also emphasizes the importance of introducing strong inductive biases specific to generalization, rather than solely focusing on sample efficiency or in-distribution performance.

5. **Lowering the Entry Barrier:** By providing a ready-to-use benchmark with implemented baselines and a unified codebase, RL-ViGen significantly lowers the barrier for new researchers to enter the field of visual RL generalization. This fosters broader participation and accelerates scientific progress.

While the authors acknowledge the limitation that RL-ViGen has not yet been evaluated with real-world robots, its design and the insights it provides are crucial steps toward achieving that goal. RL-ViGen is poised to become a vital tool for the research community, guiding the development of the next generation of generalizable RL agents capable of navigating the complexities and unpredictability of the real world.
