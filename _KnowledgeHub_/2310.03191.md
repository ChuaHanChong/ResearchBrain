# Sim-to-Real Learning for Humanoid Box Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Oregon State University, with support from NSF and DARPA, developed a sim-to-real reinforcement learning approach to enable the Digit humanoid robot to autonomously perform complex loco-manipulation. The learned controller successfully walks to, picks up, carries, and sets down boxes of various masses and sizes on real hardware, marking the first such demonstration of a fully learned system for this type of task.

## Problem

- Coordinating whole-body movement and maintaining balance while simultaneously manipulating objects is inherently difficult for high-DOF bipedal humanoids.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches for robotics largely focused on either pure locomotion or manipulation with stable bases, leaving combined loco-manipulation for humanoids underexplored.
- Transferring complex learned skills from simulation to real-world humanoid hardware for contact-rich tasks presents substantial sim-to-real challenges.

## Method

- The complex loco-manipulation task was decomposed into five sequential, learned policies (WALK, STAND, PICKUP, WALKWITHBOX, STANDWITHBOX), each controlled by an LSTM neural network.
- Policies were trained using PPO in the MuJoCo simulator with extensive dynamics and scenario randomization, employing carefully designed reward functions including phase-based hand trajectory guidance and stability terms.
- A crucial "relative" action space, adding policy outputs to current motor positions, was employed for manipulation policies to enhance learning efficiency and overcome large deviations from neutral poses.

## Results

- All five policies achieved high success rates (e.g., **96.15%** for PICKUP, **100%** for WALKWITHBOX) and low errors during quantitative evaluation in simulation.
- The learned `PICKUP` policy demonstrated strong generalization, successfully handling boxes up to **22.9** kg and 64 cm in size, significantly exceeding its training distribution.
- The full loco-manipulation sequence, including walking, picking up, carrying, and setting down boxes of various masses and sizes, was successfully transferred and demonstrated on the real Digit humanoid robot, marking a first for a fully learned controller.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured guidance, such as pre-defined hand trajectories in the reward function, significantly accelerates learning for complex manipulation skills.
> - Employing a "relative" action space (output added to current joint positions) is crucial for efficient learning of manipulation tasks involving large deviations from neutral postures.
> - Extensive domain and scenario randomization effectively enables sim-to-real transfer, leading to policies that generalize to varied box properties beyond their training distribution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing, with reinforcement learning (RL) emerging as a powerful paradigm for teaching complex behaviors to robots. However, this work positions itself at the intersection of several challenging sub-fields, addressing critical gaps in the existing literature.

Traditionally, RL research for legged robots has predominantly focused on pure locomotion skills such as walking, running, and navigating various terrains, as evidenced by works like. These studies have yielded impressive results in enabling dynamic and robust bipedal or quadrupedal movement. Concurrently, object manipulation research using RL has largely concentrated on robots with stable or fixed bases, such as grounded robotic arms. These systems benefit from inherent stability, allowing the controller to focus primarily on arm kinematics and object interaction.

The area of "loco-manipulation" – the simultaneous movement of a robot and manipulation of objects – with legged robots, and especially bipedal humanoids, remains significantly less explored by RL. This is primarily due to the inherent complexity of coordinating whole-body motion, maintaining balance, and interacting with objects, all while managing the high degrees of freedom characteristic of humanoids. Prior efforts in humanoid loco-manipulation have largely been model-based, often resulting in overly conservative, slow motions to guarantee stability. Other planning-based approaches or methods augmenting baseline gait controllers have also been explored. Furthermore, some learning-based approaches for humanoid or bimanual manipulation have relied heavily on expert trajectories or human demonstrations for imitation learning, which can introduce compounding errors from motion tracking or alignment issues.

For mobile-base or quadruped robots with manipulators, a common strategy is to decouple locomotion and manipulation control into separate modules. While effective, this can limit the potential for fully coordinated whole-body movements. More unified control approaches for mobile manipulators exist, but they often operate on different robotic platforms or rely on external planners.

This paper distinguishes itself by tackling the challenging problem of sim-to-real RL for *loco-manipulation with a bipedal humanoid robot*, specifically the Digit platform. Its primary contribution lies in demonstrating, for the first time to the authors' knowledge, a *fully learned controller* achieving such a task on *real-world hardware*. This moves beyond prior work, such as Xie et al. (2023), which targeted a similar humanoid box pickup task but focused more on animation settings rather than direct hardware execution and removed reliance on trajectory tracking for the core learning process. By focusing on sim-to-real transfer of learned, full-body coordinated skills, this research pushes the boundaries of autonomous humanoid capabilities in real-world, interactive scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **design and evaluate a sim-to-real reinforcement learning approach for loco-manipulation with a real humanoid robot**. The specific target task chosen to demonstrate this capability is a comprehensive sequence: the robot must **walk up to a box, stop, pick up the box, carry it to another table, and then set it down**.

The motivations behind pursuing this ambitious goal are multi-faceted:

- **Addressing Whole-Body Coordination and Balance:** Loco-manipulation with a bipedal robot is inherently challenging. It requires intricate whole-body coordination to simultaneously manipulate an object (which changes the robot's mass distribution and dynamics) and maintain balance, especially when moving. Traditional control methods often struggle with this complexity, making it a prime candidate for data-driven learning.
- **Generalization through Learning:** A key advantage of reinforcement learning is its potential to facilitate generalization. By training in varied simulated environments, the learned policies can potentially adapt to boxes of different sizes, masses, initial poses (position and orientation), and locations, which would be difficult and time-consuming to program manually.
- **Defining "Good" Motion for Complex Tasks:** Unlike simple locomotion, there isn't a single "best" way to pick up and move a box. The desired motion should be smooth, non-aggressive, robust, and safe. Designing such intricate motion profiles and providing a dense reward signal to guide learning is a significant challenge in itself, which the authors aim to overcome through careful reward function design.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The ultimate motivation is to achieve successful transfer of learned skills from simulation to real-world hardware. While RL has shown great promise in simulation, robust sim-to-real transfer for complex, contact-rich tasks on humanoids remains a grand challenge. Demonstrating this for loco-manipulation would mark a significant milestone.
- **Advancing Humanoid Autonomy:** By enabling humanoids to perform complex loco-manipulation tasks, this work contributes directly to the broader goal of creating more versatile and autonomous robots capable of operating in human environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a modular, learning-based approach that breaks down the complex box loco-manipulation task into a series of interconnected, individually learned skills.

**Task Decomposition and Skill Transitions:**
The full loco-manipulation task is decomposed into five distinct behaviors, each governed by its own control policy:
1. **WALK:** Basic walking skill.
2. **STAND:** Transitioning from walking to a stable standing position.
3. **PICKUP:** Picking up a box from a given position.
4. **WALKWITHBOX:** Walking while holding a box.
5. **STANDWITHBOX:** Transitioning to standing while holding a box.

These policies are designed to transition sequentially (e.g., WALK -> STAND -> PICKUP -> STANDWITHBOX -> WALKWITHBOX -> STANDWITHBOX for setting down the box), with specific allowed transitions shown in Fig. 2. To ensure smooth transitions, actions between policies are linearly interpolated over 10 timesteps during execution and training.

**Learning Framework:**
- **Policy Architecture:** Each policy is represented by an LSTM neural network, featuring two 128-dimensional recurrent hidden layers.
- **Input and Output:** The input to each policy comprises a 67-dimensional robot state vector (common to all policies) and a policy-specific command vector (e.g., target velocities for locomotion, box parameters and target height for manipulation). The policy operates at 50Hz and outputs PD setpoint targets for the Digit robot's 20 actuated degrees of freedom (DoFs). These setpoints are then fed to a low-level PD controller running at 2kHz with fixed gains.
- **Training Environment:** Policies are trained in the MuJoCo physics engine. The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used for training, incorporating a clipped objective and gradient clipping.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To mitigate the reality gap, the authors employ dynamics randomization during training. Parameters like body mass, joint damping, ground friction, and body center of mass location are randomized within specified ranges (Table I). Instead of a complex state estimator, they use the true simulation state perturbed with random noise as input. Training is distributed across 80 CPU cores.

**Reward Function Design (for PICKUP Policy - most detailed):**
A critical aspect of learning complex behaviors is the design of effective reward functions. Each reward function is composed of a sum of negative exponential terms `R = Σ w_i * exp(-r_i)`, where `r_i` represents a cost.
The PICKUP policy's reward function `R = R_traj + R_box + R_stand + R_reg` comprises:
- **`R_traj` (Hand Trajectory):** Rewards minimizing distance to a pre-defined hand position trajectory and maintaining a zero roll orientation for hands. This trajectory guides the hands through a "contact phase" (moving to the box's sides) and a "lift phase" (moving the box to a target position). A key finding from ablation studies is the significant speed-up in learning when using this guidance.
- **`R_box` (Box Interaction):** Rewards proper interaction with the box, including:
 - `r_contact`: Encourages hands to make contact with the box only after a specified `t_contact` time, preventing premature interaction.
 - `r_box pos` / `r_box orient`: Rewards moving the box to the target location with a flat orientation.
 - `r_table`: Penalizes contact forces between the box and the table once lifted.
- **`R_stand` (Robot Stability):** Rewards stable standing by maintaining:
 - `r_CoP`: Center of Pressure in the middle of the support polygon.
 - `r_base orient` / `r_foot orient`: Upright torso and forward-facing feet.
 - `r_motor vel` / `r_feet vel`: Small motor and feet velocities.
- **`R_reg` (Regularization):** Encourages smooth motions and gentle interaction:
 - `r_action` / `r_torque`: Penalizes large changes in actions and high torques.
 - `r_hand force`: Penalizes excessive force applied by hands on the box.
 - `r_box acc`: Penalizes high acceleration of the box.
- **Sparse Penalties and Termination Conditions:** Penalties are applied for self-collision or excessive hand velocity. Termination conditions are designed to guide exploration by ending episodes if critical stability or contact conditions are violated (e.g., robot falls, box drops, hands don't contact box in time).

**Action Space for PICKUP Policy:**
Crucially, the PICKUP policy uses a *relative* action space where the policy output is added to the *current motor positions*, rather than a static "neutral" offset. Ablation studies reveal this significantly accelerates learning for manipulation tasks, as the desired motion deviates greatly from a fixed neutral pose.

**Locomotion and Standing Policies:**
These policies build upon prior work.
- **WALK / STAND:** Reward functions adapted to keep hands in a neutral position.
- **WALKWITHBOX / STANDWITHBOX:** Similar to their non-box counterparts, but include additional `R_box` terms (box height, orientation, force, hand roll) to maintain the box in the desired pose. They also include a termination condition if the robot loses contact with the box.

**Scenario and Domain Randomization (for all policies):**
In addition to general dynamics randomization, specific parameters are randomized for each task:
- **PICKUP:** Box dimensions (20-45cm), mass (1-10kg), starting pose (position, yaw), and target location. The policy's internal estimate of box mass, dimensions, and starting location are also randomized (e.g., ±0.5kg for mass).
- **WALKWITHBOX / STANDWITHBOX:** Randomization of commanded velocities, turning rates, and box height, along with box mass and dimensions for the policy's input.
- **Initial State Generation:** Policies are trained sequentially, with preceding successful policies generating diverse initial states for subsequent policies (e.g., successful WALK episodes provide initial states for STAND).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both simulation and real-world hardware experiments, demonstrating the efficacy and robustness of their learned loco-manipulation system.

**Simulation Results:**
- **Overall Policy Performance:** Quantitative evaluations in the MuJoCo simulator, averaged over 10,000 episodes, show high success rates and low errors for all five learned policies (Table IV).
 - `PICKUP`: 96.15% success rate with an average box position error of 7.93 cm.
 - `STAND`: 100% success rate.
 - `STANDWITHBOX`: 95.25% success rate with 1.64 cm error.
 - `WALK`: 96.1% success rate.
 - `WALKWITHBOX`: 100% success rate with 2.39 cm error.
 These results confirm the policies' robustness to randomization in scenario distributions and initial states.

- **Limits of PickUp Policy (Generalization):** The `PICKUP` policy demonstrated remarkable generalization capabilities beyond its training distribution (Table V). It could successfully pick up boxes:
 - Up to 22.9 kg (trained on 1-10 kg).
 - With sizes up to 64 cm (trained on 20-45 cm).
 - From x-displacements up to 56 cm (trained on 35-50 cm), y-displacements up to 41 cm (trained on ±30 cm), and z-displacements up to 130 cm (trained on 0-130 cm, showing robustness to vertical variations).
 - With rotations up to 45 degrees (trained on ±22.5 degrees).
 This highlights the effectiveness of the RL approach and domain randomization in learning broadly applicable skills.

- **Ablation Studies on PickUp Policy:**
 - **Effect of Hand Trajectory Reward:** Removing the explicit hand trajectory tracking reward (instead using only the box's face center as a target) significantly hampered learning efficiency. Policies without this guidance required approximately double the number of samples to achieve similar reward levels (Fig. 4). This indicates the value of structured guidance in complex manipulation reward design.
 - **Effect of Relative Action Space:** The `PICKUP` policy's use of a "relative" action space (policy output added to current joint positions) was critical. Training with a "fixed offset" action space (output added to a static neutral pose), akin to locomotion policies, resulted in substantially slower learning, comparable to removing the hand trajectory guidance (Fig. 4). This suggests that for tasks involving large deviations from a neutral posture, a relative action space is more appropriate.

**Hardware Results (Sim-to-Real Transfer):**
- The trained policies achieved successful sim-to-real transfer on the Digit humanoid robot. The full loco-manipulation task, encompassing walking, picking up, carrying, and setting down boxes, was demonstrated on physical hardware.
- The system successfully handled boxes of varying masses (1kg to 8kg) and sizes, starting both on the ground and on tables.
- The authors state that this is, to their knowledge, the first demonstration of a learned controller achieving a loco-manipulation task on real-world humanoid hardware.

**Sim-to-Real Challenges:**
- Despite overall success, the authors observed sim-to-real issues, primarily policies leaning towards one direction on hardware. This was attributed to discrepancies in IMU/orientation estimates between simulation and reality.
- To counteract this, a "center-of-mass trim" (an offset to the orientation input) was required. This trim was policy-specific and needed adjustment for heavier boxes (e.g., 8kg), where a forward lean was observed. This suggests potential force or contact inconsistencies between simulation and hardware that still require refinement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in the field of humanoid robotics and reinforcement learning, with several profound implications and potential impacts:

**Novelty and State-of-the-Art Advancement:**
The most critical significance lies in the **first reported demonstration of a fully learned controller for humanoid loco-manipulation performing on real-world hardware via a sim-to-real approach.** This achievement bypasses many limitations of traditional model-based control (which often lead to slow, conservative motions) and imitation learning (which relies on potentially flawed expert demonstrations). It represents a crucial step towards truly autonomous, agile, and versatile humanoid robots capable of interacting with and manipulating objects in dynamic environments.

**Overcoming Foundational Challenges in Robotics:**
The work directly addresses one of the "grand challenges" in bipedal robotics: simultaneously maintaining balance and coordinating whole-body movement for object manipulation. Humanoids are inherently unstable, and introducing external loads or complex motions dramatically increases this challenge. The learned policies demonstrate robust solutions to this problem, handling varied box properties without explicitly programmed stability mechanisms for each scenario.

**Methodological Contributions:**
- **Effective Reward Function Design:** The paper provides a clear blueprint for designing complex reward functions for non-periodic, contact-rich manipulation tasks. The use of phase-based rewards, explicit contact timing, and guided hand trajectories proved instrumental in accelerating learning and achieving successful behaviors.
- **Understanding Action Spaces:** The finding that a "relative" action space is significantly more effective for learning complex manipulation movements compared to fixed-offset action spaces is a valuable insight for future RL-based robotics research.
- **Modular Skill-Based Learning:** Decomposing the complex task into a set of individually learned, transitionable skills offers a scalable approach to developing increasingly complex behaviors for humanoids.

**Practical Implications for Humanoid Deployment:**
This research moves humanoids closer to practical deployment in tasks requiring human-like dexterity and mobility. Imagine humanoids assisting in warehouses, performing maintenance, or aiding in disaster response – tasks that often involve moving and manipulating objects in unstructured, dynamic environments. The ability to autonomously pick up and transport objects of varying characteristics is fundamental to such applications.

**Future Research Directions:**
The identified sim-to-real issues, such as orientation estimation discrepancies and force inconsistencies, provide concrete avenues for future work. Enhancing domain randomization techniques (e.g., more sophisticated randomization of sensor noise, contact properties) and integrating real-world data directly into the learning process (e.g., through techniques like system identification or online adaptation) are critical next steps to bridge the remaining reality gap. The authors also highlight the need to integrate higher-level planning, navigation, and vision systems to move beyond human-operated control and reliance on markers, paving the way for truly autonomous loco-manipulation.

In conclusion, this paper not only presents an impressive technical demonstration but also contributes significant methodological insights, pushing the frontier of what learned control can achieve for humanoid robots in real-world manipulation tasks.
