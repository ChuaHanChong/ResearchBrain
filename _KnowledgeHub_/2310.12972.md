# CCIL: Continuity-based Data Augmentation for Corrective Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> CCIL improves imitation-learning robustness by synthesizing corrective labels from expert demonstrations alone. It fits a residual dynamics model regularized toward local Lipschitz continuity, then solves root-finding problems to invent out-of-distribution states whose expert or slightly disturbed actions return the agent to expert states, with rejection sampling bounding label error. Augmenting behavior cloning this way wins on 10 of 14 simulated robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning needs broad data coverage; policies act unpredictably under sensor noise, disturbances and compounding errors once they reach states expert demonstrations never covered.
- Classic augmentation demands an interactive expert or hand-specified domain invariances, information that is burdensome, expensive or practically infeasible to supply across diverse robotic domains.
- Covariate-shift remedies lean on extras: extra environment sampling, ground-truth rewards, known dynamics or abundant offline data; earlier learned-model augmentation offered no guarantee on label quality.

## Method

- **CCIL**'s ==corrective-label formulation==: state-action-state triplets whose action drives an out-of-distribution state back into expert data support, quality defined by bounded error of the approximate dynamics model.
- ==Locally Lipschitz dynamics learning==: ==residual model== trained with mean-squared error plus a sampling-based ==penalty on local Lipschitz violations==, keeping continuity where data permits and discarding discontinuous regions.
- Two label generators, ==BackTrack== and ==DisturbedAction==, solved by a ==Backward Euler root-finder==, plus ==rejection sampling== that drops labels beyond a distance threshold to control the error bound.

## Results

- On Pendulum, generated labels had empirical L2 error **0.02367** against analytic ground-truth dynamics, inside the theoretical bound of **0.065**; an unregularized dynamics model gave weaker gains.
- F1tenth LiDAR racing: **56.4%** success and **0.75** average score versus **39.3%**/**0.62** for NoisyBC and **31.9%**/**0.58** for behavior cloning, while MOREL and MILO scored **0%**.
- Under sensor and actuator noise CCIL led **4 of 8** MuJoCo and MetaWorld tasks (Hopper **2631** versus **1984** for BC) and was best on **10 of 14** tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local continuity of physical dynamics is a nearly free prior: it licenses trusting a learned model a bounded distance beyond its training support.
> - Enforce Lipschitz bounds locally rather than globally so contact discontinuities get skipped instead of smoothed away; naive dynamics models degrade generated-label quality.
> - Corrective labels only stabilize the agent toward demonstrated states, so they bound drift but cannot guarantee optimality, and evidence here is simulation-only with low-dimensional states.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL) is a prevalent approach for training robotic policies, particularly through methods like behavior cloning (BC), which leverages expert demonstrations. However, a significant challenge in real-world robotics applications is the requirement for extensive, comprehensive datasets to ensure robust policy performance. Such datasets are often difficult and expensive to acquire. Furthermore, robotic policies trained via IL can exhibit unpredictable or unsafe behavior when encountering states that deviate from the expert demonstrations, a phenomenon known as covariate shift. This shift can arise from sensor noise, environmental stochasticity, external disturbances, or compounding errors. Addressing this robustness issue is critical for the widespread deployment of IL in robotics.

Existing strategies to enhance robustness in IL typically involve augmenting the training dataset. These strategies often fall into categories:
1. **Interactive Experts**: Methods like DAGGER (Ross et al., 2011) or DART (Laskey et al., 2017) require an expert to provide corrective actions in out-of-distribution states encountered during the learning process. This can be burdensome and expensive.
2. **Domain-Specific Invariances**: Techniques leverage known invariances of the system (e.g., image transformations in self-driving cars, Bojarski et al., 2016) to generate synthetic data. These methods are limited by their reliance on domain-specific knowledge, which may not generalize across diverse tasks.
3. **Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) / Generative Models**: Approaches such as GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016) or MILO (Chang et al., 2021) aim to minimize distribution divergence or learn dynamics models using additional offline data or interaction with the environment. Offline RL methods often require access to a reward function, and dynamics model learning can demand abundant, high-fidelity data.

Behavior cloning, which assumes only access to expert demonstrations, remains a baseline solution due to its simplicity and applicability across various domains where other information is scarce. The work presented in this paper, CCIL, positions itself within this landscape by focusing on enhancing the robustness of IL agents that rely solely on expert demonstrations. It proposes a data augmentation method that does not require interactive experts, domain-specific invariances, or additional offline datasets beyond the initial expert demonstrations. Instead, CCIL leverages the inherent local continuity of physical system dynamics—a feature often assumed in classical control but less explored in data-driven IL for data augmentation. By focusing on this property, CCIL aims to provide a more generally applicable and less data-intensive approach to generating corrective labels that guide agents back to expert data support, thereby mitigating covariate shift and improving robustness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness of imitation learning (IL) agents, particularly in robotics, against compounding errors and environmental disturbances. This objective stems from the observation that IL policies, especially those trained with behavior cloning (BC), often perform poorly when confronted with states not perfectly represented in the expert demonstration dataset. Such out-of-distribution states can lead to policy failures, making real-world deployment challenging.

The core motivation is to overcome the limitations of existing data augmentation and robustness-enhancing techniques in IL. These limitations include:
1. **High Cost of Interactive Experts**: Many methods require an expert to intervene and provide corrective actions during policy execution, which is time-consuming and expensive.
2. **Reliance on Domain-Specific Invariances**: Other methods depend on pre-defined knowledge about a system's invariances (e.g., how an image transforms under certain movements), limiting their general applicability across diverse robotic tasks.
3. **Additional Data or Reward Functions**: Some advanced techniques, such as those in offline reinforcement learning, may necessitate large amounts of additional offline interaction data or knowledge of a ground truth reward function, which are often unavailable in the imitation learning setting.

To address these challenges, the authors identify an under-exploited property of dynamic systems: local continuity. Even complex physical systems, governed by physical laws, exhibit local continuity, meaning that small changes in state or action generally result in small, predictable changes in the subsequent state. While discontinuities may exist in specific regions (e.g., due to contact events), the presence of locally continuous regions offers a valuable opportunity.

The key insight is to leverage this local continuity to synthesize "corrective labels." These labels are designed to guide an agent, when it encounters an unfamiliar or out-of-distribution state, back towards the manifold of expert-demonstrated states. The rationale is that if a dynamics model can be learned to exhibit local Lipschitz continuity around the expert data, it can reliably predict transitions and, more importantly, "backtrack" or "correct" states and actions within this locally continuous neighborhood. This allows for the generation of synthetic data that extends the support of the training distribution, making the policy more resilient to perturbations.

Therefore, the specific objectives are:
- To formally define "corrective labels" that facilitate recovery from out-of-distribution states back to expert data support.
- To develop a practical algorithm, CCIL, that learns locally continuous dynamics models from expert demonstrations.
- To leverage these learned models to generate high-quality corrective labels with theoretical guarantees on their accuracy, even in regions slightly beyond the original expert data.
- To empirically validate the effectiveness of training IL agents with these CCIL-generated labels across diverse robotic domains, including scenarios with potential discontinuities, to demonstrate improved robustness and performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CCIL (Continuity-based data augmentation for Corrective labels for Imitation Learning) framework comprises three main steps: learning a locally continuous dynamics model, generating corrective labels, and augmenting the expert dataset for policy training.

#### 3.1. Corrective Labels Formulation

The paper formally defines a **corrective label** as a triplet $(s_G, a_G, s^*)$, where $s_G$ is a starting state, $a_G$ is a corrective action, and $s^*$ is a target expert state. The condition for a corrective label is that executing action $a_G$ from state $s_G$ brings the agent to state $s^*$ within a bounded error $\epsilon_c$, i.e., $||[s_G + f(s_G, a_G)] - s^*|| \le \epsilon_c$, where $f$ is the true dynamics function. The goal is to find such labels for out-of-distribution states $s_G$.
Since the true dynamics $f$ are unknown, an approximate dynamics model $\hat{f}$ is used. A corrective label generated with $\hat{f}$ is considered "high-quality" if the approximate dynamics function $\hat{f}$ also has a bounded error with respect to the ground truth dynamics $f$ at the state-action pair $(s_G, a_G)$, i.e., $||f(s_G, a_G) - \hat{f}(s_G, a_G)|| \le \epsilon_{co}$. This ensures that the generated labels are reliable.

#### 3.2. Learning Locally Continuous Dynamics Models from Data

The core of CCIL relies on learning a dynamics model $\hat{f}$ that exhibits local Lipschitz continuity from expert demonstrations $D^* = \{(s_j^*, a_j^*, s_{j+1}^*)\}$. The dynamics model predicts the state residual, $\hat{f}(s_t^*, a_t^*) \rightarrow s_{t+1}^* - s_t^*$.
The base learning objective is a Mean Squared Error (MSE) regression:
$\min_{\hat{f}} E_{s_j^*, a_j^*, s_{j+1}^* \sim D^*} [ ||\hat{f}(s_j^*, a_j^*) + s_j^* - s_{j+1}^*|| ]$.
To ensure local continuity and enable extrapolation beyond training data, this objective is modified. The paper highlights a **sampling-based penalty** approach:
$\min_{\hat{f}} E_{s_j^*, a_j^*, s_{j+1}^* \sim D^*} [ MSE + \lambda \cdot \mathbf{1}_{||\hat{f}'(s_j^*, a_j^*)|| > L} ]$
where $\hat{f}'(s_j^*, a_j^*)$ is an estimate of the local Lipschitz constant, approximated by perturbing data points with small Gaussian noise $\Delta_s$. This penalty term enforces a local Lipschitz constraint around expert data points, promoting smoothness in the learned dynamics model while remaining predictive of expert transitions. The paper also discusses alternative methods in the appendix, such as spectral normalization for global Lipschitz bounds, and techniques using slack variables or weighted losses to accommodate for inherent discontinuities in some physical systems. For the practical algorithm, the sampling-based penalty is primarily used.

#### 3.3. Generating High-Quality Corrective Labels

With a locally continuous dynamics model $\hat{f}$, CCIL generates corrective labels using two techniques, both relying on solving a root-finding problem:

1. **BackTrack Label**: Given an expert state-action pair $(s_t^*, a_t^*)$ leading to $s_{t+1}^*$, this technique searches for an alternative starting state $s_G$ such that executing the expert action $a_t^*$ from $s_G$ would lead to $s_t^*$. The optimization problem is: $\min_{s_G} ||s_G + \hat{f}(s_G, a_t^*) - s_t^*||$.
 Theorem 4.2 provides a theoretical bound on the error $||f(s_G, a_t^*) - \hat{f}(s_G, a_t^*)||$ as $\epsilon + (K_1+K_2) ||s_G - s_t^*||$, where $\epsilon$ is the dynamics model training error, and $K_1, K_2$ are Lipschitz constants for $\hat{f}$ and $f$ respectively. This bound holds when $s_G$ is in the neighborhood of local continuity.

2. **DisturbedAction Label**: Given an expert tuple $(s_t^*, a_t^*, s_{t+1}^*)$, this technique samples a small action noise $\Delta$ to create a disturbed action $a_G = a_t^* + \Delta$. It then searches for an imaginary previous state $s_G$ such that executing $a_G$ from $s_G$ would lead to the expert state $s_{t+1}^*$. The optimization problem is: $\min_{s_G} ||s_G + \hat{f}(s_G, a_t^* + \Delta) - s_{t+1}^*||$.
 Theorem 4.3 bounds the error $||f(s_G, a_t^* + \Delta) - \hat{f}(s_G, a_t^* + \Delta)||$ by $K_A||\Delta|| + (1+K_S) ||s_G - s_t^*|| + ||\epsilon_{opt}||$, where $K_A, K_S$ are Lipschitz constants for action and state respectively, and $\epsilon_{opt}$ is the error from the root solver.

Both root-finding problems are solved iteratively using a Backward Euler-like update, $s_G \leftarrow s^* - \hat{f}(s^*, a_G)$, to find $s_G$ given $a_G$ and $s^*$.

#### 3.4. Rejection Sampling

To ensure high-quality labels and control the error bound, generated labels are rejected if the resulting state $s_G$ is too far from the original expert state $s^*$, i.e., $||s_G - s^*|| > \epsilon_{rej}$, where $\epsilon_{rej}$ is a hyper-parameter. This step filters out labels from regions where the model might be less reliable.

#### 3.5. CCIL Algorithm Summary

The practical CCIL algorithm consists of:
1. **Learn Dynamics $\hat{f}$**: Train a neural network-based dynamics model on expert data, incorporating a loss term to enforce local Lipschitz continuity (e.g., sampling-based penalty).
2. **Generate Labels**: For each expert demonstration, apply either the BackTrack or DisturbedAction technique, solving the corresponding root-finding problem to obtain candidate $(s_G, a_G)$ pairs.
3. **Filter and Augment**: Apply rejection sampling based on $\epsilon_{rej}$ to select high-quality corrective labels. Combine these generated labels $D_G$ with the original expert dataset $D^*$ to form an augmented dataset.
4. **Policy Training**: Train a standard imitation learning policy (e.g., Behavior Cloning) on the augmented dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of CCIL was evaluated across 14 robotic tasks in simulation, spanning classical control, drone flying, high-dimensional car navigation, legged locomotion, and tabletop manipulation. Comparisons were made against Behavior Cloning (BC), NoisyBC, MILO, and MOREL, with the latter requiring access to a reward function.

**Q1. Empirical Verification of Theoretical Contributions on Label Quality:**
- **Pendulum Task**: The study utilized the classic Pendulum task, where ground truth dynamics are analytically computable, to assess the quality of generated labels. Visualizations showed that generated states were outside the expert data support but within a reasonable neighborhood.
- **Quantitative Error**: The empirical error for generated labels, measured as the average L2 norm distance to the target expert state, was found to be 0.02367. This value was observed to be within the proposed theoretical bound of 0.065, calculated using the derived error bounds (Theorem 4.2). This finding provides empirical support for the theoretical guarantees on the quality of CCIL's generated labels.

**Q2. Handling Discontinuities in Environmental Dynamics:**
- **Discontinuous Pendulum**: A variant of the Pendulum task with an explicit discontinuity (bouncing the ball back at certain angles) was introduced.
- **Performance with Discontinuity**: CCIL improved BC performance even in the presence of this discontinuity. While the margin of improvement was smaller than in the continuous Pendulum, it indicated a degree of robustness.
- **Ablation Study**: An ablation using a naive dynamics model (without explicit Lipschitz continuity enforcement) performed worse than CCIL, highlighting the importance of explicitly enforcing local Lipschitz continuity for reliable corrective label generation in both continuous and discontinuous environments.
- **High-Dimensional Inputs (F1tenth Racing)**: The F1tenth racing car simulator, using high-dimensional LiDAR inputs, presented dynamics with potentially complex discontinuities. CCIL demonstrated an empirical advantage, achieving a higher success rate and better scores compared to baselines, suggesting its capability to handle complex, implicitly discontinuous environments.
- **Locomotion and Manipulation (MuJoCo & MetaWorld)**: Tasks involving contact dynamics (e.g., Hopper, Walker2D, Ant, HalfCheetah, CoffeePull) inherently contain discontinuities. CCIL consistently outperformed or achieved comparable performance to vanilla BC across these tasks, even with added Gaussian noise to observations and actions, indicating its applicability in domains with varying forms of discontinuity.

**Q3. Impact on Imitation Learning Agent Performance:**
- **Pendulum (Continuous & Discontinuous)**: CCIL improved BC performance in both continuous and discontinuous Pendulum tasks, demonstrating its ability to enhance learning with the same amount of initial expert data.
- **F1tenth Racing**: CCIL achieved the highest success rate (56.4%) and average score (0.75) among all methods, outperforming BC (31.9% success, 0.58 score) and other baselines, indicating better performance and fewer crashes in a high-dimensional, complex driving scenario.
- **Drone Navigation**: In high-frequency and noise-sensitive drone tasks (hover, circle, fly-through-gate), CCIL significantly outperformed all baselines. For example, in the "FlyThrough" task, CCIL achieved a score of -0.78E8, while BC achieved -1.06E8, and other baselines performed even worse, showcasing its ability to enhance performance and robustness to observation and action noises.
- **Locomotion and Manipulation**: Across 8 tasks, CCIL was the leading agent in 4 tasks (Hopper, Walker2D, HalfCheetah, CoffeePull). In the remaining tasks, it achieved performance comparable to or better than vanilla behavior cloning. Notably, CCIL generally surpassed other model-based baselines like MILO and MOREL, which often struggled (e.g., MOREL's scores in MuJoCo tasks were significantly lower than BC).

**Summary of Findings:**
The experimental results indicate that CCIL can empirically verify its theoretical bounds on label quality. It demonstrates robustness in environments with both continuous and explicit/implicit discontinuities. Furthermore, training with CCIL-generated labels consistently improved the performance of imitation learning agents, outperforming behavior cloning in 10 out of 14 tasks and achieving comparable results in the others, without requiring additional data or assumptions beyond expert demonstrations and local continuity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper introduces CCIL as a method to enhance the robustness of imitation learning (IL) agents, particularly in robotic applications. Its significance stems from several aspects:

1. **Addressing Covariate Shift with Minimal Assumptions**: A central challenge in IL is covariate shift, where an agent encounters states unseen in expert demonstrations, leading to performance degradation. CCIL provides a mechanism to generate corrective data for these out-of-distribution states. Unlike many existing methods, it does not require an interactive expert, domain-specific invariances, or additional offline datasets beyond the initial expert demonstrations. This lean assumption profile broadens its applicability to domains where such extra information is unavailable or prohibitively expensive.

2. **Leveraging Local Continuity in Dynamics**: The core insight of exploiting local continuity in environmental dynamics offers a principled approach to data augmentation. While local Lipschitz continuity is a fundamental assumption in classical control theory, its application to robustifying data-driven imitation learning without pre-specified models or reward functions is a contribution. The theoretical guarantees on the quality of generated labels provide a foundation for trusting the augmented data.

3. **General Applicability Across Diverse Robotic Tasks**: The extensive empirical validation across 14 tasks in 4 distinct robotic domains (classic control, drones, cars with high-dimensional sensors, legged locomotion, and manipulation) suggests the method's versatility. Its consistent performance improvements over baseline behavior cloning, even in the presence of varying forms of discontinuity, indicates its potential for broad utility in robotics.

4. **Practical Algorithm for Data Augmentation**: CCIL provides a practical, three-step algorithm (learning dynamics, generating labels, augmenting data) that can be integrated with standard behavior cloning. The use of techniques like sampling-based penalties for Lipschitz continuity and Backward Euler for root-finding makes the approach tangible for implementation.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Increased Robustness of IL Policies**: By enabling IL agents to recover from deviations, CCIL can lead to more reliable and safer robotic policies, reducing the risk of failures during deployment in real-world, noisy, and dynamic environments.
- **Reduced Data Requirements**: For applications where expert data is scarce, CCIL offers a way to intelligently expand the training dataset's support, potentially reducing the overall amount of costly expert demonstrations needed.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The paper explicitly outlines limitations and future research directions, such as quantifying local continuity, optimizing dynamics model training for continuity, handling complex visual discontinuities, and real-world robotics applications. This indicates the potential for CCIL to serve as a building block for further advancements in robust and data-efficient imitation learning.

**Limitations Acknowledged by Authors:**
- **Optimality of Corrective Labels**: The generated corrective labels aim to constrain the learner within the expert distribution, acting like a stabilizing controller. However, they do not guarantee an optimal solution. There is a possibility that following these labels might confine the agent to a limited subset of expert-visited states, potentially hindering optimal task completion.
- **Complexity of Discontinuities**: While CCIL demonstrated resilience to some discontinuities, its application to domains with highly intricate or frequent discontinuities (e.g., pixel-based images) remains an open challenge. Solutions may require robust compact state representations or methods for enforcing smoothness in latent spaces.

Overall, CCIL presents a method for enhancing imitation learning robustness by synthesizing corrective data based on the principle of local continuity in system dynamics. This approach holds promise for making imitation learning more practical and deployable in a wider range of real-world robotic applications.
