# Sim-to-Real Learning for Bipedal Locomotion Under Unsensed Dynamic Loads

> [!summary] Summary
> This work trains reinforcement-learning locomotion policies for the bipedal robot Cassie carrying dynamic loads (tray box, dragged cart, swinging carrying pole, sloshing water jug) using only proprioception. An LSTM policy trained with PPO, periodic foot-cost rewards, and dynamics randomization needs no load sensing or reward redesign. Load-specific, bootstrapped, and single general-load policies all walk successfully and transfer to hardware, though the sim-to-real gap widens markedly.

## Problem

- Prior sim-to-real bipedal locomotion work targets varied terrain but ignores external loads that substantially alter whole-system dynamics during walking.
- Legged loco-manipulation studies mostly use quadrupeds with rigidly attached static weights, relying on static stability and treating the load as another rigid body.
- Bipedal load-carrying work separates walking from manipulation, uses statically stable slow gaits, or needs extra wrist-force and liquid-height sensing plus hand-built sloshing models.

## Method

- Base locomotion framework: two-layer 128-unit ==LSTM== policy trained by ==PPO== in MuJoCo, outputting 40 Hz joint setpoints to a 2 kHz ==PD controller==, with joint damping, mass, and friction ==dynamics randomization==.
- Reward replaces the prior probabilistic swing/stance weighting with a ==piecewise-linear periodic foot-cost clock== parameterized by swing ratio and cycle time, plus fixed speed-dependent gait heuristics.
- Five simulated loaded Cassie models (unloaded, tray box, dragged cart, carrying pole, spring-approximated water jug), trained as load-specific, bootstrapped-from-base, or one general policy sampling all loads equally.

## Results

- On the carrying pole, the base policy passes **0.14%** of command sets while the load-specific policy reaches **95.78%**; average push-force resistance more than doubles from **95 N** to **221 N**.
- The general load policy matches specialists on most loads (pass rates **0.83-1.0**) but loses roughly **30 N** force tolerance on hard loads and drops unloaded max speed to **2.6 m/s**.
- Bootstrapping from the unloaded policy saves about **100 million** samples at equal reward, with equal or better performance: cart max speed **3.2 m/s** versus **2.6 m/s** from scratch.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simply training inside the loaded dynamics suffices; extra sensing, reward shaping, and architecture changes are unnecessary when the load's effects reach proprioceptive state.
> - Pretrain-then-finetune transfers to control: an expert unloaded gait bootstraps loaded policies without catastrophic unlearning, even on loads where it initially falls immediately.
> - Dynamics randomization is nearing its limit; added moving bodies widen the sim-to-real gap, cutting hardware top speed to under half the simulated value.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in bipedal locomotion research have leveraged reinforcement learning (RL) and sim-to-real transfer techniques to develop controllers exhibiting notable robustness and agility across various terrains. These methods often involve training policies in simulation and then transferring them to physical robots, frequently employing techniques like dynamics randomization to bridge the fidelity gap between simulated and real-world physics. However, the majority of prior work in bipedal locomotion, including these recent successes, has predominantly focused on unloaded scenarios.

The current research positions itself by addressing a recognized gap: the problem of bipedal locomotion while carrying or interacting with a variety of external loads, particularly *dynamic* ones. Existing literature on loco-manipulation, while active, has largely concentrated on quadrupedal robots, which benefit from inherent static stability, or has treated loads as rigidly attached static masses, simplifying their dynamic effects. For bipedal robots, previous studies on carrying loads often rely on statically stable walking gaits, leading to slow movement, or employ sophisticated control architectures that require additional sensing capabilities for the load (e.g., force sensors, liquid wave height sensors, or approximate sloshing models). These approaches typically assume the load is either a fixed part of the robot or that its dynamic impact on the robot's center of gravity is not destabilizing enough to necessitate fundamental gait changes.

This work specifically investigates the capacity of RL and sim-to-real transfer to enable bipedal robots to handle diverse *dynamic* loads using only proprioceptive feedback, without requiring specialized load sensors. It builds upon established RL frameworks for bipedal locomotion, particularly those that utilize principled reward functions and dynamics randomization for sim-to-real transfer, and extends them to scenarios where the external object's internal dynamics significantly influence the robot's overall system dynamics. By demonstrating the limitations of unloaded policies in loaded contexts and developing methods to train robust loaded policies, this research contributes to enhancing the practical utility of bipedal robots in real-world applications that involve interaction with dynamic external objects. Furthermore, it identifies emergent challenges in sim-to-real transfer when confronted with increased dynamic complexity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the capabilities of reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer for robust bipedal locomotion under dynamic external loads, utilizing only proprioceptive feedback. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and questions:

Firstly, the broader motivation stems from the aim of developing practical robots capable of useful action in real-world environments. While significant progress has been made in bipedal locomotion, the ability to merely walk is insufficient for many practical applications. Robots are frequently required to handle, carry, or interact with various objects and loads. Current bipedal locomotion research, however, has largely overlooked these loaded scenarios, especially those involving dynamic loads.

Secondly, the authors highlight the distinction between static and dynamic loads. Dynamic loads, such as a pulled cart or a container of sloshing liquid, possess their own internal dynamics that can profoundly influence the robot's stability and movement, even when the robot is stationary. Managing these loads demands more than just robustness to disturbances in the robot's base dynamics; it necessitates the locomotion gait itself to actively control the external object. This introduces additional underconstrained degrees of freedom, making the control problem considerably more complex than with rigidly attached static masses.

Thirdly, a key desideratum is to achieve load-carrying capabilities without requiring additional, specialized load-sensing hardware. This would allow for simple and efficient swapping of different loads without reconfiguring the robot's sensing suite or wiring. The research explores whether a policy can learn to infer and adapt to load dynamics solely from its existing proprioceptive sensors (e.g., joint positions, velocities, pelvis orientation).

Fourthly, the work challenges the assumption that existing RL policies, trained for unloaded locomotion, would be sufficient to handle dynamic loads, perhaps by adopting slow, conservative gaits. Instead, the motivation is to achieve dynamic walking performance with loads, comparable to the agility demonstrated by recent unloaded controllers.

In light of these motivations, the specific objectives of the study include:
- **Evaluating the performance of existing unloaded RL policies:** To determine if prior policies trained for unloaded locomotion fail or degrade significantly when subjected to various dynamic loads.
- **Developing and evaluating load-specific policies:** To demonstrate that training policies directly in the context of specific dynamic loads, with minimal modifications to the learning setup, can result in successful and improved locomotion performance.
- **Comparing specialized versus general policies:** To assess the feasibility and performance trade-offs of training a single, general policy capable of handling multiple different dynamic loads, as opposed to multiple load-specific policies.
- **Investigating learning efficiency:** To determine if bootstrapping the learning process from a pre-trained unloaded policy can accelerate the acquisition of load-carrying skills without compromising performance.
- **Analyzing gait adaptations:** To identify and characterize the specific strategic changes in the robot's gait that policies learn to accommodate different types of dynamic loads.
- **Assessing sim-to-real transfer:** To conduct hardware experiments to evaluate the success and limitations of transferring simulation-trained loaded locomotion policies to a physical bipedal robot, specifically observing potential changes in the sim-to-real gap compared to unloaded scenarios.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aims to advance the understanding and capabilities of bipedal robots in practical, load-carrying applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adapts an existing reinforcement learning framework for bipedal locomotion, developed by Siekmann et al., to address scenarios involving dynamic loads. The core of the methodology involves training neural network policies in simulation and evaluating their transferability to a physical Cassie bipedal robot.

**A. Locomotion Learning Framework (Base Setup)**
The foundation of the control system is an LSTM neural network controller with two 128-dimensional recurrent hidden layers.
- **Inputs:** Robot state information (pelvis orientation, rotational velocity, joint positions and velocities), a clock signal (sine and cosine of desired cycle time), and desired forward velocity.
- **Outputs:** Joint position set points at 40 Hz, which are then passed to a fixed-gain PD controller operating at 2 kHz.
- **Training:** The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used for training in simulation. The simulation environment is built on `cassie-mujoco-sim` and the MuJoCo physics engine.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Dynamics randomization, including joint damping, joint mass, and ground friction, is applied during training to enhance policy robustness for hardware transfer, consistent with prior work.
- **Reward Function:** The reward structure, similar to, includes terms for minimizing error between commanded and actual velocity/orientation, penalizing pelvis acceleration, torque, and action differences, and encouraging alternating foot swing and stance. A piecewise linear periodic function replaces the probabilistic function for foot cost weighting, defined by swing ratio and cycle time.
- **Gait Parameters:** For simplification, the training focuses on fixed swing ratios and stepping frequencies for different speed ranges (0 to 4 m/s), with a fixed phase offset of 0.5 for symmetrical walking.

**B. Adapting for Dynamic Loads**
The primary adaptation for dynamic loads involves using specifically designed loaded robot models within the simulator. No additional load-sensing capabilities are introduced; the policies rely solely on proprioceptive feedback. Five distinct Cassie robot models are used, including the unloaded baseline and four qualitatively different dynamic loads:
1. **Unloaded Cassie:** The baseline model.
2. **Cassie Tray Box:** A 5 kg box freely moving on a tray attached to the pelvis, testing control of a free external object.
3. **Cassie Cart:** A cart attached by ropes to the pelvis, testing backwards pulling forces.
4. **Cassie Carrying Pole:** A pole attached to the pelvis with two 5 kg swinging weights on each end, introducing significant lateral and torsional forces.
5. **Cassie Water Jug:** A box rigidly attached to the pelvis containing "water" whose sloshing dynamics are approximated by a spring-mass system.

- **Reward Function Modifications for Loads:** For most loads (Unloaded, Cart, Carry Pole, Water Jug), the standard base walking reward function is used. For the Tray Box load, an additional reward component is included to penalize the distance between the box and the center of the tray, alongside a termination condition if the box's Z-height falls below 50 cm.

**C. Training Strategies**
The study explores three training strategies for load policies:
1. **Load-Specific Policies (from scratch):** Each load-specific policy is trained independently starting from a randomly initialized neural network.
2. **Load-Specific Policies (bootstrapped):** Learning is initialized using the weights of the pre-trained "base policy" (trained for unloaded locomotion), then fine-tuned for the specific load.
3. **General Load Policy:** A single policy is trained to handle all five load models simultaneously. During each training iteration, the sampled 50,000 timesteps are split evenly across all models. For this general policy, the base walking reward function is used for all environments, including the Tray Box, though the box-falling termination condition remains. This strategy is tested both from scratch and via bootstrapping from the base policy.

**D. Evaluation Metrics (Simulation)**
Walking performance under dynamic loads in simulation is assessed using four metrics:
- **Pass Rate:** The percentage of 5000 randomly sampled command sets (speed and orientation changes) that the robot successfully follows without falling.
- **Average Speed Error:** The average difference between desired and actual robot velocity for successful trials.
- **Average Force Resistance:** The maximum average external force (0.2-second impulse to the pelvis) the policy can resist before falling, averaged over 36 directions.
- **Max Speed:** The maximum forward speed the policy can achieve without falling.

**E. Gait Analysis**
Phase portraits of selected joint angles, such as the hip roll joints, are compared between the base policy and load-specific policies to identify and analyze learned gait adaptations.

**F. Hardware Evaluation**
Selected policies (Base, Tray Box, Carry Pole, and General Load) are transferred to a physical Cassie robot to validate sim-to-real performance. Tests involve walking in place, then gradually increasing speed until the robot falls or reaches its maximum stable speed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding bipedal locomotion under dynamic loads:

**A. Base vs. Specialized Policies (Simulation)**
- **Performance Degradation:** The base policy, trained for unloaded locomotion, exhibited significant performance degradation when evaluated on dynamic loads it had not encountered during training. For instance, its pass rate on the challenging Carry Pole load was 0.0014, indicating near-immediate failure.
- **Effectiveness of Load-Specific Training:** Training policies specifically for each dynamic load resulted in substantial performance improvements across all metrics. For the Carry Pole load, the pass rate improved to 0.9578 with a specialized policy.
- **Improved Robustness:** Load-specific policies consistently demonstrated higher average force resistance (10-20 N more on average) compared to the base policy on the respective loads. The Carry Pole specific policy more than doubled the base policy's force resistance.
- **Unexpected Benefits:** Some dynamic loads, such as the Cart load, showed a stabilizing effect on the gait, even for the base policy. The Water Jug specific policy achieved a lower average speed error than the base policy in the unloaded condition, suggesting that some loaded conditions could unintentionally lead to improved control.
- **Max Speed Trade-offs:** For Cart and Water Jug loads, the base policy sometimes achieved higher maximum speeds than the load-specific policies. This was hypothesized to be a result of the load-specific policies being more conservative due to termination conditions emphasizing stability over peak speed, a trade-off inherent in balancing high-performance and robustness in novel scenarios.

**B. Specialized vs. General Load Policies (Simulation)**
- **Viability of General Policies:** A single general load policy, trained on all five models, demonstrated comparable performance to the load-specific policies in most cases.
- **Performance Compromises:** The general policy exhibited higher average speed error and approximately 30 N lower push force resistance on more difficult loads like Tray Box and Carry Pole, indicating some compromise in specialized robustness.
- **Reduced Max Speed for Unloaded:** The general policy's maximum speed in the unloaded condition was lower (2.6 m/s) compared to the base policy (3.9 m/s), as it learned a gait robust to all loads, which inherently restricted performance in the least constrained scenario.
- **Enhanced General Robustness:** The general policy often achieved higher pass rates on other loads compared to both base and specialized policies, suggesting it gained a broader robustness from exposure to diverse dynamic conditions.

**C. Learning from Scratch vs. Bootstrapping (Simulation)**
- **Sample Efficiency:** Bootstrapping the learning process by initializing with a pre-trained unloaded policy significantly accelerated training. Bootstrapped policies reached similar reward levels using approximately 100 million fewer samples compared to policies trained from scratch.
- **Performance Equivalence:** Bootstrapped policies generally achieved comparable, and in some instances, even superior, walking performance (e.g., higher maximum speed, lower average speed error) to policies trained from scratch, without experiencing catastrophic unlearning.

**D. Gait Analysis (Simulation)**
- **Adaptive Strategies:** Comparison of hip roll joint phase portraits revealed distinct gait adaptations for different loads:
 - **Tray Box Policy:** Learned to take narrower steps (legs closer to the pelvis center). This strategy was inferred to enhance pelvis stability, thereby minimizing box movement and preventing it from falling.
 - **Carry Pole Policy:** Adopted wider steps. This was attributed to the need to resist larger lateral and torsional forces exerted by the swinging weights, requiring a broader stance for stability.

**E. Hardware Results (Sim-to-Real Transfer)**
- **Successful Transfer:** All tested policies (base, Tray Box, Carry Pole, and bootstrapped general load) successfully transferred to the physical Cassie robot, demonstrating stable walking for over a minute in trials.
- **Widened Sim-to-Real Gap:** A significant finding was that the presence of dynamic loads dramatically increased the sim-to-real gap.
 - The base unloaded policy achieved 3.0 m/s on hardware (sim: 3.9 m/s).
 - The Tray Box policy only reached 1.0 m/s on hardware (sim: 2.1 m/s).
 - The Carry Pole policy performed worse, achieving only 0.8 m/s on hardware (sim: 2.5 m/s).
- **Training Strategy and Transfer:** For the more challenging Carry Pole load, the bootstrapped general policy successfully transferred to hardware, whereas the general policy trained from scratch failed. This suggested that the initial period of training on the base (unloaded) model reduced noise and improved the transferability of the bootstrapped policy, particularly when the sim-to-real gap was larger.
- **Implications for Sim-to-Real:** The increased sim-to-real gap with dynamic loads indicates that dynamics randomization, a common sim-to-real solution, may be insufficient for highly dynamic and complex multi-body tasks. More sophisticated sim-to-real methods are likely necessary to achieve simulation-level performance on hardware for these challenging scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates several significant contributions to the field of bipedal robotics and reinforcement learning:

Firstly, it establishes the feasibility of training bipedal robots to locomote robustly under a variety of *dynamic* external loads, such as freely moving objects or swinging masses, using only proprioceptive feedback. This is a notable advancement as it expands the practical capabilities of bipedal robots beyond unloaded scenarios, addressing a critical, yet underexplored, aspect of real-world robot deployment. The ability to handle complex loads without requiring additional, specialized sensors simplifies hardware requirements and enhances operational flexibility.

Secondly, the work highlights the adaptability of reinforcement learning to learn complex, dynamic control strategies. The observation that policies develop distinct gait adaptations—such as narrower steps for balancing a tray or wider stances for counteracting swinging weights—illustrates the ability of RL to discover non-intuitive, task-specific behaviors directly from interaction with dynamic environments. This underscores RL's potential for generating robust and efficient control policies for highly nonlinear and underconstrained robotic systems.

Thirdly, the investigation into training strategies offers practical implications for robotic learning. The demonstration that bootstrapping from a pre-trained unloaded policy significantly accelerates learning for loaded tasks, without compromising performance, provides a method for more sample-efficient and rapid development of new robot skills. This reduces the computational resources and time typically required for training complex RL policies, facilitating faster iteration and deployment of robotic capabilities. The successful development of a single general load policy, albeit with some performance trade-offs, also indicates a potential pathway towards more versatile robots capable of adapting to a range of un-modeled or varied loads.

Finally, the hardware results, while demonstrating successful transfer, also expose a critical limitation: dynamic loads significantly widen the sim-to-real gap. This finding is significant as it challenges the sufficiency of current standard sim-to-real transfer methods, such as dynamics randomization, for tasks involving complex, multi-body dynamics. By identifying this gap, the research provides a clear impetus for future work in developing more sophisticated and effective sim-to-real techniques, which is crucial for translating simulation-level robotic performance to robust real-world applications.

In summary, this research advances the state of bipedal locomotion by enabling dynamic load handling, showcases the adaptive power of RL, offers efficient training strategies, and critically informs the ongoing challenge of sim-to-real transfer in complex robotic domains. These contributions pave the way for bipedal robots to undertake a broader array of practical tasks in human-centric environments, such as logistics, assistance, and physically interactive roles.
