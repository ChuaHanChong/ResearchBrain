# Small Batch Size Training for Language Models: When Vanilla SGD Works, and Why Gradient Accumulation Is Wasteful

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NYU and Columbia demonstrated that small batch sizes, even down to one, can stably and effectively train large language models when Adam's beta2 parameter is properly scaled, revealing that gradient accumulation is often inefficient and vanilla SGD can be competitive. This approach enables memory-efficient full fine-tuning on resource-constrained hardware, challenging the conventional necessity of large batches for LLM training.

## Problem

- Conventional wisdom in LLM training suggests that large batch sizes are crucial for stable and efficient training.
- Gradient accumulation is widely adopted to simulate large batch sizes, often assumed necessary despite its memory overheads.
- Small batch sizes were generally considered unstable or underperforming for LLMs, frequently necessitating complex adaptive optimizers like Adam.

## Method

- Conducted extensive empirical studies on ==decoder-only transformer== models ranging from **30M** to **4B** parameters across diverse datasets.
- Performed exhaustive hyperparameter tuning, especially for Adam's decay parameters (β1 and β2), introducing a =='moment half-life' metric== for principled scaling.
- Compared the performance and memory footprint of various optimizers (SGD, Adam, Adafactor, Muon) across a wide spectrum of batch sizes, incorporating ==bfloat16 training with stochastic rounding==.

## Results

- Small batch sizes, including batch size one, achieved performance on par with or superior to larger batch sizes when hyperparameters were correctly tuned.
- Vanilla SGD, even without momentum, performed competitively with sophisticated adaptive optimizers like Adam for models up to **1.3 billion** parameters when utilizing small batches.
- Gradient accumulation was found to be generally unnecessary and memory-inefficient, with direct small batch training recommended instead.
- Combining small batch sizes with memory-efficient optimizers like Adafactor enabled full fine-tuning with a memory footprint comparable to LoRA.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Small batch sizes exhibit significantly greater robustness to hyperparameter choices, offering a broader optimal performance region than large batches.
> - The optimal learning rate for small batches scales more slowly than the commonly assumed square-root rule.
> - Scaling Adam's β2 parameter by maintaining a constant 'token half-life' is critical for stabilizing small batch training, while β1 can often remain at its default value.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper challenges a widely held "conventional wisdom" in the domain of large language model (LLM) training: the belief that large batch sizes are crucial for stable and efficient training. For years, practitioners have scaled up batch sizes, often resorting to techniques like gradient accumulation to simulate massive batch sizes that exceed single-device memory limits. This trend has also fueled the development and adoption of sophisticated adaptive optimizers like Adam, which are often perceived as necessary for stable training at these large scales.

The existing literature is divided, with many works supporting the necessity of large batches and complex optimizers (e.g., Zhang et al., Shallue et al., Zhao et al.) and some noting instability or underperformance of small batches when standard hyperparameters are used (e.g., Filatov et al., Xiao). This paper meticulously re-evaluates these claims, arguing that previous conclusions about small batch instability were often artifacts of inadequate hyperparameter tuning, specifically failing to adjust Adam's decay rates (β1 and β2) with batch size.

The authors position their work against prior research by:
- **Contradicting instability claims:** They show that small batch sizes, down to one, can be entirely stable, provided Adam's β2 parameter is scaled correctly. This directly refutes the "U-shaped curve" phenomenon observed by Xiao where excessively small or large batch sizes underperformed.
- **Rehabilitating simple optimizers:** They challenge the notion that sophisticated optimizers are indispensable for LLMs, demonstrating that even vanilla Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) can be competitive at small batch sizes, in contrast to findings by Zhao et al. and others. This aligns partially with some older work on SGD for small-scale tasks (Shallue et al., Kidambi et al.) but extends it rigorously to modern LLMs.
- **Refining hyperparameter scaling:** While previous works (Porian et al., Zhang et al.) have noted the importance of adjusting β2 for small batches, this paper proposes a more principled scaling rule based on fixing the "token half-life" of the second moment, providing a concrete and generalizable heuristic.
- **Addressing memory constraints:** The paper also intersects with the growing interest in efficient LLM training and fine-tuning, particularly for memory-constrained environments. It offers an alternative to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA by showing that full fine-tuning can be memory-efficient with small batches and specific optimizers like Adafactor, without sacrificing performance.

Ultimately, this research seeks to reorient best practices for LLM training by advocating for the reconsideration of small batch sizes, not as a compromise, but as a potentially superior and more robust approach. It connects with the broader themes of understanding optimization landscapes in deep learning, the interplay between batch size and generalization, and the pursuit of more computationally and memory-efficient training paradigms for increasingly larger models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations driving this research are multifaceted, aiming to challenge established norms and offer practical, data-driven recommendations for language model training:

1. **Revisit and Validate Small Batch Training:** The core objective is to thoroughly investigate the efficacy and stability of small batch sizes, ranging down to batch size one, for both pre-training and fine-tuning large language models. The authors aim to determine if the conventional wisdom against small batches in LLMs holds under careful hyperparameter tuning.
2. **Propose and Validate a Hyperparameter Scaling Rule:** Motivated by observations that default Adam hyperparameters often lead to instability with small batches, a key objective is to develop a principled rule for scaling Adam's decay rates, particularly β2. The proposed rule focuses on maintaining a constant "token half-life" for the second moment across different batch sizes.
3. **Assess Optimizer Robustness and Performance:** The study seeks to systematically compare the performance and robustness of various optimizers (SGD, Adam, Adafactor, Muon) across a wide range of batch sizes. A specific motivation is to ascertain if simpler optimizers like vanilla SGD can be competitive with more sophisticated adaptive optimizers, especially in the small batch regime.
4. **Debunk the Necessity of Gradient Accumulation:** The paper explicitly aims to demonstrate that gradient accumulation, a common technique to simulate large batch sizes, is often "wasteful." By showing that small actual batch sizes can perform equally well or better, the authors motivate practitioners to reconsider its widespread use.
5. **Enable Memory-Efficient Full Fine-tuning:** A significant practical motivation is to find methods for fine-tuning large models in memory-constrained settings without resorting to parameter-efficient techniques like LoRA that can sometimes compromise performance. The objective is to combine the benefits of small batch sizes with memory-efficient optimizers to achieve full fine-tuning performance with a reduced memory footprint.
6. **Provide Practical Recommendations:** Ultimately, the researchers are motivated to translate their findings into clear, actionable recommendations for practitioners regarding optimal batch size selection, optimizer choice, and hyperparameter tuning strategies for modern language models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a rigorous empirical methodology, complemented by theoretical insights and practical considerations, to investigate the role of batch size in language model training.

1. **Model Architectures and Datasets:**
 - The study uses a range of decoder-only transformer models, from a small 30M parameter model to GPT-2 (124M), GPT-3 (1.3B), and Gemma 3 (4B).
 - These models incorporate modern architectural choices like rotary embeddings (RoPE), QK-normalization, RMSNorm, and GELU activations, ensuring relevance to current LLM practices.
 - Training is conducted on diverse datasets: FineWeb-Edu, C4, FineWeb, and MATH (for fine-tuning).
 - Models are mostly trained following Chinchilla scaling laws, with the 1.3B GPT-3 model being an exception, trained for 10B tokens.

2. **Optimizers Under Study:**
 - The experiments involve a comprehensive comparison of four optimizers: Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) (vanilla, without momentum), Adam, Adafactor, and Muon. This allows for a direct assessment of optimizer performance and the necessity of adaptive methods.

3. **Extensive Hyperparameter Tuning and Ablations:**
 - For the smaller 30M parameter model, the authors perform an exhaustive grid search over learning rates and Adam's decay parameters (β1 and β2) for each batch size (from 1 to 4096 tokens). This meticulous tuning is crucial for their central claim that small batch failures often stem from poor hyperparameter choices.
 - They introduce the concept of "moment half-life" (t1, t2) for Adam's decay rates, measured in tokens, as a more intuitive and scalable way to parameterize β1 and β2.
 - Based on these comprehensive searches, they derive and validate a principled scaling heuristic for β2: holding its token half-life fixed across batch sizes. This contrasts with common practice where β2 is often held constant.

4. **Validation at Scale:**
 - The derived hyperparameter scaling rules and general findings from the small-scale experiments are then tested on larger models (GPT-2 124M and GPT-3 1.3B) with FineWeb, and Gemma 3 (4B) for fine-tuning. This validates the generalizability of their insights to more realistic LLM training scenarios.
 - For the larger models, full grid search is computationally prohibitive, so they apply their derived heuristics and minimal learning rate sweeps for SGD and Adafactor, comparing against established baselines like AdamW with default settings.

5. **Memory Footprint Analysis:**
 - A significant part of the methodology involves analyzing the memory footprint of different optimizers and training configurations. They detail memory usage for model parameters, activations, optimizer states (first and second moments), and accumulated gradients.
 - This analysis directly supports their claims about gradient accumulation being wasteful and the memory efficiency benefits of Adafactor with small batch sizes for fine-tuning.

6. **Stochastic Rounding for Mixed Precision:**
 - The authors investigate the impact of mixed-precision training (bfloat16 weights) on small batch sizes, particularly the importance of stochastic rounding. They show that stochastic rounding is critical to prevent bias in weight updates when master weights are stored in bfloat16, thus enabling bfloat16 training to perform comparably to float32.

7. **Toy Example for Intuition:**
 - A simple 2D quadratic function is used to visually illustrate why momentum might be less necessary with small batch sizes and small learning rates, providing an intuitive explanation for one of their key findings.

8. **Computational Resources:**
 - All experiments were performed on Google's TPU v4 chips, highlighting the significant computational power required and available to the researchers. They quantify the total TPU v4 chip-hours expended, emphasizing the scale of their empirical validation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several counter-intuitive yet robust findings that challenge prevailing practices in language model training:

1. **Small Batch Sizes Enable Stable and Robust Training:**
 - Contrary to conventional wisdom, small batch sizes, down to batch size one, can train language models stably and achieve performance on par with or even better than larger batch sizes, provided hyperparameters are correctly tuned.
 - Small batch sizes exhibit significantly greater robustness to hyperparameter choices (learning rate, β1, β2) compared to large batch sizes. The optimal performance region for small batches is much broader, implying less need for exhaustive hyperparameter tuning.

2. **Simpler Optimizers Perform Competitively with Small Batches:**
 - The performance gap between different optimizers (SGD, Adam, Adafactor, Muon) shrinks dramatically at small batch sizes. All optimizers achieve similar optimal loss in this regime.
 - Notably, vanilla SGD, even without momentum, becomes competitive with sophisticated adaptive optimizers like Adam when paired with small batch sizes, even for models up to 1.3 billion parameters. This is a significant finding as SGD requires no optimizer state storage, offering memory advantages.

3. **Principled Adam Hyperparameter Scaling is Crucial:**
 - The commonly recommended square-root scaling of the learning rate with batch size does not align with empirical observations; the optimal learning rate scales more slowly.
 - While β1 = 0.9 (default) generally performs well across batch sizes, β2 must be scaled with batch size. The paper proposes and validates a heuristic: fixing the "token half-life" (t2) of the second moment estimate across batch sizes. This ensures stable and effective training, especially for small batches, where a fixed β2 would otherwise lead to suboptimal performance.

4. **Gradient Accumulation is Often Wasteful:**
 - The findings suggest that gradient accumulation, used to simulate larger batch sizes, is generally unnecessary and memory-inefficient for most practitioners. When a small batch size can be used directly with proper tuning, it avoids the memory overhead of storing accumulated gradients. The authors explicitly recommend against gradient accumulation unless training on multiple devices with bandwidth bottlenecks.

5. **Memory-Efficient Full Fine-tuning is Achievable:**
 - For memory-constrained fine-tuning, combining a small batch size with an optimizer that has a small state (like Adafactor) provides performance benefits akin to full parameter fine-tuning while maintaining a memory footprint comparable to LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation). This offers a compelling alternative to PEFT methods, which often trade performance for memory efficiency.
 - The study also highlights the importance of stochastic rounding when using bfloat16 weights in such memory-efficient setups, especially with small batch sizes, to prevent performance degradation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has profound implications for the way large language models are trained and fine-tuned, potentially leading to a paradigm shift in best practices:

1. **Re-evaluation of LLM Training Paradigms:** By meticulously demonstrating the viability and advantages of small batch sizes, the paper challenges a foundational assumption in LLM training. This could lead to a widespread re-evaluation of current practices, moving away from an automatic embrace of large batch sizes and gradient accumulation.

2. **Increased Accessibility and Reduced Resource Requirements:** The finding that vanilla SGD can be competitive with small batches means that simpler, state-free optimizers can be effectively used. This significantly reduces memory requirements by eliminating the need to store large optimizer states (e.g., first and second moments in Adam), making LLM training and fine-tuning more accessible on hardware with limited memory (e.g., consumer GPUs, edge devices).

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Reduced Tuning Effort:** The observed robustness of small batch training to hyperparameter choices is a major practical benefit. It can substantially reduce the computational cost and time dedicated to hyperparameter tuning, a notoriously expensive part of LLM development. This frees up resources for model development or other research.

4. **Improved Efficiency and Throughput:** By allowing more frequent updates for a given compute budget (if Model FLOPs Utilization, MFU, is maintained), small batch sizes can achieve equal or better per-FLOP performance. The recommendation to use the smallest batch size that maximizes model throughput could lead to more efficient use of computational resources.

5. **Practical Alternative to PEFT for Full Fine-tuning:** The demonstration that a small batch size combined with Adafactor can provide full fine-tuning performance with LoRA-like memory footprints is highly impactful. It offers practitioners a method to achieve the full expressive power of a large model during fine-tuning, even on memory-constrained devices, without the potential performance trade-offs associated with PEFT methods.

6. **Guidance for Future Optimizer Design:** The insights into why certain optimizers perform better or worse at different batch sizes, and the specific scaling rule for β2, provide valuable guidance for designing and tuning new optimization algorithms for large-scale deep learning. The strong performance of vanilla SGD in the small batch regime might encourage a renewed focus on simple, state-free optimizers.

7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper opens several avenues for future work, including the interaction with batch size schedules, further precision reduction in fine-tuning, the design of more effective small-state optimizers, and a deeper theoretical understanding of the proposed scaling heuristics.

In essence, this research offers a compelling argument for revisiting and optimizing the fundamental choices in LLM training, potentially leading to more efficient, accessible, and robust development of advanced language models.
