# GRAM: Generalization in Deep RL with a Robust Adaptation Module

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories (MERL) and MIT developed GRAM, a deep reinforcement learning framework that unifies adaptive performance in familiar environments with robust generalization to novel, unseen conditions. GRAM dynamically adjusts robot behavior based on environmental uncertainty, achieving strong adaptive performance in in-distribution scenarios and robust generalization to out-of-distribution dynamics on a quadruped robot, both in simulation and hardware.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning policies struggle to generalize effectively to both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) environmental conditions.
- Existing contextual RL methods excel at ID adaptation but often fail in OOD scenarios, while robust RL methods provide OOD generalization but typically lead to conservative and suboptimal ID performance.
- There is a need for a unified deep RL framework that can seamlessly integrate adaptive ID performance and robust OOD generalization without a significant trade-off.

## Method

- **GRAM** (Generalization in Deep RL with a Robust Adaptation Module) extends a ==teacher-student architecture== with an ==Epistemic Neural Network (Epinet)== that quantifies deployment-environment ==uncertainty== via variance in its latent feature estimates, distinguishing ID (low variance) from OOD (high variance) contexts.
- The robust adaptation module dynamically blends the Epinet's mean latent feature estimate (for adaptive behavior) with a predefined ==robust latent feature== (for robust behavior), based on this uncertainty, using a ==confidence score==.
- A ==joint training pipeline== combines standard RL with ==adversarial RL==, using different latent feature inputs, to simultaneously optimize for adaptive ID performance and robust OOD generalization within a single policy.

## Results

- GRAM achieved ID performance (**0.92** normalized task return) comparable to contextual RL and domain randomization, while significantly outperforming them in near OOD (**0.79**) and far OOD (**0.58**) contexts in simulation.
- On a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot, GRAM demonstrated faster time-to-goal in moderate tasks (average **8.17s**) and maintained a **100%** success rate in challenging, far OOD hardware tasks (e.g., **9 kg** payload, slippery surfaces), where baselines largely failed.
- The framework showed robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, successfully navigating diverse outdoor terrains and transitions in real-world experiments, indicating strong practical applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Epistemic Neural Networks can effectively quantify environmental uncertainty, allowing a policy to differentiate between familiar (ID) and novel (OOD) dynamics in real-time.
> - Dynamically combining an adaptive latent feature estimate with a fixed robust latent feature, guided by uncertainty, enables a seamless transition between adaptive and robust control strategies.
> - A unified training approach, incorporating both standard and adversarial reinforcement learning, can train a single policy that inherently possesses both the capacity to adapt to known conditions and remain robust to unforeseen ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The reliable deployment of deep reinforcement learning (RL) in real-world applications, particularly in complex robotics tasks like legged locomotion, necessitates policies that can generalize effectively across a range of environmental conditions. This includes both scenarios observed during training (in-distribution, ID) and novel, unforeseen scenarios (out-of-distribution, OOD). The existing research landscape for achieving such generalization in deep RL has largely focused on two complementary, yet often separate, paradigms: contextual RL and robust RL.

Contextual RL methods, often implemented through teacher-student architectures, aim to enable policies to identify and adapt to the specific environment dynamics present during deployment. These techniques have demonstrated success in adapting to variations within the distribution of environments seen during training. However, their generalization capabilities are typically limited to this ID range, and they may perform inadequately or fail entirely when confronted with OOD dynamics not explicitly encountered during the training phase. Approaches such as variational inference and contrastive learning have also been applied for context inference from historical observations, but they similarly focus on adaptation within known distributions.

Conversely, robust RL methods focus on achieving reliable performance in OOD scenarios by maximizing worst-case outcomes over a set of possible environment dynamics. Adversarial training is a common technique within this paradigm, introducing perturbations during training to build resilience against unknown dynamics or disturbances. While robust RL can provide generalization to unseen OOD contexts, it often does so at the expense of optimal performance in ID environments, frequently resulting in conservative behavior.

Some research has explored training collections of policies or using switching mechanisms for different modes of operation (e.g., task-based vs. recovery policies). However, these approaches often require explicit mechanisms for policy selection or fail to provide a unified framework that seamlessly integrates both adaptive ID performance and robust OOD generalization.

This work addresses the challenge of integrating the strengths of contextual RL and robust RL into a single architecture. It seeks to develop a deep RL framework that can both adapt to familiar ID dynamics and exhibit robustness to novel OOD dynamics without requiring a trade-off between these two objectives. By aiming to unify adaptive ID and robust OOD generalization, the paper positions itself as a development towards more practical and deployable deep RL systems in uncertain real-world environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a deep reinforcement learning framework capable of achieving reliable generalization across both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) environment dynamics within a single, unified architecture. The motivation stems from the inherent uncertainty and diversity of real-world settings, which make it impractical to expose a learning-based system to all possible environmental variations during training. Therefore, a deployed policy must be able to:
1. **Adapt to familiar training conditions (ID contexts):** This involves recognizing known environment parameters and adjusting behavior to optimize performance.
2. **Exhibit robustness to novel, unforeseen conditions (OOD contexts):** This requires maintaining acceptable performance even when faced with dynamics significantly different from those encountered during training.

The existing approaches typically necessitate a trade-off between these two generalization types. Contextual RL methods excel at ID adaptation but often fail in OOD scenarios due to their sensitivity to the training data distribution. Robust RL methods provide OOD generalization but frequently lead to conservative and suboptimal performance in ID environments.

To overcome this dichotomy, the authors aim to:
- **Introduce a robust adaptation module:** This module is designed to identify both ID and OOD environment dynamics using a measure of uncertainty about the deployment environment. The goal is for the module to enable adaptation when uncertainty is low (ID) and trigger a robust behavior when uncertainty is high (OOD).
- **Propose a joint training pipeline:** This pipeline is intended to combine the benefits of teacher-student architectures for adaptive ID performance with adversarial RL training for robust OOD performance. The objective is to produce a single policy that inherently possesses both adaptive and robust capabilities.
- **Demonstrate performance on a complex robotics task:** The authors aim to validate the framework's effectiveness through comprehensive locomotion experiments on a quadruped robot, both in simulation and on hardware, under a variety of ID and OOD conditions.

Ultimately, the core motivation is to enhance the deployability and reliability of deep RL agents in real-world applications by enabling them to generalize across the full spectrum of anticipated and unanticipated environmental variations, without compromising performance in either domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, GRAM (Generalization in Deep RL with a Robust Adaptation Module), unifies adaptive ID and robust OOD dynamics generalization through a novel robust adaptation module and a joint training pipeline.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Baseline

The problem is modeled as a Contextual Markov Decision Process (CMDP), where context `c` defines the transition model `p_c`. The context is available during training but not at deployment. The goal is to train a single policy `π` that performs well across both a known set of ID contexts (`C_ID`) and unknown OOD contexts (`C_OOD`).
The baseline for ID adaptation is the teacher-student architecture:
- **Teacher Policy:** Trained with privileged context information `c_t` which is mapped to a latent feature `z_t = f(c_t)`. The policy `π(a_t | s_t, z_t)` and critic `V_π(s_t, z_t)` are trained using an RL algorithm (PPO in this work) to minimize `L_RL`.
- **Student Policy/Adaptation Module:** After RL training, a student policy is trained to imitate the teacher. An adaptation module `ϕ(h_t)` maps a recent history of observations `h_t` to a latent feature `ˆz_t`, minimizing the encoder loss `L_enc` based on the squared difference between `f(c_t)` and `ϕ(h_t)`. This `ˆz_t` is then used by `π(a_t | s_t, ˆz_t)` at deployment. The limitation is that `ϕ` is only reliable for ID histories.

#### 3.2. Robust Adaptation Module

GRAM extends the teacher-student architecture by incorporating a mechanism to identify and react to OOD contexts by quantifying uncertainty in the latent feature estimate.
- **Epistemic Neural Network (Epinet):** The adaptation network `ϕ` is designed as an epistemic neural network `ϕ(h_t, ξ) = ϕ_base(h_t) + ϕ_epi(˜h_t, ξ)`. Here, `ξ` is a random input sampled from a multivariate standard Gaussian distribution, and `˜h_t` combines `h_t` with an output from `ϕ_base`. This architecture, instead of providing a single point estimate, yields a distribution of latent feature estimates for a given history `h_t`.
- **Uncertainty Quantification:** From `N` random input samples `ξ^(i)`, the sample mean `ˆμ_ϕ(h_t)` and variance `ˆσ^2_ϕ(h_t)` of the latent feature estimates are calculated per dimension. The total variance `∥ˆσ_ϕ(h_t)∥^2` is used as a measure of uncertainty. This variance is expected to be small for ID histories (seen during training) but high for OOD histories (not seen).
- **Robust Adaptation Module `Φ_GRAM`:** At deployment time, `Φ_GRAM` combines the mean estimate `ˆμ_ϕ(h_t)` with a predefined "robust latent feature" `z_rob` (set to `0_d`) based on the uncertainty:
 `Φ_GRAM(h_t) = (1 − α_t) z_rob + α_t ˆμ_ϕ(h_t)`
 `α_t = exp(−β(∥ˆσ_ϕ(h_t)∥^2 − δ)_+)`
 The coefficient `α_t` acts as a confidence score, ranging from 0 to 1. When uncertainty `∥ˆσ_ϕ(h_t)∥^2` is low (indicative of an ID context), `α_t` approaches 1, and `Φ_GRAM` outputs `ˆμ_ϕ(h_t)`, enabling adaptive behavior. When uncertainty is high (indicative of an OOD context), `α_t` approaches 0, and `Φ_GRAM` outputs `z_rob`, triggering robust behavior. Parameters `β` and `δ` are finetuned using a validation set of uncertainty values from ID contexts.
 `z_rob` serves as a robust anchor point in the latent space, towards which the policy is biased when OOD dynamics are detected.

#### 3.3. Training for Robust Adaptation

GRAM employs a joint training pipeline that simultaneously aims for adaptive ID performance and robust OOD performance. This involves parallel training environments assigned to either adaptive or robust training, with assignments alternating between iterations.
- **RL Training:**
 - **Adaptive Training Environments:** Standard data collection occurs using the teacher policy `π(a_t | s_t, z_t)` with `z_t = f(c_t)` for `c_t ∼ C_ID`. The policy, critic, and context encoder are updated via the standard RL loss `L_ID_RL`.
 - **Robust Training Environments:** Data collection uses the policy `π(a_t | s_t, z_rob)`. An adversary policy `˜π_adv` is trained to apply worst-case external forces to the robot (e.g., forward and lateral forces) to minimize the returns of `π`. This adversarially collected data is then used to update `π`, `V_π`, and `˜π_adv` using `z_rob` as input, forming `L_OOD_RL`. This forces the policy to learn robust behaviors when `z_rob` is presented.
 - The total RL loss is `L_GRAM_RL = L_ID_RL + L_OOD_RL`. This mixed data collection ensures the policy learns both adaptive and robust skills.
- **Adaptation Module Training:** After RL training, a supervised learning phase trains the epistemic neural network `ϕ(h_t, ξ)`. It minimizes a modified encoder loss `L_GRAM_enc` which averages the `∥f(c_t) − ϕ(h_t, ξ)∥^2` over `ξ ∼ q`, encouraging the epinet to produce low variance estimates for ID contexts. Data for this phase is collected using the student policy `π(a_t | s_t, ˆz_t)` where `ˆz_t` is either `ˆμ_ϕ(h_t)` or `z_rob` depending on the training assignment.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

The base RL algorithm is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with a parallel training scheme. Feedforward neural networks are used for all trainable components. The adversary applies external forces during 5% of the timesteps, with the direction learned by the adversary and impact on linear velocity increasing throughout training. Hyperparameters for `β` and `δ` are tuned post-training using a validation set of `∥ˆσ_ϕ(h_t)∥^2` values from ID contexts, ensuring `α_t` is near 1 for ID and decreases for OOD.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of GRAM was evaluated through extensive simulation and hardware experiments on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot. The experiments focused on velocity-tracking locomotion tasks. Policies were trained in simulation on flat, solid ground with moderate ID context variations (Table II).

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Benchmark Comparison (Table III):**
 - **ID Contexts:** GRAM achieved an average normalized task return of 0.92, comparable to contextual RL (0.94) and domain randomization (0.93), indicating effective ID adaptation without being overly conservative. Robust RL showed slightly lower performance (0.87), consistent with its conservative nature.
 - **Near OOD Contexts:** GRAM demonstrated superior performance (0.79) compared to contextual RL (0.75), robust RL (0.74), and domain randomization (0.74). This suggests that GRAM's robust adaptation module effectively handles moderate OOD variations. The `α_t` value in these scenarios was moderate on average, indicating a blend of adaptive and robust behaviors (Fig. 4).
 - **Far OOD Contexts:** GRAM (0.58) achieved performance comparable to robust RL (0.59), both significantly outperforming contextual RL (0.42) and domain randomization (0.43). This indicates GRAM successfully defaults to a robust behavior when confronted with substantial OOD dynamics, as `α_t` values were lower, especially the 25%-CVaR, reflecting increased uncertainty.
 - Overall, GRAM avoided the typical trade-off, maintaining strong ID performance while offering OOD robustness.

- **Impact of ID Context Set for Training (Fig. 4 & 5):**
 - When trained with the default moderate ID context set, a 9 kg added base mass was a far OOD scenario. In this case, robust RL outperformed non-robust baselines. GRAM's `α_t` decreased for this OOD scenario, activating robust behavior.
 - When the training ID context set was expanded to include a wide range of added base masses (e.g., up to 9 kg), this scenario became ID. Contextual RL then achieved the best performance. GRAM's `α_t` remained high, indicating it recognized the scenario as ID and applied adaptive behavior, matching contextual RL's performance.
 - This demonstrated GRAM's ability to dynamically adjust its behavior based on whether a deployment context is considered ID or OOD relative to its training experience, adapting `α_t` accordingly.

- **Ablation Analysis (Table III):**
 - Ablations such as "No `Φ_GRAM`" (joint training without the robust adaptation module), "Separate Data" (separate ID and adversarial data collection), and "Modular" (combining separate robust and adaptive policies) were tested.
 - These ablations either showed similar or slightly improved ID performance but consistently resulted in reduced robustness in OOD scenarios. This validated that GRAM's unified architecture with the robust adaptation module and joint training pipeline is crucial for achieving both adaptive ID and robust OOD performance simultaneously.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

- **Controlled Experiments (Tables IV & V):**
 - **Moderate Tasks (e.g., nominal ground, frozen hip, 5° inclines/declines):** GRAM achieved the fastest time-to-goal in all scenarios (average 8.17 s), demonstrating effective adaptive behavior. Robust RL was slower (average 8.71 s) due to conservative gait, and contextual RL was the slowest (average 9.38 s) due to sim-to-real gap and lack of robustness. All methods achieved 100% success rate.
 - **Difficult Tasks (e.g., 9 kg payload, slippery surface, ramp to foam):** These represented far OOD environments. GRAM achieved a 100% success rate (15/15 trials), showcasing its robust generalization. Robust RL succeeded in 12/15 trials, with failures attributed to its conservative gait causing the robot to get stuck. Contextual RL performed poorly, succeeding in only 2/15 trials, particularly failing during transitions or when dynamics changed significantly.
- **Qualitative Outdoor Experiments (Fig. 7):** Despite training only on flat, solid ground in simulation, GRAM demonstrated robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to various outdoor terrains including pavement, grass with storm drains, uneven dirt, and wood chips. It also handled transitions between these terrains, confirming its real-world generalization capabilities.

In summary, the findings indicate that GRAM effectively unifies adaptive ID performance and robust OOD generalization. It achieves competitive ID performance while providing strong OOD robustness, outperforming baselines that typically exhibit a trade-off between these two objectives across both simulation and physical hardware experiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, GRAM, that addresses a critical challenge in the deployment of deep reinforcement learning agents: achieving generalization across both familiar (in-distribution) and novel (out-of-distribution) environmental dynamics within a single architecture. The ability to unify these two distinct types of generalization, which previously often required separate approaches or incurred performance trade-offs, represents a notable development.

The significance of GRAM lies in several key aspects:
- **Enhanced Reliability for Real-World Deployment:** By enabling policies to adapt effectively to known conditions and remain robust to unknown variations, GRAM contributes to the development of more reliable and trustworthy autonomous systems. This is particularly crucial for complex robotic applications like legged locomotion in unstructured and unpredictable environments, where encountering unforeseen dynamics is common.
- **Unified Generalization Strategy:** The proposed robust adaptation module, powered by an epistemic neural network and a special robust latent feature, provides a mechanism for the agent to assess its uncertainty about the environment and adjust its behavior accordingly. This allows for seamless transitions between adaptive and robust control strategies without requiring external switching logic or multiple distinct policies.
- **Mitigation of Performance Trade-offs:** Prior methods typically either prioritized ID adaptation (e.g., contextual RL, leading to OOD fragility) or OOD robustness (e.g., robust RL, leading to conservative ID performance). GRAM demonstrates that it is possible to achieve both simultaneously, maintaining strong adaptive performance in ID scenarios while providing a robust fallback in OOD situations.
- **Practical Applicability in Robotics:** The extensive validation on a quadruped robot, encompassing both simulation and hardware experiments under various moderate and difficult conditions (e.g., added payload, slippery surfaces, rough terrain, frozen joints), underscores GRAM's practical utility. The zero-shot sim-to-real transfer capabilities and success in challenging outdoor environments further support its potential for real-world robotics.

The potential impact of this work extends to:
- **Broader Adoption of Deep RL in Robotics:** By addressing a fundamental generalization limitation, GRAM could facilitate the more widespread and confident deployment of deep RL solutions in diverse robotic control tasks beyond quadruped locomotion.
- **Foundational Research in RL Generalization:** The methodology, particularly the use of epistemic neural networks for uncertainty quantification to drive behavioral shifts, offers a principled approach that could inspire further research into integrated generalization strategies in RL. It suggests a path towards agents that are not only capable but also aware of their limitations and can react accordingly.
- **Development of Resilient Autonomous Systems:** The principles demonstrated by GRAM could be applied to other domains requiring high resilience, such as autonomous vehicles, industrial automation, and exploration robots operating in unknown or dynamically changing environments.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as the dependence of OOD generalization on the quality of the robust RL training pipeline (e.g., the adversary's ability to capture worst-case dynamics), it also identifies avenues for future work, including exploring different robust RL techniques and applying the framework to a broader range of robotic systems and tasks. Overall, GRAM represents a substantive contribution towards enabling deep RL agents to perform reliably across the spectrum of real-world variability.
