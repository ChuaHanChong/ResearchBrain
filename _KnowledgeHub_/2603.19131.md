# From Inference Efficiency to Embodied Efficiency: Revisiting Efficiency Metrics for Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper introduces "embodied efficiency" metrics to evaluate Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models beyond traditional inference costs, demonstrating that common model compression techniques often degrade real-world robotic performance despite computational savings. The research from Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and Georgia Institute of Technology shows these techniques can increase task duration and motion jerk, and that direct adaptation methods offer mild, trade-off-laden improvements in embodied efficiency.

## Problem

- Conventional efficiency metrics for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models primarily focus on computational inference costs (e.g., parameters, FLOPs), overlooking the physical execution aspects of robotics.
- A mismatch exists between a model's 'inference efficiency' and its 'embodied efficiency,' which encompasses robotic actuation costs, task completion time, and motion quality.
- Power consumption from robotic actuation can be comparable to or exceed model inference power, making isolated inference optimization insufficient for overall system-level energy savings.

## Method

- The study defines a suite of 'embodied efficiency' metrics including task completion time, end-effector path length, joint-space path length, average jerk L2 norm, and average action rate.
- It systematically evaluates various model compression (weight pruning, quantization, visual token pruning) and action compression techniques on VLA models (π0, π0.5, MolmoAct) using both conventional and the newly proposed embodied metrics.
- Two adaptation strategies, efficiency-aware in-context learning and supervised fine-tuning with auxiliary jerk/action rate loss, are investigated to directly improve embodied efficiency in VLA models.

## Results

- Weight pruning and quantization preserved task success rates (max -2.7% drop) but increased end-effector path length by up to +5.6% and average jerk L2 norm by up to +19.5% for VLA models.
- Visual token pruning for π0.5 on Libero-Spatial increased average jerk L2 norm from 204% to 375% of the unpruned model as pruning ratios rose from 12% to 78%.
- In-context learning and supervised fine-tuning reduced average action rate and jerk (up to 25.8%) but frequently resulted in increased task completion times (up to +11.2%).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reducing computational inference costs in VLA models often degrades embodied execution efficiency, leading to longer task durations or less smooth motions, even when task success rates are maintained.
> - Embodied efficiency metrics reveal crucial performance differences in VLA policies that are not detectable through traditional inference-centric evaluations, highlighting the trade-offs between computational savings and physical execution quality.
> - Common adaptation strategies can improve specific embodied efficiency aspects (e.g., reducing jerk) but typically introduce new trade-offs, such as increased task completion time.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm in robotics that enables embodied agents to perform complex, instruction-conditioned tasks by integrating visual observations, natural language goals, and robot states. Building upon the success of Vision-Language Models (VLMs), VLA models like the Robotics Transformer (RT) family, π0, and π0.5, have demonstrated capabilities across a range of manipulation tasks. As these models continue to increase in scale and complexity, the efficiency of their operation becomes a critical factor for practical deployment, particularly in real-world settings with latency or energy constraints.

The broader research landscape in efficient AI for large models, including VLM and VLA, has predominantly focused on "inference efficiency." This has led to the development and application of techniques such as weight pruning, quantization, token sparsification, and lightweight inference architectures. These methods are typically evaluated using metrics like parameter count, Floating Point Operations (FLOPs), or token decoding throughput, which quantify the computational cost and speed of the model's forward pass. Prior work on efficient VLA, such as OpenVLA, QAIL, GLUESTICK, SP-VLA, SQAP-VLA, RoboMamba, FAST, and RynnVLA-001, has largely adopted this inference-centric approach, aiming to reduce model size, memory footprint, or token processing cost.

This paper identifies a critical gap in this conventional approach: the potential disconnect between inference efficiency and the "embodied efficiency" of a robotic system. In embodied agents, efficiency is not solely determined by the model's computational cost but also by the physical actuation of the robotic platform. The authors highlight that power consumption associated with robotic actuation can be comparable to or even exceed that of model inference. For example, they cite scenarios where servo power draw in a robot kit (22 W) is similar to an edge GPU's maximum power budget (20 W), or a robot arm's consumption (430 W) is on par with a high-end GPU (400 W). This suggests that optimizing inference alone may not translate to overall system-level efficiency.

The work argues that current evaluation metrics overlook crucial aspects of robotic performance such as task completion time, trajectory smoothness, and joint-space movement, which directly influence energy consumption and wear-and-tear in physical systems. By introducing and applying a suite of "embodied efficiency" metrics, this paper aims to provide a more comprehensive framework for evaluating VLA models, challenging the prevailing assumptions of inference-centric efficiency and advocating for a holistic perspective that integrates both model and actuation costs. It positions itself as a re-evaluation of what "efficiency" means in the context of embodied AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate the limitations of conventional "inference efficiency" metrics when applied to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic control, and to advocate for the adoption of "embodied efficiency" metrics that capture the system-level performance of robotic platforms. The authors' motivation stems from several observations:

- **Mismatch between Inference and Embodied Performance:** The central motivation is the observed disparity between metrics commonly used to evaluate VLA model efficiency (e.g., parameter count, FLOPs, decoding throughput) and the actual performance and energy consumption of these models when deployed on physical robots. The paper illustrates this with an example where a VLA model with 20% of its weights pruned, while achieving greater parameter reduction and maintaining task success rate, exhibits a longer task completion time and less smooth execution compared to a 5%-pruned model (Figure 1). This demonstrates that a model deemed "inference-efficient" might not be "embodied-efficient."
- **Significant Actuation Costs:** The authors highlight that the power consumption associated with robotic actuation is often of the same order of magnitude as, or even exceeds, the power consumed by model inference. They provide specific examples with NVIDIA Jetson platforms and Franka Emika robot arms, demonstrating that optimizing inference in isolation might not yield substantial overall energy savings if actuation costs are not considered. This underscores the necessity of a system-level view of efficiency.
- **Incomplete Evaluation of Current Compression Techniques:** Existing efficient VLA research predominantly applies compression techniques (like pruning, quantization, token sparsification) borrowed from large language or vision models and evaluates them using inference-centric metrics. The paper argues that this approach fails to capture the full impact of these techniques on the robot's physical behavior, such as motion quality, trajectory length, and execution duration.
- **Need for Comprehensive Metrics:** There is a recognized need for a set of metrics that can reliably reflect the real-world performance characteristics of VLA-driven robots, moving beyond task success rate and computational cost. Such metrics would enable a more faithful assessment of how different VLA models and optimization strategies affect practical deployment aspects like energy usage, operational wear, and task execution quality.

In summary, the paper aims to demonstrate that current efficiency evaluations for VLA models are incomplete and can lead to misleading conclusions regarding real-world performance. By introducing and thoroughly evaluating VLA models and their compressed variants against a new set of embodied efficiency metrics, the research seeks to establish a more robust and practically relevant framework for assessing and developing efficient robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic approach to compare the impact of various efficiency-improving techniques on both conventional inference metrics and newly defined embodied efficiency metrics.

**A. VLA Model Formulation:**
The authors adopt the standard formulation for VLA model deployment on robotic platforms, modeling it as a sequential decision-making process (S, O, A, P). At each timestep `t`, the robot observes `o_t` and, conditioned on a language goal `g`, the VLA model `π_θ` outputs an action `a_t`. Low-level controllers then translate these high-level actions into joint torques and motions. This framework allows for the analysis of the closed-loop execution.

**B. Evaluation Metrics for Embodied Efficiency:**
To address the shortcomings of inference-centric metrics, the paper defines a set of embodied efficiency metrics, categorized as:

1. **Task Duration Metrics:** Quantify accumulated execution cost.
 - **Task completion time (τ):** Total time from start to completion. A smaller `τ` indicates higher efficiency.
 - **End-effector path length (L_ee):** Total Euclidean distance traveled by the robot's end-effector. Shorter `L_ee` implies less actuator travel and higher efficiency.
 - **Joint-space path length (L_joint):** Total change in joint configurations. Smaller `L_joint` correlates with reduced mechanical work.
2. **Motion Smoothness Metrics:** Measure jerk or jitter in robotic actuation.
 - **Average jerk L2 norm (J):** Measures the L2 norm of the discrete second-order difference of joint configurations, averaged over time. Higher `J` indicates more frequent accelerations/decelerations, leading to higher instantaneous power and dissipative losses.
 - **Average action rate (R):** Measures how rapidly high-level actions change across timesteps. Higher `R` suggests more aggressive command variations and potentially lower energy efficiency.

All these metrics, along with the **Task success rate (SR)**, are averaged over successful episodes to reduce variation and focus on effective policies. The metrics are reported as kinematic and control-based proxies for energy efficiency, acknowledging that direct energy estimation in simulators has limitations.

**C. Experimental Setup:**
- **Benchmarks:** Experiments are conducted on various task suites from the Libero benchmark (Libero-Spatial, Libero-Object, Libero-Goal, Libero-10) and the Bridge benchmark.
- **Evaluation Episodes:** Each policy is evaluated across N=50 episodes, or until 10 successful episodes are obtained, aligning with existing benchmark protocols.
- **Baseline Models:** The study utilizes several representative VLA models: π0, π0.5, and MolmoAct.

**D. Efficiency-Improving Techniques Investigated:**
The study applies and evaluates common efficiency-improving techniques across three domains:

1. **Model Compression:**
 - **Weight Pruning:** Unstructured magnitude pruning is applied to π0, π0.5, and MolmoAct at various ratios (5%, 10%, 20%).
 - **Weight Quantization:** Post-training quantization from bfloat16 to int8 and int4 is applied to π0, π0.5, and MolmoAct. A fake quantization scheme is used during evaluation to simulate quantization error.
2. **Token Compression:**
 - **Visual Token Pruning:** This technique is applied to π0.5 and MolmoAct at different pruning ratios (e.g., 12%, 56%, 78%).
3. **Action Compression:**
 - **Temporal Action Compression:** The FAST action tokenizer, which leverages discrete cosine transform to reduce temporal redundancy in action chunks, replaces the action head in π0.

**E. Adaptation Strategies Investigated:**
To explore methods for improving embodied efficiency directly, two adaptation strategies are tested on π0.5:

1. **In-Context Learning:**
 - The model's prompt is augmented with "efficiency-aware" context. Two settings are used: "Brief" (a high-level directive to minimize energy) and "Detail" (an explicit optimization objective to minimize action rate `R` and total steps).
2. **Supervised Fine-Tuning:**
 - The π0.5 model is fine-tuned with an auxiliary loss term that includes Jerk-L2 loss and Action-Rate loss, aiming to regularize action generation towards smoother and less aggressive motions.

By systematically applying these techniques and evaluating them with the proposed embodied efficiency metrics, the methodology aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the trade-offs involved in optimizing VLA models for real-world robotic deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several observations that challenge conventional assumptions about VLA model efficiency, supported by experimental results across different compression techniques and adaptation strategies.

**A. Reduced Computational Inference Cost Does Not Necessarily Improve Embodied Execution Efficiency:**
The study demonstrates that methods designed to reduce computational inference costs (FLOPs, tokens) often lead to increased end-to-end execution costs or degraded motion quality, even when task success rates are maintained.

- **Weight Pruning:**
 - When unstructured weight pruning (5% to 20%) was applied to π0, π0.5, and MolmoAct models, task success rates were largely preserved (at most a -2.7% drop).
 - However, end-to-end execution costs increased noticeably. For instance, end-effector path length increased by up to +5.6%.
 - Motion smoothness degraded, with the average jerk L2 norm increasing by up to +11.0%.
 - Specifically, pruning 5% of π0's weights on the Bridge benchmark resulted in a 46.2% increase in end-effector path length with only a 0.2% drop in success rate, and a 13.6% longer task completion time, as shown in Figure 4. This indicates longer, less efficient paths despite computational savings.
- **Weight Quantization:**
 - Quantizing models from bfloat16 to int8 and int4 largely preserved task success rates (worst-case drop of -1.8%).
 - Despite this, motion smoothness degraded, particularly with int4 quantization. The average jerk L2 norm increased by up to +19.5% for π0, +14.1% for MolmoAct, and +3.5% for π0.5. Task completion time and path lengths also showed slight increases in some cases (e.g., +2.8% τ for π0 with int4).
- **Visual Token Pruning:**
 - Experiments on π0.5 and MolmoAct showed that higher visual token pruning ratios led to jerkier trajectories. For example, on the Libero-Spatial suite, increasing pruning ratio for π0.5 from 12% to 78% caused the average jerk L2 norm to rise from 204% to 375% of the unpruned model (Figure 5). This highlights a trade-off between token-level compression and motion smoothness.

**B. System-Level Embodied Metrics Reveal Differences That Conventional Evaluations Overlook:**
The research found that policies appearing equivalent under conventional accuracy- or throughput-based evaluations could differ significantly in their motion dynamics and energy usage when assessed with embodied metrics.

- **Action Compression (FAST tokenizer):**
 - Replacing the action head in π0 with the FAST action tokenizer led to a mixed impact on efficiency metrics. While FAST reduced task completion time (by 1.5% to 5.6%), it consistently generated less smooth actions.
 - The average jerk L2 norm increased significantly (by 28.0% to 50.6% across different Libero task suites). End-effector path length also varied, increasing by 10.0% on Libero-Object but decreasing by 11.1% on Libero-Goal.
 - These results illustrate that while FAST might offer faster execution, it often comes at the cost of jerkier motions, revealing trade-offs in action tokenization strategies that are not apparent from success rates alone.

**C. Common Adaptation Methods Show Mild and Metric-Specific Improvements in Embodied Efficiency:**
The study investigated whether standard adaptation methods could effectively steer VLA behavior toward higher embodied efficiency, finding that improvements were often partial and accompanied by trade-offs.

- **In-Context Learning:**
 - Augmenting the prompt for the π0.5 model with efficiency-aware context (Brief or Detail) consistently reduced the average action rate (¯R) and average jerk L2 norm (¯J) across all tested Libero task suites. For example, the "Detail" prompt reduced jerk by up to 25.8% on Libero-Goal.
 - However, these reductions in jerk and action rate often came at the cost of increased task completion time, with increases of up to +11.2% (e.g., on Libero-Spatial with "Detail" prompt).
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning:**
 - Fine-tuning the π0.5 model with an auxiliary Jerk-L2 and Action-Rate loss successfully reduced the average jerk across task suites by 13.2% to 24.0% (e.g., 24.0% reduction on Libero-Goal) without compromising success rate.
 - Similar to in-context learning, this jerk reduction was accompanied by an increase in task completion time, rising by up to 3.8% (e.g., on Libero-Goal).

Taken together, these findings indicate that while computational compression techniques can reduce model-level costs, they often introduce negative impacts on the physical execution of a robot, which are only visible through embodied efficiency metrics. Furthermore, adaptation strategies aimed at improving embodied efficiency often result in trade-offs between different aspects of performance, such as smoothness versus completion time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the development, evaluation, and practical deployment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in robotics. Its primary impact lies in fundamentally challenging the prevailing notion of "efficiency" within the VLA research community.

**Re-evaluation of Efficiency Metrics:** The most immediate significance is the demonstration that conventional inference-centric metrics (parameters, FLOPs, throughput) are insufficient predictors of real-world robotic performance. This work highlights that a VLA model deemed "inference-efficient" may not be "embodied-efficient," meaning it could lead to longer task durations, increased energy consumption, and degraded motion quality on a physical robot. This necessitates a paradigm shift in how VLA models are assessed.

**Comprehensive Policy Evaluation:** By introducing and thoroughly validating a set of embodied efficiency metrics (task completion time, end-effector path length, joint-space path length, average jerk, average action rate), the paper provides a more complete and realistic framework for evaluating VLA policies. These metrics expose performance differences in learned action policies that remain hidden under traditional evaluations, enabling a deeper understanding of a policy's behavior beyond mere task success. This comprehensive view can reveal crucial trade-offs between computational savings and physical execution quality.

**Guidance for VLA Model Design and Optimization:** The findings offer concrete guidance for future VLA model development. Researchers and engineers are prompted to consider both inference efficiency and embodied execution efficiency jointly. Instead of solely minimizing model size or FLOPs, the work suggests that optimizing for embodied metrics directly, or at least evaluating against them, will lead to more practically viable and robust robotic systems. For instance, the observed trade-offs with adaptation strategies indicate that multi-objective optimization approaches may be necessary to balance speed, smoothness, and energy consumption effectively.

**Impact on Resource-Constrained Robotics:** For applications requiring VLA models on latency- or energy-constrained platforms (e.g., edge devices, battery-powered robots), this research is particularly relevant. It underscores that optimizing for inference efficiency alone might not achieve the desired system-level energy savings, as actuation costs can be dominant. Therefore, incorporating embodied efficiency into the design cycle can lead to VLA systems that are genuinely energy-efficient and suitable for real-world deployment.

**Enabling Fairer Comparisons:** The proposed embodied efficiency metrics facilitate fairer and more comprehensive comparisons of different VLA models and compression techniques. By providing a standardized set of metrics that reflect physical performance, the research enables a more objective assessment of various approaches, moving beyond speculative claims of "efficiency" based solely on computational benchmarks.

In conclusion, this work serves as a critical re-assessment of efficiency in embodied AI. Its findings emphasize the need for a holistic perspective that integrates computational costs with the physical realities of robotic actuation, ultimately guiding the development of VLA systems that are not only capable but also practically efficient and robust in real-world applications.
