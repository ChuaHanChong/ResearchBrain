# Agent as Policy for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Agent as Policy (AGP) turns a general-purpose coding agent into the robot policy. A preparation agent writes a reusable task definition; at runtime the execution agent inspects calibrated camera and depth observations, writes Python to compute motion targets, commands the arm through a bridge, and revises on physical feedback. With no task-specific training it succeeds in at least 8 of 10 trials on assembly, block construction and dice flipping.

## Problem

- Pre-generated programs and computation graphs must encode runtime decisions as explicit rules, so they cannot anticipate how uncertain observations and unexpected outcomes should change execution.
- Policy-orchestration agents choose which learned skill runs next, but motion generation stays bounded by whatever the selected pretrained policies or primitives can physically perform.
- Learned visuomotor and vision-language-action policies require task-specific or environment-specific training data, leaving zero-shot manipulation with novel recovery behaviour in unseen scenes unaddressed.

## Method

- An ==agent-robot bridge== exposes calibrated overhead and wrist-depth observations, proprioceptive state, and seven commands covering state, frames, end-effector and joint motion, and gripper, with ==Mink inverse kinematics== plus enforced workspace limits.
- A ==two-agent split==: a preparation agent produces a reusable task definition holding goal, constraints, budgets and criteria; the execution agent then samples tool calls conditioned on task, interface, history and workspace files.
- A ==runtime programming loop== where the agent authors Python during execution for depth measurement, ray triangulation, surface fitting and grasp offsets, and saves procedures, measurements and corrections as reusable experience files.

## Results

- Zero-shot on real YAM arms with fixed weights: **8 of 10** four-pair assembly, **29 of 30** across three block-construction configurations, **10 of 10** dice flipping, **2 of 2** throwing, but only **3 of 5** simultaneous towel folding.
- Model choice dominates on two-pair assembly: **GPT-6 Astra** and **Claude Opus 5** reach **5 of 5** while weaker models reach **1 of 5** and **0 of 5**, and extra thinking effort adds little.
- Accumulated experience cuts two-pair assembly task time **29.3%** over five cycles, with reasoning and programming latency down **47.0%**; transferring a stronger agent's files lifts a weaker model from **1 of 5** to **4 of 5**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A frozen general-purpose agent can act as the policy when the interface supplies calibrated observations and validated motion commands, with no action-specific training.
> - Most execution time goes to the visual loop and model reasoning rather than robot motion, so persisting programs and procedures is the main efficiency lever.
> - Cost binds practicality: tens of minutes and roughly 7 to 24 USD per trial, and coordinated deformable manipulation stays the least reliable capability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the problem of connecting general-purpose multimodal large language models (MLLMs) with physical robot control. Earlier robotics research typically separated perception, task planning, motion planning, and feedback control into distinct modules. Classical systems use geometric models and explicit planners, while more recent systems use learned visuomotor policies, diffusion policies, or vision-language-action (VLA) models trained on robot demonstrations.

A parallel line of research has applied language-model agents to robotics. One approach, represented by “code as policy” systems, asks a model to generate programs that translate observations into robot actions. Another uses the model as an orchestrator that selects and sequences existing learned skills. In both cases, the agent’s authority is limited: generated programs generally follow predefined logic, while orchestrator systems rely on the capabilities of separately trained motion policies.

The paper proposes a different boundary between the agent and the robot. In **Agent as Policy (AGP)**, the general-purpose agent remains in the execution loop and functions as the robot policy itself. It decides what to observe, interprets visual and proprioceptive evidence, writes or modifies programs, issues motion commands, and responds to physical outcomes. This is intended to address situations where an initial plan fails and the robot must change its perception, geometry estimates, grasp, trajectory, or recovery procedure.

The work is also related to agent research in software development and computer use. Those systems combine reasoning, tool use, persistent files, execution feedback, and iterative correction. AGP transfers these mechanisms to physical manipulation. Its principal research contribution is therefore conceptual and architectural: it treats the runtime coding agent, rather than a separately trained action model or fixed skill library, as the policy responsible for embodied behavior.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether a general-purpose agent can directly control a physical robot across diverse manipulation tasks without task-specific or environment-specific model training. The authors seek to test whether a single agent can generalize across different instruction modalities, object types, motion patterns, and task horizons.

The motivation arises from the limitations of fixed programs and pre-trained action policies. Physical manipulation is affected by uncertain object locations, camera errors, grasp variation, contact dynamics, and unexpected failures. For example, an insertion that appears geometrically correct may fail because of a small alignment error. Recovery may require a new camera view, a revised object pose estimate, a different gripper orientation, or a modified approach direction. The paper argues that these decisions should be made jointly at runtime rather than encoded entirely in advance.

The study evaluates several capabilities:

1. **Long-horizon reasoning:** The system must infer a sequence of operations from a human demonstration or goal image while preserving earlier progress.
2. **Action diversity:** The robot must perform actions beyond standard pick-and-place, including dice reorientation and timed throwing.
3. **Object diversity:** The system must manipulate rigid objects and deformable material, including a towel requiring bimanual coordination.
4. **Runtime adaptation:** The agent must use physical feedback to revise its actions.
5. **Experience reuse:** The authors examine whether saved programs, measurements, and corrections reduce the cost of repeated execution.
6. **Experience transfer:** They test whether a more capable but more expensive agent can establish procedures that a less capable agent can subsequently reuse.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. AGP Architecture

AGP uses two agent stages. During **task preparation**, a preparation agent reads the user’s request and any reference video, image, or language instruction. It creates a reusable task definition containing the goal, constraints, allowed variations, success criteria, interface rules, budgets, and reporting requirements.

During **task execution**, a separate execution agent receives this task definition, the current scene information, and documentation for the robot interface. The agent maintains a workspace containing scripts, notes, images, measurements, and execution records. Model parameters remain fixed during each trial; adaptation occurs through new observations, newly written programs, and revised decisions.

Formally, the agent selects a tool call based on the task specification, interface, interaction history, and workspace. A tool call can request an observation, inspect or process data, execute a local program, or submit robot commands. This makes the agent’s policy a sequence of tool decisions rather than a conventional neural network mapping directly to low-level motor commands.

#### 3.2. Robot-Agent Interface

The physical platform is an I2RT YAM arm with six revolute joints and a parallel gripper. The main observations come from an overhead Logitech BRIO camera and a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 camera providing aligned RGB and depth. Camera calibration relates image measurements to a shared robot coordinate system. The robot also reports joint positions, end-effector pose, gripper state, and target errors.

The agent can request:

- Camera frames and depth data.
- Joint and end-effector state.
- End-effector movements specified by position and orientation.
- Joint-angle movements.
- Gripper opening and closing.
- Status and error information.

For Cartesian targets, the interface uses Mink inverse kinematics and a trajectory generator subject to workspace, velocity, acceleration, and step-size limits. The controller independently executes submitted motions while the agent reasons between tool calls. For bimanual tasks, commands to different arms can be executed concurrently. Targeted throwing uses a specialized timed motion interface that interpolates joint and gripper commands at 50 Hz.

#### 3.3. Runtime Programming and Feedback

The execution agent writes Python programs to analyze images and calculate action targets. Recorded examples include depth-based measurement, color segmentation, ray intersection, triangulation from two camera views, surface fitting, grasp-offset estimation, and motion-command composition.

After each physical action, the agent examines updated images and robot feedback. It can decide whether to gather another observation, update a geometric estimate, retry a grasp, change the insertion direction, or continue with the next task step. Programs can be extended or reused within a session. The authors therefore characterize AGP as combining perception, planning, control, and recovery under runtime agent supervision.

#### 3.4. Tasks and Evaluation

The main evaluation uses real robots and five task types:

- **Video-guided assembly:** Eight parts must be assembled into four pairs in the order shown in a human video. Some mating clearances are below one millimeter radially.
- **Image-guided block construction:** Six cubes must form a pyramid, two towers, or a six-block tower based on a goal image.
- **Dice flipping:** Six randomly placed dice must be reoriented so that a specified face is upward.
- **Targeted throwing:** The robot must swing and release a potato so that it lands in a bowl.
- **Bimanual towel folding:** Two arms must reproduce sequential or simultaneous folds shown in a video.

Trials begin from predefined scenes with fixed time and interaction budgets. The primary metrics are success rate, completion time, token usage, and estimated inference cost. The main configuration uses GPT-6 Astra at high thinking effort. The authors also compare other models and reasoning-effort settings on a two-pair assembly task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

AGP completed a range of tasks without task-specific model training or simulation rehearsal. In the main evaluation, it achieved:

- **Four-pair assembly:** 8 successful trials out of 10.
- **Pyramid construction:** 10/10.
- **Two-tower construction:** 10/10.
- **Six-block tower:** 9/10.
- **Dice flipping:** 10/10.
- **Targeted throwing:** 2/2.
- **Sequential towel folding:** 5/5.
- **Simultaneous towel folding:** 3/5.

Thus, at least 80% success was observed in seven of eight configurations, although the number of trials was small. The results indicate that the agent could interpret video, images, and language instructions and generate appropriate geometric or dynamic actions across different tasks.

Resource use was substantial. Successful four-pair assembly required an average of 37.2 minutes, 13.02 million tokens, and an estimated cost of 16.62 USD. Dice flipping required 37.9 minutes and 17.08 million tokens. Sequential towel folding required 50.8 minutes and 18.12 million tokens. Block construction was less expensive for simpler configurations, with pyramid construction averaging 21.6 minutes and 9.24 million tokens. These measurements include observation, reasoning, programming, motion, recovery, and verification.

The towel experiments exposed limitations in deformable-object manipulation. Sequential folding succeeded in all five trials but required considerable time. Simultaneous folding succeeded in only three of five trials and produced residual skew, wrinkles, and edge offsets. The six-block tower also revealed a failure mode in which a structure collapsed during arm withdrawal, moving a cube outside the robot’s reachable workspace and preventing recovery.

Model comparisons showed that model choice affected reliability and efficiency. GPT-6 Astra achieved 5/5 on the two-pair assembly task at low, medium, and high thinking effort, with mean completion times of approximately 9–10 minutes. GPT-5.6 Sol also achieved 5/5 but took 14.2 minutes on average. GPT-5.6 Terra achieved 1/5, while GPT-5.6 Luna achieved 0/5. Claude Opus 5 achieved 5/5 with a mean time of 22.2 minutes, whereas Claude Fable 5.1 achieved 3/5. These comparisons suggest that the AGP framework does not eliminate dependence on model capability.

Experience reuse improved efficiency. Across five repeated two-pair assembly trials, task execution time decreased by 29.3% from the first to the fifth trial. The reasoning and programming component decreased by 47.0%, while tool execution increased slightly. The agent reused previously validated grasp parameters, insertion procedures, object measurements, and image-processing scripts while updating object positions from current observations.

Experience transfer also improved a weaker model’s performance. GPT-5.6 Terra succeeded in 1/5 trials with no prior experience and 4/5 when given files produced by GPT-6 Astra. Among successful trials, mean completion time decreased by 34.3% and token usage by 11.4%. However, this comparison used only five trials per condition, so the result should be treated as preliminary.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides evidence that a general-purpose coding agent can serve as a runtime robot policy for certain real-world manipulation tasks. Its significance lies in reducing the separation between high-level reasoning and low-level action generation. Instead of requiring a new trained policy for every task, the system can write task-specific computation during execution and adapt to physical feedback.

Potential applications include rapid prototyping of robot behaviors from videos, images, or language instructions; automated generation of reusable manipulation procedures; and collection of robot trajectories for later training of dedicated VLA policies. The saved programs and failure records could also support research on recovery and robustness.

The approach has important limitations. Execution is slow and inference costs are high, especially for long-horizon or visually complex tasks. Reliability decreases for deformable materials and coordinated dynamic actions. The experiments use a limited number of trials, a tabletop environment, and a small set of robot embodiments. The system also depends on calibrated cameras, documented interfaces, motion constraints, and the capabilities of the selected foundation model. The preparation stage introduces task-specific definitions even though the model itself is not fine-tuned.

Safety is another central concern. Incorrect perception, generated code, or motion commands could damage equipment or injure people. The paper mitigates some risks through workspace limits, controller validation, and separation between agent reasoning and motion execution, but deployment around humans would require independent safeguards, supervision, collision detection, and broader testing. Overall, AGP establishes a useful research direction for agent-controlled manipulation while leaving efficiency, reliability, safety, and scalability as open problems.
