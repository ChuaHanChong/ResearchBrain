# Active Real-World Factor-Based Evaluation for Generalist Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Minnesota Twin Cities developed an active evaluation framework for generalist robot manipulation policies, demonstrating 20-40% savings in real-world evaluation trials compared to random testing. This approach efficiently characterizes policy performance and generalization across diverse real-world task factors by using Bayesian active testing and probabilistic surrogate models.

## Problem

- Current robot policy evaluation relies on narrow test suites and limited real-world trials, failing to uncover critical failure modes and overstating deployment readiness.
- Real-world environments present a vast, combinatorial space of task factors, making exhaustive policy evaluation prohibitively slow and resource-intensive.
- Simulators cannot fully bridge the sim-to-real gap, necessitating real-world evaluation while grappling with its inherent inefficiencies and biases.

## Method

- The authors proposed an active evaluation framework that frames policy assessment as a ==sequential experimental design== problem.
- ==Bayesian active testing== is employed, leveraging probabilistic ==surrogate models== (e.g., ==Deep Ensembles==, ==Gaussian Processes==) to learn policy performance across a structured space of real-world task factors.
- ==Acquisition functions== (e.g., ==BALD==, ==PSD==) guide the selection of the most informative task configurations for real-world execution, minimizing trials needed to reduce uncertainty.

## Results

- Active testing achieved equivalent RMSE values to random testing with **20-40 fewer trials** out of **100**, demonstrating a **20-40%** reduction in real-world evaluation budget.
- Active surrogates matched the best predictive log-likelihood values achieved by random surrogates in **50-65 fewer trials**, indicating improved uncertainty calibration.
- The combination of a Deep Ensemble surrogate model and the BALD acquisition function generally yielded the best performance, showcasing faster convergence and robust predictive capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Active testing significantly improves sample efficiency, achieving comparable accuracy to random testing with substantially fewer real-world trials (20-40% reduction for RMSE).
> - The probabilistic surrogate models can accurately predict both in-distribution and out-of-distribution policy performance, offering insights into generalization capabilities.
> - Policy performance is most sensitive to object position, moderately sensitive to camera viewpoint, and least sensitive to table height, with multi-factor variations exhibiting an additive impact on performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has seen significant advancements, particularly with the emergence of generalist robot manipulation policies. These policies, often termed Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, draw inspiration from foundation models in vision and language, employing similar architectures and training paradigms to perform a diverse array of manipulation and navigation tasks. This progress has enabled robots to tackle complex, long-horizon, and dexterous tasks previously considered beyond their capabilities.

Despite these advancements, a fundamental challenge persists in the real-world deployment and evaluation of these generalist policies: generalization. Real-world environments present a vast, combinatorial space of task factors, including object poses, camera viewpoints, lighting conditions, and language instruction variations. Policies frequently exhibit failures or "shortcut behaviors" when encountering conditions outside their training distribution. Current evaluation practices often involve narrow test suites, employing binary success rates and limited trials on real hardware. This approach is prone to missing critical failure modes, overstating policy performance, and obscuring true deployment readiness. While high-fidelity simulators offer partial solutions by enabling more extensive evaluations, they still suffer from visual and physics discrepancies, making real-world evaluation the benchmark for true policy readiness.

This research addresses the critical gap in efficient and robust real-world evaluation of generalist robot policies. It builds upon existing efforts in benchmarking generalist policies for out-of-distribution conditions and sample-efficient robot policy evaluation. Previous work has explored active testing in simulation for policy-task combinations, but real-world, factor-based evaluation for a single policy under strict budgetary constraints remains a significant challenge. By treating policy evaluation as a sequential experimental design problem, this work aims to provide a sample-efficient methodology for comprehensively characterizing policy performance in diverse real-world conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:
1. Develop a sample-efficient, factor-based active evaluation framework for generalist robot manipulation policies operating on real hardware.
2. Accurately characterize the policy's performance distribution across a structured space of real-world task factors, identifying failure-prone regions.
3. Demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed active evaluation approach compared to traditional random testing methods in terms of the number of real-world evaluation trials required.
4. Assess the ability of the framework's probabilistic surrogate model to predict policy performance across both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) factor variations.

The motivation stems from the inherent difficulties and limitations of current robot policy evaluation practices. Real-world evaluation is crucial for understanding a policy's true capabilities and generalization potential, but it is prohibitively slow, resource-intensive, and susceptible to biases. The large combinatorial space of task factors means exhaustive evaluation is intractable, and even high-fidelity simulators cannot fully bridge the sim-to-real gap. The current practice of using narrow test suites can mask critical weaknesses and inflate reported performance, leading to an inaccurate understanding of a policy's deployment readiness. This work is motivated by the need for a principled, data-efficient method to systematically uncover the strengths and weaknesses of generalist robot policies in real-world environments, thereby guiding further policy development and refinement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose an active evaluation framework that conceptualizes policy evaluation as a sequential experimental design problem. The core idea is to employ Bayesian active testing, where a probabilistic surrogate model is used to learn the policy's performance over a structured space of task factors. This model's uncertainty estimates then guide the selection of the most informative task configurations for subsequent real-world evaluation.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The generalist robot policy, denoted $\pi_\theta$, maps visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. The evaluation occurs across a design space $X$ of real-world task configurations. Each configuration $x \in X$ is defined by a set of $d$ task factors ($F_1, F_2, \ldots, F_d$), which represent relevant dimensions of variation (e.g., object position, table height, camera viewpoint). The authors specifically define a design space with three factors:
- **Object position (x, y):** Discretized into an 11x11 grid on the table surface.
- **Table heights:** Three distinct heights (1, 2, 3 inches above the robot platform).
- **Scene camera viewpoint:** Three distinct locations (back, right, back-right).
This design space leads to hundreds of valid configurations per task.

The objective is to estimate an unknown performance function $f: X \to R$, which maps a task configuration to a scalar score (e.g., 0 for complete failure to 10 for full success), reflecting the policy's real-world outcome. Each evaluation of $f(x)$ is costly, requiring a physical execution on real hardware. The goal is to estimate the full performance distribution across the entire factor design space with minimal real-hardware evaluations.

**B. Active Testing:**
The framework employs Bayesian active testing to reduce uncertainty about the performance function $f$ efficiently. This involves iteratively:
1. **Fitting a Probabilistic Surrogate Model (SM):** The surrogate model learns a distribution over $f$, providing both a mean prediction and uncertainty estimates. The authors investigate three families of models:
 - **Gaussian Processes (GPs):** A non-parametric Bayesian approach that defines a distribution over functions. It provides principled uncertainty quantification through a posterior mean $\mu_t(x)$ and variance $\sigma^2_t(x)$. An RBF kernel with Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) is used to allow the model to learn the importance of each factor dimension. Kernel hyperparameters are optimized by maximizing the marginal log-likelihood. A noise floor of $10^{-1}$ is used to account for potential aleatoric noise.
 - **Mixture Density Networks (MDNs):** Neural networks whose output layer models a mixture of Gaussians $p(y|x)$. Uncertainty estimates are derived using Monte-Carlo Dropout, performing multiple forward passes and calculating the variance of predictions.
 - **Deep Ensembles:** This approach trains multiple independent neural networks with different random initializations on the same dataset. The ensemble's prediction is a mixture of the individual networks' Gaussian predictions, providing robust uncertainty estimates.
The surrogate models are trained exclusively on the real-world evaluation data, without leveraging the policy's training data, due to the different nature of their outputs and practical data availability issues.

2. **Selecting Next Configurations with Acquisition Functions (AFs):** Acquisition functions leverage the surrogate model's predictions and uncertainty to select the most informative configurations for the next real-world evaluation. Four acquisition functions are explored:
 - **Posterior Standard Deviation (PSD):** A purely exploration-driven criterion, selecting the configuration with the highest posterior uncertainty.
 - **Negative Integrated Posterior Variance (NIPV):** A global criterion that selects the candidate minimizing the expected posterior variance integrated over the entire design space.
 - **Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement (BALD):** Selects points maximizing the mutual information between the predicted outcome and the model parameters, aiming to identify regions of high epistemic uncertainty.
 - **Expected Predictive Information Gain (EPIG):** Calculates the expected mutual information between the label of a candidate point and a target prediction, directly targeting information gain about the surrogate model’s predictive performance.

**C. Full Procedure (Algorithm 1):**
The active testing procedure is initialized by randomly sampling $N_0$ configurations and executing the policy to form an initial dataset $D_0$. In each subsequent round:
1. The chosen surrogate model is fit to the accumulated data $D_{t-1}$.
2. The acquisition function is computed for all unevaluated candidate configurations.
3. The configuration(s) with the highest acquisition value are selected for real-world execution.
4. The policy's performance score is obtained, and the dataset $D_t$ is updated.
This process repeats until the evaluation budget $T$ is exhausted. For the experiments, $N_0=30$ initial random points were used, and the budget was up to 100 trials.

**D. Experimental Details:**
- **Tasks:** Three real-world tabletop manipulation tasks were used with a UR5e robot arm and visual observations from a scene and wrist camera: "Pick up the blue block", "Set the cup upright", and "Put the green block in the pot." These tasks vary in complexity, from basic grasping to multi-step, longer-horizon manipulation. An off-the-shelf VLA model ($\pi_0$) was fine-tuned for each task.
- **Continuous Outcome Ranges:** Performance for each task was graded on a discretized continuous scale based on task progress (e.g., 0.0 for complete failure to 2.0 for success in "Pick up the blue block," up to 6.0 for success in "Put the green block in the pot").
- **Factors:** The three factors (object position, table height, scene camera viewpoint) were varied. Training demonstrations for fine-tuning the robot policy were collected in specific subsets of these factor ranges (e.g., bottom-left/top-right quadrants for object position, 1 or 3 inches for table height, back or right for camera viewpoint), while evaluation was performed over broader ranges, including out-of-distribution (OOD) values (e.g., top-left/bottom-right quadrants, 2 inches table height, back-right camera).
- **Evaluation:** Ground truth performance was established by manually evaluating the policy across the entire design space (2331 valid configurations across 3 tasks). Active and random testing were simulated offline by sampling from these ground truth results, assuming negligible policy stochasticity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides several key findings regarding the efficiency and predictive capabilities of the active evaluation framework:

**A. Efficiency of Active vs. Random Testing:**
- **Sample Efficiency:** Across all three tasks, active testing variants generally matched or exceeded uniform random testing in terms of approximating the ground truth performance distribution. The surrogate models using active testing typically achieved the best RMSE values (accuracy of mean prediction) in 20-40 fewer trials (20-40% savings) out of a budget of 100 trials, compared to random testing. For predictive log-likelihood (quality of the predictive probability distribution, including uncertainty calibration), active surrogates typically matched the best values achieved by random surrogates in 50-65 fewer trials. This indicates that active testing improves both mean prediction and uncertainty calibration.
- **Reduced Variance:** Active testing methods exhibited lower variance across multiple runs compared to random testing for both RMSE and log-likelihood. This suggests more consistent convergence of the surrogate model, which is valuable in real-world scenarios where evaluations are often run only once.
- **Optimal Combinations:** The combination of Deep Ensemble as the surrogate model and BALD (Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement) as the acquisition function generally performed best, showing faster convergence in RMSE and competitive performance in log-likelihood. Variance-maximizing acquisition functions (PSD, NIPV) were competitive in RMSE but less so in log-likelihood.
- **Convergence to Nonzero RMSE:** RMSE converged to a nonzero value for all methods, attributed to potential aleatoric noise (e.g., human labeling inconsistency, policy stochasticity) and imperfect models. The inclusion of a small noise floor in the Gaussian Process surrogate aided faster convergence.

**B. Generalization:**
- **Accurate ID and OOD Prediction:** The surrogate model trained on evaluation results demonstrated the ability to accurately predict both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) policy performance across single and multiple factors. This offers a sample-efficient approach to evaluating the policy's generalization capabilities fairly.
- **Factor Sensitivity:** Policy performance was consistently most sensitive to object position (x, y), moderately sensitive to camera viewpoint, and least sensitive to table height. This aligns with findings in related literature, though the magnitude of factor variation is important for this conclusion.
- **Additive Impact of Multi-Factor Variations:** Multi-factor variations appeared to have an additive impact on performance, meaning the performance drop for combined OOD factors was roughly equivalent to the sum of drops for individual OOD factors.
- **"Harder" Regions:** Some regions of the factor space were inherently "harder" for the policy, showing worse performance despite having an equal number of demonstrations during training. For example, table height 1 resulted in significantly worse performance than table height 3 for the robot policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research proposes a principled and sample-efficient framework for real-world evaluation of generalist robot policies, offering significant implications for robotic learning and deployment.

**A. Significance:**
- **Addressing Real-World Evaluation Bottleneck:** The primary significance lies in mitigating the high cost and time investment associated with real-hardware robot evaluations. By reducing the required number of trials by 20-40% compared to random testing, the framework enables more thorough and accurate characterization of robot policy performance within practical budgetary constraints.
- **Comprehensive Policy Characterization:** The approach moves beyond simple success rate metrics to provide a detailed performance distribution across a structured factor space. This deeper insight allows researchers and developers to systematically identify specific failure modes and regions where a policy struggles, facilitating targeted improvements.
- **Improved Generalization Understanding:** The ability of the surrogate model to accurately predict both in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance provides a robust method for understanding a policy's generalization capabilities and limitations across diverse real-world conditions. This is crucial for developing more robust and reliable generalist robots.
- **Reliable Evaluation Metrics:** The lower variance observed in active testing methods provides greater confidence in the evaluation results, which is particularly important given that real-world evaluations are often performed only once.

**B. Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Robot Development:** By making real-world evaluation more efficient, the framework can accelerate the iterative development cycle of generalist robot policies, allowing for faster identification and rectification of weaknesses.
- **Informed Policy Deployment:** A clearer understanding of policy performance across various factors enables more informed decisions regarding the safe and effective deployment of robots in diverse real-world scenarios.
- **Guiding Data Collection:** While the primary focus is evaluation, the insights gleaned from performance distributions (e.g., identifying "harder" regions) can inform strategies for targeted data collection to improve policy performance, as explored in the paper's appendix. This bridges evaluation with active learning for policy refinement.
- **Foundation for Future Evaluation Tools:** The framework provides a strong foundation for developing more sophisticated evaluation tools for complex robotic systems, potentially incorporating more factors, higher-dimensional output spaces, and multi-robot scenarios.

**C. Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations and areas for future research:
- The benefits of active testing might be more pronounced with more complex performance distributions (e.g., more factors, wider factor/outcome ranges).
- More expressive representations for the design space and advanced surrogate model architectures (e.g., leveraging existing vision-text encoders) could further improve model accuracy and efficiency.
- Exploring transfer learning opportunities for surrogate models across different tasks could enhance sample efficiency.
- Further analysis is needed to understand the relationship between collecting demonstrations in certain regions and overall policy generalization, particularly concerning observations that some "worse" points are not optimal for collecting demos and that increasing data in one region might negatively impact performance in another.
- Incorporating more sophisticated methods like influence functions or combined active-testing and active-learning approaches, with necessary adaptations for the robotic context, could lead to more optimal selection strategies.
