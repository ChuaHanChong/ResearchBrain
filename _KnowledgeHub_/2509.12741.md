# Force-Modulated Visual Policy for Robot-Assisted Dressing with Arm Motions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed a Force-Modulated Visual Policy (FMVP) for robot-assisted dressing, integrating vision and real-world force feedback to adapt to human arm motions. This approach achieved an average upper arm dressed ratio of 0.64 in simulation and 0.79 in a human study, outperforming baselines and improving user comfort and safety.

## Problem

- Prior robot-assisted dressing systems often assume static human limbs or only account for cooperative movements, limiting real-world applicability with dynamic users.
- Manipulating deformable garments presents challenges due to their complex, non-linear dynamics and the necessity of applying appropriate, safe forces when interacting with a human body.
- Vision-only methods can struggle with occlusions and risk exerting excessive pressure during contact-rich interactions, while existing force integration methods are often separate from the core policy.

## Method

- A visual policy is initially pre-trained in simulation using ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== on point cloud observations, focusing on task progress and avoiding excessive force.
- Real-world data is collected using the pre-trained policy, and preference rewards are generated via a hybrid approach combining a ==Vision-Language Model== and time-based labeling.
- The policy is fine-tuned in the real world using ==Implicit Q Learning (IQL)==, integrating real-time force feedback directly into the visual policy network via ==Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)== layers to form the **Force-Modulated Visual Policy (FMVP)**.

## Results

- In a cross-physics simulation environment, FMVP achieved an average upper arm dressed ratio of **0.64**, outperforming all baselines and ablations which ranged from **0.36 to 0.49**.
- A human study with 12 participants showed FMVP achieved an upper arm dressed ratio of **0.79** and a whole arm dressed ratio of **0.86**, demonstrating higher objective performance than comparison methods (e.g., FCVP with **0.50** upper arm ratio).
- Participants rated FMVP significantly higher (**p < 0.05**) than baselines across metrics for successful dressing, appropriate force, comfort, and robustness to arm motion, indicating improved subjective user experience.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly conditioning visual features on real-world force feedback, rather than using separate force-based action filtering, significantly enhances robustness and safety during robot-assisted dressing with dynamic human arm motions.
> - A multi-stage training approach combining simulation pre-training with real-world multi-modal fine-tuning using a small dataset effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap for complex deformable object manipulation and human-robot interaction.
> - Hybrid reward labeling, using both VLM-generated preferences and time-based signals, provides robust supervision for offline reinforcement learning, especially when dealing with suboptimal real-world trajectories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot-assisted dressing addresses a significant challenge for individuals with mobility impairments, as evidenced by a 2016 study reporting that 92% of nursing facility residents and at-home care recipients require assistance for dressing. Such systems aim to enhance the quality of life and independence for these individuals, while also potentially reducing caregiver workload. However, the task presents several inherent difficulties, including the complex, non-linear dynamics of deformable garments, the necessity of applying appropriate forces when interacting with a human body, and the need to adapt to user movements throughout the process.

Previous research in robot-assisted dressing has often relied on simplifying assumptions that limit real-world applicability. A common assumption is that the human limb remains static during dressing. While some approaches incorporate collaborative human motions, they typically do not demonstrate robustness to arbitrary or disruptive limb movements, such as a person scratching an itch. Additionally, vision-only methods risk exerting excessive pressure in contact-rich interactions. While some prior work augments simulation-trained visual policies with real-world force sensing, these often focus on force minimization or treat vision and force as separate streams, rather than learning a unified policy across modalities.

This work addresses these limitations by developing a robot-assisted dressing system specifically designed to be robust to a broad spectrum of arm motions, including non-cooperative movements. It aims to generalize across different people and two long-sleeve everyday garments. The approach leverages multi-modal feedback from vision and force sensing to fine-tune a simulation-trained policy in the real world, thereby bridging the sim-to-real gap and improving adaptability and safety. This positions the work as a step toward more practical and reliable robot-assisted dressing systems that account for the dynamic nature of human behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robot-assisted dressing system that can effectively and comfortably assist individuals with mobility impairments, specifically by adapting to human arm motions during the dressing process.

The detailed objectives and their underlying motivations are as follows:

- **Develop a robot-assisted dressing system capable of handling challenging deformable garments:** Manipulating soft, unconstrained objects like clothing is inherently difficult due to their complex physical properties and the absence of a simple state representation. This necessitates a robust manipulation strategy that can manage these complexities.
- **Develop a system that can operate in proximity to a person, applying appropriate forces and ensuring comfort:** Robot interaction with humans, especially involving physical contact through garments, requires careful force control. Excessive force can cause discomfort or injury, while insufficient force can lead to task failure. The motivation is to achieve effective dressing while prioritizing user safety and comfort.
- **Enable the system to adapt to various limb movements, including non-cooperative arm motions:** A key motivation for this work is the limitation of prior research, which often assumes static human limbs or only considers cooperative movements. Individuals with mobility impairments may struggle to maintain a stable arm position or may make unpredictable movements. A truly assistive system must accommodate these dynamic behaviors. This work specifically targets robustness to non-cooperative, arbitrary, or disruptive arm motions.
- **Handle partial observations and visual occlusions during dressing:** As a garment is donned, it naturally occludes parts of the human limb, making it challenging for vision-based systems to infer the full state of the arm. The system must be able to operate effectively despite these visual limitations.
- **Generalize the dressing policy across diverse human body shapes and garment types, specifically long-sleeve everyday garments:** To be practical, a robot-assisted dressing system should not be restricted to a single garment or a specific user. The motivation is to demonstrate broad applicability, enhancing the system's utility.
- **Propose a method to fine-tune a simulation-trained policy in the real world using a small amount of data and multi-modal feedback from vision and force sensing:** The limitations of current simulators (e.g., lack of realistic force modeling for deformable garments, instability with actuated human limbs) create a significant sim-to-real gap. The motivation is to efficiently transfer a policy trained in simulation to the real world by leveraging real-world sensory information (both vision and force) to overcome these simulator shortcomings and improve adaptability and safety.

In essence, the research is motivated by the critical need for more adaptable, safe, and generally applicable robot-assisted dressing solutions that move beyond simplifying assumptions about human behavior and interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for developing the Force-Modulated Visual Policy (FMVP) involves a three-stage process: simulation vision pre-training, real-world data collection and reward labeling, and multi-modal fine-tuning in the real world. The work operates under the assumption that the robot has already grasped the garment opening, and arm motions primarily occur after the arm is partially inserted into the sleeve.

#### 3.1. Vision-Based Pre-training in Simulation (πvis)

The initial policy, π_vis, is trained in a simulated environment based on prior work.
- **Simulation Environment:** NVIDIA FleX, integrated with SoftGym.
- **Reinforcement Learning:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) is used as the underlying RL algorithm.
- **Policy Architecture:** A segmentation-type PointNet++ architecture, which processes point cloud data.
- **Observation Space (O):** Consists of segmented point clouds representing the garment (P_g), the *visible* (unoccluded) portion of the human arm (P_h_vis), and the robot end-effector position (P_r). This design specifically addresses potential occlusions, contrasting with prior methods that assumed a complete, pre-captured arm point cloud.
- **Action Space (A):** A 6D vector representing the delta translation and rotation (axis-angle) of the robot end-effector.
- **Reward Function:** Comprises a major term measuring task progress (distance the garment is dressed onto the arm) and auxiliary terms to discourage excessive force or close proximity to the human.
- **Simulation Limitations:** The pre-training is conducted with static human arm meshes because current simulators like FleX struggle with stable and accurate cloth-human interaction for moving limbs. No force information is used due to the lack of realistic force modeling for deformable garments in simulation.
- **Policy Distillation:** To enhance robustness, π_vis is distilled from a filtered dataset of over 8000 simulated trajectories, retaining 2514 high-quality trajectories that achieve an upper arm dressed ratio of at least 0.7 and avoid undesirable "early turning" behaviors.

#### 3.2. Real-World Point Cloud Segmentation and Masking

For deployment in the real world, accurate segmentation of the scene is crucial.
- **Garment Segmentation:** Achieved by combining Grounding DINO with EfficientSAM, using "cloth" as the text prompt.
- **Robot Arm Segmentation:** A fine-tuned Detectron2 model (Mask R-CNN ResNet-50) is used, pre-trained on the COCO dataset and then fine-tuned on 50 manually labeled real-world images of the Sawyer arm.
- **Mask Dilation:** Both garment and robot segmentation masks are dilated by 11 pixels to ensure full coverage at boundaries.

#### 3.3. Data Collection and Reward Labeling in the Real World

A small real-world dataset is collected to enable fine-tuning.
- **Data Collection:** The simulation-trained π_vis policy is rolled out in a user study with 8 participants, covering 3 garments and 8 arm motions, resulting in 192 dressing trials. Segmented point clouds and force measurements are recorded.
- **Reward Labeling for Offline RL:** Implicit Q Learning (IQL) is used for fine-tuning. Rewards are learned from preference labels, which are generated using a hybrid approach:
 - **VLM-generated labels:** 4000 preference labels are generated by querying a Vision-Language Model (RL-VLM-F) to determine which of two images better achieves the dressing goal.
 - **Time-based preference labels:** 4000 labels are generated assuming steady progress, where later time steps are preferred over earlier ones in a trajectory. This helps to supplement VLM labels, especially for successful trajectories, while VLM labels address unreliable time-based signals in failed trials.
- **Reward Model Training:** A reward model, with a classification-type PointNet++ backbone, is trained using the Bradley-Terry formulation on the combined 8000 preference labels.
- **Total Real-World Reward:** The final reward (r_total) for fine-tuning combines the learned preference reward (r_pref) with a force penalty (r_force), calculated as a negative quadratic function of the normalized contact force magnitude, capped at 1.

#### 3.4. Multi-modal Fine-tuning (Force-Modulated Visual Policy - FMVP)

The core of the proposed method lies in fine-tuning the pre-trained vision policy using both visual and force feedback.
- **Offline RL:** IQL is employed for fine-tuning with the collected real-world dataset.
- **Force Integration via FiLM Layers:** The 3D force vector (f ∈ R^3) measured at the robot end-effector is incorporated into the visual policy network using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) layers.
 - One FiLM layer is used for each feature propagation layer within the PointNet++ network.
 - Each FiLM layer generates modulation vectors (γ_i and β_i) that are broadcasted and applied to the visual feature map (F_i) as F'_i = Γ_i ⊙ F_i + B_i.
- **Force Smoothing:** Exponential moving average is applied to the force vector to mitigate noise from joint torque-derived readings.
- **Rationale for Multi-modality:** Force feedback is critical because visual occlusions and noise make precise inference of arm position difficult from point clouds alone (e.g., subtle elbow movements causing snagging are hard to see). Force provides direct information about garment-arm interactions, compensating for visual ambiguity and simulator-to-real-world discrepancies in dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed Force-Modulated Visual Policy (FMVP) was evaluated through extensive simulation experiments and a real-world human study.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments (Sim-to-Sim Transfer Setup)

The simulation evaluation was conducted in Assistive Gym (PyBullet), which offers a different physics engine from the training environment (NVIDIA FleX) to simulate a sim-to-real gap.
- **Experimental Design:** Four body sizes (small, medium, large, extra large), three garments (from Cloth3D), and 14 arm motions (seven base motions and their reverses) were used. A dataset of 204 trajectories was collected in PyBullet for fine-tuning. Evaluation involved 1680 trials per method, measuring the Upper Arm Dressed Ratio.
- **Performance Comparison:**
 - **Overall Outperformance:** FMVP achieved an average upper arm dressed ratio of 0.64, significantly outperforming all baselines and ablations (ranging from 0.36 to 0.49). This represents an improvement of 0.15 to 0.28 over other methods.
 - **Generalization to Body Sizes:** FMVP demonstrated consistent performance across all tested body sizes, including those unseen during fine-tuning (Large and Extra Large), whereas other methods exhibited clear performance degradation as body size increased from the training size (Medium).
 - **Value of Fine-tuning:** The substantial performance gap between FMVP and the Vision-based Policy (0.36 average) highlighted the benefits of fine-tuning in the target environment.
 - **Effectiveness of Force Integration:** FMVP outperformed FCVP (0.37 average), indicating that directly conditioning the policy on force feedback for task optimization is more effective for dynamic arm motions than merely filtering actions based on force predictions.
 - **Synergy of Multi-modality:** FMVP also surpassed Vision-only Fine-tuning (0.44 average) and Force-only Fine-tuning (0.49 average), demonstrating that leveraging both modalities during fine-tuning provides greater gains than using either in isolation.
 - **Importance of Pre-training:** The comparison with Scratch-IQL (FiLM) (0.49 average) and Scratch-IQL (Concat) (0.47 average) underscored the importance of pre-training in simulation across diverse arm poses and garment types.
 - **Offline RL Algorithm Choice:** FMVP, utilizing IQL, performed better than BC Fine-tuning (0.45 average), suggesting that the RL approach can learn beyond the limitations of suboptimal demonstration data.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments and Human Study

A human study was conducted with 12 participants using a Sawyer arm and Intel RealSense D435i camera, with impedance control for safety. Two new garments and seven arm motions (including improvised ones) were introduced. Evaluation metrics included Whole Arm Dressed Ratio, Upper Arm Dressed Ratio, and four 7-point Likert items assessing user experience.
- **Objective Performance (on 48 comparable trials):**
 - FMVP achieved an Upper Arm Dressed Ratio of 0.79 and a Whole Arm Dressed Ratio of 0.86, which were the highest among the tested methods.
 - Vision-based Policy achieved 0.63 and 0.81, respectively.
 - FCVP achieved 0.50 and 0.73, respectively.
- **Subjective User Feedback (Likert Items):** Participants generally rated FMVP higher across all four questions.
 - FMVP received median scores indicating "Agree" for successful dressing, appropriate force, comfort, and robustness to arm motion.
 - Baselines generally received median scores of "Somewhat Agree" or "Neutral."
 - Statistical analysis (Friedman test and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) confirmed significant differences (p < 0.05) in participant ratings between FMVP and baselines for most comparisons.
- **Overall FMVP Performance (full 168 trials):** Across all trials, FMVP achieved an average whole arm dressed ratio of 0.85 and an upper arm dressed ratio of 0.74.
- **Identified Failure Cases:**
 - **"Lower Arm" Motion:** Performance dropped during this motion, attributed to severe camera occlusion and potentially out-of-distribution high-force interactions when participants pulled down against the garment.
 - **"Scratch Head" Motion:** Failures occurred when the policy initiated turning too early while the garment was still on the forearm, leading to stalled progress. Visual ambiguity due to occlusion and garment stretching during this motion made elbow localization difficult.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robot-assisted dressing, addressing critical limitations of prior work and demonstrating practical applicability.

- **Enhanced Adaptability to Human Motion:** The development of a robot-assisted dressing system capable of robustly adapting to diverse and non-cooperative arm motions represents a key advancement. This is crucial for real-world assistive robotics, where users may not be able to hold static poses or actively cooperate throughout a task.
- **Improved Safety and User Comfort:** By incorporating force sensing and conditioning the policy on real-world force signals, the system demonstrably enhances safety and comfort during physical human-robot interaction. This is evidenced by positive user feedback in the human study regarding appropriate force application and overall comfort.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer with Multi-modal Learning:** The proposed fine-tuning method, leveraging a small amount of real-world multi-modal data (vision and force) to refine a simulation-trained policy, provides a practical strategy for overcoming the inherent sim-to-real gap, particularly for deformable object manipulation and human-robot interaction where simulators often lack fidelity.
- **Advancement in Multi-Modal Policy Learning:** The Force-Modulated Visual Policy (FMVP) introduces an effective way to unify visual and force information, directly conditioning visual features on force signals. This offers an alternative to methods that treat modalities separately or only use force for filtering, contributing to the broader field of multi-modal learning for robotic manipulation.
- **Potential for Increased Independence and Reduced Caregiver Burden:** Successful deployment of such a system, capable of reliably dressing individuals with mobility impairments, could significantly improve their independence and quality of life. Simultaneously, it could alleviate the substantial workload on human caregivers, allowing them to focus on other critical tasks.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies several limitations and avenues for future work, such as integrating garment grasping techniques, automating initial limb alignment, employing multiple cameras or active sensing to mitigate occlusion, improving the speed and accuracy of real-time segmentation, and incorporating dedicated force-torque sensors for more precise force readings. These insights guide subsequent research efforts in assistive robotics.

In summary, this work moves robot-assisted dressing closer to practical implementation by developing a more adaptive, safe, and generalizable system, with demonstrated superiority over existing baselines in rigorous simulation and real-world human studies.
