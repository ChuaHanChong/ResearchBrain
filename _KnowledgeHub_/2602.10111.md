# Learning Agile Quadrotor Flight in the Real World

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Zurich developed a self-adaptive framework for quadrotors that enables continuous, on-policy agility improvement directly from real-world interaction, moving from conservative to near-limit flight. This system autonomously increased peak speeds from 2.0 m/s to 7.3 m/s on test trajectories within 100 seconds of adaptation and proved robust to hardware degradation and wind disturbances.

## Problem

- Learning-based quadrotor controllers, typically trained in simulation, assume static dynamics and struggle with non-stationary real-world conditions like hardware wear, battery degradation, and complex aerodynamic effects.
- Deployed controllers often operate with conservative safety margins to ensure reliability under uncertainties, preventing them from reaching their full agility potential.
- Safely pushing a quadrotor towards its agility envelope in the real world is challenging due to inherent stability risks, data scarcity from limited battery life, and the safety-exploration paradox.

## Method

- The framework uses ==Online Residual Dynamics Learning== to capture internal dynamic discrepancies and external disturbances by augmenting nominal rigid-body dynamics with a neural residual network, trained online.
- ==Real-World Anchored Short-Horizon Backpropagation Through Time (RASH-BPTT)== optimizes the control policy by anchoring differentiable rollouts to the instantaneous real-world state, mitigating compounding errors and enabling efficient online learning.
- ==Adaptive Temporal Scaling (ATS)== dynamically adjusts the reference trajectory's temporal evolution to balance flight agility and safety, integrated into a continuous closed-loop cycle that uses a differentiable simulation proxy for counterfactual inference and gradient computation.

## Results

- The quadrotor autonomously evolved from conservative flight (**2.0 m/s**) to near-limit agility (**7.3 m/s** peak speed) within approximately **100 seconds** of in-flight adaptation on test trajectories.
- The framework demonstrated robustness to hardware variations (e.g., added mass, propeller damage) and unknown wind fields, adapting the quadrotor's operating envelope and maintaining stability.
- In a multi-stage inspection mission with unknown wind disturbances, the system reduced total mission time by **42%** (from **12 s** to **7 s**) while maintaining accurate tracking and adhering to safety constraints.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous, on-policy adaptation directly in the real world can drive quadrotor agility towards physical limits, rather than merely compensating for initial modeling errors.
> - Accurate short-horizon predictions provided by an online-learned residual dynamics model are crucial for safely exploiting platform agility through adaptive temporal scaling without violating safety constraints.
> - Anchoring differentiable policy optimization rollouts to the current real-world state significantly enhances stability and convergence by mitigating distribution shift in real-time adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of agile quadrotor flight control has seen substantial advancements through learning-based methods, which often surpass traditional model-based techniques in performance. Many existing approaches, inspired by human skill acquisition, utilize imitation learning or reinforcement learning, typically involving large-scale training in simulated environments with extensive domain randomization. Policies derived from this offline training are usually fixed upon deployment in the real world.

However, this reliance on fixed policies presents a significant challenge in dynamic and unpredictable real-world scenarios. Quadrotor flight conditions are inherently non-stationary, influenced by factors such as hardware wear, battery degradation, and complex aerodynamic effects. These elements introduce discrepancies between the training model and physical reality, which fixed policies cannot adequately address. Consequently, deployed controllers often operate with conservative safety margins to ensure reliability, thereby constraining their full agility potential.

Previous efforts to bridge the "sim-to-real" gap include extensive domain randomization, which can make policies robust to moderate modeling errors, but performance can still degrade under conditions significantly different from the training distribution. Real2Sim2Real pipelines attempt to refine simulation models with real-world data, followed by offline policy retraining. While effective, these methods demand considerable data collection and repeated retraining cycles, limiting their practicality for rapid adaptation or evolving system dynamics. More recent work utilizing differentiable simulation has enabled rapid refinement during deployment, primarily focusing on disturbance rejection. However, these methods typically yield conservative flight speeds (around 1-2 m/s), indicating they do not sufficiently push platforms towards their agility limits.

This research addresses a critical gap: enabling continuous, on-policy improvement of low-level control directly through real-world interaction, a capability that remains largely underexplored but is fundamental to human motor learning. The challenge lies in safely pushing a quadrotor, which operates near its stability limits, towards its agility envelope despite data scarcity and inherent safety risks in the physical world. This work proposes a framework to overcome these limitations, moving beyond merely compensating for modeling errors to actively and safely enhancing agility in real-time.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a self-adaptive framework that enables quadrotors to autonomously evolve from conservative flight operations to their full agility potential in the real world. This framework aims to eliminate the need for precise system identification or extensive offline sim-to-real transfer, which are common bottlenecks in deploying agile learning-based controllers.

The motivation behind this work stems from several critical limitations of current agile quadrotor control methods:

1. **Susceptibility to Real-World Uncertainties:** Learning-based controllers, typically trained in simulation, assume static dynamics. In reality, quadrotor dynamics are subject to evolving uncertainties, including external aerodynamic disturbances (e.g., wind) and internal hardware degradation (e.g., battery depletion, propeller damage). Fixed policies cannot account for these non-stationary conditions, leading to performance degradation or unsafe operation when real-world conditions deviate from the training distribution.
2. **Conservative Performance:** To maintain safety under these evolving uncertainties, current controllers are often forced to operate with conservative safety margins. This inherently restricts their agility and prevents the quadrotor from reaching its physical performance limits outside of highly controlled settings. The authors aim to enable the system to exploit its true agility ceiling imposed by the control interface, rather than being bounded by modeling inaccuracies or worst-case assumptions.
3. **Cost and Complexity of Offline Methods:** Existing approaches to mitigate the sim-to-real gap, such as extensive domain randomization or Real2Sim2Real pipelines, require significant computational resources, substantial data collection, and time-consuming offline retraining. This limits their practicality for systems that need to adapt rapidly or when system dynamics change unpredictably.
4. **Safety-Exploration Paradox in Real-World Learning:** While online adaptation offers a solution, safely pushing a quadrotor towards its agility envelope in the real world is challenging. Agile flight involves operating near stability limits, where even minor control deviations can lead to catastrophic failure. Simultaneously, learning directly in the physical world is constrained by data scarcity due to limited battery life and the cost of real-world experimentation.

To address these motivations, the research focuses on developing a framework that can:
- Continuously improve low-level control through direct real-world interaction.
- Safely push the platform towards its physical capabilities by dynamically adjusting aggressiveness.
- Achieve high sample efficiency for online learning.
- Adapt to both external environmental shifts and internal hardware variations without human intervention or expensive offline processes.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is a self-adaptive framework structured as a continuous closed-loop cycle that integrates physical execution with differentiable simulation. The workflow commences with an initialization phase where a base policy is trained using standard Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) on a nominal quadrotor model, a process that typically converges rapidly. Following deployment, the system enters a real-world adaptation loop, comprising three hierarchical components: Online Residual Learning, Real-World Anchored Short-Horizon BPTT (RASH-BPTT), and Adaptive Temporal Scaling (ATS).

#### 3.1. A. Online Residual Dynamics Learning

To manage the reality gap without precise offline system identification, the framework employs a hybrid dynamics model. This model augments nominal rigid-body dynamics with a neural residual network, which is trained online to capture internal dynamic discrepancies and external disturbances by minimizing one-step state prediction error.

1. **Hybrid Continuous-Time Dynamics:** The quadrotor state is defined by position (p), velocity (v), and rotation (R). The control input is a collective-thrust/body-rate (CTBR) command (c, ω_cmd). The dynamics are formulated as:
 - ˙p = v
 - ˙v = c R e3 − g e3 + a_res(ζ; θ)
 - ˙R = R (ω_cmd + ω_res(ζ; θ))∧
 Here, a_res and ω_res represent the unmodeled translational accelerations and body-rate discrepancies, respectively, learned by the residual network f_res(ζ; θ). The input features ζ include the current state and control input.
2. **Differentiable Integration and Online Training:** A differentiable fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) integrator, realized as a Lie-group integrator, is used to obtain discrete-time state transitions. This integration approach is selected for its numerical accuracy and its ability to make translational predictions sensitive to rotational residuals. The residual model parameters (θ) are optimized online by minimizing the integrated one-step prediction error between the simulated and measured states from a sliding-window replay buffer. The loss function includes Euclidean translation error, geodesic distance on the rotation manifold, and a spectral norm regularization term for numerical stability.

#### 3.2. B. Real-World Anchored Short-Horizon Backpropagation Through Time (RASH-BPTT)

This component focuses on efficient and robust online policy optimization using the learned hybrid dynamics. To mitigate the compounding errors inherent in long-horizon predictions, RASH-BPTT anchors each differentiable rollout to the instantaneous real-world state.

1. **Policy Parameterization:** The control policy π_ϕ is an MLP that takes an observation vector (current state, reference trajectory state, and history of past actions) and outputs a four-dimensional CTBR command.
2. **Anchored Rollouts and Optimization:** At each real-world time step, the differentiable rollout is initialized with the current state estimate from the physical quadrotor. The policy then generates control actions, and the short-horizon dynamics are unrolled for H steps using the learned discrete map. The objective is to maximize the cumulative discounted reward along this predicted trajectory. Gradients for policy parameter updates are computed via BPTT using JAX's automatic differentiation engine, leveraging Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation and automatic vectorization (vmap) for parallelized, efficient updates on a GPU.

#### 3.3. C. Adaptive Temporal Scaling (ATS) via Differentiable Optimization

ATS dynamically adjusts the temporal evolution of the reference trajectory, allowing the system to balance flight agility with safety. This mechanism provides "temporal elasticity," enabling aggressive time compression (increased agility) when tracking is precise and relaxation of kinematic demands (increased conservatism) during disturbances.

1. **Parameterization:** The reference trajectory is parameterized as a piecewise polynomial sequence, where a scalar scaling parameter α acts as a time dilation factor to control the execution rate: p_ref(τ; α) = Cβ(τ/α).
2. **Optimization Objective and Update:** ATS is formulated as an online optimization problem. It minimizes a composite potential function J_ATS(α) over a short horizon, balancing execution speed (achieved by decreasing α) and safety (ensuring tracking error E_k(α) remains below a predefined threshold E_th via a Softplus barrier function). The differentiable hybrid model is used as a proxy for counterfactual inference to analyze the sensitivity of closed-loop performance to α. The gradient ∇_α J_ATS is computed using closed-loop sensitivity analysis, combining the analytical gradient of the reference with the recursive sensitivity of the system state, approximated by the hybrid model dynamics. The parameter α is updated via projected gradient descent, allowing it to naturally maintain an equilibrium near the safety boundary.

The integration of these components forms a tightly coupled system: online residual learning provides an accurate, adaptive dynamics model; RASH-BPTT uses this model for robust, real-world-anchored policy optimization; and ATS dynamically adjusts trajectory aggressiveness based on the system's current competence and safety margins, leveraging the accurate predictions from the learned hybrid model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents comprehensive real-world and simulation experiments to validate the self-adaptive framework, addressing questions regarding agility, robustness, and the contribution of individual components.

#### 4.1. A. Real-World Agile Flight and Adaptation

1. **Approaching the CTBR Interface Limit (Agility - RQ1):**
 - The framework demonstrated autonomous evolution from conservative flight to near-limit agility. On a Linear Shuttle trajectory, the quadrotor’s peak speed increased from an initial 2.0 m/s to 7.3 m/s within approximately 100 seconds of in-flight adaptation. Similarly, on a Figure-8 trajectory, speed evolved from 2.2 m/s to 7.2 m/s over the same adaptation period.
 - Analysis of commanded body rates (ω_cmd) revealed persistent saturation at the 6 rad/s input bound during later adaptation stages, indicating that the Adaptive Temporal Scaling (ATS) successfully pushed the system to the saturation limit of the CTBR command interface.
 - Comparison with zero-delay offline lower bounds from trajectory optimization showed that the achieved Figure-8 lap time (2.60 s) approached the ideal CTBR bound (2.24 s), confirming operation near the command-interface ceiling. A larger gap for the Shuttle trajectory (2.30 s vs. 1.49 s CTBR bound) was attributed to the trajectory’s sensitivity to actuation delays and aerodynamic effects not accounted for in the ideal reference.

2. **Robustness to Hardware Variations (Robustness - RQ2):**
 - The system’s resilience was evaluated under three hardware degradation scenarios on the Figure-8 trajectory: added mass (60g payload), propeller damage (clipped tips), and a combination of both.
 - In all cases, the framework successfully identified the new feasible operating envelopes for the compromised hardware, allowing the drone to operate at the boundary of CTBR body-rate saturation while maintaining stability.
 - A significant finding was the rapid adaptation of the Residual Dynamics Learning module. Following hardware alterations, the nominal policy exhibited substantial tracking errors. After just a single update iteration of the residual model, tracking error decreased sharply, enabling ATS to confidently reduce α and increase agility. This indicates the residual network effectively captured platform-specific dynamic changes.

3. **Application: Time-Optimal Inspection under Unknown Disturbances:**
 - In a multi-stage inspection mission with unknown wind fields, the system maintained stable tracking through online residual adaptation despite turbulent aerodynamic disturbances.
 - This robustness allowed the ATS mechanism to progressively shorten the execution duration, reducing the total mission time by 42% (from 12 s to 7 s) while adhering to safety constraints.

#### 4.2. B. Simulation Evaluation (Ablation - RQ3)

An ablation study in simulation, where all baselines were augmented with the ATS framework for fair comparison, yielded three key insights:

1. **Residual Learning Lifts Agility Ceiling:** Methods incorporating residual dynamics significantly reduced tracking errors. Since ATS accelerates execution only when predicted error is within safety margins, lower RMSE directly translated into more aggressive time-scaling capabilities. Without residuals (Baseline), the maximum speed was capped early due to hitting the error threshold.
2. **Angular Dynamics Fidelity is Critical:** The full residual model (linear acceleration + angular velocity) outperformed a variant adapting the LOTF architecture (acceleration residuals only) in tracking accuracy, particularly as speeds increased. This highlights the importance of compensating for rotational dynamics mismatches (e.g., aerodynamic drag moments) for maintaining prediction fidelity during aggressive maneuvers.
3. **Anchored Rollouts Enhance Optimization Stability:** Comparing the "Anchor Only" variant (RASH-BPTT with anchored rollouts but without learned residuals) against standard initialization revealed that anchoring simulation rollouts to the current real-world state mitigated distribution shift, resulting in more stable convergence by keeping the optimization trajectory aligned with the on-policy region.
4. **Overall Performance:** The complete method (Ours) achieved the optimal speed–accuracy trade-off, demonstrating a synergistic effect between residual dynamics (ensuring prediction accuracy) and anchored optimization (ensuring stable adaptation).

#### 4.3. C. Online Adaptation under Wind Disturbance (Simulated)

- Benchmarking against the Baseline in a simulated 5 m/s wind field, the residual-enhanced policy reduced tracking RMSE by approximately a factor of four within a single update. This rapid error suppression provided the necessary safety margin for ATS to aggressively scale agility.
- Over ten iterations, the proposed method increased peak speed by a factor of 2.6 (from 2.6 m/s to 6.8 m/s), maintaining tracking error below the safety threshold. The baseline, in contrast, showed negligible acceleration due to persistent model mismatch. This confirmed the efficacy of residual learning in compensating for external disturbances, enabling ATS to push the system towards its saturation limit.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a self-adaptive framework that represents a notable step in autonomous quadrotor control. Its significance lies in demonstrating that real-world adaptation can serve not merely to compensate for modeling errors but as a practical mechanism for sustained and aggressive performance improvement in dynamic environments.

The framework's primary impact is its ability to eliminate the need for precise offline system identification and extensive pre-training in simulation for agile flight. By achieving significant speed increases (e.g., from 2.0 m/s to 7.3 m/s) within approximately 100 seconds of flight time, the approach offers a pathway for rapid deployment and continuous performance enhancement of robotic systems in complex, real-world scenarios. This capability directly addresses the limitations of fixed policies that struggle with non-stationary real-world conditions, internal hardware degradation, and external disturbances.

The observed synergy between online residual learning, Real-World Anchored Short-Horizon Backpropagation Through Time (RASH-BPTT), and Adaptive Temporal Scaling (ATS) is a key contribution. Residual learning provides the necessary accurate short-horizon predictions, which are a prerequisite for ATS to safely exploit the platform's agility without violating safety constraints. RASH-BPTT stabilizes the policy optimization process by grounding simulation rollouts in real-world states. This combined approach allows the system to autonomously identify and optimize for its instantaneous capabilities, dynamically pushing performance toward the physical limits imposed by the control interface.

The practical utility of the framework is demonstrated in its application to a multi-point inspection mission under unknown wind disturbances, where it reduced mission completion time by 42% while maintaining tracking accuracy. This suggests potential for deployment in various real-world tasks requiring high-speed, robust, and adaptive aerial maneuvers, such as search and rescue, infrastructure inspection, or autonomous delivery.

Looking forward, this work opens avenues for future research. Exploring individual-rotor-thrust level control could unlock further agility beyond the CTBR command interface. Integrating joint spatial-temporal optimization would enable more flexible adaptation and obstacle avoidance capabilities. Additionally, research into safe active exploration could help model boundary regions of the state-action space more effectively, and incorporating perception-aware constraints could ensure optimized trajectories remain within the platform's sensing limits during aggressive flight. Overall, this research advances the practical viability of autonomous systems operating reliably and at peak performance in uncertain and dynamic real-world settings.
