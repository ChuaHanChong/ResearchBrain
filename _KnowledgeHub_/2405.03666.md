# ScrewMimic: Bimanual Imitation from Human Videos with Screw Space Projection

> [!summary] Summary
> SCREWMIMIC learns bimanual manipulation from a single human RGB-D demonstration by representing two-hand coordination as a 1-DoF screw joint (prismatic, revolute, or revolute3D) between the hands, then predicting this screw action from point clouds and refining it via self-supervised CEM fine-tuning on a real robot, reaching 90% success across six tasks.

## Problem

- Bimanual policies must explore a large, tightly coupled two-arm action space, making random or RL exploration on real hardware prohibitively costly and largely confined to simulation.
- Coordination strategies for bimanual tasks typically rely on kinesthetic teaching, specialized teleoperation hardware, or motion capture, limiting scalability and availability of demonstrations.
- Direct imitation from noisy human hand-tracking trajectories does not transfer to robots due to embodiment gaps and lack of synchronization, requiring costly interactive fine-tuning in the raw motion space.

## Method

- **SCREWMIMIC**'s ==screw action== representation: a novel action space where a bimanual behavior is fully specified by grasp/placing locations, a 1-DoF screw axis, and an optional reference-hand trajectory, using ==MAP estimation== to classify joint type from a human demo.
- ==PointNet==-based screw prediction model: trained on segmented object point clouds with geometric augmentations (translation, rotation, scaling) to regress the axis and predict grasp contact points for novel object views.
- Self-supervised ==CEM== fine-tuning: ==cross-entropy method== exploration in screw-axis parameter space guided by an autonomous reward from episode length and force-torque sensor readings, with successful actions fed back to retrain the prediction model.

## Results

- SCREWMIMIC achieves **90%** aggregated success across six real-world bimanual tasks on a Tiago++ robot, needing on average **1-3** CEM epochs (**7-18** episodes) per task to succeed.
- Against FM+Nx6-DoF and Screw+Nx6-DoF baselines exploring in raw waypoint space, SCREWMIMIC is the only method reaching task success (e.g., **16/25**, **7/25**, **11/25** episodes) versus **0%** task completion for both baselines.
- Retraining the prediction model on corrected screw actions enables near zero-shot success on the same object (**0-1** episodes) and strong generalization to novel object instances of the same category.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Projecting noisy, high-DoF human demonstration data into a low-DoF structured action space (screw axis) can simultaneously denoise perception and shrink the exploration space for fine-tuning.
> - A biomechanically inspired kinematic prior (treating two hands as a serial linkage) generalizes across both physically-constrained articulated objects and unconstrained tasks needing a virtual joint.
> - Combining sparse autonomous reward signals (episode length plus force magnitude) is necessary; either signal alone fails to guide fine-tuning to a successful policy in several tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bimanual manipulation represents a pinnacle of robotic dexterity, crucial for interacting with complex, human-centric environments. However, it presents significant challenges due to the high number of degrees of freedom (DoF) involved and the stringent spatial and temporal synchronization required between two robotic arms. Traditional approaches to bimanual manipulation have explored planning, control, reinforcement learning, and imitation learning.

- **Challenges in Bimanual Manipulation:** Previous methods often struggle with the prohibitively large state and action spaces, making random exploration on real robot hardware infeasible. Reinforcement learning, while powerful, is often too costly to train in the real world, leading to sim-to-real gaps. Planning approaches can be computationally intensive, and traditional control methods often require explicit modeling of constraints, which can be difficult to generalize.
- **Limitations of Prior Imitation Learning:** Imitation learning (IL) offers a promising alternative by allowing robots to learn from human demonstrations. However, direct imitation from human videos faces several hurdles:
 - **Embodiment Gap:** Differences in morphology between humans and robots make direct trajectory execution challenging.
 - **Noise in Perception:** Human pose tracking from videos is inherently noisy, leading to imprecise demonstrations.
 - **Scalability of Data Acquisition:** Many IL approaches require large datasets or specialized teleoperation hardware (e.g., kinesthetic teaching, motion capture), limiting their practicality and scalability.
 - **Lack of Coordinated Action Space:** Most IL methods interpret bimanual tasks as two independent unimanual tasks or simple joint-space coordinations, failing to capture the underlying synergistic motion.

SCREWMIMIC addresses these limitations by introducing a novel action representation: **screw actions**. Inspired by biomechanics and psychology, which model bimanual interaction as a "serial kinematic chain," SCREWMIMIC hypothesizes that many bimanual tasks can be effectively represented by a 1-DoF screw joint between the hands. This dramatically reduces the action space, inherently coordinates both arms, and provides a robust prior for interpreting noisy human demonstrations. By leveraging this compact representation, SCREWMIMIC facilitates one-shot visual imitation learning from a single RGB-D human video and enables efficient self-supervised fine-tuning. This work distinguishes itself from prior approaches by offering a unified framework that can handle both articulated objects (with physical constraints) and non-articulated objects (where the screw action creates a *virtual* joint), all from easily accessible human video data, without requiring extensive data collection or specialized teleoperation. It builds upon ideas from visual imitation learning (e.g., DEFT) but focuses on the critical aspect of action representation for bimanual tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable robots to learn complex bimanual manipulation behaviors efficiently from a single human video demonstration and refine them through self-supervised interaction.**

The core motivations driving this work are:

- **Addressing Bimanual Complexity:** Acknowledge the inherent complexity of bimanual tasks due to high DoF and strict synchronization requirements, which make learning and exploration difficult for robots.
- **Leveraging Human Insight:** Draw inspiration from how humans learn bimanual skills—by observing others and practicing. This translates to the idea of learning from human video demonstrations.
- **Overcoming Imitation Learning Challenges:** Recognize the limitations of direct imitation from human videos (embodiment gap, perceptual noise, large action spaces) and seek a more robust and efficient representation.
- **Introducing Screw Actions as a Prior:** Propose a novel, biologically inspired insight: many bimanual tasks involve relative motion between hands that can be simplified and constrained by a 1-DoF screw joint (prismatic, revolute, or screw motion). This "screw action" aims to:
 - Provide a powerful prior for interpreting noisy human sensor signals.
 - Define a reduced, coordinated action space for more efficient robot exploration and fine-tuning.
- **Enabling Self-Supervised Refinement:** Develop a framework that can automatically correct initial imitative behaviors, adapting them to the robot's specific embodiment and real-world conditions through interaction, without requiring human intervention for reward signaling.
- **Generalization:** Facilitate generalization of learned skills to novel object instances and configurations, even from a single initial human demonstration, through geometric augmentations and an iterative learning loop.

In essence, SCREWMIMIC aims to make bimanual manipulation learning more accessible and effective for robots by providing a structured, constrained action space that simplifies both perception from human demonstrations and real-world policy refinement.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SCREWMIMIC is a comprehensive framework composed of three interconnected modules designed to learn and refine bimanual manipulation policies:

**A. Extracting a Screw Action from a Human Demonstration (Perceptual Module)**
The first step is to interpret a human bimanual demonstration from an RGB-D video into a "screw action" representation, $\sigma_h = (g_h^l, g_h^r, S_h, \tau_h^l)$.
1. **Grasp/Placing Location Extraction:** Off-the-shelf hand-object detectors (e.g.,) are used to identify the initial grasping and placing locations ($g_h^l, g_h^r$) by projecting the first intersection of hand and object bounding boxes into the 3D point cloud.
2. **Hand Trajectory Extraction:** An off-the-shelf hand-tracking solution (FrankMocap) extracts the 6-DoF wrist poses over time for both hands, $\tau_h^l$ and $\tau_h^r$.
3. **Screw Axis Inference:** Instead of directly using these raw, noisy trajectories, SCREWMIMIC converts them into a screw action. It assigns a "reference" role to the left hand and an "acting" role to the right hand, analyzing the relative motion of the right hand with respect to the left. It considers three canonical screw joint types:
 - **Prismatic (h=∞):** Linear motion; fits a 3D line to the right wrist trajectory.
 - **Revolute (h=0):** Pure rotation; transforms right wrist poses relative to the left hand to exponential coordinates, then averages the derived screw axis parameters.
 - **Revolute3D:** Rotation with fixed orientation; fits a plane to the right wrist trajectory (normal defines axis direction), then projects trajectory onto the plane and fits a circle (center defines point on axis).
 A Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation method is used to determine the most probable screw joint type by comparing the observed human trajectory with trajectories computed from each screw type. This process effectively filters noise and imposes a coordinated structure on the bimanual motion.

**B. Predicting a Screw Action from a Point Cloud (Prediction Model)**
To generalize the learned screw action to novel object instances and configurations, SCREWMIMIC employs a PointNet-based perception model, $M: P \rightarrow \sigma_M = (g_M^l, g_M^r, S_M)$.
1. **Input:** A partial 3D point cloud ($P$) of the object, extracted using MDETR for segmentation.
2. **Model Architecture:** Two PointNet-based networks are used:
 - A regression network (Conv1D + FC) predicts the screw axis parameters (6 values for point and direction vector components).
 - A segmentation network predicts grasp contact points.
3. **Training Data Generation:** Crucially, this module leverages the 3D nature of the input and output. A single human demonstration's extracted screw action is augmented geometrically (translation, rotation, scaling) along with the object's point cloud to create a diverse synthetic training dataset. This enables generalization from limited real-world demonstrations. Training is rapid, allowing for quick model refinement.

**C. Self-Supervised Screw-Action Policy Fine-Tuning**
The predicted screw action from the perception model provides a good initial hypothesis but may not be perfect due to human tracking noise and the embodiment gap. This module refines the policy through self-supervised robot interaction.
1. **Behavior Generation:** Given a predicted screw action $\sigma_M = (g_M^l, g_M^r, S_M)$, the robot's grippers first move to the predicted grasp points. Then, the right hand moves relative to the left hand according to the specified screw axis $S_M$, creating waypoints along the axis. The left hand can also execute its own trajectory ($\tau_l$) if the task requires it.
2. **Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) Optimization:** A sampling-based optimization framework, CEM, is used to iteratively refine the initial screw action parameters.
 - **Initialization:** An initial sampling distribution is set around the predicted screw axis parameters.
 - **Sampling and Execution:** In each epoch, $E$ screw action variations are sampled from the distribution and executed by the robot.
 - **Self-Generated Reward:** Crucially, the robot autonomously generates a reward signal based on two criteria:
 - **Episode Length:** Longer episodes without failure are preferred, indicating sustained manipulation. Failures include insufficient force (moving in free space), excessive force (incorrect manipulation), or loss of grasp (finger proprioception).
 - **Mean Wrench:** Among successful (long) episodes, those with lower mean force (wrench) applied by the right hand are preferred, indicating more efficient manipulation.
 - **Elite Set and Distribution Update:** The top $T$ trajectories (elite set) based on these rewards are selected. The sampling distribution is then updated (e.g., mean and variance adjusted) based on the elite set, guiding exploration towards successful regions. This process repeats for $N$ epochs or until success.
3. **Self-Improving Loop:** Successfully executed and fine-tuned screw actions ($\sigma_r$) are added back to the training dataset for the prediction model. This continuous feedback loop allows the robot to iteratively improve its prediction model and expand its capabilities over time and to new objects.

This methodology forms a powerful closed-loop system, where human demonstrations provide an initial structured prior, the prediction model generalizes it, and the robot's self-supervised interaction fine-tunes and further refines the learned policies, leading to robust bimanual manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluated SCREWMIMIC across six diverse real-world bimanual tasks using a PAL-Robotics Tiago++ manipulator: open bottle, close zipper, insert roll, close laptop, stir, and cut. These tasks covered prismatic, revolute, and revolute3D screw types, as well as manipulation of articulated and non-articulated objects.

**Q1) Is a single human demonstration enough for SCREWMIMIC to achieve success?**
- **Result:** SCREWMIMIC achieved an aggregated success rate of **90%** across all tasks and trials (16 out of 18 trials successful).
- **Detail:** For tasks like "insert roll" and "cut," success was achieved very quickly (1 epoch, 8 and 7 episodes respectively). "Close zipper" and "close laptop" also showed high success with moderate exploration (2 epochs, 11-12 episodes). "Open bottle" and "stir" were slightly more challenging, failing in one out of three trials, but still achieving success in the others within 3 epochs (16-18 episodes).
- **Conclusion:** A single human video demonstration, combined with SCREWMIMIC's screw action representation and self-supervised fine-tuning, is largely sufficient for robust bimanual task success. The failures observed were attributed to the small number of exploration episodes (max 25), suggesting room for further robustness with slightly extended exploration.

**Q2) Can a policy fine-tuning and model retraining loop enable SCREWMIMIC to continually improve and generalize to new objects?**
- **Result:** Yes, the retraining loop significantly improves performance and enables generalization.
- **Detail:**
 - **M1 (Human Demo Trained):** Initial model trained on human demonstration. Robot fine-tunes this (e.g., "open bottle" took 3 epochs, 16 episodes).
 - **M2 (Retrained on Robot Success for Same Object):** After successful fine-tuning on the robot, the corrected screw action is used to retrain the prediction model. This M2 model then performs almost zero-shot on *the same object* in new poses (e.g., "open bottle" M2 took 0 epochs, 1 episode, indicating direct success).
 - **Generalization to New Objects (M2 on New Object):** When tested on a geometrically novel object from the same category (e.g., a different bottle), M2 might initially perform sub-optimally (e.g., "open bottle" M2 took 2 epochs, 14 episodes).
 - **M3 (Retrained on Robot Success for New Object):** Once M2 is fine-tuned on the new object, the corrected action is again used to retrain a new model, M3. M3 then performs almost zero-shot on *the new object* (e.g., "open bottle" M3 took 0 epochs, 1 episode).
- **Conclusion:** This demonstrates a robust self-learning loop where robot successes iteratively enhance the perception model, leading to better generalization and reduced exploration time for subsequent tasks, even with new object instances.

**Q3) What benefits does the screw axis representation have compared to a more direct, N×6-DoF representation?**
- **Result:** The screw action representation is critical for task success, outperforming baselines significantly.
- **Detail (Ablation Study):**
 - **FM + N×6-DoF (Baseline 1):** Directly used FrankMocap (FM) hand trajectories as N 6-DoF waypoints and explored in this raw space. **Result: Zero success, very low task completion percentage (e.g., 0% for bottle, 10% for laptop).** This validates the paper's initial hypothesis that raw, noisy, high-DoF exploration is inefficient.
 - **Screw + N×6-DoF (Baseline 2):** Parsed human demonstration into a screw axis but then explored in the N×6-DoF space. **Result: Zero success, but slightly better task completion (e.g., 10% for bottle, 50% for roll/laptop).** This shows that the initial screw-based parsing helps, but the fine-tuning space is still too unconstrained.
 - **Screw + Screw (SCREWMIMIC):** Parsed human demonstration into a screw axis AND explored in the screw axis space. **Result: 100% success for all tested tasks (bottle, roll, laptop).**
- **Conclusion:** The screw action representation is crucial not only for interpreting human demonstrations by cleaning noise and imposing structure but also for significantly improving the efficiency of the fine-tuning process by constraining exploration to a semantically meaningful and coordinated 1-DoF space.

**Q4) Are both autonomous reward signals correctly guiding the policy fine-tuning stage?**
- **Result:** Both reward components—episode length (grasp/fixation loss, FT threshold) and mean episode wrench—are critical for successful policy fine-tuning.
- **Detail (Ablation Study):**
 - **Without Grasp Lost Detection:** For "open bottle" and "stir" tasks, the fine-tuning failed (0% success). For "insert roll," it still succeeded (100%) because grasp loss was never an issue in this specific task.
 - **Without Mean Episode FT (Force-Torque) Wrench:** For "open bottle," "roll," and "stir," the fine-tuning failed completely or achieved very low task completion (10-30%).
 - **SCREWMIMIC (Both Rewards):** Achieved 100% success for all these tasks.
- **Conclusion:** The combination of episode length (as a proxy for task progression and stability) and mean applied force (as a proxy for efficiency and correctness of interaction) provides robust and effective self-supervised feedback for the CEM-based optimization.

**Additional Findings from Appendix:**
- **Robustness to Noisy Demonstrations:** Experiments with artificially added noise and naturally occurring perceptual noise demonstrated that SCREWMIMIC's axis extraction, while affected by increasing noise, still yields a sufficiently accurate initial screw axis that the fine-tuning process can successfully refine. Even with high noise, successful task completion was achieved.
- **Pretrained PointNet Model:** Using a PointNet model pretrained on ModelNet-40 and then fine-tuning it for screw axis prediction resulted in improved generalization to *novel* objects compared to training from scratch. This suggests a path towards more robust generalization across object instances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SCREWMIMIC introduces a highly significant contribution to robotic manipulation, particularly in the domain of bimanual tasks and learning from human demonstrations. Its impact stems from several key aspects:

- **Novel Action Representation:** The core innovation is the "screw action" representation. By framing complex bimanual motions as 1-DoF screw joints, the authors introduce a powerful abstraction that inherently coordinates two arms, drastically reduces the action space, and provides a robust prior. This is a novel and intuitive way to model bimanual interaction, bridging insights from biomechanics and robotics.
- **Efficiency in Learning:**
 - **One-Shot Imitation:** Learning from a *single* human video demonstration is a major leap, significantly reducing the data collection burden compared to methods requiring large datasets or specialized kinesthetic teaching setups.
 - **Sample-Efficient Exploration:** The reduced and semantically meaningful screw action space enables highly efficient real-world policy fine-tuning, making exploration on physical robots practical within reasonable timeframes (e.g., 40 minutes for fine-tuning).
- **Self-Supervised Autonomy:** The integrated self-supervised fine-tuning mechanism, driven by autonomously generated reward signals (episode length and force), eliminates the need for human intervention during the refinement process. This is crucial for enabling robots to learn and adapt independently in real-world settings.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** The iterative self-improving loop, where successful robot executions retrain the prediction model, demonstrates a clear path toward continuous skill improvement and generalization to novel objects and poses within an object category.
- **Unified Framework for Diverse Tasks:** SCREWMIMIC's ability to handle both physically constrained articulated objects and non-constrained objects (where the screw action creates a virtual constraint) offers a versatile approach applicable to a wide range of real-world scenarios.

**Potential Impact:**
SCREWMIMIC has the potential to accelerate the deployment of bimanual robots in various applications, from industrial assembly and assistive robotics to household tasks. By making it easier for robots to acquire complex, coordinated manipulation skills from natural human demonstrations, it democratizes robot programming and reduces the expertise required. This could lead to more adaptable and intelligent robots capable of performing nuanced human-like tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors candidly discuss several limitations, which also define exciting avenues for future research:
1. **Scope of Screw Actions:** Not all bimanual tasks perfectly fit the 1-DoF screw action model (e.g., zig-zag cutting). Future work could explore sequences of screw axes or more complex, variable-DoF interactions.
2. **Object Class Specificity:** The current approach trains separate prediction models for each object class. A single multi-task model capable of generalizing across diverse objects would be a significant advancement, potentially leveraging large-scale human activity datasets.
3. **Sensor Dependence:** Reliance on RGB-D cameras (especially for depth) can be limiting for certain materials (e.g., reflective surfaces). Integrating more robust 3D perception methods or RGB-only depth estimation could broaden applicability.
4. **Automated Success Verification:** Manual success verification could be automated using vision-language models for more scalable evaluation.
5. **Grasp Point Fine-Tuning:** While screw axis prediction is fine-tuned, grasp contact points could also benefit from an iterative refinement process.

Despite these limitations, SCREWMIMIC represents a significant step forward in making complex bimanual manipulation accessible to robots through efficient, self-supervised learning from human demonstrations, laying a strong foundation for future research in robotic dexterity and autonomy.
