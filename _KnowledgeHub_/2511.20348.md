# Material-informed Gaussian Splatting for 3D World Reconstruction in a Digital Twin

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces a camera-only pipeline for 3D world reconstruction in Digital Twins, integrating 3D Gaussian Splatting with automated physics-based material assignment. This approach enables accurate sensor simulation, demonstrating LiDAR reflectivity prediction accuracy comparable to LiDAR-camera fusion methods while reducing hardware complexity.

## Problem

- Current 3D reconstruction methods for ADAS/AI validation, including LiDAR-based and fusion approaches, struggle with photorealism, precise material details, and handling transparent or highly reflective surfaces.
- Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) are computationally intensive and do not provide explicit mesh representations required by physics-based simulation engines.
- A gap exists in efficiently and automatically endowing camera-only 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) reconstructions with physics-based material properties for accurate sensor simulation.

## Method

- A modular, ==camera-only pipeline== that leverages ==3D Gaussian Splatting== (adapted ==H3DGS== for visuals, ==MiLO== for geometry) to reconstruct large-scale scenes.
- ==Monocular material extraction== using ==RMSNet== refined by ==FastSAM== generates shape-aware semantic material masks from 2D images.
- A ==per-pixel material projection== module transfers 2D material labels to 3D Gaussians and then to explicit mesh surfaces, followed by assignment of ==PBR textures== for physics-based properties.

## Results

- The camera-only method achieved comparable LiDAR reflectivity prediction accuracy to a LiDAR-camera fusion baseline, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of **10.05** versus **10.14**.
- Novel view synthesis using the adapted H3DGS model showed strong perceptual quality, achieving the best LPIPS score (**0.44**) compared to baselines.
- Incorporating segmentation masks via FastSAM improved material classification accuracy by **3.2%** and mIoU by **1%** over using RMSNet alone, enhancing material consistency on 3D surfaces.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A camera-only pipeline can achieve accurate physics-based sensor simulation in Digital Twins, offering an alternative to complex LiDAR-based or multi-sensor fusion setups.
> - Integrating 2D semantic segmentation (RMSNet + FastSAM) with 3D Gaussian Splatting allows for consistent and accurate projection of material properties onto reconstructed meshes.
> - Photorealistic visual representations from 3DGS can be effectively combined with physics-based material properties on explicit meshes, bridging the gap between rendering fidelity and simulation requirements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Digital Twins, particularly for validating Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, necessitates accurate and high-fidelity 3D world reconstruction. This reconstruction is crucial for creating synthetic sensor data that is consistent with real-world observations, thereby enabling realistic simulation and testing of complex multimodal sensor setups.

Traditional approaches to 3D reconstruction for these applications have largely relied on LiDAR-based methods. While LiDAR excels at providing accurate geometric information, it inherently lacks the rich semantic and textural details naturally captured by cameras. Moreover, LiDAR technology faces challenges with transparent and highly reflective materials, such as glass and polished metal surfaces, which are frequently encountered in urban driving environments but are poorly represented in point clouds.

To address the limitations of LiDAR, hybrid approaches combining LiDAR with cameras have been explored. These methods aim to fuse geometric accuracy with visual appearance. However, they introduce significant complexities, including the need for intricate sensor synchronization and demanding calibration procedures. Even with fusion, traditional reconstruction methods often struggle to generate photorealistic results and may provide limited texture quality, especially under sparse-view conditions.

Recent advancements in novel view synthesis, particularly Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), have demonstrated the capability to produce photorealistic results from camera-only input. Nevertheless, NeRF-based methods are computationally intensive and, critically for simulation applications, do not directly produce explicit, discrete surface representations (meshes) required by modern graphics and physics-based simulation engines.

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) emerged as a more efficient alternative to NeRF, offering fast, differentiable rendering and high-quality reconstructions from multi-view images while maintaining photorealistic visual fidelity. Subsequent research has extended 3DGS to improve geometric accuracy, handle transparency, and enable shading, as well as scale to large outdoor environments using strategies like scene partitioning and Level-of-Detail (LoD) techniques. Concurrently, methods have been developed to extract explicit mesh surfaces from Gaussian Splatting representations, enabling the integration of photorealistic visualizations with physics-based simulations.

Despite these advancements, a significant gap remains in efficiently and automatically endowing these camera-only 3DGS-derived reconstructions with physics-based material properties suitable for accurate sensor simulation. While 2D image-based semantic segmentation can classify materials, accurately transferring these 2D material labels onto 3D reconstructed surfaces—especially for large-scale outdoor scenes—within a camera-only Gaussian Splatting pipeline, and subsequently converting them into physically accurate parameters for simulation engines, is an open challenge.

This paper addresses this specific gap by proposing a camera-only pipeline that integrates photorealistic 3D Gaussian Splatting with an automated process for semantic material extraction, projection onto 3D meshes, and assignment of physics-based material properties. This approach aims to achieve sensor simulation fidelity comparable to LiDAR-camera fusion while circumventing the hardware complexity and calibration requirements of LiDAR-based systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a camera-only pipeline for 3D world reconstruction that enables accurate physics-based sensor simulation within Digital Twin environments. This is achieved by combining the photorealistic rendering capabilities of 3D Gaussian Splatting with a systematic method for assigning physics-based material properties to the reconstructed scene.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and requirements in the field of ADAS and AI system development:

1. **Need for High-Fidelity Digital Twins**: Modern ADAS and AI systems rely on complex multimodal sensor setups. Validating these systems safely and effectively requires Digital Twins that can accurately replicate real-world scenarios, ensuring that virtual sensors behave identically to their physical counterparts and generate synthetic data consistent with reality.
2. **Limitations of Existing 3D Reconstruction Methods**:
 - **LiDAR-based methods**: While providing accurate geometry, these methods lack the visual richness (semantics and textures) necessary for photorealistic simulations. Critically, LiDAR sensors perform poorly with transparent and highly reflective materials (e.g., glass, metal), which are common in urban settings and essential for realistic sensor simulation. Incorporating material properties into LiDAR-based reconstructions can improve accuracy but does not resolve the issues of texture acquisition or the need for LiDAR hardware.
 - **LiDAR-camera fusion**: These approaches attempt to combine the strengths of both sensor modalities. However, they demand complex sensor synchronization and calibration procedures, which can be time-consuming and error-prone. Additionally, they may still yield limited texture quality, particularly under sparse-view conditions.
 - **Traditional camera-based methods**: These can capture rich visual information but often produce noisier geometry and struggle with challenging materials.
 - **Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)**: While achieving photorealistic novel view synthesis from camera input, NeRF models are computationally expensive and do not readily provide the explicit, discrete mesh surfaces required by graphics engines for physics-based simulation.
3. **Emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting**: Recent advancements in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) offer an efficient, differentiable, and photorealistic rendering technique from sparse input views. This camera-only approach presents a compelling alternative to LiDAR-based methods, potentially avoiding complex hardware and calibration while delivering superior texture quality. Furthermore, recent works have shown the feasibility of extracting geometric surfaces from 3DGS representations.
4. **Bridging the Gap between Photorealism and Physics-Based Simulation**: For a Digital Twin to support accurate sensor simulation, the reconstructed environment must not only be visually realistic but also possess physics-based material properties. This allows virtual sensors, such as LiDAR, to interact realistically with surfaces based on properties like reflectivity. Current 3DGS pipelines, while photorealistic, typically do not inherently provide this physics-based material information, nor do they offer a streamlined method to assign it from 2D observations to 3D meshes.

Based on these motivations, the authors define three main contributions:

1. To develop an automated methodology for projecting 2D semantic material masks, derived from vision models, onto 3D mesh surfaces that are reconstructed via Gaussian Splatting. This projection aims to enable accurate physics-based LiDAR reflectivity simulation.
2. To establish a modular, camera-only pipeline that integrates 3D Gaussian Splatting for photorealistic geometry reconstruction with an automated, mesh-based material assignment process.
3. To comprehensively evaluate this camera-only method, demonstrating that its sensor simulation accuracy is comparable to traditional LiDAR-camera fusion approaches, as validated through LiDAR reflectivity analysis and rendering quality metrics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed pipeline reconstructs large-scale outdoor scenes exclusively from multi-view RGB images, subsequently assigning physics-based material properties to enable accurate sensor simulation. The approach is characterized by its modularity, decoupling geometric reconstruction from material assignment, and its hybrid representation, utilizing photorealistic Gaussian Splatting alongside explicit mesh geometry. The pipeline comprises five sequential stages:

#### 3.1. A. Monocular Material Extraction

This initial stage focuses on deriving per-pixel semantic material labels from input RGB images. A two-stage process is employed:
1. **Texture-based Material Segmentation**: RMSNet is utilized for its performance on autonomous driving datasets and its ability to operate solely on RGB images. It classifies pixels into various material categories, such as asphalt, concrete, glass, and metal.
2. **Shape-aware Refinement**: To address the fragmented distribution of material regions and ensure consistent labeling with sharp boundaries, FastSAM is used to identify object instances and generate pixel-wise boundaries. These boundaries are post-processed to remove overlaps, then overlaid with the RMSNet predictions. For each segmented region, a majority voting scheme is applied, where the most frequent RMSNet material class within that boundary is assigned to all enclosed pixels. This process results in a set of shape-aware material masks for each input image.

#### 3.2. B. Large-Scale Gaussian Splatting

The 3D reconstruction process combines photorealistic rendering with the generation of explicit mesh geometry.
1. **Initial Scene Reconstruction**: COLMAP is first applied to the multi-view RGB images to generate a sparse point cloud. This provides essential priors, including GPS-derived camera poses and pre-calibrated intrinsic and extrinsic parameters. The moving ego-vehicle is masked in all input images to prevent reconstruction artifacts.
2. **Two Complementary Models**: From the COLMAP output, two distinct Gaussian Splatting models are trained:
 - **H3DGS (adapted)**: This model is chosen for its visual quality in autonomous driving datasets and is used for photorealistic visualization. H3DGS employs a global scaffold and subdivides the scene into chunks for hierarchical representation. However, to ensure compatibility with standard 3DGS rendering tools (which typically expect the `.ply` format), its proprietary `.hier` format, hierarchical Level-of-Detail (LoD) features, and anti-aliasing mechanism are disabled, and the chunks are custom-merged.
 - **MiLO**: This model is selected for its ability to extract geometrically accurate mesh representations, which are crucial for physics-based simulation. MiLO produces both a 3D Gaussian model and an explicit mesh. The MiLO Gaussian model serves as the basis for semantic material projection, while its extracted mesh provides the geometric foundation for physics-based simulation.
3. **Monocular Depth Supervision and Alignment**: Both H3DGS and MiLO are trained with monocular depth supervision, utilizing Depth Anything V2, following their default configurations. This ensures enhanced geometric accuracy. Both reconstructions share the same coordinate system, maintaining alignment between visual appearance and geometry.

#### 3.3. C. Per-Pixel Material Projection

This module bridges the 2D material masks (from Sec. III-A) with the 3D MiLO Gaussian representation and then transfers these labels to the extracted mesh.
1. **2D to 3D Gaussian Projection**: Unlike methods relying on camera-to-LiDAR transformations, the MiLO Gaussian model is intrinsically aligned in world coordinates. Differentiable rasterization is employed to render the Gaussians to 2D image planes, establishing a correspondence between 3D Gaussian positions and 2D pixel coordinates. When multiple Gaussians project to the same pixel, depth sorting during alpha blending ensures the label of the closest Gaussian is assigned.
2. **Cross-View Consistency**: To resolve inconsistencies in material labels across different views (e.g., a static object receiving conflicting labels), SegAnyGS is utilized. This technique enforces consistent assignment of the material masks to the Gaussians. Majority voting across overlaid masks further ensures label consistency for each Gaussian.
3. **Gaussian to Mesh Transfer**: Each material-labeled Gaussian is assigned to its nearest mesh triangle using a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm. The mesh triangle then inherits the material label from its closest Gaussian, resulting in a per-triangle material-labeled mesh as the final output of this stage.

#### 3.4. D. Physics-Based Material Assignment

This stage assigns physics-based material properties to the mesh, making it compatible with modern graphics and simulation engines.
1. **PBR Shader Compatibility**: The Principled BSDF shader, adhering to the Disney BSDF standard, is used to represent materials. This shader is parameterized by physically meaningful properties such as base color, specular reflectivity, metallic factor, roughness, opacity, and surface normals, ensuring compatibility with engines like Blender, Unreal Engine, and Simcenter Prescan.
2. **Material Class Mapping**: From the 20 material classes initially detected by RMSNet, a subset of 10 common urban driving environment categories is selected (e.g., glass, brick, concrete, asphalt, vegetation, metals, plastics, gravel, tree trunks, rubber).
3. **PBR Texture Assignment**: Physically Based Rendering (PBR) textures corresponding to these categories are sourced from the Matsynth dataset. These textures are matched to Prescan’s laboratory-tested material database to ensure validated reflectivity properties crucial for LiDAR simulation. Textures within each category are balanced across key parameters (base color, roughness, metallic factor, clear coat). The selected PBR textures are then applied to the corresponding mesh triangles based on their assigned material labels, producing a fully textured mesh ready for simulation.

#### 3.5. E. Simulation Validation

The final stage validates the entire material-informed reconstruction pipeline by integrating the reconstructed scenes into Simcenter Prescan for physics-based sensor simulation.
1. **Scene Integration**: The material-labeled mesh from Sec. III-D is imported into Prescan. Laboratory-measured PBR material properties are assigned to each triangle according to its material label, enabling realistic interaction with physics-based sensors.
2. **Sensor and Trajectory Replication**: The original sensor configuration of the instrumented test vehicle, including LiDAR specifications and mounting positions, is replicated in Prescan. The ego-vehicle's trajectory, extracted from the real-world data, is imported via the Simcenter Prescan MATLAB API, ensuring the virtual sensor follows the exact path and poses.
3. **Synthetic LiDAR Generation and Comparison**: The simulation generates synthetic LiDAR point clouds, which include material-specific reflectivity values for each detected point. These simulated LiDAR responses are then compared against the real-world LiDAR measurements collected by the instrumented test vehicle. This comparison aims to demonstrate that the camera-only pipeline generates 3D models suitable for physics-based sensor simulation with accuracy comparable to traditional LiDAR-based reconstruction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the camera-only reconstruction pipeline was conducted using an internal dataset acquired from an instrumented test vehicle. This dataset included data from an Ouster OS1-128 LiDAR, six Sony IMX490 surround-view cameras, and a Septentrio AsteRx GNSS/RTK system. Five static road scenes, approximately eight seconds each, were captured in Leuven, Belgium, featuring diverse materials typical of urban automotive testing environments under varying weather conditions. 50 novel viewpoints per scene were reserved for evaluation.

#### 4.1. A. Baselines

- **Visual Quality**: For evaluating visual fidelity, the adapted H3DGS model was compared against three state-of-the-art camera-only Gaussian Splatting methods: 3DGS, 3DGUT, and CityGaussianV2.
- **Sensor Simulation Accuracy**: For assessing sensor simulation, the camera-only approach was compared against a LiDAR-camera fusion baseline. This baseline utilized NKSR for mesh reconstruction from LiDAR point clouds, combined with the same camera-derived material labels as the proposed camera-only method. This controlled setup allowed for direct comparison of geometric reconstruction quality and its impact on reflectivity accuracy.

#### 4.2. B. Visual Quality Evaluation

The adapted H3DGS model's novel view renderings were assessed qualitatively and quantitatively.

- **Qualitative Analysis**: The adapted model produced competitive visual quality, effectively filling masked ego-vehicle regions and filtering sensor noise present in the ground truth. However, limitations were observed in reconstructing highly reflective and transparent surfaces, occasionally leading to misalignments. Rendering quality was also noted to be highest for front-facing cameras, with some reduction in quality for side cameras due to motion blur from vehicle movement, and in scenes with dense object distributions or occlusions.
- **Quantitative Analysis (Table I)**: The adapted H3DGS model exhibited lower PSNR (average 18.27) and SSIM (average 0.41) values compared to baseline methods (PSNR range: 20.07-21.22; SSIM range: 0.56-0.58). However, it achieved the best LPIPS score (average 0.44), outperforming all baselines on four out of five scenes. This suggests that while pixel-level fidelity (PSNR, SSIM) was lower, the perceptual quality, as measured by LPIPS (which correlates with human perception), was maintained or improved. The reported degradation in PSNR and SSIM was attributed to disabling H3DGS's hierarchical optimization and anti-aliasing mechanism, which was necessary for compatibility with standard 3DGS rendering tools.

#### 4.3. C. LiDAR Simulation Accuracy

The camera-only Gaussian Splatting reconstruction was evaluated against the LiDAR-camera fusion baseline by comparing simulated LiDAR reflectivity with real-world ground truth.

- **Qualitative Analysis (Figure 4)**: Both the camera-only method and the LiDAR-camera fusion baseline produced comparable reflectivity patterns. A consistent overestimation of vegetation reflectivity was observed in both approaches. This overestimation was attributed to several factors: temporal inconsistency caused by wind-induced vegetation movement, high intra-class material variance (e.g., leaves, bark, grass sharing one label), the use of idealized PBR materials not fully capturing weathered conditions, and geometric complexity impacting surface normal estimation. These issues were considered bottlenecks in static scene assumptions and material parameterization, rather than limitations of the reconstruction modality itself. The comparable reflectivity patterns indicated the pipeline's suitability for sensor simulation despite increased geometric noise in the camera-only method.
- **Quantitative Analysis (Table II, Figure 5)**: The camera-only method achieved comparable reflectivity accuracy to the LiDAR-camera fusion baseline. The mean absolute error (MAE) for the camera-only method was 10.05, very close to the LiDAR-based baseline's MAE of 10.14. Similarly, the median error for the camera-only method was 6.48, compared to 6.78 for the LiDAR-based baseline. These minimal differences suggest that the camera-only reconstruction can achieve sensor simulation accuracy comparable to LiDAR-based methods without requiring LiDAR hardware. While the camera-only results showed higher within-scene variability (attributed to increased geometric noise from MiLO's camera-based mesh extraction), this geometric uncertainty did not significantly degrade the overall reflectivity simulation accuracy.

#### 4.4. D. Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted to evaluate the impact of incorporating segmentation masks on material classification accuracy.

- **Experimental Setup**: The study quantitatively assessed segmentation quality on the MCubes multi-modal dataset. Three configurations were compared: RMSNet alone, RMSNet + FastSAM, and RMSNet + SAM2.
- **Results (Table III)**: Adding segmentation masks improved both accuracy (from 0.63 to 0.65 for FastSAM and 0.67 for SAM2) and mIoU (from 0.28 to 0.29 for FastSAM and 0.31 for SAM2) over RMSNet alone. Although SAM2 achieved the highest scores, FastSAM was selected for the pipeline due to its significantly lower computational cost and faster inference speed, which are critical for processing large-scale multi-view datasets, accepting a 2% accuracy difference.
- **Analysis**: The 3.2% accuracy improvement achieved by FastSAM demonstrated that shape cues effectively enhance material classification consistency. However, a potential drawback noted was that incorrect segmentation masks could occasionally propagate errors across larger regions, potentially overwriting fine details. The trade-off between segmentation quality and computational efficiency was deemed acceptable for practical large-scale applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in 3D world reconstruction for Digital Twins, particularly in the context of ADAS and AI system development. The proposed camera-only pipeline offers a distinct alternative to traditional LiDAR-based or LiDAR-camera fusion approaches by leveraging recent innovations in 3D Gaussian Splatting.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to bridge the gap between photorealistic visual reconstructions and the assignment of physics-based material properties essential for accurate sensor simulation. By demonstrating that a camera-only approach can achieve LiDAR reflectivity prediction accuracy comparable to LiDAR-based methods, the research challenges the conventional reliance on LiDAR hardware and complex multi-sensor calibration procedures. This simplification of the data acquisition and processing pipeline has several potential impacts:

1. **Reduced Hardware Complexity and Cost**: Eliminating the need for LiDAR sensors and the intricate synchronization and calibration processes associated with multi-modal setups significantly reduces the hardware investment and operational complexity for creating high-fidelity 3D environments. This could make advanced Digital Twin generation more accessible and cost-effective.
2. **Enhanced Material Fidelity and Coverage**: Cameras naturally capture textures and visual properties that LiDAR struggles with, such as transparent and highly reflective materials. The pipeline's ability to extract and project semantic material masks from camera images, and then assign corresponding physics-based properties, allows for a more comprehensive and visually accurate representation of diverse materials in the simulated environment.
3. **Improved Digital Twin Utility for ADAS/AI Validation**: By providing both photorealistic visuals and accurate physics-based material interactions, the reconstructed Digital Twins can facilitate more realistic and robust sensor simulations. This is crucial for safely validating autonomous driving algorithms in virtual environments, covering high-risk scenarios and diverse environmental conditions before real-world deployment. The capability to accurately simulate LiDAR reflectivity, for instance, directly contributes to the development and testing of perception systems that rely on such sensor data.
4. **Modular and Scalable Framework**: The pipeline's modular design, separating geometry reconstruction from material assignment, allows for independent improvements and adaptations of individual components. Its foundation on large-scale Gaussian Splatting techniques also suggests potential for scalability to extensive urban environments, a critical requirement for realistic ADAS testing.
5. **Foundation for Future Research**: This work lays the groundwork for several future research directions. Improvements in camera-based geometric reconstruction could further enhance fidelity. Extending the approach to dynamic scenes, exploring learned material representations to reduce reliance on predefined databases, and developing more sophisticated vegetation modeling techniques (e.g., environment-specific calibration, hierarchical classification, refined geometric alignment for surface normals) are all avenues for further development that could lead to even more accurate and versatile Digital Twins. The planned conversion of the internal dataset to public formats like nuScenes indicates an intention to facilitate broader benchmarking and generalization across diverse urban environments.

In summary, this research offers a practical and efficient camera-only solution for creating material-informed 3D Digital Twins, contributing to safer, more efficient, and cost-effective development and validation of advanced autonomous systems.
