# Distilled Domain Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces Distilled Domain Randomization (DiDoR), an algorithm that enables zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for robot control policies by training multiple specialized "teacher" policies on fixed randomized simulation environments and then distilling their collective knowledge into a single "student" policy. The approach achieves performance comparable to or superior to baseline methods in locomotion and manipulation tasks while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for real-time robotic applications.

## Problem

- Training deep reinforcement learning policies on physical robots is costly, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe due to extensive data requirements.
- The 'reality gap' causes policies trained solely in simulation to perform poorly on real robots due to inevitable discrepancies between simulated and physical environments.
- Naive domain randomization methods can lead to unstable training for a single policy if the parameter distribution is too broad, hindering convergence.
- Ensemble methods, while robust, impose significant memory and computational overhead at deployment, which is a critical limitation for real-time robot control.

## Method

- **DiDoR** trains a set of individual 'teacher' policies in parallel, with each teacher assigned to a unique, fixed instance of a randomized simulation environment.
- After teachers are trained, their combined knowledge is transferred to a single 'student' policy through ==policy distillation==. The student is updated by minimizing the sum of Kullback-Leibler divergences between its action distribution and those of each teacher.
- The student policy is a single, smaller neural network designed to synthesize the diverse expertise of the teachers, offering computational efficiency during deployment.

## Results

- DiDoR demonstrated competitive or superior performance compared to Uniform Domain Randomization, Ensemble Methods, and P2PDRL baselines in both sim-to-sim (Hopper, Half-Cheetah, Ant) and sim-to-real (Furuta pendulum, Cart-pole) environments.
- The method achieved successful zero-shot transfer to a physical Furuta pendulum, and showed effective generalization to unseen simulation instances across all tasks, though the Cart-pole task presented challenges due to complex friction modeling.
- DiDoR maintained computational efficiency for real-time control, requiring no increased memory capacity or action computation time during deployment compared to single-policy baselines, unlike ensemble methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training individual teacher policies on fixed domain instances promotes more stable and reproducible reinforcement learning than continuously varying environments.
> - Consolidating knowledge from multiple teachers into a single student policy significantly reduces memory footprint and action computation time at deployment, crucial for real-time robot control applications.
> - The distillation process enables the student policy to effectively generalize by synthesizing diverse expertise, leading to robustness against model mismatches during sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated effectiveness in learning robot control policies from initial states. However, its application to real robots is often hindered by the extensive data requirements and the associated costs and safety concerns of real-world data collection. To address this, learning from simulations has emerged as a practical alternative, offering faster, safer, and cheaper data generation. A significant challenge in this approach is the "reality gap"—the inherent discrepancies between simulated and real-world data and dynamics.

A prominent strategy to bridge this reality gap is Domain Randomization (DR). DR involves training an RL agent on a distribution of varied simulated environments, rather than a single fixed one, to encourage the learning of a policy robust to variations in system parameters. Domain randomization methods can be broadly categorized into those that adapt the domain parameter distribution using target domain data (adaptive DR) and those that do not (static or zero-shot DR). This paper focuses on the latter, where the target real-world environment is either inaccessible until deployment, iterating a sim-to-real loop is too expensive, or the simulation fidelity is considered sufficient for direct policy transfer.

A known limitation of naive static DR is the potential for the domain parameter distribution to be excessively broad. Such a wide distribution can lead to instability during training, as the agent struggles to learn a coherent policy due to the high variance in policy updates derived from diverse environments in each step.

Separately, policy distillation is a technique rooted in knowledge distillation, which involves transferring the knowledge from one or more "teacher" models (often larger or an ensemble) into a single "student" model (often smaller and more efficient). In the context of RL, this typically means training a student policy to imitate the decision-making behavior of a teacher policy or an ensemble of teachers. While policy distillation has been explored for multi-task learning or model compression, its application to achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, particularly in combination with domain randomization, has not been extensively studied in the literature prior to this work.

This paper introduces Distilled Domain Randomization (DiDoR) to integrate these two approaches, aiming to develop a robust, efficient, and stable method for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. DiDoR positions itself as a static domain randomization method that leverages policy distillation to address the instability and computational overheads associated with other DR and ensemble-based methods, particularly for real-time robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an algorithm capable of learning robot control policies entirely within simulation, such that these policies can be directly deployed and effectively operate on real robotic systems without requiring any real-world data during the training phase. This is referred to as "zero-shot sim-to-real transfer."

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several challenges in current deep reinforcement learning for robotics:

1. **High Training Data Cost and Risk:** Training deep RL policies on physical robots is often prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and potentially damaging due to the enormous amount of data required. Simulations offer a safer and cheaper alternative.
2. **The "Reality Gap" in Simulation-to-Reality Transfer:** Policies trained solely in simulation often fail to perform adequately on real robots due to inevitable discrepancies (the "reality gap") between the simulated and physical environments.
3. **Limitations of Naive Domain Randomization (DR):** While domain randomization helps bridge the reality gap by training policies across varied simulated environments, simple randomization approaches can struggle. If the randomized parameter distribution is too wide, the learning process for a single policy can become unstable, leading to high variance in policy updates and difficulty in converging to a meaningful solution.
4. **Computational Constraints of Ensemble Methods:** An alternative to training a single robust policy is to train an ensemble of expert policies, each specialized in a specific domain, and then combine their outputs (e.g., by averaging actions) during deployment. However, this approach significantly increases the memory footprint and the computational time required to generate an action, as multiple neural networks must be queried simultaneously. These factors can be critical limitations for real-time control on physical robots, which often operate under strict time constraints (e.g., control loops running at 500 Hz).

To overcome these limitations, the authors propose Distilled Domain Randomization (DiDoR). The specific motivations for DiDoR's design are to:

- **Improve Training Stability:** By training individual "teacher" policies on *fixed* instances of randomized domains, the learning process for each teacher becomes more stable and reproducible compared to methods where the environment parameters change at every iteration.
- **Reduce Computational Overhead at Deployment:** By distilling the collective knowledge of multiple teacher policies into a *single* "student" policy, DiDoR aims to yield a final controller that has a significantly smaller memory footprint and faster inference time (i.e., time to compute the next action). This is crucial for meeting real-time requirements on physical hardware.
- **Enhance Generalization and Robustness:** The distillation process is intended to synthesize the diverse expertise of specialized teachers into a single student policy that is robust to the model mismatches encountered when transferring from simulation to reality.
- **Facilitate Implementation and Parallelization:** The modular nature of training individual teachers is designed to be easy to implement and inherently parallelizable, potentially accelerating the overall training process.

In essence, DiDoR seeks to combine the benefits of robust policy learning via domain randomization with the efficiency of knowledge distillation to create a performant and deployable zero-shot sim-to-real robot controller.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm, Distilled Domain Randomization (DiDoR), integrates domain randomization with policy distillation to achieve zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. The approach consists of two main phases: training individual teacher policies and then distilling their knowledge into a single student policy.

**Problem Formulation:**
The problem is framed as maximizing the expected discounted return of a policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$ for a distribution of domain parameters $p(\xi)$. The environment is characterized by parameters $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{n_\xi}$ (e.g., masses, friction coefficients) which are drawn from a user-defined parametric distribution $p_\phi(\xi)$. The goal is to find policy parameters $\theta$ that maximize $J(\theta) = E_{\xi \sim p(\xi)} [J(\theta, \xi)]$, where $J(\theta, \xi)$ is the expected return for a specific domain instance $\xi$.

**DiDoR Algorithm Steps:**

1. **Initialization:**
 - A set of $N$ teacher policies, $\theta_{t_{1:N}}$, and a single student policy, $\theta_s$, are randomly initialized.
 - For each teacher, a unique domain parameter configuration $\xi_{n}$ is sampled from the predefined domain parameter distribution $p_\phi(\xi)$. These $\xi_{n}$ values remain fixed for their respective teachers throughout the teacher training phase.

2. **Teacher Training (Parallelized):**
 - Each teacher policy $\theta_{t_n}$ is trained independently on its assigned, fixed environment instance $M_{\xi_n}$.
 - The paper specifies the use of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) as the reinforcement learning algorithm for training teachers.
 - The policy architecture chosen for all policies (teachers and student) is a feedforward neural network (FNN) with two hidden layers of 64 units each, using tanh activation functions.
 - This phase is highly parallelizable, as each teacher trains in its own isolated environment.

3. **Student Training (Policy Distillation):**
 - After the teachers are trained, their knowledge is transferred to the student policy $\theta_s$ through an iterative distillation process.
 - **Data Collection:** In each iteration, the current student policy $\pi_{\theta_s}$ is executed in *every* teacher's domain ($\xi_{1:N}$). Rollouts $\tau_n = \{s_t, a_t, r_t\}$ are collected for each teacher domain. This step is also parallelizable.
 - **Loss Computation:** A policy distillation loss $L$ is computed. This loss is the sum of Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergences between the action distributions of each teacher policy and the student policy, given the collected rollouts:
 $L = \sum_{N}^{n=1} \text{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{t_n}}(\tau_n) \Vert \pi_{\theta_s}(\tau_n))$
 This corresponds to a multi-teacher variant of on-policy distillation. The authors state that an entropy penalty, often included in policy distillation, was found unnecessary in their sim-to-real setting, as the resulting student policy's entropy is implicitly regulated by the domain randomization applied during teacher training.
 - **Student Update:** The student policy parameters $\theta_s$ are updated by minimizing the computed distillation loss $L$.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Simulation Environment:** MuJoCo physics engine and the SimuRLacra framework are used for simulating the randomized dynamical systems.
- **Baselines for Comparison:**
 - **Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR):** A single policy is trained, but at every iteration, a new set of domain parameters is randomly sampled.
 - **Ensemble Methods (ENS):** The trained teacher policies from DiDoR are used as an ensemble. During deployment, the actions from all teacher policies are averaged.
 - **Peer-to-Peer Distillation Reinforcement Learning (P2PDRL):** A contemporary policy distillation method where multiple "workers" (teachers) are trained simultaneously on different randomized domains, sharing knowledge via a special distillation loss. Unlike DiDoR, P2PDRL workers are assigned a *new* random domain at *every* iteration, and all workers need to be kept in memory during training.
- **Tasks:**
 - **Sim-to-Sim:** Hopper, Half-Cheetah, and Ant locomotion tasks from OpenAI Gym, augmented with domain randomization. Policies are evaluated on 32 unknown simulation instances.
 - **Sim-to-Real:** Furuta pendulum and cart-pole, both underactuated swing-up and balance tasks. These tasks involve strict real-time control constraints (500 Hz control cycle, < 2 ms action computation time).
- **Hyper-parameters:** Common hyper-parameters like policy architecture (FNN 64-64 tanh), rollout length (8000 steps), discount factor ($\gamma=0.99$), and PPO-specific parameters are kept consistent across methods where applicable. DiDoR uses 16 teachers for sim-to-real and 8 for sim-to-sim experiments. P2PDRL's worker count was reduced due to memory constraints (4 for cart-pole, 2 for other tasks). Domain parameter distributions for sim-to-real tasks are detailed in the appendix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated DiDoR against three baseline methods (Uniform Domain Randomization (UDR), Ensemble Methods (ENS), and Peer-to-Peer Distillation Reinforcement Learning (P2PDRL)) across three sim-to-sim and two sim-to-real environments.

**Sim-to-Sim Experiments (Hopper, Half-Cheetah, Ant):**

- **Performance Comparison:** DiDoR's performance, when evaluated on randomly sampled, unseen simulation instances, was observed to be comparable to or superior to the baselines. The relative performance varied based on the specific metric applied (e.g., highest median return or highest peak performance).
- **Generalization:** DiDoR's student policy demonstrated consistent performance across its teacher domains and the randomly sampled, unseen domains, suggesting effective generalization capabilities.
- **Training Robustness:** The observed high variance in returns across some tasks and methods was attributed to overly aggressive domain randomization (i.e., a too broad domain parameter distribution), indicating a general challenge in setting randomization parameters.

**Sim-to-Real Experiments (Furuta Pendulum, Cart-Pole):**

- **Generalization to Unseen Domains (Simulated):** Similar to sim-to-sim, DiDoR exhibited strong generalization by achieving comparable returns in randomly sampled domains to those obtained in its fixed teacher domains.
- **Furuta Pendulum Transfer:** The sim-to-real deployment on the Furuta pendulum was relatively successful for most methods. DiDoR, along with the majority of baseline policies, successfully transferred to the physical system, with only a few negligible failures observed for two baseline methods.
- **Cart-Pole Transfer:** This task proved more challenging for all methods, exhibiting higher variance and failure rates in real-world deployment. This was hypothesized to be due to difficulties in accurately modeling highly nonlinear friction effects between the cart's wheel and the metal rail in the simulator.
- **UDR Performance:** Policies trained with UDR generally struggled with sim-to-real transfer, particularly on the more challenging cart-pole task. The authors attributed this to potentially too narrow a domain parameter distribution during training, combined with high-variance gradient estimates, preventing it from capturing sufficient real-world variation.
- **Ensemble Methods Observations:** During experiments, it was noted that individual elements within the ensemble baselines sometimes commanded actions that approximately canceled each other out, potentially hindering overall performance.
- **P2PDRL Performance:** P2PDRL exhibited relatively low performance in these sim-to-real settings. This was attributed to a potentially suboptimal hyper-parameter selection, as the suggested parameters from prior sim-to-sim tasks did not yield satisfactory results for these real-world scenarios.
- **Computational Efficiency:** A key finding was that DiDoR did not increase the required memory capacity or the time to compute an action. This is attributed to the deployment of a single, distilled student network, which contrasts with ensemble methods that require querying multiple networks in parallel or sequentially. This characteristic is beneficial for meeting the stringent real-time constraints of physical robot control (e.g., 2 ms for a 500 Hz control loop).

In summary, DiDoR demonstrated competitive or superior performance compared to the baselines in both sim-to-sim and sim-to-real settings, particularly highlighting its ability to generalize to unseen domains and its computational efficiency for real-time robotic applications. The challenges observed in cart-pole transfer underscore the persistent difficulties in accurately modeling complex physical interactions in simulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Distilled Domain Randomization (DiDoR) represents an advancement in the field of reinforcement learning for robotics, particularly in addressing the challenges of sim-to-real transfer. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Effective Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** DiDoR provides a method that enables robot control policies to be learned entirely in simulation and then directly deployed on real physical systems without requiring any real-world data for training or adaptation. This capability is crucial for reducing the cost, time, and risks associated with robot development and deployment.
2. **Enhanced Robustness to Model Mismatch:** By distilling knowledge from multiple "teacher" policies, each specialized in a different randomized instance of the problem, the resulting "student" policy becomes inherently more robust to the inevitable discrepancies between simulation and reality (the "model mismatch"). This approach leverages the diversity of randomized domains more effectively than single-policy domain randomization.
3. **Improved Training Stability:** The methodology of training individual teacher policies in fixed, distinct simulation instances contributes to a more stable and reproducible reinforcement learning process for each expert. This addresses the instability often encountered in naive domain randomization approaches where environment parameters change frequently, leading to high variance in policy updates.
4. **Computational Efficiency for Real-Time Control:** A notable advantage of DiDoR is its computational efficiency at deployment. Unlike ensemble methods that require simultaneous querying of multiple policies, DiDoR deploys a single, typically smaller, student policy. This significantly reduces memory footprint and, critically, the time required to compute an action. For high-frequency control tasks on real robots (e.g., 500 Hz control loops), meeting strict real-time constraints (e.g., < 2 ms action computation) is essential, and DiDoR's design directly supports this requirement.
5. **Scalability and Parallelization:** The teacher training phase of DiDoR is inherently parallelizable, allowing for simultaneous learning across multiple simulation instances. This can potentially accelerate the overall training process, making it more scalable for complex tasks or larger numbers of randomized parameters.
6. **Ease of Implementation:** The authors state that DiDoR is easy to implement, which can lower the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners looking to apply robust sim-to-real transfer techniques.
7. **Contribution to Open Science:** By making all implementations open-source, including that of the P2PDRL baseline, the authors facilitate reproducibility and encourage further research and development in the field. This open-source contribution allows other researchers to build upon and extend DiDoR's capabilities or use it as a benchmark.

The findings suggest that DiDoR offers a viable and efficient solution for bridging the reality gap in robotics, particularly in scenarios where zero-shot transfer is required and real-time computational constraints are significant. Its impact lies in advancing the practical applicability of deep reinforcement learning for controlling physical robots in diverse and uncertain real-world environments.
