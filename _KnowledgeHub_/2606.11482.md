# Building Social World Models with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Carnegie Mellon University developed the Social World Model (SWM), an LLM-driven generative framework that predicts the evolution of collective social beliefs in response to real-world events. Leveraging prediction market data as a quantifiable proxy for public opinion and employing a posterior-guided training mechanism, SWM achieved high directional accuracy in forecasting belief trajectories on a novel benchmark.

## Problem

- Predicting the evolution of social beliefs (public opinion) in response to various real-world events, such as policy changes or scientific advancements.
- The inherent difficulty in quantifying semantic-driven social beliefs and establishing high-fidelity time-series data for evaluation and benchmarking.
- A lack of explicit attribution labels connecting specific events to observed shifts in collective beliefs, hindering models from learning underlying causal mechanisms.

## Method

- Introduced the **Social World Model** (SWM), an LLM-driven generative framework that characterizes social belief dynamics through a state-transition paradigm, where collective belief forms semantic states and social events act as exogenous perturbations.
- Utilized prediction market data from platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi to represent quantifiable social belief states, leveraging financial incentives for aggregated, high-quality signals of public opinion.
- Implemented a 'posterior-guided training' mechanism where a frozen LLM (posterior attributor) performs inverse inference on realized belief shifts to generate pseudo-labels for latent event attribution, enabling the model to learn event-to-shift relationships without direct supervision.

## Results

- SWM demonstrated superior directional accuracy on the Kalshi dataset (DA **0.845** for SWM-prior) and competitive performance on the Polymarket dataset (DA **0.685**) compared to time-series foundation models and advanced LLMs.
- Ablation studies confirmed that increasing the LLM backbone size of the world model improved prediction performance, and accurate real-time event attribution by the prior attributor is the primary bottleneck for end-to-end forecasting.
- Case studies illustrate SWM's ability to forecast belief trajectories by integrating unstructured news and to simulate the impact of hypothetical events, functioning as a causally aligned explanatory filter, though limitations exist in complex, multi-step economic reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Language Models (LLMs) can effectively model macro-level social dynamics, interpreting semantic event complexities and directly parameterizing belief evolution, offering an alternative to computationally intensive agent-based simulations.
> - Prediction market data provides a robust and scalable proxy for quantifying collective social beliefs, addressing the challenge of data scarcity and enabling empirical evaluation of belief shifts.
> - The decoupled architecture with posterior guidance allows for learning event attribution and belief transitions even without explicit causal labels, leveraging hindsight to infer the most probable driving events.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in social science: understanding and predicting the evolution of social beliefs in response to various events, such as policy changes or scientific advancements. The work positions itself at the intersection of social science, machine learning, and natural language processing, specifically leveraging the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs).

Within the existing landscape, several areas of research are relevant:
- **Social Event Forecasting:** Traditional approaches forecast future occurrences from historical data using semantic and time-series modeling. More recently, LLMs have been explored for their social reasoning capabilities in forecasting events. This work differentiates itself by focusing on predicting the *trajectory of public opinion* following an event, rather than the event's outcome itself. The rationale is that collective reactions may be more tractable to model than sudden, unpredictable events.
- **Social Simulation:** This field often employs LLM-driven agent-based modeling to derive macro-level patterns from individual micro-decisions. Current "social world models" in this area tend to focus on micro-level behavioral or interactive tasks. The present work diverges by directly parameterizing macro-level social dynamics, bypassing the computational cost and calibration challenges of per-agent simulation. It specifically targets macro-level forecasting in data-rich domains like prediction markets.
- **World Models:** The concept of a "world model" typically involves predicting future states of a physical environment based on current observations and actions. This paper extends this concept to the social domain, treating collective beliefs as semantic states and social events as exogenous actions, thereby introducing the "Social World Model" (SWM).

The paper identifies three hierarchical challenges that necessitate a new approach:
1. **Quantifiability and Data Scarcity (C1):** Social beliefs are semantic-driven and difficult to capture as structured, high-fidelity time-series data, making it challenging to establish benchmarks for evaluating their evolution.
2. **Semantic Complexity of Social Transitions (C2):** The mechanisms governing social shifts are non-symbolic and semantic-driven, influenced by nuanced psychology and cultural context, which traditional statistical or symbolic models struggle to capture.
3. **Lack of Explicit Attribution Labels (C3):** Observing a belief shift does not automatically provide clear labels linking specific events to that shift, hindering models from learning the underlying mechanisms.

This research proposes SWM as a framework to address these challenges by integrating LLMs with prediction market data and a posterior-guided training mechanism, aiming to provide a new pipeline for modeling and predicting collective consensus.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can effectively model the dynamics of social beliefs in response to social events. To achieve this, the authors introduce the concept of the Social World Model (SWM), a general framework designed to capture how social beliefs evolve following major events.

The motivation for this work stems from the inherent difficulties in understanding and predicting social belief evolution, summarized by three primary challenges:

1. **Challenge 1: Quantifiability and Data Scarcity:** Social beliefs, which are collective convictions, are primarily semantic-driven. Unlike physical phenomena, they are not easily reducible to structured numerical data points that can be measured and tracked over time. This absence of high-fidelity, time-series data to represent evolving social beliefs creates a significant barrier to establishing standardized benchmarks for evaluation. Without such data, it becomes nearly impossible to empirically assess how beliefs shift in response to real-world stimuli. The motivation here is to find a reliable and scalable proxy for measuring collective social beliefs.

2. **Challenge 2: Semantic Complexity of Social Transitions:** Even if data were available, the underlying mechanisms that govern social shifts are non-symbolic and deeply semantic. Social dynamics are driven by intricate psychological factors and cultural contexts, rather than by explicit, codified laws or simple statistical relationships. Traditional quantitative or symbolic models often prove inadequate in capturing these rich, implicit transition rules that dictate how a community's beliefs react to a novel event. The motivation is to leverage a model capable of understanding and reasoning about complex textual information and nuanced social contexts.

3. **Challenge 3: Lack of Explicit Attribution Labels:** A critical hurdle in learning the drivers of social belief changes is the absence of direct, explicit labels linking specific events to observed belief shifts. When a belief changes, it is often unclear which specific event or combination of events was responsible. Without this explicit supervision (i.e., "event-to-shift" labels), models struggle to learn the causal mechanisms behind social belief dynamics. The motivation is to devise a training paradigm that can infer these latent attributions without requiring expensive human annotation.

To address these challenges, the research sets forth several key objectives:
- To establish a quantifiable representation of social belief states.
- To develop an LLM-driven architecture capable of interpreting the semantic complexity of social events and their impact on beliefs.
- To create a training methodology that can bridge the attribution gap between events and belief shifts, even without direct supervision.
- To validate the proposed framework with a novel benchmark derived from real-world data, demonstrating its efficacy against existing time-series and LLM-based forecasting methods.

In essence, the motivation is to harness the "commonsense knowledge and social intelligence" of LLMs to move beyond traditional forecasting and simulation methods, providing a robust, interpretable, and scalable framework for modeling collective belief dynamics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the Social World Model (SWM), a generative framework that characterizes social belief dynamics through a state-transition paradigm: $P(s_{t+1} | s_t, e_t)$. This is analogous to classical world models, but adapted for social contexts where collective belief forms the semantic states and social events act as exogenous perturbations.

#### 3.1. Data Selection for Social State Description

To address the challenge of quantifiability (C1), the authors ground the "state" of social beliefs in prediction market data.
- **Source Data:** Data is collected from Polymarket and Kalshi, two prominent prediction market platforms. These platforms are chosen because rational individuals stake financial capital based on their expectations, providing a high-quality, aggregated signal of public opinion.
- **Key Features of Prediction Markets:**
 1. **Uncertainty Constraint:** Markets naturally form around uncertain outcomes, filtering out trivial information.
 2. **Diversity and Scale:** A broad participant base ensures price shifts track societal consensus.
 3. **Investment-driven Quality:** Financial stakes incentivize deliberate decision-making, allowing prices to approximate mean beliefs.
- **Definitions:**
 - **Social Belief ($b_t = (q, v_t)$):** Represents a collective opinion on a binary proposition $q$ (e.g., "Will OpenAI release GPT-5 in Feb 2025?"), where $v_t \in$ is the market-implied probability of a "Yes" resolution (sampled daily as the closing price).
 - **Social Event ($e^i_t = (c^i_t, t)$):** A natural-language news description of a real-world occurrence, occurring at time $t$, that may drive belief evolution. A "null event" ($e^\emptyset_t$) is defined for days without significant external shocks.
 - **State Space ($s_t = (q, (v_{t-k}, \dots, v_t))$):** For a given proposition, the social state is an ordered window of its most recent $k+1$ daily values, capturing temporal context, momentum, and volatility.
 - **Event Space ($E_t = \{e^\emptyset_t, e^1_t, \dots, e^{m_t}_t\}$):** The set of candidate news events available for a transition from day $t$ to day $t+1$, acting as the externally supplied action space.

#### 3.2. Modeling with Latent Event Attribution

To capture the semantic complexity of social transitions (C2) and address the lack of explicit attribution labels (C3), the SWM models the driving event for each transition as a categorical latent variable $Z_t \in \{0, 1, \dots, m\}$.
- **Components:**
 1. **Social World Model ($P_\theta(s_{t+1} | s_t, e^i_t)$):** This component, an LLM, acts as the "transition engine," predicting the next belief value $v_{t+1}$ (and thus $s_{t+1}$) conditioned on the current state $s_t$ and a specific event $e^i_t$. It leverages LLMs' pre-training on human discourse for commonsense reasoning.
 2. **Prior Attributor ($P_\eta(Z_t = i | s_t, E_t)$):** This component scores each candidate event $e^i_t$ from the event set $E_t$ based on the context available *prior* to the belief shift.
- **Null Event:** The null event $e^\emptyset_t$ (indexed by $Z_t=0$) is a special case where the model expects no change, i.e., $E_{P_\theta}[s_{t+1} | s_t, e^\emptyset_t] = s_t$. This allows the model to learn that only non-null events drive changes.

#### 3.3. Training with Posterior Guidance

Directly training SWM is challenging due to the unobserved nature of $Z_t$ and the large number of irrelevant candidate events. The core innovation is "posterior guidance":
- **Posterior Attributor ($Q_\phi(Z_t | s_t, s_{t+1}, E_t)$):** A *frozen* LLM (specifically, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B) is used to perform "inverse inference." By observing the *realized* outcome $s_{t+1}$ (hindsight), this LLM can reliably judge which candidate event most likely explains the observed shift, producing a sharply peaked distribution $\pi_t$. This acts as a source of "pseudo-labels."
- **Training Objective:** The training minimizes a combined loss function:
 $L_{\theta,\eta} = - E_{Z_t \sim \pi_t} [\log P_\theta(s_{t+1} | s_t, e_{Z_t}^t)] + D_{KL}(\pi_t || P_\eta(\cdot | s_t, E_t))$
 1. **World Model Term ($L_{wm}$):** Trains $P_\theta$ to predict the belief change $\Delta_t = s_{t+1} - s_t$ given $s_t$ and the hindsight-attributed event $e_{Z_t}^t$. It uses a weighted squared error, where weights are from $\pi_t$. The null branch's prediction is fixed to zero change.
 2. **Attributor Term ($L_{attr}$):** Distills the hindsight attribution $\pi_t$ into the prior attributor $P_\eta$ by minimizing KL divergence. This teaches $P_\eta$ to predict attribution without seeing $s_{t+1}$.
- **Decoupled Training:** $P_\theta$ and $P_\eta$ are separate models with disjoint LLM backbones (Qwen3-8B and smaller for SWM and prior attributor), allowing independent optimization.
- **First-order Event Approximation (A1):** Each transition is approximated by conditioning on a single hypothesized event, learning a soft distribution over the most explanatory single event.
- **Conditional Event Exogeneity (A2):** Candidate events are assumed to be conditionally exogenous given $s_t$, supporting causal interpretation for scheduled announcements and external news.

#### 3.4. Inference for Forecasting and Simulation

The decoupled architecture supports two inference modes:
- **Forecasting:** Given $s_t$ and a candidate event set $E_t$, the model marginalizes $P_\theta$ over $P_\eta$'s predicted distribution of events:
 $\hat{s}_{t+1} = \sum_{i=0}^m P_\eta(Z_t = i | s_t, E_t) E_{P_\theta}[s_{t+1} | s_t, e^i_t]$
 This results in a forecast equal to the persistence baseline plus an attributor-weighted expected move from non-null events.
- **Simulation:** To simulate the impact of a hypothetical event $e_h$, the model directly evaluates $E_{P_\theta}[s_{t+1} | s_t, e_h] = s_t + \mu_\theta(s_t, e_h)$. This provides an interventional "what-if" estimate of the belief shift caused by a specific shock.

#### 3.5. Experimental Settings

- **SWM-Bench:** Created from Polymarket and Kalshi data (Dec 2022 to Jan 2026), filtering for high price volatility and keyword-matched news to focus on news-driven belief shifts. The dataset contains 2,601 transitions for Kalshi and 10,188 for Polymarket.
- **Baselines:** Include time-series only models (e.g., Autoformer, DLinear), prompting-based LLMs (e.g., GPT-5.5, Qwen3-8B), and fine-tuned LLMs (e.g., Time-LLM, LLMForecasting). SWM uses a Qwen3-8B backbone.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), 3-way Directional Accuracy (DA), and Pearson Correlation (Corr).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of SWM on the SWM-Bench benchmark yielded several findings regarding its performance and the underlying dynamics of social belief evolution.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Kalshi Dataset:** SWM significantly outperformed various baselines, including time-series foundation models and advanced LLMs like GPT-5.5. On the attributed subset (where the posterior attributor identified at least one non-zero probability event), SWM (prior) achieved a Directional Accuracy (DA) of 0.845, which was 4% higher than baselines. It also demonstrated strong performance across other attributed metrics (MASE 0.800, MAE 0.084, Corr 0.380). This suggests that the SWM training approach is effective when event signals are clear and relevant.
- **Polymarket Dataset:** On Polymarket, SWM maintained the highest directional accuracy (DA 0.685) but exhibited competitive rather than superior performance in magnitude-based metrics (MASE, MAE, Corr) compared to some large LLM baselines.
- **Attribution Bottleneck:** When SWM's prior attributor was replaced with an oracle posterior (which has perfect hindsight for event attribution), performance improved substantially across all metrics (e.g., Kalshi DA 0.894, Corr 0.525). This indicates that the core world model (P_$\theta$) is accurate in simulating transitions once the driving event is correctly identified; the primary bottleneck for end-to-end forecasting is the prior attributor's (P_$\eta$) ability to precisely identify and weight the causal shock in real-time.

#### 4.2. Market Dynamics Differences

- The performance discrepancy between Kalshi and Polymarket datasets suggests structural differences in market behavior. Kalshi questions are more often tied to fundamental events, leading to more learnable and attributable updates. In contrast, Polymarket may experience heavier algorithmic or endogenous trading, where price movements are less directly driven by specific news, making event attribution more challenging and weakening the conditional exogeneity assumption.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Social World Model Size:** Larger LLM backbones (0.6B to 8B Qwen3) for the world model (P_$\theta$) consistently improved prediction performance in terms of MASE (decreasing from 0.884 to 0.738 on Kalshi) and Correlation (increasing from 0.290 to 0.525 on Kalshi). This indicates that increased model capacity enhances the translation of textual event evidence into numerical belief dynamics.
- **Time-series Window Size:** Increasing the historical price window (from w=1 to w=16 days) yielded modest performance gains on the attributed subset of Kalshi (MASE 0.787 to 0.738; Corr 0.430 to 0.525). Most of these gains materialized with a short history (w=4), suggesting that a brief price history is sufficient to capture relevant social momentum for one-step belief prediction.
- **Event Set Size:** When using posterior-guided or prior-guided event selection, the model achieved performance comparable to using the full event set with a very small number of events (m=1 for posterior-guided, m=5 for prior-guided). This supports the concept of attribution sparsity, where the causal signal for a market shift is often concentrated in a few relevant events. Random selection of events performed much worse initially and required a larger pool to catch up.
- **Prior Attributor Model Size:** Scaling the prior attributor's backbone (from 0.6B to 8B Qwen3) improved its ability to approximate the oracle hindsight posterior. For Kalshi, the median KL divergence dropped from 2.10 to 1.18, and Recall@3 (oracle's top-1 event in attributor's top-3) rose from 78% to 88%. This indicates that larger attributors provide more reliable signals for downstream world model training.
- **Posterior Attributor Model Size:** The choice of the frozen posterior model (for generating pseudo-labels) involved a trade-off. A larger Qwen3.5-397B posterior generated sharper, more confident attributions (top-1 mass 0.787) but attributed external news causes to a smaller fraction of transitions (11.9%). A smaller Qwen3-32B posterior had flatter attributions (top-1 mass 0.603) but attributed more transitions (47.8%). The sharper 397B posterior led to higher correlation on the *attributed subset* but lower correlation on the *full test set* due to its limited coverage.

#### 4.4. Case Studies

- **Causality in Posterior Attribution:** Case studies illustrated that LLM-based posterior attribution acts as a "causally aligned explanatory filter," identifying news semantically relevant, temporally precedent, and directionally consistent with a belief shift. Examples showed both genuinely causal attributions (e.g., specific BOJ news leading to rate hike probability shift) and spurious correlations (e.g., generic Ethereum news attributed to price drops across different contracts).
- **Forecasting and Simulation:** SWM successfully integrated unstructured news to forecast belief trajectories, as shown in a Trump-Putin meeting scenario where the model weighted news about a peace deal to predict an upward correction. In simulation mode, SWM could estimate belief shifts for both real and hypothetical events (e.g., predicting a reduction in hike probability due to a synthetic oil price crash). However, a case involving corporate profits and the weak yen highlighted a limitation: the model's LLM backbone might rely on surface-level heuristics rather than fully internalizing complex multi-step systemic or economic mechanisms, leading to incorrect predictions for nuanced scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of the Social World Model (SWM) and the SWM-Bench benchmark presents a framework for predicting the evolution of collective beliefs in response to global events. By integrating prediction market data as a proxy for social beliefs and leveraging the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), this work offers a scalable toolkit for quantitative digital sociology.

**Positive Impact:**
- **Enhanced Decision-Making:** SWM's capacity to anticipate public reactions to critical developments, such as public health crises, macroeconomic shocks, or policy interventions, can assist policymakers, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and economists. This predictive capability could facilitate more proactive, informed, and resilient governance, potentially leading to policies that are better aligned with societal needs and public sentiment.
- **Insights into Social Dynamics:** The framework offers interpretable insights into the mechanisms driving social belief dynamics, potentially advancing the understanding of how information influences collective consensus.

**Ethical Risks and Concerns:**
- **Manipulation and Disinformation:** A significant risk lies in the potential for malicious deployment. If a social world model were used to identify informational stimuli that trigger desired belief shifts, it could be weaponized to optimize targeted disinformation campaigns, engineer persuasive social engineering efforts, or manipulate public opinion. This raises concerns about the erosion of trust in information ecosystems and the democratic process.
- **Algorithmic Feedback Loops:** The reliance on prediction markets as a proxy for public sentiment, combined with the public forecasting capabilities of such a system, could lead to algorithmic feedback loops in live environments. Public forecasts generated by the model might inadvertently amplify market volatility or artificially distort the very consensus they are designed to measure, creating self-fulfilling prophecies or unstable market conditions.
- **Limitations of Causal Inference:** While the framework aims for causally aligned explanations, the paper acknowledges that LLM-based posterior attribution does not formally certify causation and can occasionally latch onto spurious correlations. Overreliance on such systems for causal insights without careful validation could lead to misinformed decisions.

**Mitigation Strategies:**
The authors advocate for transparent and defensive deployment of social world models.
- **Defensive Applications:** Instead of optimizing for public intervention, these tools are suggested to be best utilized for detecting unnatural, abrupt, or inorganic shifts in collective belief. This capability could aid researchers in identifying and countering coordinated influence operations and disinformation campaigns, thereby protecting open information ecosystems.
- **Rigorous Ethical Oversight:** The application of SWM, and similar tools, requires rigorous ethical oversight to ensure their use promotes societal well-being rather than exploitation. This includes careful consideration of data privacy, bias in LLMs, and the potential for unintended consequences.

In summary, while SWM provides a powerful analytical lens into collective human beliefs, its development and application necessitate a balanced approach that harnesses its predictive potential while actively addressing and mitigating its inherent ethical risks.
