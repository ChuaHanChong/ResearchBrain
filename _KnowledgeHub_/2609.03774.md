# Rethinking World Models for Safety-Critical Embodied Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> This position paper proposes the Risk-Informed World Model (RIWM), reorienting world modeling from predicting likely futures toward identifying futures that could change an action. It frames capability as a predictive, consequential, reflective progression and specifies four interdependent components: decision-relevant representation, counterfactual reasoning, safety-critical episodic memory, and runtime safety assurance. The headline outcome is a research agenda plus five open challenges, not benchmark evidence.

## Problem

- Generative driving world models optimize likelihood, reconstruction and visual fidelity, so they can suppress low-probability but consequential evidence that should change the current action.
- Action-conditioned prediction and multi-agent simulators do not identify how the world would respond to unchosen interventions, conflating conditioning with genuine counterfactual reasoning.
- Frame-wise safety and longer prediction horizons do not preserve why past events mattered; existing agent-memory work retains history without its safety relevance.

## Method

- A ==three-level cognitive progression==, predictive to consequential to reflective, defines consequential futures as credible branches altering feasibility, acceptability or recoverability of candidate actions.
- **RIWM**'s four capabilities: ==decision-relevant representation==, ==bounded counterfactual reasoning== with adaptive branch budgets, ==safety-critical episodic memory== storing failures with provenance and assumptions, and ==runtime safety assurance==.
- Consequences split into physical, social and operational domains kept uncompressed, with ==epistemic uncertainty== qualifying evidence and an expanded response set: act, revise, sense, defer, abstain.

## Results

- No experiments, benchmarks or ablations are reported; the paper offers no quantitative comparison against GAIA-2, UniSim, OccWorld or the other world models it cites.
- Evidence is a worked unprotected-left-turn case where a **0.20**-probability pedestrian emergence, not the **0.70** clear-passage branch, governs whether acceleration is defensible.
- Support is argumentative, citing K-Risk, RiskNet, LEAD and lifelong-learning memory work as partial ingredients that individually fail to determine which evidence shifts an action boundary.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decision relevance, not visual salience or frequency, should shape what a world model represents; risk appended after prediction cannot recover collapsed structure.
> - Action conditioning is not causal intervention; counterfactual claims need explicit assumptions, uncertainty bounds and stated branch coverage, since generability is not credibility.
> - Useful memory stores consequences, failed assumptions and recovery outcomes with provenance, and must stay revisable, since sensor, policy or rule changes invalidate past episodes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of world models for embodied systems has seen substantial advancements, progressing from initial concepts focusing on compact latent dynamics for control to the development of sophisticated generative, controllable, and interactive simulators. Recent contributions in this area include GAIA-2, which facilitates controllable multi-view synthesis; UniSim, enabling interactive real-world simulation; and models such as TrafficBots, OccWorld, and DriveDreamer4D, which extend capabilities to multi-agent behavior modeling, 3D occupancy prediction, and 4D scene representation, respectively. These developments collectively enhance the generative and predictive capacities of world models, often prioritizing aspects like visual fidelity, reconstruction accuracy, and the likelihood of future events.

However, the authors identify a critical gap within this landscape, particularly concerning safety-critical embodied systems. They contend that while current world models excel in generating accurate and visually realistic scenarios, they do not inherently improve a system's understanding of decision-relevant information crucial for safety. The established paradigms, primarily driven by objectives such as maximizing likelihood or visual fidelity, may inadvertently suppress or overlook evidence that should prompt a change in an autonomous system's action. This oversight can be detrimental in scenarios where low-probability but high-consequence events dictate safe operation. The presented work proposes a conceptual shift, advocating for world models that are explicitly designed to prioritize safety-critical decision-making over mere prediction or visual realism, thereby addressing the limitations of current approaches in ensuring robust safety for autonomous agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the observation that existing world models, despite their advanced predictive and generative capabilities, often fall short in providing the specific insights required for safety-critical decision-making in embodied systems. The authors illustrate this through the example of an unprotected left turn, where the most likely future (an unobstructed crossing) may not be the most *consequential* one. A lower-probability event, such as a pedestrian emerging from behind an occluder, could fundamentally alter the appropriate action. Furthermore, the system's own actions (e.g., waiting, creeping, or accelerating) actively shape the future by influencing other road users' responses, available information, and recovery space. The current paradigm, which often optimizes for likelihood, reconstruction accuracy, or visual fidelity, may inadvertently de-emphasize or fail to represent this decision-relevant, safety-critical evidence.

Given this motivation, the paper outlines several key objectives:

1. **Establish a Cognitive Progression for World Model Capability:** The authors propose a three-level framework to categorize world model capabilities:
 - **Predictive Modeling:** Focuses on "what is most likely to happen."
 - **Consequential Modeling:** Addresses "what could change the action."
 - **Reflective Modeling:** Asks "is the evidence sufficient to act?"
 This framework aims to move beyond prediction to integrate decision-making and assessment of evidence sufficiency.

2. **Propose the Risk-Informed World Model (RIWM) as a Research Direction:** The central objective is to introduce RIWM as a conceptual framework for developing world models explicitly organized around action consequences in safety-critical embodied systems. RIWM aims to integrate decision-relevant evidence into representation, counterfactual reasoning, episodic memory, and runtime safety assurance, with continuous revision based on execution feedback.

3. **Address Three Structural Mismatches in Current World Models:** The paper seeks to articulate and provide a framework to resolve three fundamental limitations that hinder the safety relevance of current world models:
 - **Likelihood-Risk Mismatch:** To address the problem where the futures most critical for action are not necessarily the most frequent. This mismatch highlights the "curse of rarity" in safety-critical events and emphasizes the need for prioritizing informative scenarios that carry high consequences, even if their probability is low, and considering epistemic uncertainty.
 - **Prediction-Intervention Mismatch:** To rectify the issue that embodied agents do not merely predict the future but actively shape it through their actions. The objective is to move beyond simple action-conditioned prediction towards modeling how responses vary with interventions, enabling counterfactual comparison, and making explicit the assumptions behind causal claims.
 - **Horizon-Accumulation Mismatch:** To resolve the problem that a sequence of locally safe decisions can still accumulate into an unsafe long-term outcome. This objective calls for mechanisms to retain the consequential structure of experience (actions, environmental responses, consequences, failed assumptions, recovery outcomes) rather than just indiscriminate historical data, ensuring that past failures and recoveries inform future judgment.

By addressing these objectives, the paper aims to shift the focus of world model research from prediction-centric to decision-centric design, thereby enhancing the safety and trustworthiness of autonomous systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper presents a conceptual framework rather than an empirical study involving specific algorithms or experimental results. The methodology consists of:

1. **Conceptual Elaboration of Mismatches:** The authors systematically identify and detail three "structural mismatches" inherent in traditional world model approaches when applied to safety-critical systems:
 - **Likelihood-Risk Mismatch:** This mismatch is described by explaining how safety-critical events are often rare, and thus, objectives based on likelihood or scalar probability-severity scores may obscure crucial decision structure. The role of epistemic uncertainty, which reflects limited knowledge and may lead to unrepresented relevant branches, is emphasized.
 - **Prediction-Intervention Mismatch:** This mismatch highlights that an agent's actions are interventions that shape the future, rather than just passively predicting it. It distinguishes between action-conditioned prediction, intervention-sensitive rollout, counterfactual comparison, and causal identification, arguing that current models often conflate these.
 - **Horizon-Accumulation Mismatch:** This mismatch focuses on the limitation that local safety does not guarantee long-term recoverability. It points out the need for retaining the consequential structure of experience, including failures, near misses, and recoveries, rather than merely extending prediction horizons or context windows.

2. **Introduction of a Cognitive Progression Framework:** The paper proposes a three-level cognitive progression (Predictive, Consequential, Reflective World Modeling) to characterize the desired evolution of world model capabilities, shifting from a prediction-centric to a decision-centric orientation.

3. **Development of the Risk-Informed World Model (RIWM) Framework:** The core of the methodology is the introduction of RIWM, a conceptual architecture designed to address the identified mismatches. RIWM integrates four interdependent capabilities:
 - **Decision-Relevant Representation:** The approach advocates for prioritizing information that directly impacts action boundaries—such as conflict regions, occlusion, right of way, social norms, and escape space—over general visual or occupancy accuracy. It emphasizes preserving distinctions across physical, social, and operational consequence domains, and qualifying evidence with epistemic uncertainty.
 - **Counterfactual Reasoning:** This capability proposes that the allocation of modeling resources (rollout breadth, horizon, fidelity) should adapt to decision relevance. It stresses the importance of preserving both the most likely baseline and *credible, decision-relevant alternatives* that might alter safety boundaries, supported by observation, calibrated uncertainty, or valid episodic memory, rather than arbitrary hazard generation.
 - **Safety-Critical Episodic Memory:** The framework suggests retaining a rich, structured memory of consequential experiences, including failures, near misses, and successful recoveries, linked with the situation, action, responses, consequences, assumptions, confidence, and provenance. This memory is not merely a history for visual similarity retrieval but a revisable record that can inform and update current judgment.
 - **Runtime Safety Assurance:** RIWM's approach is to inform existing assurance mechanisms (e.g., Model Predictive Control, Control Barrier Functions) by providing decision-relevant information (conflicts, occlusions, recovery space, evidence sufficiency). It highlights the necessity for the model to expose its boundaries and monitor critical assumptions, triggering revision, active sensing, deferral, or abstention when premises are not supported by current evidence.

4. **Integration of Key Concepts:** Throughout the RIWM framework, the methodology consistently integrates three central concepts:
 - **Decision-Relevant Consequences:** Categorized into physical (collision, recovery space), social (norms, interactions), and operational (progress, efficiency, information gain, deadlock) domains.
 - **Epistemic Uncertainty:** Used to qualify the strength of evidence supporting consequence assessment and action selection.
 - **Execution Feedback:** Continuously revises the evidence and assumptions within the model.

5. **Identification of Open Challenges:** The paper concludes its methodological approach by articulating specific open challenges for the practical implementation and further development of RIWM, thereby guiding future research. These challenges include defining decision-relevant consequences, ensuring reliable counterfactual reasoning, managing the trustworthiness of episodic memory, translating learned consequences into enforceable constraints, and determining when evidence is sufficient for action.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a conceptual paper, the "findings" are primarily the articulated framework, identified challenges, and proposed shifts in research priorities, rather than experimental results. The main findings and results are:

1. **Identification of Structural Mismatches:** The paper identifies and systematically describes three critical structural mismatches in current world model approaches that limit their applicability and effectiveness in safety-critical embodied systems:
 - The **likelihood-risk mismatch**, where low-probability but high-consequence events are overlooked due to a focus on statistically common scenarios.
 - The **prediction-intervention mismatch**, where models fail to adequately capture how an agent's own actions actively shape and alter future outcomes, requiring a shift from passive prediction to active intervention modeling.
 - The **horizon-accumulation mismatch**, highlighting that local, short-term safety does not guarantee long-term recoverability, necessitating a mechanism to accumulate and leverage safety-critical experience over extended operational horizons.

2. **Proposed Three-Level Cognitive Progression:** The paper introduces a conceptual framework for world model capabilities, progressing from Predictive (what is most likely), to Consequential (what could change the action), and finally to Reflective (is evidence sufficient to act). This framework serves as a guide for developing more decision-centric world models.

3. **Introduction of the Risk-Informed World Model (RIWM) Framework:** The central result is the proposal of RIWM, a comprehensive conceptual framework for world models specifically designed for safety-critical embodied systems. RIWM is characterized by:
 - **Decision-Relevant Representation:** A focus on preserving information that directly impacts action boundaries (e.g., conflict regions, escape space, occlusion, right-of-way) rather than generic visual or physical accuracy.
 - **Consequence-Driven Counterfactual Reasoning:** The allocation of computational resources for rollouts (breadth, horizon, fidelity) is dynamically adapted based on decision relevance, prioritizing credible alternatives that could alter safety boundaries over exhaustive or arbitrary scenario generation.
 - **Safety-Critical Episodic Memory:** A mechanism to store and retrieve structured experiences of failures, near misses, and successful recoveries, along with their context, actions, responses, and underlying assumptions, enabling continuous learning and revision of current judgments.
 - **Integration with Runtime Safety Assurance:** RIWM is positioned to provide critical, decision-relevant information (e.g., conflicts, recovery space, evidence sufficiency) to existing executable safety mechanisms (like MPC or CBFs), rather than replacing them. It also emphasizes exposing model boundaries and monitoring the validity of critical assumptions at runtime.

4. **Emphasis on Epistemic Uncertainty and Distinct Consequence Domains:** The framework highlights the crucial role of epistemic uncertainty in qualifying evidence and the need to treat physical, social, and operational consequences as distinct, identifiable decision domains, rather than compressing them into a single scalar risk score.

5. **Identification of Open Challenges:** The paper concludes by presenting a set of concrete open challenges that must be addressed to translate the RIWM framework into practical, implementable systems. These challenges range from defining and combining heterogeneous forms of decision-relevant consequences to ensuring the trustworthiness of episodic memory and establishing robust interfaces between learned consequences and enforceable constraints.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of this research are substantial, particularly for the development and deployment of autonomous systems in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving.

1. **Paradigm Shift Towards Decision-Centric World Modeling:** The paper advocates for a fundamental reorientation in world model research, moving from a prediction-centric to a decision-centric paradigm. This shift implies that the design, training, and evaluation of world models should prioritize how they inform and enhance safe decision-making, rather than solely focusing on the accuracy or fidelity of future predictions. This change in perspective could lead to more robust and reliable autonomous systems.

2. **Enhanced Safety and Trustworthiness:** By explicitly incorporating concepts of risk, consequences, and epistemic uncertainty into the core design of world models, RIWM offers a path towards significantly improving the safety and trustworthiness of embodied systems. It addresses the critical problem of rare but high-consequence events ("curse of rarity") by directing computational and representational resources towards what matters for safety, potentially reducing incidents and improving public acceptance of autonomous technologies.

3. **Structured Framework for Future Research:** RIWM provides a comprehensive conceptual framework that integrates various aspects of autonomy (perception, prediction, planning, control, learning, assurance) under a unified safety-centric objective. By clearly articulating the problems (mismatches), proposing solutions (RIWM capabilities), and identifying specific open challenges, the paper offers a structured roadmap for future research and development in world models for safety-critical applications.

4. **Improved Handling of Uncertainty and Interactivity:** The emphasis on epistemic uncertainty, action-conditioned rollout, and counterfactual reasoning enables autonomous systems to better understand their own knowledge boundaries, the impact of their interventions on the environment, and the range of possible outcomes. This leads to more cautious and adaptive decision-making, especially in highly interactive and uncertain environments.

5. **Adaptive Resource Allocation:** The proposal for dynamically allocating modeling resources based on decision relevance and recovery margins offers a practical approach to managing computational complexity. Instead of uniformly simulating all futures, resources can be focused on critical scenarios, making world models more efficient and scalable for real-time safety applications.

6. **Continuous Learning and Adaptation:** The concept of safety-critical episodic memory, which retains and revises consequential experiences (failures, near misses, recoveries), provides a mechanism for continuous learning and adaptation. This enables autonomous systems to learn from past incidents, update their understanding of risks, and improve their decision-making over their operational lifespan, even under changing conditions.

In summary, this work provides a foundational conceptual shift for world model development, redirecting focus from generalized prediction to tailored, safety-informed decision support. Its principles have the potential to significantly impact the design, verification, and operational safety of future safety-critical embodied autonomous systems.
