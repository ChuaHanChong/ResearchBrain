# Human-assisted Robotic Policy Refinement via Action Preference Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Action Preference Optimization (APO) facilitates continuous, iterative refinement of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by learning from real-time human-assisted corrections and encountered failures post-deployment. The method yielded a 48.0% average success rate in RoboMimic simulation, outperforming baselines, and improved real-world task success (e.g., 85% for insertion) while reducing human intervention frequency.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models primarily rely on large-scale offline expert demonstrations, limiting their ability to learn and adapt post-deployment to dynamic, real-world environments.
- Existing interactive learning methods, like behavior cloning, suffer from catastrophic forgetting when exposed to sub-optimal human intervention, while reinforcement learning approaches are often unstable and not scalable for high-dimensional VLA models.
- Direct application of preference optimization techniques (successful in LLMs) to VLA models faces unique challenges due to irreversible robotic actions and the mismatch between discrete action token probabilities and continuous action regression errors.

## Method

- **APO** introduces a human-robot collaboration framework to collect interaction trajectories, where human interventions label desirable actions and identify preceding K steps as undesirable for learning.
- It adapts ==preference optimization== using a ==prospect theory==-based utility function and a reward estimated from the log-likelihood ratio, allowing learning from binary desirability signals without requiring perfect paired preference data.
- A novel ==adaptive reweighting== mechanism bridges the gap between discrete token prediction and continuous action regression by dynamically adjusting sample importance based on L1 loss, focusing optimization on failure-prone actions.

## Results

- APO achieved the highest average success rate of **48.0%** across four fine-grained manipulation tasks in RoboMimic simulation, surpassing baseline behavior cloning and other preference optimization methods like DPO and KTO.
- The method demonstrated superior generalization to novel disruptions, achieving a **28.0%** average success rate in environments with position, background, and texture changes, and mitigated catastrophic forgetting on original tasks.
- Real-world experiments confirmed APO's practical utility, improving a fine-grained insertion task's success rate from **65%** to **85%** and exhibiting continuous iterative improvement with reduced human intervention over successive learning loops.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Iteratively refining VLA models using human-assisted action preferences and explicitly identified failure trajectories leads to continuous performance improvement and reduced human intervention over time.
> - Adaptively reweighting samples based on continuous action prediction errors significantly improves learning stability and effectiveness, especially for correcting specific failure modes in VLA models.
> - Incorporating a regularization term and adaptive reweighting allows VLA models to adapt to novel disruptions without catastrophic forgetting, preserving or enhancing performance on original tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing towards more intelligent and adaptable systems, with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models emerging as a cornerstone. These models, akin to foundation models in natural language processing, are designed to enable robots to understand complex commands, perceive their environment, and execute diverse actions. However, a significant challenge in deploying VLA models in dynamic, real-world environments is their current reliance on large-scale datasets of *offline expert demonstrations*. This paradigm fundamentally limits their ability to continuously learn, adapt to unforeseen circumstances, or correct mistakes once deployed.

This paper positions itself directly at the intersection of VLA models, human-robot interaction, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), addressing the critical need for post-deployment refinement. The authors highlight that while VLA models offer generalizable reasoning, their "prevailing training paradigm...severely limits their post-deployment refinement, as they lack intrinsic ability to continually learn from failures or adapt to novel scenarios."

Traditional approaches to robot learning from interaction include:
- **Interactive Imitation Learning (e.g., Behavior Cloning - BC):** While simple, BC often suffers from distributional shift when fine-tuned on sub-optimal human intervention data, leading to "catastrophic forgetting" of the base policy's knowledge. The paper explicitly states that "behavior cloning fails to fully exploit failure trajectories, which are valuable signals for learning robust policies."
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) from human feedback:** Methods like RLIF and HIL-SERL attempt to use human intervention as a reward signal. However, these are often unstable and face scalability limitations when applied to large, high-dimensional VLA models due to complex value function learning and optimization challenges.

The paper argues that an "effective adaptation of VLA models for downstream manipulation tasks remains understudied, particularly within sub-optimal human intervention paradigms." It seeks to bridge this gap by proposing **Action Preference Optimization (APO)**, which moves beyond the limitations of both behavior cloning and traditional RL. APO draws inspiration from recent advances in **preference alignment for Large Language Models (LLMs)**, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and Kahneman-Tversky Optimization (KTO). These methods have demonstrated stable and efficient ways to fine-tune LLMs using human preferences, bypassing the instability of explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning.

However, the authors identify unique challenges when transferring these LLM-centric preference optimization techniques to VLA models:
1. **Irreversible Robotic Actions:** Unlike text generation where alternative outputs can be easily compared, physical robot interactions are irreversible, making it difficult to collect perfect paired positive/negative action samples under identical conditions.
2. **Token Probability Mismatch:** Autoregressive VLA models discretize continuous actions into tokens. The mapping from discrete token probabilities to continuous action errors is non-trivial, complicating direct preference alignment.

By adapting and enhancing preference optimization with novel mechanisms like binary desirability signals and adaptive reweighting, APO directly addresses these VLA-specific challenges. This work contributes to the broader landscape by providing a more stable, efficient, and robust mechanism for VLA models to learn from imperfect human feedback during deployment, thereby pushing towards the realization of truly adaptable and generalist robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **establish a reliable and iteratively refined robotic system** that can be effectively deployed in real-world applications. This ambitious goal is motivated by several critical limitations of current state-of-the-art robotic systems, particularly those built upon Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

The primary motivations and specific objectives can be broken down as follows:

- **Mitigate Limitations of Offline Expert Demonstrations:** VLA models are recognized as foundational for robotic deployment due to their generalizable reasoning and scalable learning capabilities. However, their reliance on large-scale, offline expert demonstrations is a significant bottleneck. This training paradigm severely restricts their ability to improve *after* deployment, meaning they struggle to learn from failures or adapt to novel scenarios encountered in dynamic real-world environments. The paper aims to overcome this "critical limitation."
- **Enable Continuous Learning from Interaction:** To make VLA models viable for real-world deployment, they must possess the intrinsic ability for continuous learning and refinement. This entails learning from mistakes and incorporating human feedback effectively. The motivation is to "equip VLA models with the crucial capability to learn from failure."
- **Address Challenges in Interactive Imitation Learning:** Existing interactive imitation learning frameworks, while designed for refinement, have their own drawbacks. Behavior cloning often fails to fully exploit valuable failure trajectories and can suffer from catastrophic forgetting. Reinforcement learning methods, while powerful, face significant scalability and stability issues when applied to large-scale VLA models. The paper seeks a new paradigm that "moves beyond the limitations of both behavior cloning and reinforcement learning."
- **Leverage Human-Robot Collaboration for Data Collection and Reliability:** Real-world deployment of robots necessitates mechanisms for reliable operation. Human intervention is crucial not only for correcting failures in real-time but also for generating valuable data. A key objective is to "ensure reliable deployment while simultaneously generating data for policy refinement" through a human-robot collaboration framework.
- **Overcome VLA-Specific Challenges in Preference Optimization:** Direct application of preference alignment methods (successful in LLMs) to autoregressive VLA models presents unique hurdles:
 - **Irreversible Robotic Actions:** Physical interactions cannot be undone, making it difficult to obtain paired positive and negative samples under identical conditions. APO aims to address this by using a preference alignment objective that learns from "binary desirability signals," relaxing the stringent demands of preference pairs.
 - **Token Probability Mismatch:** The discretization of continuous actions into tokens in VLA models creates a discrepancy between token classification probabilities and continuous action regression errors. APO proposes an "adaptive reweighting method" to bridge this gap, focusing optimization on failure-prone actions.

In essence, the paper's core objective is to develop a novel method, APO, that allows VLA models to iteratively refine their policies by learning from real-time human-assisted corrections and failures encountered during deployment. This ensures both operational reliability and continuous performance improvement, paving the way for more robust and adaptable robotic systems in complex, unstructured environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Action Preference Optimization (APO) method is designed to enable continuous iterative improvement of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models through human-assisted preference alignment. It comprises two main interconnected components: a human-robot collaboration framework for data collection and an action preference optimization process with adaptive reweighting.

#### 3.1. Human-robot Collaboration Deployment

This phase focuses on ensuring reliable robotic deployment while systematically collecting interaction trajectories for policy refinement.

1. **Warm-start Phase:**
 - An initial VLA model ($\pi_{0\theta}$) is trained using **behavior cloning (BC)** on a dataset of expert demonstrations ($D_e$). These demonstrations consist of observation-action pairs, with actions executed by a human expert ($c_t=1$). This establishes a baseline policy.

2. **Deployment-Optimization Loop:**
 - The current policy ($\pi_{i\theta}$) is deployed in the environment.
 - **Human Intervention:** A human operator actively monitors the robot's execution. If the robot encounters a challenging situation or is about to fail, the human intervenes in real-time, taking control to correct the robot's actions. This intervention ensures task completion and provides crucial corrective feedback.
 - **Interaction Trajectory Collection:** During this process, a new set of interaction trajectories ($D_h$) is collected.
 - Actions executed by the human intervention are labeled with $c_t=2$ (explicitly corrected/desirable).
 - Actions executed by the policy are labeled with $c_t=1$ (policy-generated/desirable).
 - A crucial step is the re-labeling of *undesirable* actions: The actions taken by the policy in the **K steps immediately preceding a human intervention** are identified as failure-prone and are relabeled with $c_t=0$. This provides explicit negative feedback without requiring paired examples. The paper uses K=10.
 - **Data Aggregation and Balanced Sampling:** The collected expert demonstrations ($D_e$) and interaction trajectories ($D_h$) are combined. To prevent class imbalance issues during training, especially with underrepresented failure actions, a **balanced sampling method** is employed. For instance, in experiments, each batch contains 50% expert actions, 25% human intervention actions, and 25% failure actions.

#### 3.2. Action Preference Optimization

This is the core learning mechanism where the VLA model is fine-tuned using the collected interaction data. The goal is to maximize the utility of sub-optimal interaction trajectories while ensuring stable optimization.

1. **Reward Function Estimation:** Following standard approaches in preference-based learning (e.g., DPO, KTO), the reward function $r_\theta(o, \hat{a})$ for the current model $\pi_\theta$ is estimated as the log-likelihood ratio between the current policy and a reference model ($\pi_{ref}$), which is the base model from the beginning of the current deployment-optimization loop:
 $r_\theta(o, \hat{a}) = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(\hat{a}|o)}{\pi_{ref}(\hat{a}|o)}$
 Here, $\hat{a}$ represents the discrete action token.

2. **Utility Function Formulation (Prospect Theory Adaptation):** To handle the challenge of irreversible robotic actions and the need for binary desirability signals, APO adopts Kahneman & Tversky's prospect theory. A utility function $v(o, \hat{a})$ is defined:
 - For desirable actions ($\hat{a}_{desirable}$), the utility is $\lambda_D \sigma (r_\theta(o, \hat{a}) - z_0)$.
 - For undesirable actions ($\hat{a}_{undesirable}$), the utility is $\lambda_U \sigma (z_0 - r_\theta(o, \hat{a}))$.
 - $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.
 - $z_0$ is a penalty term defined as $KL(\pi_\theta || \pi_{ref})$. This term acts as a regularizer, preventing the policy from deviating too much from the reference model and preserving prior knowledge, while also enabling learning from preferences without requiring perfect paired data. The KL divergence is computed using the KTO method's approach for mismatched sample pairs.

3. **Loss Function:** The model $\pi_\theta$ is optimized by minimizing the loss function $L(\pi_\theta, \pi_{ref}) = E_{x,y \sim D_h}[-v(x,y)]$. This objective pushes the model to assign higher rewards to desirable actions and lower rewards to undesirable ones, relative to the reference model.

4. **Adaptive Reweighting (Key Novelty):** This mechanism addresses the "token probability mismatch" challenge by bridging the gap between discrete token prediction (VLA output) and continuous action regression (ground truth action error).
 - For each sample $i$ in a batch, the **L1 loss** for the continuous action ($l_i = |\pi_\theta(o_i) - a_i|_1$) is calculated. This measures how far the VLA model's *decoded continuous action* is from the ground truth.
 - These losses are normalized within the batch to derive sample-wise weights: $w_i = \frac{l_i}{\sum_{j=1}^B l_j}$.
 - These normalized weights $w_i$ are then used to dynamically modulate the importance sampling factors $\lambda_D$ and $\lambda_U$ in the utility function:
 - $\lambda_D = 1 - e^{-\beta_D * w_i}$: For desirable data, samples with *high action prediction errors* (meaning the model performed poorly on a good action) receive increased weight, encouraging the model to improve on areas it struggles with.
 - $\lambda_U = e^{-\beta_U * w_i}$: For undesirable data, samples whose actions are *proximate to failure actions* (meaning the model almost made the same mistake) receive increased weight. This focuses gradient optimization on preventing common failure modes and learning specific corrections.
 - This adaptive reweighting scheme allows for fine-grained control over sample influence, concentrating learning on critical failure points and challenging desirable actions.

Through this iterative cycle of human-robot collaboration for data generation and the sophisticated preference optimization with adaptive reweighting, APO aims to achieve stable and continuous improvement of VLA models in dynamic, interactive settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across both simulation (RoboMimic) and real-world scenarios provide strong empirical evidence for the effectiveness, robustness, and generalizability of the Action Preference Optimization (APO) method.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness in In-Distribution Scenarios (RoboMimic Simulation)

- **Comparison with Baselines (Table 1):** APO consistently achieved the highest average success rate (48.0%) across four fine-grained manipulation tasks in RoboMimic.
 - Behavior cloning methods (Dagger, Sirius) performed poorly, often worse than the base policy, illustrating their struggle with distribution shifts and catastrophic forgetting when mixing expert and sub-optimal interaction data.
 - Preference optimization methods (DPO, TPO, KTO) generally outperformed behavior cloning and the base policy, demonstrating the value of preference alignment.
 - APO surpassed all comparison methods, including KTO (43.5%), which also uses binary desirability signals. This highlights the crucial contribution of APO's adaptive reweighting, which allows for more precise control over sample importance and thus more stable and effective learning from corrections. DPO's reliance on synthetic negative samples was shown to be less effective than real interaction data.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Novel Tasks and Disruptions

- **Disruption Scenarios (Table 2):** APO demonstrated superior adaptation to three types of novel disruptions (position, background, texture changes). It achieved the highest average success rate (28.0%) in these challenging environments, outperforming the base policy (21.3%) and other methods (KTO at 24.0%). This validates APO's capability to learn robust policies that generalize to unseen perturbations.
- **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting (Table 3):** After fine-tuning with disruption data, APO not only adapted to new scenarios but also preserved or even improved performance on the original tasks (45.3% mean success rate, compared to 39.3% for the base policy). In contrast, behavior cloning methods suffered severe catastrophic forgetting, with significant performance degradation on the original tasks. This underscores the effectiveness of APO's regularization term ($z_0 = KL(\pi_\theta || \pi_{ref})$) and adaptive reweighting in balancing adaptation with knowledge retention.

#### 4.3. Lifelong Learning Performance

- **Iterative Improvement (Figure 4a-b):** Experiments showed that APO enables continuous, iterative improvement of the robot's success rate over multiple deployment-optimization loops in tasks like 'Coffee_D0' and 'StackThree_D0'. This contrasts with the diminishing returns often observed with increasing expert demonstrations.
- **Reduced Human Intervention (Figure 4c):** The performance gains achieved by APO were directly correlated with a reduction in the frequency of required human interventions. This indicates that the robot became progressively more autonomous and capable of handling challenging situations by itself, reducing the human operator's workload over time.

#### 4.4. Generalization to Various VLA Models

- **Support for $\pi_0$-FAST (Table 4 & 6):** APO was successfully applied to fine-tune $\pi_0$-FAST, another VLA model that uses discrete cosine transform encoding for action chunking. APO consistently yielded performance gains for $\pi_0$-FAST across different tasks, demonstrating its architectural independence and broad applicability across diverse VLA frameworks.

#### 4.5. Real-world Experiments

- **Robustness in Real-world Tasks (Table 5 & 7, Figures 5 & 8):** APO was tested on a challenging fine-grained insertion task ("Insert the square into the stick") and other tasks ("hang cup," "put lemon") in real-world settings.
 - It significantly improved the base policy's success rate in-distribution (85% vs. 65% for insertion) and exhibited robust adaptability to real-world position, background, and texture disruptions.
 - This confirms APO's practical utility and effectiveness for deploying VLA models in unstructured real-world environments.

#### 4.6. Correction from Failure Scenarios

- **Autonomous Self-Correction (Figure 6):** Visual examples provided in the paper demonstrate that APO learned not only to avoid failure modes but also to autonomously self-correct within failure scenarios. Examples included re-attempting grasps after initial failure and iteratively adjusting gripper positions for successful insertion. This ability to recover from mistakes is a critical component of robust real-world robotic behavior and directly contributes to the improved overall performance.

In summary, the results rigorously validate that APO effectively promotes adaptation, maintains robustness against disruptions, facilitates iterative lifelong learning, generalizes across different VLA models, and performs reliably in real-world scenarios, primarily by enabling models to learn from and correct failures through intelligent human interaction and adaptive preference optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Human-assisted Robotic Policy Refinement via Action Preference Optimization" (APO) work presents a significant step forward in the development and deployment of intelligent robotic systems. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

1. **Enabling Continuous Post-Deployment Refinement for VLA Models:** The most critical impact of APO is its ability to overcome a fundamental limitation of current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: their inability to learn and improve *after* initial deployment. By providing a stable and effective mechanism for continuous refinement from real-world interaction and human feedback, APO unlocks the potential for VLA models to evolve and adapt over time, moving beyond static, expert-demonstration-bound performance. This is crucial for real-world scenarios where environments are dynamic, and unforeseen challenges are inevitable.

2. **Bridging the Gap Between LLM Alignment and Robotics:** APO successfully adapts the powerful paradigm of preference alignment, which has revolutionized Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning (e.g., RLHF, DPO, KTO), to the complex domain of robotic control. By effectively addressing unique challenges in robotics—such as irreversible actions and the mismatch between discrete action tokens and continuous physical errors—APO paves the way for a broader application of these advanced learning techniques to embodied AI. This transfer of knowledge and methodology is highly impactful, allowing robotics to benefit from state-of-the-art developments in foundation models.

3. **Enhancing Robustness and Generalization in Dynamic Environments:** Learning from explicit failure trajectories and human-corrected actions imbues VLA models with a higher degree of robustness. The experimental results, particularly in novel disruption scenarios (position, background, texture changes) and real-world settings, demonstrate that APO-trained policies are more resilient and generalizable. This means robots can operate more reliably in unstructured and unpredictable environments, reducing the need for extensive, task-specific re-training. The ability to "learn from failure" is a cornerstone of intelligent behavior, and APO brings this crucial capability to VLA models.

4. **Promoting Efficient Human-Robot Collaboration:** APO establishes an effective human-robot collaboration framework where human intervention is not merely for task completion but also a vital source of learning data. This "human-in-the-loop" approach is efficient because it leverages human expertise only when the robot struggles, minimizing direct supervision while maximizing the learning signal from critical failure events. The reduction in human intervention frequency observed in lifelong learning experiments underscores this efficiency, suggesting a path toward increasingly autonomous robots that require less oversight over time.

5. **Mitigating Catastrophic Forgetting:** A common pitfall in iterative learning is catastrophic forgetting, where a model loses previously acquired knowledge when learning new tasks or refinements. APO's incorporation of a regularization term (KL divergence from a reference model) and adaptive reweighting significantly mitigates this issue, allowing VLA models to adapt to new scenarios while preserving their original capabilities. This stability is essential for practical, long-term deployment.

6. **Catalyzing Lifelong Learning for Robotics:** The iterative deployment-optimization loop inherent in APO is a strong foundation for true lifelong learning in robotics. Robots can continuously collect data, learn from new experiences (including mistakes and human corrections), and update their policies in an ongoing fashion. This moves towards a vision of robots that truly "grow" their capabilities and knowledge through interaction, rather than being static programs.

In conclusion, APO is a significant contribution that pushes VLA models from being powerful but static tools to adaptable, continuously improving agents capable of learning from real-world failures and human guidance. This work brings valuable insights into stable optimization of large VLA models and holds immense potential for accelerating the deployment of highly capable and robust robotic systems across a multitude of industries and applications, from manufacturing and logistics to service robotics and human assistance.
