# Remember What You Did: Action-History Memory with Dual-Expert Denoising for Long-Horizon Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> ActMem-VLA adds memory to a frozen, fine-tuned flow-matching VLA (pi0.5) through a plugin. A Mamba-2 module encodes the history of executed actions, and a lightweight PreAction Expert uses that memory for the early high-noise denoising steps. It then hands off to the frozen Action Expert for refinement. It adds only 3.45% parameters and reaches 80.8% on LIBERO-Mem, versus 65.2% for pi0.5.

## Problem

- Memoryless VLAs act only on the current observation and robot state, so similar views at different task stages cause repeated actions, skipped steps, or hesitation.
- History methods that feed in past observation frames raise compute and inference latency, and redundant static pixels obscure the changes that actually mark task progress.
- Existing memory methods inject history into policy inputs or features, so adding memory to an already fine-tuned VLA requires costly joint retraining of the action generator.

## Method

- **Mamba-Based Execution Memory**: a two-layer ==Mamba-2== recurrently compresses blocks of executed actions only, no visual frames, into a compact task-progress vector projected into history keys and values.
- **PreAction Expert**: a lightweight 4-block transformer initialized from Action Expert layers, conditioned on history KV plus frozen VLM prefix KV, which predicts the ==velocity field== during early high-noise denoising.
- **Dual-expert denoising handover**: at handover point r=0.4, the PAE's partially denoised action passes to the frozen Action Expert for the remaining 6 of 10 steps; only Mamba and PAE train via ==flow-matching loss==.

## Results

- LIBERO-Mem with step limits taken from the demonstrations: **80.8%** average success versus **65.2%** for pi0.5 and **49.5%** for MemoryVLA; T4 **85.6%** versus **40.0%** and **20.0%**, T5 **95.0%** versus MemoryVLA's **3.3%**.
- Real-world DOBOT Nova, four tasks: average success rises from **35.0%** to **63.75%**; mango weighing jumps **10%** to **65%**; R1 **85%** to **100%**, R3 **15%** to **35%**, R4 **30%** to **55%**.
- Ablations on T6-T8: removing memory drops average **55.0%** to **40.6%**, and T6 falls **80.0%** to **38.3%**; removing the PAE gives **53.3%**; handover r=0.4 beats 0.3, 0.5, and 0.6.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The history of executed actions is a cheap, low-dimensional signal of task progress that resolves perceptual aliasing without processing redundant past visual frames.
> - Splitting one denoising trajectory between experts lets a trainable module steer coarse high-noise structure while a frozen expert keeps fine control, which makes memory plug-and-play.
> - Standard episode limits let cyclic, looping policies pass memory benchmarks; step limits tied to demonstration lengths are needed to show that a policy actually tracks progress.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot can see the same scene at two different stages of a task and need to do something different. In the authors' illustrative weighing example, holding fruit above a scale can mean "lower it onto the scale" or, after weighing and re-grasping, "move it to the basket." A policy that uses only the current view may not know which action comes next. The authors propose giving the policy a compact record of actions it has already executed. That history steers part of action generation, while the original action expert stays frozen. In a real-world mango-weighing task (R2), which evaluates whether the policy can distinguish stages before and after weighing, the method, ActMem-VLA, succeeds 65% of the time versus 10% for π₀.₅. Each method ran 20 trials on that task.

The authors' conceptual contrast. (a) A memoryless policy faces "Have I weighed it?" ambiguity. (b) A fine-tuning-based memory approach uses historical keyframes. (c) Executed actions feed a memory module, early PreAction Expert guidance, and a frozen Action Expert. The apple sequence is a motivating example, not experimental evidence.

### 2. A memory that steers

A flow-matching VLA iteratively turns random noise into an action chunk. ActMem-VLA feeds executed actions only, not historical images, into a Mamba-2 module. That module compresses them into a recurrent execution-memory representation.

The frozen VLM processes the current observation and instruction. Its context and the memory condition a lightweight, trainable PreAction Expert (PAE). The PAE handles the first, high-noise portion of the denoising trajectory. It then passes the intermediate action state to the frozen Action Expert (AE) without restarting the noise process, and the AE performs the remaining low-noise refinement. In the default ten-step schedule, the handover comes after four PAE steps, which leaves six AE steps.

Only Mamba and the PAE are optimized. The VLM and AE remain frozen. The authors interpret the division of labor as history-guided progress early and fine-grained refinement later. The plugin adds 3.45% of the base model's parameters. This is a parameter-overhead result, not evidence of reduced latency or training time.

Executed-action history feeds Mamba-2, and current views and instruction feed the VLM. Both condition the PAE. Random noise passes through four PAE steps, a handover, and six AE steps to produce the action chunk.

### 3. What the tests establish

The closest direct comparator is [[2508.19236|MemoryVLA]], a memory-augmented VLA that stores historical observations and retrieves relevant entries to condition action generation.

The authors report that the standard 600-step LIBERO-Mem protocol can count cyclic action sequences as successful. High success under that protocol therefore does not reliably show that a policy tracks task progress. They instead use task-specific step limits. Each limit is derived by rounding the training demonstrations' 95th-percentile length up to a multiple of 20, capped at 600 steps, and the same limit applies to every method on a given task.

The ten-task average success rate is the mean of the task-level success rates. Under these limits, ActMem-VLA averages 80.8%, versus 65.2% for π₀.₅ and 49.5% for MemoryVLA. The advantage is task-dependent: MemoryVLA leads on T1, and π₀.₅ leads on T9 and T10.

Success rate (%) on T1–T10 and the average for π₀.₅, MemoryVLA, and ActMem-VLA. Step limits appear under each task label. Episodes not completed within their task limit count as failures.

The ablation on T6–T8 tests the mechanism more directly. Removing execution memory drops the full model's average from 55.0% to 40.6%. Removing the trainable PAE yields 53.3%; that variant keeps memory and directly conditions the frozen expert during the first four denoising steps. On T8, both ablations outperform the full model (45.0%), and the no-PAE variant reaches 63.3%. The results support the components in the tested settings, especially on repetition-intensive T6, rather than a universal benefit. The ablations use one training seed and three evaluation seeds.

### 4. Memory in the loop

The method changes the policy's information flow. A record of prior actions provides progress context before the frozen expert refines the action. Across four real-world tasks, ActMem-VLA averages 63.75% (51 of 80 trials) versus 35.0% (28 of 80) for π₀.₅. These results do not isolate perceptual aliasing as the sole cause of the gains, and three-repetition tasks remain difficult, at 35% success on R3 and 55% on R4.
