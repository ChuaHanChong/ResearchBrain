# Least-Action-Guided Diffusion for Physical Extrapolation

> [!summary] Summary
> The LEAST-ACTION-GUIDED DIFFUSION (LAPG) framework enhances score-based diffusion models to achieve physically consistent extrapolation by employing a two-stage inference process guided by the principle of least action. This method accurately predicted complex physical phenomena across diverse ODE and PDE systems, including long-time dynamics and flow fields over unseen geometries, with lower errors than training-time physics-informed baselines in out-of-distribution scenarios.

## Problem

- Data-driven models, including generative models, frequently produce physically inconsistent predictions when extrapolating beyond their training data distribution in terms of time, parameters, or geometries.
- The learned scores of diffusion models are valid primarily within the training distribution, leading to physically unsound extrapolations when the reverse-time sampling process encounters out-of-distribution conditions.
- Existing physics-informed machine learning methods predominantly impose physical knowledge during model training, leaving inference-time predictions susceptible to error accumulation and physical violations when operating outside the training support.

## Method

- A two-stage inference process: first, a conditional score-based diffusion model generates an in-distribution "proposal" sample by evaluating the learned score at the nearest training condition.
- Second, this proposal undergoes a physics-guided refinement stage, where the reverse-time SDE is augmented with an action-derived physical guidance score, actively steering the sample towards a physically consistent state.
- Physical consistency is enforced by defining an "unphysicality" measure from the system's action functional and using its gradient as a differentiable physical score to minimize action variation during the refinement process.

## Results

- LAPG consistently achieved lower normalized root-mean-square error (nRMSE) in both temporal and parameter extrapolation scenarios across five benchmark systems (ODEs and PDEs), significantly outperforming training-time physics-informed neural network (PINN) baselines.
- For trajectory problems (e.g., free fall, spring-mass, point vortices), LAPG accurately preserved long-time phase accuracy, amplitudes, and decay behavior, even when time horizons, gravitational forces, stiffness, mass, or vortex parameters were extrapolated beyond training ranges.
- In spatial field generation (potential flow over airfoils), LAPG reproduced dominant flow features and predicted lift coefficients close to reference values for out-of-distribution angles of attack and cambered geometries, whereas diffusion-only and PINN models significantly underestimated these properties.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating physical principles directly into the generative (inference) phase provides a robust mechanism to maintain physical consistency when extrapolating to out-of-distribution conditions.
> - Leveraging a global scalar action functional for guidance offers a differentiable, inference-time correction that can be more effective than local, pointwise residual penalties in minimizing accumulated errors in complex dynamics.
> - Combining data-driven generation (for plausible proposals) with physics-based refinement (for OOD accuracy) enables models to bridge the gap between learning complex data distributions and adhering to fundamental physical laws during extrapolation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of machine learning into computational physics has led to the development of models that function as surrogates for complex physical systems, facilitating prediction, modeling, and design. A significant challenge for these data-driven models is their ability to reliably extrapolate beyond the distribution of data on which they were trained. When tasked with predicting system behavior over extended timeframes, unseen parameter regimes, or novel geometries, models often yield physically inconsistent results. This limitation is particularly critical for applications requiring reliable predictions in unexplored physical conditions.

Generative models, such as diffusion models, have demonstrated efficacy in learning high-dimensional data distributions and generating complex fields and trajectories in scientific contexts, including turbulent flows and Lagrangian particle statistics. However, these models inherit the fundamental challenge of data-driven learning: their learned scores are primarily valid within the training distribution. Consequently, when target conditions lie outside this distribution, the generative process relies on an extrapolation of the learned score, which may not adhere to underlying physical laws. This can manifest as phase drift in long-time trajectories, incorrect amplitudes under parameter shifts, violations of physical invariants or boundary conditions, or distorted patterns for unseen geometries.

To address these issues, a substantial body of research has focused on incorporating physical structure into machine learning models. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) integrate governing equations, initial conditions, and boundary conditions as penalty terms in the training loss function. Other approaches embed physical inductive biases directly into model architectures, such as Hamiltonian or Lagrangian neural networks for dynamical systems, or symmetry-preserving networks. Physics-informed generative models have also emerged, which regularize diffusion model training with physical residuals. These methods have improved data efficiency and physical fidelity within the training distribution.

However, a common characteristic of most existing physics-informed strategies is that physical knowledge is predominantly imposed during model construction or training, often through soft penalty terms or architectural constraints. Once the model parameters are fixed after training, extrapolative predictions still depend on the behavior of the learned map or score outside its observed domain. This research proposes a complementary strategy: instead of relying solely on physics enforcement during the learning phase, it advocates for the direct use of physical principles during the generation (inference) phase to actively guide each sample toward a physically consistent state. This work aims to bridge the gap between data-driven generative power and robust physical extrapolation by introducing an inference-time physical guidance mechanism.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to enhance the physical consistency and reliability of generative models, particularly score-based diffusion models, when performing extrapolation in computational physics applications. The authors aim to enable these models to produce physically sound predictions even when operating outside the data distributions they were trained on.

The motivation for this research stems from several limitations observed in current data-driven approaches:
- **Extrapolation challenges:** Generative models, including diffusion models, trained on finite ranges of time, parameters, or geometries frequently produce physically inconsistent predictions when extended to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions. This is a critical barrier for their utility as general-purpose surrogates in scientific and engineering fields.
- **Inference-time physical inconsistency:** While diffusion models excel at learning complex data distributions, their learned scores are inherently constrained by the training data. When encountering OOD conditions, the reverse-time sampling process extrapolates the learned score, potentially leading to violations of physical laws, such as phase drift in long-time dynamics, amplitude errors, or misrepresented physical phenomena.
- **Limitations of training-time physics enforcement:** Existing physics-informed machine learning methods primarily integrate physical knowledge during the training phase, often through penalty terms or architectural design. However, after model training, the parameters are fixed, and subsequent extrapolative predictions still rely on the generalization capabilities of the learned model. This means that errors can accumulate or physical principles can be violated during inference when the model operates outside its training support.
- **Need for active guidance:** The authors propose that instead of solely relying on static, training-time physics constraints, physical principles should actively guide the generation process during inference. This would allow each generated sample to be dynamically steered towards a physically consistent state, providing a more robust mechanism for OOD generalization.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of this work are:
1. **Develop a least-action-principle-guided (LAPG) diffusion framework:** This framework is designed to enforce physical consistency during the inference stage of generative modeling.
2. **Combine data-driven generation with physics-based refinement:** The framework aims to integrate a conditional score-based diffusion model (for generating an in-distribution proposal) with an action-derived physical guidance score (for refining this proposal towards a target OOD condition).
3. **Define an action-residual score:** This score is intended to provide a differentiable inference-time correction mechanism that leverages the principle of least action, offering an alternative to pointwise residual penalties that often require empirical loss balancing.
4. **Evaluate the method on diverse physical systems:** The framework is tested on ordinary and partial differential equation systems, including free fall, conservative and dissipative spring–mass dynamics, interacting point vortices, and potential flow over parameterized airfoils.
5. **Assess performance under various extrapolation scenarios:** The method's effectiveness is evaluated under temporal, parameter, and geometric extrapolation shifts, with comparisons against training-time physics-informed baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Least-Action-Principle-Guided (LAPG) diffusion framework is a two-stage inference process designed to generate physically admissible states X for a given physical system specified by a condition vector c, even when c is outside the training distribution.

#### 3.1. Two-Stage Diffusion Process

1. **Stage 1: Conditional Score-Based Diffusion (In-Distribution Proposal Generation)**
 - **Forward Stochastic Process:** The method utilizes a score-based diffusion model, which involves a forward stochastic differential equation (SDE) that gradually perturbs clean data X_0 into noise over a pseudo-time variable τ ∈ [0, T]. The variance-exploding SDE (VESDE) is employed, characterized by zero drift (f=0) and a diffusion coefficient g(τ) derived from an exponentially increasing noise level σ(τ) = σ_min (σ_max/σ_min)^(τ/T). Standard parameters are σ_min = 0.01 and σ_max = 50.0. This process defines Gaussian perturbation kernels p(X_τ|X_0) = N(X_τ; X_0, σ^2(τ)I).
 - **Score Learning:** The reverse process, which denoises data, is governed by the score function s(X_τ, σ_τ; c) = ∇_X_τ log p_σ_τ(X_τ|c). Since this is not analytically available, a neural network S_θ(X_τ, σ_τ; c) is trained to approximate it using denoising score matching, minimizing L(θ) = E_τ,X_0,z [λ(τ) ||S_θ(X_0 + σ(τ)z, σ(τ); c) + z/σ(τ)||^2], with λ(τ) = σ^2(τ).
 - **Conditional Sampling:** For inference, the learned score model is typically used to integrate the reverse-time SDE from noise to data. However, for out-of-distribution (OOD) target conditions c, the learned score S_θ is not expected to generalize directly. Therefore, the learned score model is evaluated at the closest *in-distribution condition* c' = argmin_c'∈C_train d(c, c'), where d is the Euclidean distance in normalized condition space. This first stage generates a high-probability "proposal" sample under the training distribution.

2. **Stage 2: Physics-Guided Refinement (Out-of-Distribution Correction)**
 - **Augmented Reverse SDE:** The standard reverse-time SDE is augmented with an additional physical guidance term (Eq. 6): dX_τ = [f - g^2 S_θ*(X_τ, σ_τ; c')]dτ + g d *w*_τ - η H(-τ) ∇_X_τ log p_s(X_τ|c) dτ.
 - **Transition to Refinement:** A Heaviside factor H(-τ) activates the physical guidance only for τ ≤ 0. The pseudo-time variable τ is extended to negative values to mark the transition from stochastic data generation (τ > 0) to deterministic physical refinement (τ ≤ 0). During the refinement stage, the noise term is removed.
 - **Optimization Update:** The update in the refinement stage becomes an optimization towards maximizing the log-probability of the physical prior: X_τ-Δτ = X_τ + (ηΔτ) ∇_X_τ log p_s(X_τ|c). Gradient-based optimizers (e.g., Adam, SGDM) are employed for this optimization, adjusting the generated state to reduce the action variation.

#### 3.2. Action-Derived Physical Prior

The core of the physical guidance is the prior p_s(X_τ|c), constructed from an action or action-like functional A(X; c) of the physical system. Physically admissible states (trajectories or fields) correspond to stationary points or minimizers of this functional.
- **Unphysicality Measure:** A scalar "unphysicality" measure is defined as U(X_τ; c) = (⟨δA(X_τ; c)⟩_f / σ_A)^2, where ⟨δA⟩_f is a numerical approximation of the action variation, and σ_A is a non-tuned scaling factor for dimensional consistency.
- **Physical Prior:** The physical prior is then defined as p_s(X_τ|c) ∝ exp[-U(X_τ; c)], assigning higher probability to states with smaller action variation.
- **Physical Score:** The gradient of the log-prior yields the physical score: ∇_X_τ log p_s(X_τ|c) = -∇_X_τ U(X_τ; c). This score provides the direction for reducing the action variation.
- **Numerical Action Variation:** For trajectory problems, the generated state X is a discretized path. A multi-directional finite-difference stationarity residual ⟨δA⟩_f is used. This involves applying R virtual perturbations X^(a) = X + ϵξ^(a) (where ϵ is small and ξ^(a) are admissible directions, e.g., sine modes for coordinates vanishing at endpoints) and computing finite differences D_a A(X; c) = (A(X + ϵξ^(a); c) - A(X; c)) / ϵ. The residual is the root-mean-square over these directions. For continuous fields like airfoil flow, the refinement is applied to variational degrees of freedom (e.g., circulation parameter in potential flow), and the gradient is computed via automatic differentiation.

#### 3.3. Benchmark Systems

Five benchmark systems (Q1-Q5) are used for evaluation:
- **Q1: Free Fall:** Conservative ODE, tests temporal and parameter (gravity) extrapolation.
- **Q2: Undamped Spring-Mass Oscillator:** Conservative ODE, tests long-time phase accuracy and parameter (mass, stiffness) extrapolation.
- **Q3: Damped Spring-Mass System:** Dissipative ODE, tests preservation of decay behavior under parameter (mass, stiffness, damping) extrapolation. Action functional augmented with dissipation potential.
- **Q4: Two-Point-Vortex System:** Hamiltonian dynamics, tests interacting systems and parameter (initial separation, circulation) extrapolation.
- **Q5: Potential Flow over Joukowsky Airfoils:** PDE system for spatially distributed fields, tests geometric (camber) and condition (angle of attack) extrapolation. Action derived from variational fluid dynamics.

For Q1-Q4, states are represented as discretized phase-space trajectories X = {[q_i, p_i]^T}. Time is reconstructed, and temporal extrapolation is handled by terminal constraints. For Q5, the state is a 2D flow field, and reference solutions are from OpenCFD-EC. All quantities are non-dimensionalized.

#### 3.4. Score Model Architecture and Training

A conditional U-Net architecture (Fig. 3) is used for the score function S_θ. It takes the noisy state X_τ, a positional embedding of the noise level σ_τ, and an MLP embedding of the condition vector c. For Q5, a mesh embedding is also included. The architecture features multi-scale encoder-decoder structure with residual blocks, skip connections, and self-attention modules.

#### 3.5. PINN Baseline

LAPG is compared against a training-time physics-constrained PINN. For Q1-Q4, the PINN is a fully connected MLP. For Q5, a deeper fully connected network is used. PINNs are trained by minimizing a composite loss combining governing equation residuals, initial conditions, boundary conditions, and data agreement terms, each with a specific weight.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LAPG across five benchmark systems demonstrates its capabilities in improving physical consistency and accuracy during extrapolation, compared to training-time physics-informed baselines and diffusion-only models.

#### 4.1. Trajectory Generation (Q1-Q4)

- **Free Fall (Q1):** LAPG maintained the correct free-fall trajectory even when the time horizon was doubled and the gravitational acceleration was outside the training range.
- **Undamped Spring-Mass (Q2):** When trajectories were extended to two periods and spring stiffness or mass were extrapolated, LAPG preserved phase accuracy and maintained correct amplitudes. In contrast, the PINN baseline exhibited visible phase drift and amplitude errors that increased with extrapolation distance.
- **Damped Spring-Mass (Q3):** This system, involving both oscillatory motion and dissipative decay, showed LAPG accurately following the decay envelope under extrapolated damping coefficients and remaining stable under simultaneous shifts in mass, stiffness, and damping.
- **Point Vortices (Q4):** LAPG effectively captured the long-time motion of interacting vortices, remaining accurate when initial separation or circulation ratios were outside the training range. In these cases, the action refinement visibly steered the nearest-condition diffusion sample towards the target dynamics.
- **Diffusion-Only Output:** For all trajectory problems, the "diffusion-only" samples (generated at the nearest in-distribution condition before refinement) provided physically plausible, but not accurate, representations for the specific out-of-distribution target conditions. The LAPG refinement stage was observed to correct these proposals towards the target physics.

#### 4.2. Flow Field Generation (Q5 - Potential Flow over Airfoils)

- **In-Distribution Case:** For an airfoil within the training distribution (symmetric airfoil, zero angle of attack), both LAPG and PINN accurately reproduced the reference velocity field and lift coefficients (C_l ≈ 0).
- **Out-of-Distribution Cases:** For test cases involving high angles of attack (30° vs. training range [0°, 10°]) and a cambered geometry (β* = 0.1 vs. training β* = 0), LAPG continued to reproduce the dominant flow features, including leading-edge acceleration and the asymmetric velocity distribution characteristic of lift. The corresponding lift coefficients predicted by LAPG were close to the reference values (e.g., C_l = 3.00 for a reference of 3.20, and 3.50 for a reference of 3.60).
- **Comparison:** The diffusion-only output for OOD cases captured an in-distribution flow pattern but did not reflect the OOD angle of attack or geometry, resulting in significantly underestimated lift coefficients (e.g., C_l = 1.30 and 1.25 for the two OOD cases). The PINN prediction for OOD cases was more diffusive, underestimated the high-velocity regions near the leading edge, and substantially underestimated the lift coefficients (e.g., C_l = 0.32 and 0.74 for the same OOD cases).

#### 4.3. Quantitative Errors

Quantitative evaluation using normalized root-mean-square error (nRMSE) across all test cases (Figure 6) indicated that LAPG consistently maintained lower errors in both temporal and parameter extrapolation scenarios compared to the PINN baseline. The PINN's error increased significantly in OOD cases, particularly when extrapolation led to qualitative changes in the solution behavior, such as oscillation period, decay rate, vortex orbit, or aerodynamic lift. These results support the proposed mechanism that inference-time variational guidance, derived from a global scalar action functional, effectively reduces accumulated phase errors in long-time dynamics and improves performance in parameter and geometry extrapolation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method for improving the extrapolation capabilities and physical consistency of data-driven generative models in computational physics. By moving physical constraint enforcement from solely the training phase to an active inference-time guidance mechanism, LAPG addresses a fundamental limitation of machine learning models when operating outside their training data distributions.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:
- **Enhanced Reliability of Generative Models:** The framework provides a mechanism for diffusion models to produce physically reliable predictions in previously unobserved conditions (temporal, parametric, geometric extrapolation), which is crucial for their adoption as surrogate models in scientific discovery and engineering design.
- **Novel Approach to Physics Integration:** The concept of using a global scalar action functional to guide inference offers an alternative to traditional pointwise residual penalties common in PINNs. This approach potentially simplifies the process of integrating physics, as it avoids the need for empirical balancing of multiple loss terms (e.g., equation residuals, initial conditions, boundary conditions, data losses).
- **Broader Applicability:** The method has been demonstrated across diverse physical systems, including conservative and dissipative ordinary differential equations, Hamiltonian dynamics, and partial differential equations governing fluid flow. This suggests its potential applicability to a wide range of problems in mechanics, fluid dynamics, and other fields where variational principles can be formulated.
- **Foundation for Robust Scientific Machine Learning:** By combining the strengths of data-driven generative models (for generating plausible initial proposals) with the rigor of physical principles (for ensuring OOD consistency), LAPG contributes to the development of more robust and trustworthy machine learning tools for scientific applications. This could accelerate scientific simulations, enable efficient exploration of design spaces, and facilitate inverse problem solving.

However, the authors also acknowledge certain limitations and areas for future work:
- **Requirement for Action Functionals:** The method relies on the availability and formulatability of an appropriate action or action-like variational functional for the system, which may be challenging for highly complex, non-variational, turbulent, multiphysics, or strongly constrained systems.
- **Computational Cost:** The inference-time refinement step introduces additional computational overhead, as the action variation and its gradient must be evaluated during sampling.
- **Dependency on Refinement Parameters:** The final results may exhibit some dependency on the quality of the initial diffusion-generated proposal, the specific perturbation functions used to estimate the action variation, and the optimization parameters employed during the refinement stage.
Future research could explore combining global action-based guidance with complementary local constraints to address systems where a single variational functional may not fully capture all relevant physical requirements.
