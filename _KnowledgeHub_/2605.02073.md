# Enhanced LLM Reasoning by Optimizing Reward Functions with Search-Driven Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Oklahoma developed a search-driven reinforcement learning framework to automatically optimize reward functions for enhancing LLM mathematical reasoning. This methodology, which generates and refines executable Python reward functions, led to a 0.19 absolute F1 gain on the GSM8K benchmark over a fixed-reward baseline and outperformed several off-the-shelf models in its size category.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for complex LLM tasks like mathematical reasoning is a significant, labor-intensive bottleneck in reinforcement learning applications.
- Current reward strategies, particularly coarse outcome-based signals, often provide insufficient guidance for complex multi-step reasoning, limiting the performance of LLMs.
- Traditional reinforcement learning pipelines typically treat reward functions as static, pre-defined components, overlooking opportunities for their systematic optimization.

## Method

- The paper introduces an ==iterative search framework== where a frontier LLM generates and refines executable Python ==reward functions==, guided by empirical performance feedback.
- ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== is employed as the reinforcement learning algorithm for efficient policy optimization, minimizing memory requirements and enabling fine-tuning on consumer-grade GPUs.
- Top-performing discovered rewards are combined with a set of fixed base rewards into ==reward ensembles== to achieve synergistic performance improvements on reasoning tasks.

## Results

- The `ens_diverse_rounds` ensemble achieved the highest F1 score of **0.795** and an accuracy of **0.660** on the GSM8K test set, representing an absolute F1 gain of **0.19** over the baseline (**0.609 F1**) using only fixed rewards.
- The GRPO-trained Llama-3.2-3B model, leveraging the discovered ensemble rewards, surpassed five off-the-shelf LLMs in the 2-4B parameter range, including Qwen3:4b (F1=**0.776**, Acc=**0.634**) and Phi-4-Mini:3.8b (F1=**0.773**, Acc=**0.629**).
- The complete reward search pipeline, which generated **50 candidate functions**, was computationally efficient, requiring approximately **40 hours** on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reward functions for LLM reasoning can be systematically optimized through an automated search process, treating reward specification as a dynamic, searchable object within the reinforcement learning pipeline.
> - Process-oriented rewards, particularly those evaluating the structural quality of reasoning chains, were highly effective and achieved higher mean F1 scores when combined with outcome-based base rewards.
> - The iterative feedback loop, which provides performance summaries of prior reward candidates to the generating LLM, is critical for improving reward quality, as random reward selection significantly degrades performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research operates within the field of large language model (LLM) development, specifically focusing on improving mathematical reasoning capabilities. Mathematical reasoning serves as a critical benchmark for LLMs, requiring consistent intermediate steps, quantity tracking, and precise final answers, moving beyond mere fluent text generation.

Prior work has explored prompting-based methods like chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting and self-consistency to enhance reasoning. However, these methods modify how models are queried rather than their underlying training objectives. Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a key post-training mechanism for aligning LLMs with desired behaviors, notably through techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). While these methods allow models to learn from preferences or rewards, the design of effective reward functions remains a significant challenge.

Current reward functions often fall into two categories: outcome supervision (evaluating only the final answer) or process supervision (evaluating intermediate steps). Outcome supervision is simpler but coarser, potentially failing to distinguish robust reasoning from shallow pattern matching. Process supervision offers richer credit assignment but is difficult to design, annotate, and validate at scale. This challenge is further complicated by the varying quality and robustness of reward models themselves. The broader RL community has long recognized reward design as a bottleneck, with poorly chosen rewards leading to issues like reward hacking or brittle behavior.

Recent advancements in LLMs, particularly their ability to synthesize and critique executable code, have opened avenues for automating reward design. Previous research has utilized LLMs to generate proxy reward functions, translate instructions into reward parameters, or iteratively improve reward programs. However, much of this work has concentrated on domains like robotics and control. In LLM reasoning, while RL optimizers like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have shown promise in improving mathematical reasoning, the reward function is typically treated as a fixed component.

This paper addresses the gap by treating the *reward specification itself* as an object of optimization within an iterative, search-driven framework. It explores whether the reward function, rather than being a static design choice, can be progressively improved through a systematic search process, thereby complementing existing efforts in LLM reasoning and automated reward design. The work aims to reduce the manual and expert-driven effort traditionally associated with crafting effective reward functions for complex reasoning tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether the reward specification used during reinforcement learning post-training for language models can be treated as a searchable and optimizable object, rather than a fixed, hand-designed component. The authors posit that, in a scenario where the base LLM is fixed, the reward function becomes the most significant remaining design variable for enhancing reasoning capabilities.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges in current LLM reasoning improvement paradigms:
- **Difficulty of Reward Function Design:** Crafting effective reward functions for complex tasks like mathematical reasoning is acknowledged as a bottleneck in RL. Expert intuition, extensive trial-and-error, and deep domain knowledge are often required, making the process time-consuming and prone to errors.
- **Limitations of Fixed Rewards:** Manually designed rewards are often proxies for desired behaviors, which can lead to issues like reward hacking or shortcut learning if not precisely aligned with the true objective. A static reward may not fully capture the nuances of robust reasoning.
- **Complementary to Policy Optimization:** While advanced policy optimization algorithms (e.g., GRPO) and efficient fine-tuning techniques (e.g., LoRA) improve how policies are learned, their performance is inherently capped by the quality of the guiding reward signal. Optimizing the reward itself could unlock further performance gains.
- **Leveraging LLM Capabilities:** Modern LLMs possess strong code generation and self-critique abilities. The authors are motivated to leverage these generative capabilities to synthesize and refine reward functions programmatically, thereby automating a historically manual process.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The desire to make advanced LLM alignment techniques accessible to academic and smaller industrial budgets motivates the use of efficient algorithms (GRPO) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA), ensuring the search process is feasible on limited hardware.

By making the reward function an object of search, the research aims to automate reward discovery, reduce human design effort, and potentially uncover novel reward strategies that are more effective and robust than manually engineered ones, ultimately leading to improved mathematical reasoning performance in LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates a search-driven process for optimizing reward functions with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for fine-tuning large language models. The methodology involves an iterative generate–validate–train–rank feedback loop, followed by an ensemble construction phase.

#### 3.1. Policy Optimization with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO is chosen for its efficiency and relevance to mathematical reasoning. It replaces the separate critic network used in PPO with group-relative normalization of sampled outputs. For a group of `G` outputs generated from an old policy, rewards are computed, normalized by the group mean and standard deviation, and then assigned as the advantage for tokens in each output. The GRPO objective maximizes a clipped surrogate loss, with a KL divergence term regularizing against a reference policy to prevent reward hacking. This approach reduces memory overhead, eliminates the need to train a separate value function, and avoids staleness issues, making it suitable for parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

#### 3.2. Base Model and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

The base model used is Meta’s Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, loaded in 4-bit quantized precision. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is employed for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. LoRA adapters are attached to all attention and feed-forward projection matrices, resulting in approximately 83 million trainable parameters out of 3 billion total (2.8%). This configuration allows for repeated adaptation without full model retraining, with each reward function trial saving its own LoRA checkpoint.

#### 3.3. Dataset and Output Format

The GSM8K dataset, comprising 7,473 training examples and 1,319 test examples, is used for both training and evaluation. Models are expected to produce structured outputs with `<thinking>[Reasoning]</thinking>` and `<solution>[Final numeric answer]</solution>` tags. This format enables independent assessment of reasoning quality and answer correctness by reward functions.

#### 3.4. Base Reward Functions

Three fixed base reward functions are included in every training run:
1. `match_format_exactly`: Awards +3.0 for properly paired `<thinking>` and `<solution>` tags, 0 otherwise.
2. `match_format_approximately`: Provides graduated formatting scores (+0.5 for each tag, -1.0 for missing/duplicated tags).
3. `check_answer`: Evaluates the extracted numeric answer against ground truth with tiered scoring (+3.0 for exact match, down to -2.5 for missing/unparseable answers).
These base rewards are protected from modification by generated rewards and form an "always-on" substrate for all discovered rewards. The combined reward for any trial `R` is the sum of these three and `R` itself.

#### 3.5. Search-Driven Reward Function Generation

The core of the methodology is an iterative search process over five rounds, generating 10 new candidate reward functions per round (50 total).

- **LLM-Based Generation:** Reward functions are generated by prompting Kimi K2 (moonshotai/kimi-k2-instruct-0905) via the Groq API. The prompt includes 20 random GSM8K training examples (question + ground truth), an example reward function signature, and constraints (Python stdlib/regex only, unique names, no redefinition of base rewards, graceful handling of malformed outputs). Generation uses a temperature of 0.6. The output is a JSON array containing the reward name, code, and description.
- **Iterative Feedback Loop:** After each round, all previously evaluated reward functions are ranked by their F1 score on the GSM8K test set. A compact textual summary of these ranked results (name, round, F1, accuracy) is then injected into the prompt for the next round. This feedback mechanism guides the generative LLM to avoid semantic repeats and explore new aspects of mathematical reasoning.
- **Validation and Screening:** Each generated reward function undergoes a four-stage validation pipeline:
 1. AST pass to reject unauthorized imports.
 2. Execution in a locked-down environment with stripped `__builtins__`.
 3. Rebuilding in a restricted global namespace exposing only `re`, `math`, and tag constants.
 4. A probe call with dummy inputs to verify the correct return type (`list[float]`).
 Invalid rewards are discarded. Valid rewards are then screened via 500-step GRPO training runs, always alongside the three base rewards.

#### 3.6. Ensemble Reward Construction

After the 50 individual rewards are ranked, top-performing rewards are combined into seven ensemble configurations. Each ensemble sums its selected discovered rewards with the three fixed base rewards. Ensembles vary in size (3 to 10 discovered rewards) and selection strategy (e.g., `ens_top3`, `ens_top5_500`, `ens_diverse_rounds`, which selects one discovered reward from each of the five rounds).

#### 3.7. Training and Evaluation Configuration

GRPO training uses a group size of 4, an effective batch size of 4, and AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-6 and cosine annealing. All screening and ensemble training runs last 500 steps. Evaluation uses greedy decoding (temperature = 0). Four metrics are computed on the 1,319-question test set: precision, recall, F1 score (primary ranking metric), and accuracy (exact match under strict tag-format parsing). Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise McNemar tests are reported. Control experiments include a "base-rewards-only" configuration and a "random-5" ensemble to isolate the effects of discovered rewards and ranked selection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded a comprehensive set of findings regarding the efficacy of search-driven reward optimization for LLM mathematical reasoning.

#### 4.1. Search Trajectory and Performance Over Rounds

Over five rounds, the search mechanism generated 50 candidate reward functions. The F1 scores of these rewards varied significantly, with the top 6 achieving F1 scores above 0.75, while the majority clustered between 0.55 and 0.65. The mean F1 score of generated rewards improved from 0.596 in Round 1 to 0.632 in Round 5. This improvement was not strictly monotonic, with a dip in Round 3, suggesting the search explored diverse regions of the reward function space. Analysis of precision-recall indicated that most rewards encouraged format compliance (high recall) but had moderate precision, while top-performing rewards balanced both metrics above 0.7.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Top-Performing Individual Reward Functions

The top five individual rewards were: `thinking_steps_count` (F1 = 0.787, Round 5), `thinking_has_calc` (F1 = 0.786, Round 5), `numeric_reasoning_depth` (F1 = 0.783, Round 4), `step_by_step_accuracy` (F1 = 0.779, Round 2), and `reasoning_completeness` (F1 = 0.754, Round 2). Notably, three of these top five focused on the structural quality of the chain-of-thought reasoning rather than re-checking the final answer. For example, `thinking_steps_count` rewarded the model for having multiple lines in its `<thinking>` block. These process-oriented rewards achieved a higher mean F1 compared to pure-outcome rewards, indicating their complementary value when combined with the base correctness rewards. Eight of the top ten rewards used graduated scoring rather than binary, suggesting benefits from finer-grained credit assignment. Code length was not found to be predictive of reward quality. Conversely, three rewards led to catastrophic GRPO performance due to aggressive penalty terms dominating the gradient before the policy could learn to solve problems effectively, underscoring the importance of the base correctness rewards.

#### 4.3. Ensemble Performance and Statistical Significance

Seven ensemble configurations were evaluated. The `ens_diverse_rounds` ensemble, which combined the three base rewards with five discovered rewards (one from each round), achieved the highest numerical performance with F1 = 0.795 and accuracy = 0.660 (95% CI [0.635, 0.686]). This represents a 0.19 absolute F1 gain over the "Base only" GRPO baseline (F1 = 0.609).

Pairwise McNemar tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0024) revealed that the five largest ensembles (those with 5 or more discovered rewards and trained for 500 steps) were statistically indistinguishable from one another. However, both the `ens_top3` configuration (three discovered rewards) and `final_ens_300` (five discovered rewards, but trained for only 300 steps) performed significantly worse than the larger ensembles (p < 10^-6). A control experiment, `ens_random5` (five randomly selected rewards), collapsed to F1 = 0.047, demonstrating that ranked selection from the feedback loop, rather than merely combining multiple rewards, was crucial for performance gains.

#### 4.4. Robustness to Training Seeds

Retraining the `ens_diverse_rounds` configuration with two additional GRPO seeds showed F1 of 0.785 ± 0.013 and accuracy of 0.646 ± 0.017. This indicated that while the specific numerical ranking among the top ensembles could vary due to seed-induced noise, the qualitative ordering (base-only < three-reward < five large ensembles) remained robust.

#### 4.5. Reward-Hacking and Format-Penalty Audits

An audit was conducted on `thinking_steps_count`, a potentially "hackable" reward that pays for multiple lines in the `<thinking>` block. The audit revealed that the trained policy organically produced more reasoning lines (mean 6.13) than the minimum threshold (3 lines). Furthermore, correct responses contained more lines (mean 6.47) and a higher density of arithmetic-step patterns (0.63) than incorrect ones (mean 5.51 lines, 0.51 density). This evidence did not support a simple line-padding hypothesis for reward hacking. The strict tag-format penalty for accuracy was also found to be small for the large ensembles (1-2% absolute accuracy difference when using flexible parsing), suggesting high format compliance.

#### 4.6. Baseline Comparison

The GRPO-trained Llama-3.2-3B model (F1 = 0.795, Accuracy = 0.660) outperformed five off-the-shelf models of comparable size (2B–4B parameters), including Qwen 3/3.5 and Phi-4-Mini, which ranged in accuracy from 0.403 to 0.634.

#### 4.7. Computational Cost

The entire search process, including 5 rounds and 50 reward evaluations, required approximately 40 hours of wall-clock time on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU (32 GB VRAM). This demonstrates the computational feasibility of the method within an academic budget.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research demonstrates that treating the reward function itself as an object of search, guided by an iterative, LLM-driven feedback loop, can significantly enhance the mathematical reasoning capabilities of large language models. This work offers several points of significance and potential impact:

- **Automation of Reward Discovery:** The framework automates a traditionally manual and expertise-intensive process of reward function design. This reduces human effort and trial-and-error, allowing practitioners to focus on higher-level problem formulation rather than intricate reward engineering.
- **Improved Reasoning Performance:** The discovered reward functions, particularly when combined in ensembles, measurably improve mathematical reasoning quality on a fixed base model. The substantial F1 gain (0.19 absolute) over a baseline using only generic reward functions indicates the effectiveness of this approach.
- **Interpretability and Auditability:** The generated reward functions are explicit Python code, which allows practitioners to audit, understand, modify, and combine them. This transparency contrasts with black-box reward models, fostering greater control and debugging capabilities. The reward-hacking audit performed in the study contributes to this aspect by verifying the practical behavior of the discovered structural rewards.
- **Computational Accessibility:** The pipeline runs efficiently on a single consumer-grade GPU (approximately 40 hours), making this advanced alignment technique accessible to academic researchers and smaller industry groups with limited compute budgets. This lowers the barrier to entry for experimenting with and deploying improved LLMs for reasoning tasks.
- **Complementary to Existing Methods:** The approach complements existing RL-based LLM alignment methods by focusing on optimizing the reward signal itself, which is a critical yet often fixed component. It provides a practical alternative to training expensive reward models, especially when target reasoning behaviors can be specified through executable code.
- **Generalizability and Future Applications:** The paper suggests that the same recipe could transfer to other reasoning domains, such as code generation and scientific problem-solving, implying a broader applicability of the search-driven reward optimization paradigm beyond mathematical word problems. This could lead to more robust and aligned LLMs across various complex cognitive tasks.
- **Scientific Contribution to Automated RL:** By formulating reward search as an iterative generate-validate-train-rank loop, the work contributes to the growing field of automated reinforcement learning, demonstrating that the objective function itself can be progressively improved through systematic experimentation and feedback.

In summary, this research provides a practical, efficient, and effective methodology for enhancing LLM reasoning by dynamically optimizing reward functions, offering a significant step towards more autonomous and robust LLM alignment.
