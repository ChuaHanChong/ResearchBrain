# HiTac-WAM: A Hierarchical Tactile World Action Model for Contact-Rich Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> HiTac-WAM augments a pretrained world action model with a tactile branch that forecasts each candidate action chunk's tactile consequences as a directed hierarchy: contact state, contact-conditioned 3D deformation, then slip risk, via stop-gradient conditioning and a directed attention mask. Forecasts rank candidates and serve as an execution-time reference for replanning. Real-robot success rises from 31.1% to 72.2% across three contact-rich tasks.

## Problem

- Existing tactile-aware world action models represent future touch as an undifferentiated image or latent stream, ignoring the physical dependencies that order contact, deformation, and slip.
- Visually plausible candidate action rollouts can hide divergent physical outcomes like missed contact, slip, or jamming, since contact is often only indirectly observable under occlusion.
- Prior tactile forecasting, candidate evaluation, and online correction are typically built as separate systems rather than unified through one mechanism that ranks and verifies action-forecast pairs.

## Method

- ==Hierarchical Tactile Forecast==: three deterministic heads predict contact, then contact-conditioned 3D deformation, then contact-and-deformation-gated slip risk, coupled via ==stop-gradient== signals rather than a single entangled stream.
- ==Directed Attention Mask==: lets tactile queries attend to video-action context and predicted visual futures while blocking visual and action queries from attending to tactile tokens, within the shared world-action-model backbone.
- ==Forecast-Guided Selection and Online Verification==: ranks sampled candidate chunks by a weighted cost over task-progress and the three tactile costs, then retains the selected forecast as a ==KDE-calibrated== reference that triggers corrective replanning on persistent deviation.

## Results

- Mean contact F1 of **0.921**, 3D displacement L2 of **0.058mm**, and slip AUPRC of **0.247** (versus 0.0139 chance level) across chip grasping, blackboard erasing, and USB insertion tasks.
- Under matched training budgets, the directed hierarchy cuts 3D displacement L2 error by **17.6%** versus a deformation-only predictor and improves slip AUPRC by **60.4%** versus a slip-only predictor.
- Real-robot average success rate climbs from **31.1%** (single-candidate DreamZero) to **61.1%** with forecast-guided selection and **72.2%** with full online verification, beating fixed-budget task-progress ranking at **35.6%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly ordering multi-output predictions by their physical dependency chain, not just sharing a backbone, is what yields the accuracy gain; naive multi-task heads underperform single-output baselines.
> - Keeping each forecast paired with its originating action candidate through both selection and execution turns a passive predictor into an active decision and monitoring signal.
> - Tactile forecasting cost stays cheap relative to the action policy: video LPIPS and action MAE degrade by only 1.4% and 0.9% when the tactile branch is added.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements through the development of World Action Models (WAMs), which predict future visual observations and continuous actions. These models often rely on visual rollouts to plan and execute tasks. However, a limitation of purely visual rollouts emerges in contact-rich manipulation scenarios. In such tasks, crucial information about the end-effector–environment interface, such as contact state, pressure distribution, and incipient slip, may be indirectly observable or entirely occluded from visual sensors. This can lead to situations where visually plausible robot actions may result in physically distinct and undesirable outcomes, such as missed contact, insufficient pressure, or unintended slip.

To address the limitations of visual-only information, research has explored the integration of tactile sensing. Existing approaches include:
- **Tactile Policies:** These policies condition robot actions on measured tactile feedback, typically responding only after contact has occurred. Examples include visuo-tactile transformers and slow-fast controllers that provide high-rate post-contact feedback.
- **Predictive Tactile Models:** These models forecast action-conditioned changes in tactile observations. They support model-predictive selection by comparing predicted tactile states with goal states and enable prospective slip estimation. Recent visuo-tactile action models have integrated tactile prediction into shared policy contexts, learning future tactile images or latent representations alongside other policy features.

While the value of tactile state estimation and forecasting is established, prior predictive formulations in this area often treat various tactile properties—such as contact state, interface deformation, and slip risk—either as a single entangled stream or as independent targets. This approach does not explicitly model the directed physical dependencies among these tactile states (e.g., deformation only occurs under contact, and slip risk depends on contact and deformation). Furthermore, existing systems generally address tactile forecasting, candidate evaluation, and online correction as separate components, rather than through a unified mechanism that uses structured predictions for candidate ranking and execution-time verification. This gap represents a fundamental challenge in enabling robots to perform complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks with reliability and adaptability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance robot manipulation capabilities in contact-rich environments by proactively forecasting the tactile consequences of planned actions and using these forecasts for informed decision-making and execution monitoring. The authors identify a critical limitation in current world action models: their reliance on visual rollouts, which often fail to provide sufficient information about contact dynamics, especially under occlusion. This can lead to the selection of actions that appear visually correct but result in physical failures such as incorrect contact, insufficient pressure, or unintended slip.

The motivation behind HiTac-WAM stems from three specific properties that the authors propose are essential for effective tactile-forecast-based candidate selection and execution-time verification:

1. **Explicit Separation of Physically Meaningful Factors:** Existing tactile predictive models often combine distinct physical phenomena (contact, deformation, slip) into a single representation or treat them as independent. The authors argue that explicitly separating the interface state into these physically meaningful factors—contact state, 3D deformation field, and slip risk—and modeling their hierarchical dependencies can lead to more accurate and interpretable predictions. For instance, deformation is only relevant when contact occurs, and slip risk is contingent upon the nature of contact and deformation.
2. **Paired Forecasts and Candidate Actions for Ranking:** In existing world action models, candidate actions are typically generated with paired visual rollouts. The authors argue for maintaining a direct pairing between each candidate action chunk and its predicted hierarchical tactile trajectory. This pairing allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of action candidates, enabling a ranking process that considers not only visual plausibility but also the detailed physical interaction consequences predicted by the tactile forecasts. This is motivated by the need to distinguish between visually similar but physically different action outcomes.
3. **Temporally Aligned Execution-Time Reference:** Once an action candidate is selected, its corresponding tactile forecast should not be discarded. Instead, it should be retained as a temporally aligned reference for subsequent execution-time verification. This allows the robot to continuously compare observed tactile states with the predicted forecast during execution. Persistent discrepancies between prediction and observation can then trigger corrective actions, enhancing the robustness and adaptability of the manipulation process. This mechanism is motivated by the need for real-time anomaly detection and recovery in dynamic contact scenarios.

By addressing these motivations, HiTac-WAM aims to provide a unified framework that improves both the planning and execution phases of contact-rich robot manipulation, leading to higher success rates in complex tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HiTac-WAM is designed as a hierarchical tactile world action model that augments a pretrained visual world action model (specifically, a variant of the World Action Model) with a dedicated tactile prediction branch. The system operates by forecasting a sequence of future tactile states for each candidate action chunk before execution.

**A. Directed Multimodal Context**
At a given control time, the model receives a multimodal observation history comprising synchronized RGB views, robot state, bilateral tactile history, and a language instruction. For each candidate action chunk, an H-step sequence of continuous actions, the tactile branch of HiTac-WAM predicts a hierarchical tactile trajectory for the forecast horizon. This trajectory includes predictions for contact state, contact-conditioned 3D deformation, and slip risk, all temporally aligned with the action chunk.

The model utilizes a shared backbone to process tokens from visual, action, tactile, robot-state, and language inputs. The bilateral tactile history is encoded into features and projected into tactile tokens using a pretrained FG-CLTP tactile encoder. A directed attention mask is employed to manage cross-modal information flow within the transformer architecture. This mask allows tactile queries to attend to the tactile history and the video–action context (including predicted future visual and action representations). Crucially, visual and action queries are prevented from attending to tactile keys or values, ensuring that tactile information primarily informs tactile predictions without directly influencing the video and action generation of the base world model. During training, the tactile projections, queries, action-tactile fusion module, and tactile prediction heads are updated.

**B. Explicit Hierarchical Tactile Forecast**
The core of HiTac-WAM's methodology is its explicit hierarchical prediction of tactile states, reflecting their physical dependencies:
1. **Contact State:** Predicted first, as deformation and slip are contingent upon contact.
2. **3D Deformation Field:** Predicted for each contact point, containing two in-plane displacement components and one surface-normal component. Deformation is only meaningful under contact.
3. **Slip Risk:** Predicted last, as it depends on the contact and deformation states.

This hierarchy is enforced through three deterministic prediction heads and stop-gradient operations. Stop-gradient signals from preceding stages condition downstream stages, preventing downstream losses from directly updating upstream heads while still allowing the shared trainable tactile representation to couple the predictions. Specifically:
- An intermediate transition feature from the contact head conditions the deformation head.
- The detached contact-state estimate conditions the slip head.
- The ungated future deformation (current deformation plus predicted increment) conditions slip prediction through its magnitude.
The predicted contact state gates both the deformation and slip outputs, ensuring that these phenomena are considered only in the presence of predicted contact.

The model is trained jointly using a multi-component loss function:
- **Contact Loss:** Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) for contact state, augmented with onset and release terms to constrain contact transition timing.
- **Deformation Loss:** Element-wise Huber penalty on the deformation increment. This loss is masked by ground-truth contact, meaning it is applied only to forecast offsets and sensors where contact is present.
- **Slip Loss:** Class-weighted Binary Cross-Entropy (WBCE) for slip risk. Since the inputs from upstream heads to the slip head are detached (via stop-gradient), this loss does not directly update the contact or deformation heads.

**C. Forecast-Guided Candidate Selection**
At each replanning step, the pretrained world action model generates K stochastic action chunk candidates. Kinematic checks filter out invalid chunks. For the remaining K_valid candidates, the HiTac-WAM tactile branch predicts a hierarchical tactile trajectory for each.
Each tactile forecast is then reduced to normalized rollout-level costs:
- `J_C`: Measures phase-inconsistent contact.
- `J_D`: Quantifies excessive or bilaterally imbalanced deformation.
- `J_R`: Aggregates peak and average slip risk over the bilateral forecast.
These tactile-derived costs are combined with a task progress score (`ρ_task`) derived from the candidate's predicted visual rollout. The overall selection score `J^(k)` penalizes undesirable contact consequences while rewarding task progress. The action chunk `k*` with the minimum combined score is selected for execution. The corresponding predicted tactile forecast `b_T^(k*)` is then retained as a reference for online verification.

**D. Online Forecast Verification**
During execution, the robot executes a prefix of length E (E ≤ H) of the selected action chunk. At each executed offset, the observed tactile state is compared with the temporally aligned, retained forecast `b_T^(k*)`. A discrepancy function quantifies the difference by concatenating absolute errors for contact and slip, along with deformation Top-20 Z MAE. An anomaly score is computed by passing this discrepancy through a kernel density estimator (KDE) trained on residuals from successful validation episodes for each task. If the anomaly score consistently exceeds a calibrated threshold (`γ_task`) for a configured number of consecutive executed offsets, a persistent deviation is declared. This triggers a recovery mechanism: the controller aborts the remaining unexecuted part of the prefix, moves the robot to a safe state, acquires a new observation, and restarts the candidate generation and selection process.

**Platform, Data, and Training:**
Experiments were conducted on an IMETA-Y1 robot equipped with bilateral DM-Tac W2 tactile sensors and three synchronized RGB cameras. Data was collected for three contact-rich tasks: chip grasping, blackboard erasing, and USB insertion. For each task, 200 episodes were split into training, validation, and testing sets. Data streams (tactile history, actions, tactile targets) were synchronized at 30 Hz. The tactile prediction modules were trained for 10,000 steps on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs using AdamW optimizer. Loss weights were configured to balance contact, deformation, and slip supervision, with specific weights for contact onset/release and positive slip events.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated HiTac-WAM across four primary questions concerning tactile prediction, the impact of the hierarchical structure, the effectiveness of forecast-guided action selection, and the utility of online forecast verification.

**A. Tactile World-State Prediction (Q1)**
HiTac-WAM demonstrated the ability to predict various tactile states across the action horizon for the three tested tasks (chip grasping, blackboard erasing, and USB insertion).
- **Contact State:** Achieved a mean Contact F1 score of 0.921, with task-wise F1 ranging from 0.908 to 0.931. The mean onset and release Mean Absolute Errors (MAEs) were 2.1 and 2.3 frames (approximately 60–87 ms at 30 Hz), respectively.
- **3D Deformation:** The 3D displacement L2 error was a mean of 0.058 mm (range 0.054–0.062 mm). The Top-20 Z MAE, which focuses on areas of significant surface-normal displacement, was a mean of 0.076 mm (range 0.071–0.081 mm). The predicted-to-ground-truth deformation norm ratio averaged 0.96 (range 0.93–0.99), indicating that the model predicted deformation magnitudes comparable to observed values.
- **Slip Risk:** The Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) for slip prediction was a mean of 0.247 (range 0.232–0.258), which is notably above the chance level of 0.0139, especially considering that positive slip events occurred in only 1.39% of frames.
Qualitative analysis (Fig. 3) supported these metrics, showing predicted contact states tracking ground-truth transitions, deformation snapshots aligning with RGB observations, and slip risk correlating with labeled slip events.

**B. Hierarchy and Conditioning (Q2)**
Ablation studies under matched training budgets demonstrated the benefits of the directed hierarchical structure and explicit conditioning.
- **Performance Comparison:** The full directed hierarchy achieved the best performance across all metrics: F1 of 0.921, 3D L2 of 0.056 mm, Top-20 Z of 0.072 mm, and a norm ratio of 0.97.
- **Impact of Hierarchy:** The directed hierarchy reduced the 3D displacement L2 error by 17.6% compared to a deformation-only predictor (0.068 mm). It also improved slip AUPRC by 60.4% relative to a slip-only predictor.
- **Contribution of Components:** Ablations showed that simply sharing hidden states among independent prediction heads was insufficient (F1: 0.896 vs. 0.903 for contact-only; 3D L2: 0.064 vs. 0.068 mm for deformation-only). Contact conditioning recovered most of the performance improvement (F1: 0.915, 3D L2: 0.059 mm), with the full hierarchy providing additional gains.
- **Conditioning Diagnostics:** Interventions on the contact input to the deformation head (Fig. 5a) showed a monotonic increase in 3D L2 error (0.053 mm for ground-truth, 0.056 mm for predicted, 0.071 mm for shuffled, and 0.078 mm for zero contact), confirming that the deformation head effectively utilizes the contact signal. Similarly, cumulative conditioning for slip prediction (Fig. 5b) showed AUPRC increasing from 0.154 (slip only) to 0.247 (contact and deformation conditioned), confirming the hierarchical dependency.
- **Impact on Visual/Action Outputs:** Relative to the world action model without tactile prediction, the integration of HiTac-WAM resulted in a minor increase in video LPIPS (1.4%) and action MAE (0.9%), suggesting a minimal trade-off for the improved tactile capabilities.

**C. Forecast-Guided Action Selection (Q3)**
The use of tactile forecasts for action selection significantly improved task success rates.
- **Overall Improvement:** HiTac-WAM's forecast-guided selection (61.1% average real-robot success rate across tasks) substantially outperformed the baseline DreamZero (31.1% success), which executes a single sampled action chunk, and Reactive Tactile (40.0%), which uses current touch without foresight. The gains were particularly pronounced in tasks where contact is critical, such as chip grasping (70.0% vs. 33.3% for DreamZero) and USB insertion (40.0% vs. 10.0% for DreamZero).
- **Comparison to Task-Progress Ranking:** Under a fixed generation budget (K=4 candidates), tactile-forecast ranking (61.1% success) demonstrated superior performance compared to task-progress ranking (35.6% success), which relies solely on predicted visual task progress. The 95% Wilson confidence intervals for these two methods did not overlap, indicating a statistically notable difference.

**D. Online Forecast Verification (Q4)**
The full HiTac-WAM system, which includes online forecast verification, further improved success rates.
- **Enhanced Success Rate:** The full HiTac-WAM system achieved an average real-robot success rate of 72.2%, a notable increase from the 61.1% achieved by forecast-guided selection alone.
- **Anomaly Detection:** Post-hoc analysis of 90 trials indicated that the verification mechanism triggered replanning in 18 cases. Of these, 16 coincided with actual execution anomalies, and 12 of these anomaly-triggered replannings led to successful task completion. Only two instances were identified as false triggers without an observed negative effect on the outcome, and few untriggered failures involved undetected anomalies. ### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HiTac-WAM research introduces a hierarchical tactile world action model that addresses a fundamental limitation of visually-centric robot manipulation systems, particularly in contact-rich environments. The key significance of this work lies in its explicit modeling of the physical dependencies among contact state, 3D deformation, and slip risk, and its integration of this structured tactile prediction into a unified framework for both proactive action selection and reactive execution monitoring.

**Primary Contributions:**
- **Improved Tactile Prediction Accuracy:** By organizing tactile predictions according to their physical dependencies and employing stop-gradient conditioning, HiTac-WAM achieves notable improvements in the accuracy of forecasting contact, deformation, and slip risk. This structured approach demonstrated a 17.6% reduction in 3D displacement L2 error and a 60.4% improvement in slip AUPRC relative to less structured baselines.
- **Enhanced Robot Manipulation Robustness:** The system significantly elevates real-robot success rates in complex contact-rich tasks (e.g., chip grasping, blackboard erasing, USB insertion). This is achieved through two main mechanisms:
 - **Proactive Action Selection:** By ranking candidate action chunks based on their predicted hierarchical tactile consequences, robots can select actions that are not just visually plausible but also physically sound, avoiding potential failures before they occur. The reported 61.1% success rate for tactile-forecast ranking, significantly higher than visual-only or single-candidate execution, demonstrates this impact.
 - **Online Execution Monitoring:** The retention of selected tactile forecasts as execution-time references allows for continuous verification against observed tactile states. This enables the detection of persistent deviations and triggers corrective replanning, contributing to the system's robustness and adaptability, as evidenced by the full system's 72.2% average success rate.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Reliable Autonomous Systems:** HiTac-WAM's approach could lead to more reliable and robust autonomous robotic systems capable of performing intricate manipulation tasks that require precise physical interaction. This is critical for applications in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and household robotics, where fine motor skills and adaptive contact handling are essential.
- **Bridging the Perception-Action Gap:** The work contributes to bridging the gap between high-level visual perception and low-level physical interaction control by providing a structured, predictive understanding of touch. This deeper understanding allows robots to anticipate and react to physical realities beyond what visual sensing alone can provide.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The research identifies areas for future exploration, such as evaluating forecast quality on model-generated action chunks, calibrating slip risk for online detection, and developing learned corrective policies. These directions suggest a pathway toward even more sophisticated and adaptive robotic manipulation capabilities, including on-policy adaptation and uncertainty-calibrated tactile forecasting for long-horizon tasks.

In summary, HiTac-WAM presents a methodological advance in integrating tactile intelligence into robot action models, offering a framework that could enable robots to interact with their environment with greater dexterity and resilience.
