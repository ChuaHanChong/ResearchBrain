# The Art of Scaling Reinforcement Learning Compute for LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced a predictive framework for Reinforcement Learning (RL) in Large Language Models (LLMs) using a sigmoidal compute-performance curve, enabling performance extrapolation from smaller runs. Their ScaleRL recipe, demonstrated over 100,000 GPU-hours, achieves an asymptotic reward of 0.61 on verifiable math problems, outperforming established methods while exhibiting predictable scaling across model size, generation length, and multi-task settings.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) training for Large Language Models (LLMs) lacked a principled, generalizable framework to understand and predict performance at scale, unlike pre-training.
- The current approach to RL scaling relied on "ad-hoc solutions" from isolated studies, hindering the identification of truly scalable methods.
- Prohibitive compute costs made extensive large-scale experimentation impractical for most researchers to test new RL algorithms efficiently.

## Method

- A novel predictive scaling framework was introduced, employing a ==sigmoidal compute-performance== curve to model and extrapolate RL performance with increasing computational resources.
- A comprehensive, multi-stage empirical study was conducted, utilizing over 400,000 GPU-hours, to systematically evaluate the impact of various RL design choices on asymptotic performance and compute efficiency.
- The most effective design choices were integrated into a unified, predictable, and scalable RL recipe named ScaleRL.

## Results

- The proposed sigmoidal compute-performance curves effectively predicted RL performance, validated by ScaleRL's 100,000 GPU-hour run where extrapolations from early training accurately matched final outcomes.
- ScaleRL achieved a higher asymptotic reward (A=**0.61**) and improved compute efficiency on verifiable math problems, outperforming established methods like DeepSeek (GRPO) and Qwen-2.5 (DAPO).
- The ScaleRL recipe demonstrated stable and predictable scaling behavior across diverse compute dimensions, including larger model sizes (**17B**×16 MoE), extended generation lengths (32k tokens), and multi-task settings (math + code).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Asymptotic performance (A) is not universal for RL in LLMs, but is strongly influenced by loss function choice (CISPO/GSPO achieving higher asymptotes) and numerical precision (FP32 for LM logits being critical).
> - Many RL design choices primarily enhance compute efficiency (B) or shift the midpoint (C_mid) of the scaling curve, rather than increasing the absolute asymptotic performance (A).
> - The ability to extrapolate performance using the sigmoidal curve from lower-compute runs is essential for distinguishing genuinely scalable methods from those that only appear effective at smaller scales.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been largely driven by scaling up model size, data, and compute during pre-training, guided by empirical scaling laws (e.g., Kaplan et al., Hoffmann et al.). However, the subsequent, crucial phase of **Reinforcement Learning (RL) training**, which fine-tunes LLMs for advanced capabilities like reasoning and agentic behavior, has lacked a comparable scientific framework for understanding and predicting performance at scale. This gap is increasingly problematic as RL compute budgets for frontier LLMs are skyrocketing, with examples like Deepseek-R1-Zero, OpenAI's o1/o3 models, and xAI's Grok series showcasing a dramatic increase in RL-specific compute.

Currently, the field of LLM RL scaling is characterized by "ad-hoc solutions" derived from isolated studies on novel algorithms (e.g., DAPO, GRPO) or model-specific training reports (e.g., MiniMax, Magistral). These approaches offer context-specific insights but fail to provide a **principled, generalizable methodology** for identifying scalable RL candidates or predicting their performance under increasing compute. This lack of a "science of RL scaling" acts as a significant barrier, particularly for academic researchers who cannot afford the prohibitive compute costs of extensive large-scale experimentation required to test every promising idea.

This paper directly addresses this critical void. By borrowing from the established concept of scaling laws in pre-training, it seeks to transition RL scaling from "art" to "science." It introduces a **predictive framework** that allows researchers to forecast the performance of RL methods from lower-compute runs, thereby democratizing research by reducing the need for exhaustive, high-cost experiments. In essence, this work aims to provide the foundational understanding and tools for RL in LLMs that scaling laws provided for pre-training, enabling more efficient research and development in this rapidly evolving domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are multi-faceted, aiming to introduce scientific rigor and predictability into the scaling of RL for LLMs:

1. **Define a Principled Framework for RL Scaling:** The foremost goal is to establish a systematic methodology for analyzing and predicting how RL performance evolves with increasing computational resources in LLMs. This involves proposing a robust mathematical model (a sigmoidal function) to describe the relationship between compute and performance.
2. **Systematic Empirical Study of Design Choices:** To inform this framework, the authors aim to conduct the first large-scale, systematic study of various common design choices in LLM RL. The objective is to understand how these choices affect two critical aspects: the **asymptotic performance (A)**, representing the maximum achievable skill, and **compute efficiency (B)**, indicating how quickly that skill is attained.
3. **Develop a Predictable and Scalable RL Recipe (ScaleRL):** Based on the insights gained from the empirical study, a key objective is to consolidate the most effective design choices into a unified, best-practice RL recipe, termed ScaleRL. This recipe should not only achieve high performance but also exhibit stable and predictable scaling behavior across various compute axes.
4. **Enable Performance Extrapolation:** A core motivation is to empower researchers to extrapolate performance from smaller, less costly runs to higher compute budgets. This capability is essential for cost-effectively evaluating new RL algorithms and accelerating research progress without incurring exorbitant compute expenses for every experiment.
5. **Bridge the Gap with Pre-training Predictability:** Ultimately, the research is motivated by the desire to bring RL training for LLMs closer to the predictability and scientific understanding that has long been achieved in the pre-training phase, where scaling laws have become a standard tool.

The overarching motivation stems from the recognition that while RL is becoming indispensable for unlocking advanced LLM capabilities, the current ad-hoc approach to its scaling is unsustainable and inefficient. A principled understanding is urgently needed to guide future development and resource allocation in this compute-intensive domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous, multi-stage empirical methodology, underpinned by a novel predictive scaling framework.

#### 3.1. Predictive Scaling Framework

The cornerstone of the methodology is a **sigmoidal compute-performance curve** (Equation 1) which models the relationship between an LLM's reward gain ($R_C - R_0$) on an i.i.d. validation set and training compute (C).
$R_C - R_0 = (A - R_0) \times \frac{1}{1 + (C_{mid}/C)^B}$
- **Asymptotic Reward Gain (A):** Represents the maximum achievable performance or saturation point.
- **Compute Efficiency (B):** A scaling exponent determining the steepness of the curve, indicating how efficiently performance improves with compute.
- **Midpoint (C_mid):** The compute point at which half of the total gain is achieved.

This sigmoidal form was chosen over traditional power laws (used in pre-training) due to its superior robustness and stability for bounded metrics like accuracy, and its ability to accurately predict saturation behavior at high compute. The fitting procedure involves a grid search for A and C_mid, followed by fitting B, with fits beginning after an initial transient phase (~1.5k GPU hours).

#### 3.2. Experimental Setup

- **Compute Budget:** A massive undertaking involving over 400,000 GPU-hours on Nvidia GB200 GPUs. Individual large-scale runs consumed up to 100,000 GPU-hours.
- **Models:** Primarily an 8B dense model, with some experiments extending to a 17B×16 Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model (Scout).
- **Domain:** Reinforcement learning for reasoning tasks, primarily verifiable math problems from the Polaris-53K dataset (An et al., 2025), with some multi-task experiments including code (Deepcoder dataset).
- **Training Regimen:**
 - **Generator–Trainer Split:** GPUs were partitioned, with a larger subset acting as "generators" (optimized inference for rollouts) and the remainder as "trainers" (policy updates using FSDP).
 - **Sequence Length:** Training used up to 16,384 tokens (12,288 for thinking, 2,048 for solution, 2,048 for prompt), with experiments extending to 32,768 tokens.
 - **Batch Size:** Typically 48 prompts with 16 generations each (768 completions per step), scaled up to 2048 prompts.
 - **Reward Function:** Scalar rewards of ±1 for correct/incorrect traces, verified by automated checkers.
 - **Optimizer:** AdamW with a constant learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-7}$ and linear warmup.
- **Baseline RL Algorithm:** An initial "base" algorithm resembling GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) but without KL regularization, incorporating asymmetric DAPO clipping (Yu et al., 2025).

#### 3.3. Multi-Stage Empirical Study

1. **Ablations on Baseline Design Choices (3.5k–4k GPU-hours):**
 - **Asynchronous RL Setup:** Compared PPO-off-policy-k (used in Qwen3, ProRL) against PipelineRL-k (Piche et al., 2025; Magistral), varying 'k' (off-policyness).
 - **Algorithmic Choices:** Systematically ablated six axes: loss aggregation (sample, prompt, token average), advantage normalization (prompt, batch, no normalization), precision fixes (FP32 for LM logits), data curriculum (zero-variance filtering, No-Positive-Resampling), batch definition, and loss type (DAPO, GSPO, CISPO).
 - **Evaluation:** Primarily focused on identifying stable variants and preliminary impact on A and B.

2. **Development of ScaleRL and Leave-One-Out (LOO) Ablations (16k GPU-hours):**
 - **Recipe Consolidation:** The most promising choices from Stage 1 were combined into ScaleRL.
 - **LOO Validation:** Each component of ScaleRL was individually reverted to its baseline counterpart to confirm its positive contribution, assessing impact on A and B.
 - **Predictability Assessment:** Fitted curves on the first 8k GPU-hours were extrapolated to 16k GPU-hours to verify predictive accuracy. Error margins were estimated using three independent ScaleRL runs.

3. **Large-Scale Validation and Generalization of ScaleRL (up to 100k GPU-hours):**
 - **Scaling Axes:** ScaleRL's predictability and stability were tested by scaling across various dimensions: model size (17B×16 MoE), generation length (up to 32,768 tokens), global batch size, generations per prompt, and multi-task RL (math + code).
 - **Verification:** Extrapolations from early training were compared against extended training runs to confirm long-term predictive power.
 - **Downstream Evaluation:** Performance was also assessed on unseen downstream benchmarks like AIME-24 and LiveCodeBench to check generalization.

This comprehensive approach allows for both the systematic deconstruction of RL components and the construction of a robust, scalable recipe, validated at an unprecedented scale.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several critical findings that advance the understanding of RL scaling for LLMs:

1. **Validation of Predictive Scaling Framework:** The proposed sigmoidal compute-performance curves successfully fit and predict RL performance across various experiments. This was most strikingly demonstrated by ScaleRL's 100,000 GPU-hour run on an 8B model, where extrapolations from early training (up to 50k GPU-hours) accurately predicted the final observed performance. This confirms the stability and predictability of well-designed RL recipes.

2. **Asymptotic Performance Ceilings are Not Universal:**
 - **Impact of Loss Type:** Loss functions significantly alter the asymptotic reward (A). CISPO and GSPO substantially outperformed DAPO, achieving higher asymptotic pass rates. CISPO was marginally better and more stable.
 - **FP32 Precision Fix:** Using FP32 precision in the final language model head dramatically improved the asymptotic performance (A) (e.g., from 0.52 to 0.61 for the baseline). This fix was critical for numerical stability, especially for IS ratios.

3. **Efficiency vs. Asymptote Modulation:** Many common design choices primarily modulate **compute efficiency (B)** and the midpoint ($C_{mid}$) rather than materially shifting the asymptotic performance (A) ceiling.
 - **Asynchronous Setup:** PipelineRL significantly improved compute efficiency (higher B) compared to PPO-off-policy, while achieving similar or slightly better asymptotic performance. This is attributed to PipelineRL's closer alignment with on-policy training dynamics.
 - **Other Factors:** Loss aggregation (prompt-average), advantage normalization (batch-level), zero-variance filtering, and adaptive prompt filtering (No-Positive-Resampling) generally improved efficiency or slightly nudged the asymptote.

4. **"Embracing the Bitter Lesson" for RL:** Methods that appeared superior at smaller compute budgets often performed worse when extrapolated to large-compute regimes. The ability to estimate A and B early is crucial for identifying truly scalable methods. ScaleRL emerged as a state-of-the-art recipe, achieving a higher asymptotic reward (A=0.61) and better compute efficiency compared to established methods like DeepSeek (GRPO), Qwen-2.5 (DAPO), Magistral, and MiniMax-M1 (Figure 2).

5. **ScaleRL Recipe:** The final ScaleRL recipe combines:
 - PipelineRL with 8 steps off-policyness.
 - Interruption-based length control.
 - CISPO loss (truncated importance-sampling REINFORCE).
 - Prompt-level loss aggregation.
 - Batch-level advantage normalization.
 - FP32 precision at LM logits.
 - Zero-variance filtering.
 - No-Positive-Resampling (data curriculum).
 Leave-one-out ablations confirmed that each component contributes positively, primarily by enhancing efficiency, even if individual impact on the final asymptote was sometimes subtle within the combined recipe.

6. **Predictable Scaling Across Multiple Compute Axes:** ScaleRL demonstrated stable and predictable scaling behavior when increasing compute across diverse dimensions:
 - **Model Scale:** The 17B×16 MoE (Scout) model scaled predictably, showing much higher asymptotic performance than the 8B dense model with less RL compute.
 - **Generation Length:** Longer contexts (32k tokens) led to higher asymptotes, indicating it's a ceiling-raising knob, despite initial efficiency tradeoffs.
 - **Global Batch Size:** Larger batch sizes (e.g., 2048 prompts) improved the asymptote and prevented downstream performance stagnation observed in smaller runs.
 - **Multi-task RL:** Joint training on math and code also exhibited clean, predictable scaling trends for both domains.

7. **Training Stability and Truncations:** High truncation rates (e.g., 10-15%) were identified as a reliable warning signal for training instability. ScaleRL and larger models generally maintained low and stable truncation rates, which contributed to their overall robustness.

8. **Entropy as a Performance Predictor:** While entropy consistently decreased during training, it was not always a reliable predictor of downstream performance. For instance, larger batch sizes yielded better downstream performance despite similar entropy trajectories per step, suggesting other factors are more decisive.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a pivotal step towards a more scientific and predictable approach to Reinforcement Learning in Large Language Models, with profound implications for the field:

1. **Foundational Scientific Framework:** The most significant contribution is the establishment of the first principled framework for analyzing and predicting RL scaling in LLMs. By introducing and validating the sigmoidal compute-performance curve, the paper provides a quantitative tool analogous to scaling laws in pre-training. This shifts the understanding of RL optimization from an "art" of ad-hoc adjustments to a "science" of predictable scaling.

2. **Democratization of RL Research:** The ability to extrapolate performance from smaller-scale, lower-cost runs significantly lowers the barrier to entry for RL research. Academic labs and smaller research groups can now more effectively evaluate novel algorithms and design choices without needing prohibitive computational budgets for every experiment, thereby accelerating innovation across the community.

3. **State-of-the-Art and Robust Recipe (ScaleRL):** ScaleRL provides a robust, predictable, and high-performing RL recipe that integrates best practices from various prior works. Its demonstrated ability to scale reliably to 100,000 GPU-hours and achieve superior asymptotic performance and compute efficiency sets a new benchmark for practitioners and offers a strong starting point for future LLM RL endeavors.

4. **Practical Guidance for Practitioners:** The detailed ablation studies and the clear distinction between factors affecting asymptotic performance (A) and compute efficiency (B) offer invaluable guidance. Researchers and engineers can now make more informed decisions about design choices, understanding whether an intervention aims to raise the ultimate performance ceiling or merely expedite convergence. The identification of critical factors like loss type, FP32 precision, and asynchronous setup provides clear priorities.

5. **Blueprint for Future Scaling Laws:** This work lays the groundwork for deriving more comprehensive "scaling laws" for RL that could span multiple axes, including pre-training compute, model size, and RL training data. It also opens avenues for studying the scaling behavior of other complex post-training regimes like multi-turn RL, agentic interaction, and long-form reasoning, which are crucial for developing more capable AI systems.

6. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Efficiency:** By providing a methodological framework and releasing a minimal code repository for curve fitting, the authors contribute to greater reproducibility in LLM RL research. The focus on cost-effective prediction will lead to more efficient resource allocation, reducing wasted compute on unscalable or unstable methods.

In conclusion, "The Art of Scaling Reinforcement Learning Compute for LLMs" fundamentally transforms how RL for LLMs is approached. It provides both the scientific lens to understand scaling dynamics and a practical, validated recipe, ensuring that the increasing compute dedicated to RL translates into predictable, stable, and impactful improvements in LLM capabilities.
