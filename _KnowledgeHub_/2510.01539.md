# Executable Counterfactuals: Improving LLMs' Causal Reasoning Through Code

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, "executable counterfactuals," rigorously evaluates and enhances large language models' causal reasoning by requiring them to perform abduction, intervention, and prediction. Models trained with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards consistently generalize this reasoning to out-of-distribution code and math problems, unlike those trained with supervised finetuning, despite all models showing a significant accuracy drop when required to infer latent variables.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) consistently struggle with true counterfactual reasoning, a complex cognitive skill vital for human-like intelligence and high-stakes applications.
- Existing benchmarks for LLM causal reasoning often over-estimate model capabilities by conflating counterfactual reasoning (Level 3) with simpler interventional reasoning (Level 2), primarily by omitting the crucial 'abduction' step.
- There is a need for a scalable, verifiable framework to accurately evaluate and improve LLMs' causal understanding beyond superficial performance on tasks that do not fully exercise all components of counterfactual inference.

## Method

- Introduces "executable counterfactuals," a framework operationalizing ==counterfactual reasoning== through templated code and mathematical problems, explicitly requiring the three steps: Abduction, Intervention, and Prediction.
- Generates diverse, verifiable datasets where a latent variable must be abduced from observed input/output pairs, ensuring objective ground truth for evaluation.
- Compares Supervised Finetuning (SFT) using distilled reasoning traces from a stronger teacher model with Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) using outcome-based rewards to improve counterfactual reasoning.

## Results

- State-of-the-art LLMs exhibit a 25-**40%** or more accuracy drop on counterfactual tasks compared to interventional tasks, consistently failing at the abduction step.
- RL-trained models achieve substantial accuracy gains over base models on all in-distribution and out-of-distribution code and math counterfactual datasets, with a Qwen2.5-**7B**-Instruct model achieving performance comparable to or better than a Qwen2.5-**72B**-Instruct model.
- Despite learning robust reasoning strategies (planning), RLVR models still show a decrease in 'execution' scores on complex OOD tasks, indicating that computational accuracy can remain a bottleneck.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inability to perform the "abduction" step (inferring latent variables from observations) is the primary reason LLMs fail at true counterfactual reasoning, leading to a substantial accuracy gap compared to interventional tasks.
> - Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) proves to be a more effective and generalizable training paradigm for instilling complex cognitive skills like counterfactual reasoning, allowing models to internalize reasoning strategies.
> - Supervised Finetuning (SFT) leads to limited generalization, as models tend to memorize shallow patterns rather than learning fundamental causal reasoning, performing poorly on out-of-distribution tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the burgeoning field of LLM reasoning, specifically addressing the critical, yet often misunderstood, aspect of **counterfactual reasoning**. The broader research landscape recognizes that while LLMs exhibit impressive linguistic capabilities and emergent reasoning skills, their understanding of causality remains a significant challenge.

**Limitations of Prior Work:** The authors argue that existing benchmarks and evaluation efforts for LLM counterfactual reasoning suffer from a fundamental flaw: they tend to **overestimate LLM performance** by conflating true counterfactual reasoning with simpler forms of causal reasoning, specifically **interventional reasoning**. As per Judea Pearl's Causal Ladder, interventional reasoning (Level 2) involves predicting outcomes given a hypothetical action (e.g., "What would Y be if I *did* X?"), while counterfactual reasoning (Level 3) asks "What *would have been* Y if I *had done* X, given that I *did* X' and observed Y'?" The crucial distinction lies in the need to **abduce latent variables** from an observed outcome (Y') before performing the hypothetical intervention. Many previous benchmarks, such as those discussed by Wu et al. (2024), Chen et al. (2025), and others, either provide full information about the causal graph or present tasks that can be solved by simply changing input variables and re-executing a forward pass, without requiring the LLM to infer any unobserved states or noise. This effectively reduces a Level 3 counterfactual task to a Level 2 interventional one, leading to an inaccurate assessment of LLMs' true causal understanding.

**Bridging the Gap:** This paper directly addresses this gap by introducing a novel framework, **"executable counterfactuals,"** that explicitly operationalizes and requires all three cognitive steps of counterfactual reasoning: **Abduction, Intervention, and Prediction**. By incorporating latent variables into code and math problems, the framework forces LLMs to infer unobserved conditions from a given observation—a core component of true counterfactual reasoning often overlooked.

**Relation to Other Benchmarks:** While other notable work, such as Jin et al. (2024), also provides formal benchmarks for the causal ladder, the present paper distinguishes itself by focusing on "executable" problems in domains (code and math) where LLMs have shown rapid progress. This allows for objective evaluation, scalable data generation, and more actionable insights into how to train and improve LLMs for causal capabilities, moving beyond purely theoretical or graph-based assessments. The use of code as a proxy for computational graphs also connects to a broader trend of leveraging programmatic reasoning and environments for LLM development, as seen in works exploring concept learning from programs (Lake et al., 2015) or procedural generation for reinforcement learning (Cobbe et al., 2020).

Furthermore, the paper contributes to the ongoing debate about the effectiveness and generalization capabilities of different LLM post-training paradigms, specifically comparing Supervised Finetuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). This aligns with recent studies that explore the generalization differences between SFT and RL (e.g., Kirk et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2025).

In essence, this work represents a significant step towards a more rigorous and practical evaluation of LLMs' causal reasoning, aiming to close the gap between their superficial performance on "counterfactual-framed" tasks and their actual ability to perform the complex cognitive steps required for true counterfactual inference.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **improve Large Language Models' (LLMs) causal understanding, specifically their counterfactual reasoning abilities**, and to provide a robust framework for its evaluation and enhancement. The authors are driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing a Critical Weakness in LLMs:** Counterfactual reasoning, the ability to answer "what-if" questions about hypothetical alternatives to past events, is highlighted as a "hallmark of intelligence" and essential for human-like reasoning. Despite LLMs' impressive capabilities, it remains a widely documented weakness (Jin et al., 2024; Yamin et al., 2025).
2. **Expanding LLM Applications in High-Stakes Domains:** The ability to reason counterfactually is indispensable in critical fields such as scientific discovery, healthcare (e.g., understanding alternative treatments), economics, and public policy. Improving LLMs in this area would unlock their potential for more reliable and impactful applications in these high-stakes environments.
3. **Correcting Misinterpretations of LLM Abilities:** The paper strongly argues that existing evaluation methods often misrepresent LLMs' counterfactual abilities by reducing the task to simpler interventional reasoning. This happens because these methods frequently omit the crucial "abduction" step, where models must infer unobserved latent variables from observations. A primary motivation is to introduce a framework that explicitly demands all three core steps of counterfactual reasoning (Abduction, Intervention, Prediction) to provide a more accurate and nuanced assessment.
4. **Enabling Scalable and Controllable Evaluation:** Current methods struggle with the inherent unobservability of counterfactuals and the difficulty of generating diverse, verifiable tasks. A key objective is to create a framework that allows for scalable, synthetic data generation with controllable complexity, enabling both robust evaluation and effective training data creation.
5. **Guiding LLM Improvement Strategies:** Beyond evaluation, the research aims to investigate effective strategies for improving counterfactual reasoning in LLMs. Specifically, it seeks to compare the efficacy and generalization capabilities of supervised finetuning (SFT) versus reinforcement learning (RL) in instilling these complex cognitive skills.

In essence, the authors seek to move beyond superficial evaluations to uncover and address the true limitations of LLMs in causal reasoning, paving the way for more robust, intelligent, and trustworthy AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology revolves around the **"executable counterfactuals" framework**, which operationalizes causal reasoning through carefully constructed code and mathematical problems. This framework is designed to explicitly require all three steps of counterfactual reasoning: Abduction, Intervention, and Prediction.

#### 3.1. Operationalizing Counterfactual Reasoning

The authors define counterfactual reasoning as a three-step cognitive process:
1. **Abduction:** Inferring latent variables or hidden states from an initial observation. This is the critical step often skipped in prior work.
2. **Intervention:** Mentally constructing an alternative scenario by changing specific input variables while holding the previously abduced latent variables constant.
3. **Prediction:** Computing the outcome under this new, counterfactual scenario.

#### 3.2. Executable Counterfactuals: Code (Section 3.1)

- **Problem Setting:** The key idea is to use code understanding as the problem setting. Python functions are generated, featuring an unknown "latent variable" (`r`) whose value is not explicitly revealed to the LLM.
- **Template-based Generation:** A small set of diverse templates (e.g., `if-else`, `while-loop`) are used to create distinct, executable Python functions. These templates allow for variations at three levels:
 - **Fixed Placeholders:** Boilerplate elements like function names.
 - **Structural Placeholders:** Complete code blocks defining the program's logic (e.g., `if-condition`, `elif` clauses, branch content).
 - **Value Placeholders:** Specific operators and numbers to control detailed behavior.
- **Inducing Abduction:** The latent variable `r` (e.g., generated by `random.randint`) is incorporated into the function's logic. The LLM is given an observed input (`x`) and its corresponding output (`y`), which were produced with an *unknown* `r`. To answer a counterfactual question (e.g., "What if `x` was different?"), the LLM *must* first abduce the value(s) of `r` that could explain the observed `y`.
- **Ambiguity:** To better mirror real-world partial information, some training functions include a modulo operation in the return statement (`return g(·) mod m`). This can lead to multiple possible values of `r` consistent with the observed output, thus yielding multiple valid counterfactual outcomes and requiring the LLM to handle ambiguity.
- **Verifiable Ground Truth:** Since the functions are executable code, the ground-truth answers for both factual and counterfactual scenarios can be objectively computed.
- **Data Diversity and Deduplication:** The template approach ensures structural and semantic diversity, while a deduplication process (based on AST and structural fingerprints) prevents overly similar functions.
- **Dataset Splits (for evaluation):**
 - **In-Distribution (ID):** `If_else` functions (also used for training).
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD):**
 - `If_else-long`: More nested `if-else` structures to test generalization to longer code.
 - `While`: Incorporates `while` loops to test generalization to novel control flow.
 - `Multi_r`: Features multiple hidden variables and simple `for` loops, testing generalization to different causal structures.
- **Interventional Counterparts:** For comparison, parallel "interventional" versions of problems are created where the latent variable `r` is explicitly revealed in the prompt, thereby removing the need for the abduction step.

#### 3.3. GSM Math Problem Construction (Section 3.2)

- **Domain Transfer Test:** To evaluate generalization beyond code, a new dataset of counterfactual variants of GSM-8K-style math word problems is constructed.
- **Hidden Factor:** Each math problem introduces a "hidden factor" (e.g., a per-tray service fee, an unknown number of additional items) that contributes to the total but is not revealed in the problem narrative. This mandates abduction.
- **Generation:** Computational graphs are verbalized into word problems using phrasing templates, ensuring variety.
- **Interventional Counterparts:** As with code, interventional math problems are generated by revealing the hidden factor.

#### 3.4. Training Methodologies

- **Supervised Finetuning (SFT):** Base models (Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B-Instruct) are finetuned on reasoning trajectories (long Chain-of-Thought) distilled from a stronger teacher model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B). This provides high-quality, step-by-step supervision.
- **Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR):** The same base models are trained using GRPO, a reinforcement learning algorithm. This approach only requires outcome-based supervision (exact match score as reward), making it supervision-efficient.

#### 3.5. Evaluation and Analysis

- **Metrics:** Exact Match (for exact agreement with ground truth, potentially multiple answers) and F1 score (for partial coverage of multiple ground truths).
- **Models Evaluated:** A broad spectrum of models, including open-source (Qwen, Llama), commercial (GPT-4o, Claude-4-Sonnet), and specialized reasoning models, are tested.
- **Behavioral Analysis (LLM-as-a-Judge):** Model-generated reasoning traces are qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed using o4-mini as an LLM judge. The analysis focuses on two dimensions:
 - **Planning:** Whether the model correctly follows the "abduction-intervention-prediction" sequence.
 - **Execution:** The correctness of numerical computations and code simulation.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a deep dive into LLMs' causal reasoning capabilities, providing both quantitative performance metrics and qualitative insights into their reasoning processes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several critical findings regarding LLMs' counterfactual reasoning abilities and the efficacy of different training paradigms:

1. **Significant Performance Gap Between Counterfactual and Interventional Reasoning:**
 - State-of-the-art LLMs (including GPT-4o, Claude-4-Sonnet, and specialized reasoning models like o4-mini) exhibit a **substantial drop in accuracy (25-40% or more)** when transitioning from interventional reasoning (where latent variables are provided) to true counterfactual reasoning (where latent variables must be abduced).
 - For example, models like o4-mini achieve nearly perfect scores (over 90%) on interventional code tasks but drop to 40-70% on their counterfactual counterparts, especially for complex OOD structures.
 - This consistently observed gap across various model scales and families confirms the paper's central hypothesis: prior evaluations likely overestimated LLM causal reasoning by overlooking the abduction step.
 - Qualitative analysis of reasoning traces reveals that LLMs consistently fail at the **abduction step**, struggling to correctly infer latent features from observed outcomes.

2. **Supervised Finetuning (SFT) Shows Limited Generalization:**
 - SFT, when distilled from stronger models' reasoning traces, significantly improves in-distribution (ID) counterfactual reasoning performance (e.g., ~40% accuracy gain for Qwen models on `if_else`).
 - However, these improvements **do not generalize well to out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks**. When faced with unseen code structures (`while-loop`, `multi_r`) or different problem domains (counterfactual math word problems), SFT-trained models show diminished gains or even a *decrease* in accuracy compared to base models.
 - Behavioral analysis indicates that SFT models tend to **memorize shallow abduction patterns** rather than learning the fundamental cognitive skill. When confronted with increased complexity or novel structures in OOD tasks, they often revert to prototypical failure modes like brute-force enumeration or arbitrary assumptions, failing to apply the correct reasoning strategy.

3. **Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) Elicits Generalizable Skills:**
 - In stark contrast to SFT, RLVR training (using GRPO with outcome-based rewards) consistently induces and generalizes counterfactual reasoning skills.
 - RL-trained models achieve **substantial accuracy gains** over base models on *all* ID and OOD evaluation datasets, including `multi_r`, `while`, and `gsm` (counterfactual math problems).
 - The gains are robust and scale with model size; for instance, a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model trained with RLVR achieves performance comparable to a Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct model and consistently outperforms its 32B variant across the coding domain.
 - Crucially, RLVR enables generalization from code-only training to natural-language counterfactual math problems, demonstrating that models internalize the core cognitive behaviors rather than just task-specific patterns.
 - **Behavioral analysis reinforces this:** RLVR models consistently achieve the highest "planning" scores, indicating they learn to faithfully follow the "abduction-intervention-prediction" strategy, even in fundamentally OOD tasks.

4. **Computational Accuracy Remains a Bottleneck for RLVR:**
 - While RLVR models excel in learning the correct reasoning strategy (planning), their "execution" scores (correctness of mathematical computation and code simulation) still show a sharp decrease on complex OOD tasks (`multi_r`, `while`).
 - This suggests that even with a strong reasoning strategy, computational errors can still limit overall performance, highlighting an asynchronism between learning causal reasoning skills and general computational skills.

In summary, the research reveals a significant untapped potential for LLMs in true counterfactual reasoning, demonstrating that while current models struggle, RL-based training, particularly with scalable data generation like the proposed executable counterfactuals framework, offers a promising path towards achieving more robust and generalizable causal understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this paper carry significant implications for the development, evaluation, and application of Large Language Models, particularly in domains requiring sophisticated reasoning.

1. **Rethinking LLM Causal Reasoning Evaluations:** The most immediate and profound impact is on how the research community evaluates LLMs' causal understanding. By rigorously demonstrating the gap between interventional and true counterfactual reasoning, the paper forces a re-evaluation of existing benchmarks and calls for the widespread adoption of frameworks that explicitly require the abduction step. This more accurate assessment will help avoid overestimating LLM capabilities and guide research towards addressing genuine limitations.

2. **Advancing LLMs for High-Stakes Applications:** Counterfactual reasoning is crucial for decision-making in critical fields like medicine, policy-making, and scientific research. By providing a scalable and verifiable framework for training and evaluating this skill, the paper lays the groundwork for developing more reliable and trustworthy LLMs that can assist humans in complex "what-if" scenarios, potentially leading to better diagnoses, more effective policies, and accelerated scientific discovery.

3. **Guiding Future LLM Training Paradigms:** The comparative analysis of SFT and RLVR offers crucial insights into effective training strategies for complex cognitive skills. The demonstrated failure of SFT to generalize counterfactual reasoning beyond in-domain tasks, contrasting sharply with the robust generalization achieved by RLVR, challenges the prevailing reliance on SFT for advanced reasoning. This finding suggests a stronger emphasis on reinforcement learning approaches, especially those utilizing verifiable, outcome-based rewards, for instilling deep, transferable reasoning abilities in LLMs. It points towards a future where RL might play a more central role in developing general-purpose reasoning agents.

4. **Unlocking Scalable Data Generation for Reasoning:** The "executable counterfactuals" framework, leveraging template-based code and math problem generation, provides a blueprint for creating virtually unlimited, high-quality, and verifiable synthetic data. This is critical for scaling up training data for complex reasoning tasks, which are often costly and difficult to annotate manually. This capability can catalyze advancements not just in counterfactual reasoning but potentially other forms of complex reasoning that can be operationalized through code or structured environments.

5. **Fine-Grained Diagnosis of LLM Failures:** The behavioral analysis, decoupling "planning" from "execution," offers a powerful diagnostic tool. Identifying that LLMs primarily struggle with the abduction step in planning, but RL can learn planning while still being bottlenecked by execution, provides actionable insights for future research. This suggests that future efforts might need to focus not only on improving reasoning strategies but also on enhancing the models' fundamental computational accuracy and faithfulness to detailed instructions.

In essence, this research provides both a diagnostic tool that reveals a fundamental limitation of current LLMs in causal reasoning and a promising pathway—reinforcement learning with scalable, executable data—to overcome it. Its impact will likely be seen in the design of more sophisticated LLM architectures, more effective training methodologies, and ultimately, more capable and trustworthy AI systems.
