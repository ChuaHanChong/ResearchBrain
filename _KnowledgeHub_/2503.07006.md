# HELM: Human-Preferred Exploration with Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> HELM presents a framework that enables robots to incorporate human exploration preferences, expressed in natural language, directly into their autonomous navigation strategies by leveraging large language models. The system demonstrates competitive exploration efficiency compared to traditional methods while effectively adapting to user-specified directional preferences and generalizing to unseen environments without requiring retraining.

## Problem

- Traditional autonomous exploration methods rely on fixed algorithms and predefined parameters, making them difficult to adapt to specific human preferences.
- Existing learning-based methods for robot exploration are constrained by their training data and struggle to adapt to new, unencountered preferences or environments.
- A lack of intuitive interfaces prevents non-experts from easily incorporating real-time human preferences into robot exploration policies.

## Method

- **HELM** integrates ==Large Language Models (LLMs)== into an autonomous exploration system to allow robots to interpret and act on human preferences expressed in natural language.
- The framework converts robot sensor data into a structured graph representation of the environment, which is then described textually for the LLM.
- It combines this textual environment description with human preferences and task context into a 'mixed observation' prompt, enabling the LLM to make sequential planning decisions for the robot.

## Results

- HELM achieves exploration efficiency comparable to state-of-the-art traditional methods like TARE and ARiADNE in both simulated dungeons and high-fidelity Gazebo environments.
- The framework demonstrates adaptability to different environments without requiring retraining, showing strong generalization capabilities.
- HELM effectively incorporates human-expressed directional preferences (e.g., 'prioritize upper-left corner') into its path planning, aligning exploration with user intent.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating large language models (LLMs) with robot sensor data allows for intuitive human preference integration into exploration strategies.
> - Converting environment belief states and human preferences into textual prompts enables LLMs to perform sequential decision-making for robot navigation.
> - The framework's training-free nature allows it to adapt to new environments and varying human preferences without retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Autonomous exploration is a fundamental challenge in robotics where agents must efficiently navigate and map unknown environments. Traditional approaches typically employ fixed algorithms with predefined parameters, making it difficult to adapt to specific human preferences without extensive parameter tuning or retraining.

HELM (Human-Preferred Exploration with Language Models) presents a novel framework that allows robots to incorporate human preferences expressed in natural language into their exploration strategies. This approach bridges the gap between human intent and robot policy by leveraging the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs).

### 2. Background and Limitations of Current Approaches

Autonomous exploration methods have evolved along several tracks:

1. **Traditional Methods**:
 - Frontier-based planners that identify and move toward unexplored regions
 - Sampling-based methods that use randomized sampling to generate exploration paths
 - Information-theoretic approaches that maximize information gain

2. **Learning-Based Methods**:
 - Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques using convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
 - Graph neural networks (GNNs) for structured environment representation
 - Attention mechanisms for more efficient feature extraction

Despite these advances, existing methods face significant limitations:

- Traditional planners use fixed hyperparameters that require manual tuning for different scenarios
- Learning-based methods are constrained by their training data and struggle to adapt to new preferences
- Both approaches lack an intuitive interface for incorporating human preferences in real-time

### 3. The HELM Framework

HELM addresses these limitations by integrating LLMs with traditional autonomous exploration systems. The framework consists of four main components:

1. **Robot Belief Construction**: Converting sensor data into a structured graph representation
2. **Mixed Observation with Prompts**: Combining graph descriptions with human preferences
3. **LLM-Based Planning**: Using LLMs for sequential decision-making
4. **Closed-Loop Control**: Executing chosen paths and updating the belief state

The key innovation of HELM is its ability to integrate human preferences expressed in natural language, allowing users to specify exploration priorities without technical expertise. For example, users can instruct the robot to "prioritize exploring the upper-left corner of the map" or "focus on narrow corridors first."

### 4. Robot Belief Construction

The robot constructs a belief of the environment through these steps:

1. **Occupancy Grid Generation**: Raw sensor data (typically LiDAR) is processed to create an occupancy grid map distinguishing free, occupied, and unknown spaces.

2. **Graph Construction**: A collision-free graph is generated from the occupancy grid:
 ```
   G = (V, E)
   ```
 where V represents nodes (potential waypoints) and E represents edges (collision-free paths).

3. **Graph Sparsification**: The initial dense graph is sparsified to optimize its structure while maintaining connectivity:
 ```
   G_sparse = Sparsify(G)
   ```

4. **Utility Computation**: Each node is assigned a utility value based on the number of observable frontiers:
 ```
   u(v) = number of frontiers observable from node v
   ```

### 5. Mixed Observation with Prompts

The mixed observation component combines the robot's belief state with human preferences:

1. **Graph Describer**: Converts the sparse graph into a textual description:
 ```
   Currently, a robot is at coordinates [x, y].
   Here is the sparse graph information for better decision making:
   Nodes in sparse graph: node 0: Coordinates: (x0, y0), ...
   Edges in sparse graph: ((x0, y0), (x1, y1)), ...
   ```

2. **Task Prompts Questioner**: Refines the graph description with:
 - Task-specific context
 - Human preferences (e.g., "prioritize exploring the upper-left corner")
 - Intermediate memory of past decisions

3. **Mixed Observation**: The final input to the LLM planner contains:
 - Graph descriptions (environment understanding)
 - Preference statements (human guidance)
 - Task memory (recent history)
 - Output specification (format instructions)

The Task Prompts Questioner can be implemented using either manual template-based construction or by bootstrapping an LLM agent to process human preferences.

### 6. Sequential Decision-Making

HELM's sequential decision-making process leverages the powerful reasoning capabilities of LLMs:

1. The LLM planner receives the mixed observation as input
2. Based on this input, it makes decisions on the next waypoint
3. The decision is formatted as coordinates `[x, y]` representing the next node to visit
4. A connected shortest path is computed to reach the selected waypoint
5. The path is executed, and the process repeats in a closed-loop manner

This approach aligns well with the sequential nature of autonomous exploration, where decisions are made incrementally as the robot uncovers more of the environment.

### 7. Experimental Results

HELM was evaluated in both simulated dungeons and high-fidelity Gazebo environments. The results demonstrate that HELM achieves:

1. **Competitive Exploration Efficiency**: HELM shows exploration efficiency comparable to state-of-the-art traditional methods like TARE (Topology-Aware Receding-horizon Exploration).

2. **Adaptability to Different Environments**: The framework generalizes well to unseen environments without requiring retraining, demonstrating its training-free nature.

3. **Path Optimization**: HELM produces efficient exploration paths with minimal redundancy, as shown in comparison with other methods.

### 8. Preference Experiments

A key advantage of HELM is its ability to incorporate human preferences. Experiments demonstrate that HELM effectively accommodates different exploration preferences expressed in natural language:

1. **Directional Preferences**: HELM can prioritize exploration in specified regions (left, right, center) based on user instructions.

2. **Preference-Based Path Planning**: When given specific preferences like "prioritize exploring the upper-left corner," HELM generates paths that align with these preferences while maintaining exploration efficiency.

3. **Adaptive Behavior**: The framework adapts in real-time to changing preferences without requiring parameter adjustments or retraining.

### 9. Significance and Applications

HELM's integration of LLMs for human-preferred exploration has several significant implications:

1. **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction**: By allowing users to express preferences in natural language, HELM makes robotic systems more accessible to non-experts and more adaptable to specific user needs.

2. **Training-Free Adaptation**: Unlike learning-based methods that require expensive retraining for new scenarios, HELM can adapt to different preferences and environments without additional training.

3. **Broad Applicability**: The framework has applications in various domains:
 - Search and rescue operations where specific regions may need prioritization
 - Environmental monitoring where certain areas may be of greater interest
 - Service robotics where user preferences can vary significantly

4. **Computational Efficiency**: By leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, HELM achieves sophisticated behavior without requiring specialized training for exploration tasks.

### 10. Conclusion

HELM represents a significant advancement in autonomous exploration by bridging the gap between human preferences and robot behavior through natural language. The framework achieves competitive exploration efficiency while providing unprecedented flexibility in accommodating user preferences.

The key contributions of HELM include:

1. A novel framework that integrates LLMs with autonomous exploration systems
2. A method for converting robot belief states and human preferences into textual prompts
3. An LLM-based planning approach that enables preference-guided exploration
4. Empirical validation showing competitive performance and effective preference integration

HELM's approach opens new possibilities for human-robot interaction in exploration tasks, making autonomous systems more intuitive to control and more aligned with human intentions. Future work could extend this framework to multi-robot systems and more complex semantic understanding of environments.
