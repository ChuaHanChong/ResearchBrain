# X-WBC: A Cross-Embodiment Foundation Model for Humanoid Whole-Body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> X-WBC is a cross-embodiment foundation framework for humanoid whole-body control. It encodes full human motion, retargeted robot reference motion, and sparse five-point VR motion into one aligned command-token space, feeds them to a shared causal Transformer trained on mixed multi-robot PPO rollouts, and decodes actions through lightweight robot-specific modules. Joint training over nine humanoids improves tracking and deploys on four real robots.

## Problem

- Humanoid whole-body control still trains one policy per robot, so retargeted motion experience stays isolated inside each morphology instead of accumulating across platforms.
- Cross-embodiment foundation models share heterogeneous robot data, but target manipulation and vision-language-action settings, leaving closed-loop balance-critical whole-body control largely unexplored.
- Tracking policies typically use single-step MLPs over current state and reference, which lack the temporal context needed to resolve motion phase across differing bodies.

## Method

- ==Human-centered command encoding== maps full human motion, robot reference motion, and sparse VR keypoints into one token space, with a pairwise ==MSE== alignment loss added to the ==PPO== objective.
- A shared causal **Transformer** backbone over 32-step observation-token histories learns reusable temporal motion structure, using a ==KV cache== at rollout so each control step processes only the new token.
- Multi-robot joint training in ==Isaac Lab== mixes padded transitions from all embodiments into one PPO batch, with robot-ID-routed proprioception encoders and action decoders plus failure-rate adaptive motion sampling.

## Results

- Joint training reaches **98.60%** success on Unitree G1 and **93.22%** on H2, versus **97.69%** and **92.22%** for per-robot policies and **97.70%**/**91.33%** for an MLP backbone.
- On the 800-clip 100STYLE external grid, the frozen policy scores **91.13%** success with sparse VR commands, near SONIC's **93.00%** and far above TWIST's **75.62%**.
- Ablations drop success on both robots when either dense route, the alignment loss, or robot-specific modules are removed; token retrieval hits **94.2%** same-motion Recall@1 cross-source versus **1.04%** random.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating shared motion semantics from embodiment-specific execution lets heterogeneous robots act as joint data sources rather than isolated training silos.
> - Retargeting one human motion across many bodies makes robot diversity a supervision axis complementary to motion diversity for a shared backbone.
> - Gains concentrate on harder embodiments; also read tracking error alongside success rate, since early termination censors per-step error and flatters fragile policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid whole-body control (WBC) concerns the coordinated control of the entire robot, including the torso, arms, legs, head, and end effectors. Recent systems have increasingly moved away from manually designed behaviors toward motion-tracking policies trained from human motion, teleoperation, and simulation. Systems such as H2O, OmniH2O, HumanPlus, BeyondMimic, and SONIC use human motion or human-operated demonstrations to support teleoperation, imitation, locomotion, and whole-body skill learning.

A limitation of much of this work is that training is organized around one robot embodiment. Motion data may be collected from large human datasets such as AMASS, LAFAN1, and BONES-SEED, but it is generally retargeted to one humanoid morphology and used to train a policy dedicated to that platform. Consequently, experience gathered for one robot does not directly improve the policy of another robot with different dimensions, joint layouts, actuators, or observation spaces.

X-WBC connects humanoid WBC with the broader development of cross-embodiment robot foundation models. In manipulation and general robot learning, models such as RT-2, OpenVLA, π0, GR00T, and related systems train shared representations using data from heterogeneous robots. Their architectures typically combine a common backbone with embodiment-specific interfaces for observations and actions. X-WBC applies a similar principle to closed-loop humanoid control, where the policy must also maintain balance, handle physical contacts, follow temporal motion patterns, and produce joint-level actions at high frequency.

The paper’s specific contribution is to treat multiple humanoid robots as joint sources of training experience. It proposes that human motion contains relatively body-independent semantics—such as the intent and temporal organization of walking, kicking, squatting, and reaching—while the conversion of those semantics into executable robot motion remains embodiment-dependent. X-WBC therefore separates shared motion representation from robot-specific execution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether a shared representation of motion can support whole-body control across humanoid robots with substantially different morphologies. The authors ask whether motion experience from one robot can improve control on other robots rather than remaining isolated in separate policies.

This objective is motivated by three related problems.

First, humanoid platforms differ in height, mass, limb proportions, degrees of freedom, joint limits, actuator capabilities, body naming conventions, and sensing configurations. A policy that directly maps robot-specific proprioception and commands to joint actions is therefore difficult to transfer without modification.

Second, human motion data, robot-retargeted trajectories, and teleoperation signals represent related information in different formats. Full human motion contains dense semantic information but is not directly available during many deployments. Robot reference motion is executable but tied to a particular body. Sparse virtual-reality (VR) observations are practical for real-time control but provide only a small subset of the information available in full motion capture. The authors aim to align these sources so that they can be interpreted consistently by one shared controller.

Third, whole-body motion is temporally structured. A current pose alone may not reveal motion phase, intended velocity, or whether a robot is beginning, continuing, or ending an action. The proposed controller therefore needs temporal memory rather than only a single-step mapping.

The paper’s specific goals are to:

1. Train one shared control backbone jointly across multiple humanoid robots.
2. Encode full human motion, retargeted robot motion, and sparse VR observations into a common command space.
3. Retain robot-specific processing where morphology and action execution require it.
4. Evaluate whether joint training improves tracking relative to single-robot training.
5. Test whether the learned system works on motion outside the training corpus and on physical robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Multi-robot control formulation

Each robot is modeled as a separate Markov decision process with its own state space, action space, and transition dynamics. The reward family and discount factor are shared. Training uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), with rollouts from all robots mixed into a common training batch.

The actor receives robot proprioception and one motion command. Proprioceptive inputs include joint positions and velocities, base angular velocity, gravity projected into the body frame, and the previous action. The critic uses the actor inputs together with privileged simulation information, including body-link poses and base linear velocity. This asymmetric actor-critic design allows value estimation to use information unavailable to the deployed policy.

The action output is converted to torques through a low-level proportional-derivative controller. Because robots have different action dimensions, their state and action vectors are padded to shared maximum dimensions during training. At inference, each robot-specific decoder selects only the valid dimensions.

The reward contains tracking terms for root position and orientation, relative body position and orientation, and linear and angular body velocity. Penalties discourage abrupt action changes, joint-limit violations, and undesired contacts.

#### 3.2. Human-centered command tokens

X-WBC uses three command routes:

- **Full-human motion:** A shared encoder maps dense motion-capture information into a human-motion token. The input describes 22 joints, with position, six-dimensional orientation, and linear velocity features.
- **Robot motion:** A robot-specific encoder maps reference trajectories expressed in a particular robot’s body coordinates into a robot-motion token. These trajectories are generally generated by human-to-robot retargeting, although the formulation can also accept robot recordings or generated trajectories.
- **Sparse VR motion:** A shared encoder maps five tracked human points—the head and four limb endpoints—into a sparse-VR token. The same format is used during physical deployment.

Motion commands are represented in root-centered or robot-centered coordinates. The representation uses positions, orientations, and velocities rather than only joint angles, allowing the policy to capture spatial relationships and dynamic motion information. During training, the three command tokens associated with the same motion clip are normalized and encouraged to be close through a pairwise mean-squared-error alignment loss.

#### 3.3. Shared temporal backbone and embodiment-specific modules

The shared policy backbone is a causal Transformer. At each control step, the selected command token is combined with a robot-specific proprioception token to form an observation token. The Transformer processes a causal history window of 32 steps and produces an action token.

The temporal backbone is shared across all robots, while several interface components remain robot-specific. Robot-specific proprioception encoders map different state dimensions and joint orderings into a fixed token size. Robot-specific action decoders map the shared action token into valid joint commands for each platform. Robot-specific command encoders process robot-reference trajectories. Robot identity determines which interfaces are used.

The Transformer operates at 50 Hz. During online execution, a key-value cache stores previous attention states, avoiding recomputation of the entire history window at each step.

#### 3.4. Joint training and robustness measures

Training is conducted in Isaac Lab using 1,024 parallel environments per robot on eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Rollouts from all active robots are mixed into one PPO batch. For each motion clip, the three command routes are sampled with equal probability, and one route is used for the episode.

The training objective combines the PPO objective with the command-token alignment loss. Adaptive motion sampling increases the probability of segments that frequently cause early termination. Segments that remain consistently infeasible may be removed, particularly when failures are attributed to poor retargeting or physically impossible motion.

The authors also apply domain randomization to friction, restitution, default joint positions, center-of-mass offsets, and root velocity perturbations. These measures are intended to improve robustness to simulation-to-reality discrepancies and external disturbances.

The system is trained on approximately 200 hours of BONES-SEED motion resampled at 50 Hz. The simulation study includes nine embodiments: Unitree G1 variants, H1, H1-2, R1, H2, Fourier GR3, Booster T1, and Adam Lite.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Joint training and architecture

On BONES-SEED training motions, the full X-WBC model achieves a success rate of **98.60% on Unitree G1** and **93.22% on Unitree H2** when using the VR command route. Replacing the Transformer with an MLP reduces success to **97.70% on G1** and **91.33% on H2**. A Transformer trained separately for each robot reaches **97.69% on G1** and **92.22% on H2**.

The full model also has the lowest reported body-velocity error among these three settings on both robots. The improvement is more pronounced on H2, which is a taller and heavier embodiment than G1. This result is consistent with the hypothesis that shared training provides useful supervision for a more difficult robot, although the evaluation is primarily on motions from the training distribution.

#### 4.2. Consistency across command sources

The three command routes produce similar results on the evaluated training motions. For G1, success rates are **98.21% using robot commands**, **98.66% using full-human commands**, and **98.60% using sparse VR commands**. For H2, the corresponding rates are **92.08%, 93.02%, and 93.22%**.

Full-human commands provide slightly lower mean per-keypoint and velocity errors in several comparisons, while robot commands produce lower root-rotation error because they are already expressed in the target robot’s reference space. The relatively small performance gap for sparse VR is relevant because VR uses only five tracked points rather than dense full-body motion.

Ablation experiments show that removing either dense command route, removing the alignment loss, or removing robot-specific modules lowers success rates. The reductions are particularly visible on H2. The results indicate that the command routes provide complementary supervision and that the robot-specific interfaces remain useful, even though proprioception alone contains some embodiment information.

#### 4.3. External-motion evaluation

The authors evaluate the frozen G1 policy on 800 clips constructed from the 100STYLE dataset, which covers 100 locomotion styles and eight movement categories. X-WBC uses its sparse-VR interface, while SONIC and TWIST retain their native inputs and low-level controllers.

X-WBC achieves a **91.13% success rate**, completing **91.66%** of clips under the reported completion measure. SONIC reaches **93.00% success** and **93.35% completion**, while TWIST reaches **75.62% success** and **78.18% completion**. X-WBC has a mean per-keypoint position error of **0.0649 meters** and velocity error of **0.5400 m/s**. SONIC has lower velocity error at **0.3419 m/s**, whereas TWIST has lower position error at **0.0565 meters** but substantially lower rollout survival.

The comparison suggests that X-WBC remains competitive on an external motion corpus, but the systems use different input modalities and control pipelines. Therefore, the results do not isolate the effect of cross-embodiment training from differences in input representation and low-level control.

#### 4.4. Embodiment coverage and token analysis

Across all nine simulated robots, the shared policy achieves usable tracking performance, although success rates and position errors vary by platform. The authors report that these metrics do not always rank robots in the same order: a robot may survive more clips while exhibiting larger errors during active portions of the rollout.

Token retrieval experiments examine whether tokens corresponding to the same motion can be matched across command sources and robots. Cross-source Recall@1 is **86.0% for the same motion phase** and **94.2% for the same motion**. Cross-robot retrieval reaches **61.3% and 76.1%**, respectively, above random baselines of **0.17% and 1.04%**. The lower cross-robot recall indicates that the tokens retain some embodiment-specific execution information in addition to shared motion identity.

#### 4.5. Physical deployment

The model is deployed on Unitree G1, R1, H1-2, and H2 robots using the same sparse-VR command format and shared policy architecture. Demonstrations include upper-body pose control, soccer kicking, and running on grass. These experiments show that one command interface can be used across robots of different heights and joint counts, while robot-specific branches adapt the commands to each platform.

The hardware evidence is primarily qualitative. The paper does not provide extensive quantitative comparisons across manufacturers, long-duration tests, or systematic evaluations under varied terrain, sensing noise, delays, or repeated disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

X-WBC provides a framework for accumulating humanoid whole-body control experience across robot platforms. Its main conceptual contribution is the separation of motion semantics from physical execution. If this division scales, organizations could use heterogeneous humanoids as complementary data sources rather than maintaining independent training pipelines.

The aligned command space also has practical implications for teleoperation. Dense human motion can support training, while sparse VR input can provide a lower-bandwidth deployment interface. A shared controller could therefore connect motion-capture datasets, retargeted robot trajectories, and real-time human control.

However, the system is not embodiment-independent in a complete sense. It still requires robot-specific command encoders, proprioception encoders, action decoders, control logic, and motion-retargeting procedures. The current evaluation is also concentrated on humanoid robots, mostly in simulation, with physical demonstrations on four Unitree platforms. Transfer to unseen robots, other manufacturers, complex contacts, and extended manipulation tasks remains unresolved.

Overall, the paper supports cross-embodiment joint training as a viable direction for humanoid WBC. Its results indicate measurable benefits from shared training and aligned representations, while also showing that robot-specific interfaces remain necessary for reliable execution under current methods.
