# Integration of the TIAGo Robot into Isaac Sim with Mecanum Drive Modeling and Learned S-Curve Velocity Profiles

> [!summary] Summary
> TIAGo++ Omni, a dual-arm mecanum-wheeled mobile manipulator, is integrated into Isaac Sim with two omnidirectional drive controllers: a physically accurate one with procedurally generated wheels and six-sphere roller colliders, and a lightweight one setting base velocity directly. A small MLP predicts custom five-parameter S-curve acceleration profiles calibrated from brief real trajectory recordings, giving under 9% mean relative trajectory error.

## Problem

- TIAGo is simulated mainly in Gazebo, whose limited physics accuracy and graphical realism hinder precise dynamics modeling and environment perception for learning-based robotics.
- No Isaac Sim model existed for TIAGo; its Gazebo description is incompatible, and mecanum wheels demand costly roller contact physics that Gazebo plugins usually approximate away.
- The manufacturer's base controller is a black box with unknown command-to-wheel-acceleration mapping, and tuning Isaac Sim PID gains fails to reproduce the real S-shaped velocity ramps.

## Method

- Physically accurate drive: procedurally generated ==mecanum wheels== with 15 rollers at 45 degrees, each roller collider approximated by six spheres, plus a ==holonomic== Twist-to-wheel-velocity matrix controller.
- **Learned S-curve calibration**: an ==MLP== with layers [1,35,15,5] maps wheel velocity change to five parameters of a custom softplus-ramp / linear / flipped-ramp curve with enforced continuity.
- Lightweight controller: sets base velocity directly through the ==Isaac Sim== API using the same ==S-curve interpolation==, skipping roller collisions and wheel-ground friction entirely.

## Results

- Mean relative distance error against VIVE-tracked real trajectories: **8.24%** in x, **4.61%** in y, **5.68%** diagonal, **4.30%** rotation; the lightweight model is comparable or better throughout.
- Diagonal x-y commands, held out from training, still reached **5.68%** error, showing the single-input velocity-change model generalizes to combined motions.
- The lightweight controller cuts required physics steps from **360** to **60** per second, raising simulation frame rate while preserving trajectory accuracy on square and circular paths.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a low-level acceleration profile, rather than full closed-loop system identification, keeps the calibration compatible with non-differentiable physics engines and easy to plug into simulator APIs.
> - Custom asymmetric S-curves beat standard logistic fits because real velocity ramps rise sharply, transition linearly, then approach the target asymptotically.
> - Non-proportional wheel acceleration in a vendor controller makes real behavior hard to replicate; a slightly harder-to-control simulation is acceptable and may aid robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics research and development increasingly relies on high-fidelity physics simulations to train, evaluate, and refine robotic systems. This reliance has grown with the advent of GPU-accelerated simulation frameworks, such as NVIDIA’s Isaac Sim, which enable faster-than-real-time simulations and generate large datasets critical for learning-based methods, particularly in reinforcement learning (RL). Traditional simulators like Gazebo, while widely used, often present limitations in physics accuracy and graphical realism, which can hinder the development of sophisticated perception and control systems.

Mobile manipulators, combining mobility with manipulation capabilities, are a key area of robotics, with applications spanning logistics, service robotics, and healthcare. The PAL Robotics TIAGo robot is a general-purpose mobile manipulator frequently utilized in the research community, largely due to its versatility and integration with the Robot Operating System (ROS) framework. However, a significant gap existed as a model for the TIAGo robot was not readily available in advanced, GPU-acceleraccelerated simulation environments like Isaac Sim. Existing TIAGo models primarily resided in Gazebo.

This work addresses the challenge of bridging the gap between traditional simulation environments and modern GPU-accelerated platforms for a prominent robotic system. It specifically tackles the complexities associated with accurately simulating omnidirectional mecanum wheel drives, which are crucial for the TIAGo++ Omni variant. Previous efforts in modeling omnidirectional platforms have predominantly used other simulation tools (e.g., Matlab-Simulink, RecurDyn, Gazebo) or have not focused on the TIAGo robot within Isaac Sim. Furthermore, while real-to-sim approaches and data-driven simulation models exist for optimizing simulation parameters and accounting for dynamic effects, many do not directly address the non-differentiable nature of common physics engines like Isaac Sim. The presented work also contributes to the less explored area of modeling robot-specific velocity profiles, particularly S-shaped acceleration curves, using learning-based methods applied to limited real-world data, distinct from system identification for closed-loop control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to integrate the PAL Robotics TIAGo++ Omni mobile manipulator into NVIDIA Isaac Sim, thereby making a high-fidelity, GPU-accelerated simulation model available to the broader research community. This integration aims to facilitate advanced research in mobile manipulation, reinforcement learning (RL), and perception, leveraging the computational efficiencies and realism offered by Isaac Sim.

The motivation for this work arises from several factors:

1. **Limitations of Existing Simulation Platforms:** While the TIAGo robot has a Gazebo model, Gazebo's limitations in physics accuracy and graphical realism pose challenges for applications demanding precise dynamic modeling and realistic environmental perception. Modern machine learning and reinforcement learning applications often require massively parallel, faster-than-real-time simulations, which Gazebo may not adequately support.
2. **Advantages of Isaac Sim:** Isaac Sim offers significant computational efficiency through GPU-based parallelization, making it suitable for large-scale data generation and RL training. Its compatibility with modern machine learning pipelines, including frameworks like Isaac Lab, further enhances its utility for state-of-the-art algorithms.
3. **Challenges of TIAGo Integration:** Integrating the TIAGo++ Omni into Isaac Sim presented several specific challenges:
 - **Incompatibility of Existing Models:** The Gazebo URDF model for TIAGo is not directly compatible with Isaac Sim.
 - **Complex Mecanum Wheel Dynamics:** Accurately simulating the robot's omnidirectional base requires precise modeling of mecanum wheels, which involves computationally intensive physics calculations.
 - **Black-Box Controller Behavior:** Replicating the TIAGo's internal mecanum wheel controller in simulation is difficult due to a lack of access to its source code, making it challenging to predict wheel acceleration profiles. Manual tuning of PID controllers in Isaac Sim was found to be insufficient to accurately reflect the real robot's behavior, which exhibits distinct S-shaped velocity curves.
4. **Enabling Advanced Robotics Research:** By providing an accurate and efficient simulation model, the authors aim to empower researchers to experiment with the TIAGo robot and perform efficient learning-based control in diverse virtual environments, minimizing risks and costs associated with real-world robot deployment. The lack of a publicly available Isaac Sim model for a dual-arm omnidirectional robot further underscores the utility of this contribution.

To address these motivations and challenges, the paper outlines three specific contributions:

- The development of a physically accurate model of the TIAGo++ Omni, including a high-fidelity simulation of its mecanum wheels for omnidirectional driving.
- The creation of a lightweight velocity-based control model as an alternative, designed to approximate the real robot’s motion while reducing computational overhead for learning-based applications.
- A neural network-based calibration approach to align both control models with the real robot’s S-shaped velocity profile, utilizing minimal real-world movement data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involved the comprehensive integration of the TIAGo++ Omni robot into Isaac Sim, with a particular focus on accurately simulating its omnidirectional mecanum wheel drive. This entailed both physical modeling and a learning-based approach to replicate the robot's specific motion characteristics.

#### 3.1. Robot and Initial Integration

The TIAGo++ Omni is characterized as a mobile manipulator equipped with two gripper-enabled arms, an omnidirectional mecanum wheel drive, and various sensors (RGB-D camera, LiDAR). Initial integration of non-wheel components, including the torso, arms, fingers, and head, was achieved using the Isaac Sim URDF importer. Joint control and sensor data handling (LiDAR, cameras) were managed via ROS 2 and the Isaac Sim API, allowing for the transmission of joint state messages and publication of sensor data.

#### 3.2. Integration of the Omnidirectional Drive

This constituted the main technical challenge and was addressed through two distinct controller models:

**A. Physically Accurate Model (Full Physical Simulation):**
This approach aims to replicate the physical dynamics of the mecanum wheels accurately, which are not directly supported by default URDF imports in Isaac Sim. The process involved three steps:

1. **Wheel Modeling:** The original URDF wheels were replaced with custom mecanum wheels. These custom wheels, generated using a specific script, feature 15 free-spinning rollers angled at 45 degrees, scaled to match the real robot. Emphasized importance was placed on proper roller alignment and a precise circular wheel shape to avoid ground contact issues.
2. **Roller Collider Modeling:** Each roller collider was modeled using six spheres. This design choice was intended to ensure smooth motion in Isaac Sim while being computationally more efficient than mesh-based or low-polygon collision models.
3. **Holonomic Controller Implementation:** To achieve omnidirectional control, the angular velocities (ω_i) of the four wheels were calculated from desired linear (v_x, v_y) and angular (v_θ) velocities using a standard kinematic formulation for mecanum wheels (Equation 1). However, this kinematic model alone did not capture the S-shaped acceleration profiles observed in the real robot's "black-box" controller.

**B. Lightweight Velocity-Based Model (Direct Base Velocity Setting):**
As an alternative, a computationally less intensive method was developed. This model bypasses the detailed physical simulation of wheel-ground interactions. Instead, it directly sets the robot’s base velocity according to the desired Twist command using the Isaac Sim API. While still incorporating the learned S-shaped velocity profile for smooth acceleration, it does not explicitly control individual wheel velocities. This method simplifies the simulation by treating the wheels as dummy components and eliminates the need for complex roller collision computations or tuning of wheel joint physics parameters.

#### 3.3. Predicting the Wheel Velocity Profile from Data (Learning-Based Calibration)

To bridge the gap between kinematic control and the real robot's observed S-shaped acceleration, a neural network-based calibration approach was implemented:

1. **Data Collection:** Real-world wheel velocity profiles were recorded for various open-loop movements: x-direction, y-direction, and rotational motion around the z-axis. Each command was repeated three times and averaged to obtain smooth data. This data represented transitions starting from zero velocity to a target velocity.
2. **S-Curve Model:** A custom three-segment S-curve function, S_Θ(t), was designed to mathematically model the observed velocity profiles. This function is parameterized by five variables, Θ = (a, b, m, k_1, k_2), controlling initial ramp, linear transition, and final ramp characteristics (Appendix A). Continuity constraints were enforced between the segments.
3. **Neural Network Training:** A small Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with layers sized and softplus activation functions was trained. The network takes the total wheel velocity change (∆ω) as input and outputs the five parameters (Θ) for the S-curve. The Adam optimizer was used, and 100 instances with different random initializations were trained to select the best-performing model based on validation error.
4. **Runtime Integration:** During simulation, to achieve a smooth transition between a current velocity T and a desired velocity T', the neural network predicts S-curve parameters based on the calculated total velocity change (∆ω). This S-curve then generates a fractional progress parameter (p_t) that interpolates the Twist commands at each timestep (Equation 2). The wheel controllers then receive target velocities derived from this smoothly updated Twist command.

#### 3.4. Experimental Validation

Experiments were conducted to compare the simulated robot's behavior against the real robot. This involved recording multiple open-loop trajectories (without closed-loop feedback) for both simulated models and the physical robot. Motion tracking for the real robot was performed using OpenVR with a VIVE tracker and two VIVE lighthouses to capture its pose (position and orientation). The simulated and real robot's odometry data (total traveled distances for linear motion, total rotation angles for rotational motion) were compared. Training data for the S-curve model consisted of 20 different target velocities (0.05 to 1.00 m/s for linear, 0.05 to 1.5 rad/s for rotational). Diagonal (x-y) movements were explicitly excluded from training data to assess generalization capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research successfully integrated the PAL Robotics TIAGo++ Omni into Isaac Sim, providing both a physically accurate and a lightweight velocity-based simulation model. The models incorporate a learning-based calibration approach to approximate the real robot's S-shaped velocity profiles.

**A. Model Fidelity and Accuracy:**

- **S-curve Approximation:** The neural network-based approach demonstrated the ability to approximate the S-shaped velocity profiles observed in the real robot's basic x-direction, y-direction, and rotational movements (Figure 5). This learning-based calibration provides a more realistic acceleration behavior than a direct kinematic application of velocity commands.
- **Controller Inconsistencies:** A notable finding was that the real robot's internal controller does not always enforce proportional acceleration across all wheels, particularly for combined x- and y-directional movements (e.g., diagonal motion). This non-proportional acceleration leads to deviations between the predicted S-curves and the real robot's behavior for such complex movements (Figure 6). Consequently, the simulated robot may exhibit slight deviations from the intended direction during acceleration compared to the real robot.
- **Trajectory Tracking Performance:**
 - For basic 4-second open-loop velocity commands, both simulated models generally followed the intended paths of the real robot's controller (Table I).
 - The **physically accurate model** (referred to as "Value") exhibited Mean Relative Errors (MRE) ranging from 4.30% (Rotation) to 8.24% (x-direction).
 - The **lightweight velocity-based model** (referred to as "Value (Light)") generally showed slightly lower MREs, ranging from 2.97% (Rotation) to 7.36% (x-direction).
 - For diagonal (x-y) movements, which were not part of the training data for the S-curve model, the errors were 5.68% (Physically Accurate) and 5.16% (Lightweight), indicating reasonable generalization.
- **Accumulated Errors:** For longer and more complex trajectories, accumulated errors became more significant. For instance, in a square trajectory, the simulated robot navigated corners more smoothly than the real robot (Figure 7a), suggesting that the simulated robot is slightly harder to control in these scenarios due to a slower acceleration response. Despite this, the S-curve approximation was deemed sufficiently accurate for pure movements.

**B. Simulation Efficiency:**

- The lightweight velocity-based model significantly improved simulation efficiency. It reduced the required physics steps per second from 360 (for the physically accurate model) to 60. This led to a noticeable reduction in computational cost and higher simulation frame rates (FPS) in practice, making it particularly suitable for high-throughput tasks like reinforcement learning.

**C. Generalizability and Robustness:**

- The lightweight model was noted for its generalizability to any holonomic robot, regardless of the real PID controller's implementation, as it avoids detailed wheel dynamics.
- The authors argued that the simulated robot being slightly harder to control than the real robot might not be a major concern for two reasons: it could introduce artificial noise, potentially improving robustness in sim-to-real transfer, and a higher-level closed-loop trajectory controller should still be able to utilize the open-loop movement controller effectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes a publicly available, high-fidelity integration of the PAL Robotics TIAGo++ Omni mobile manipulator into NVIDIA Isaac Sim. This contribution addresses a notable gap in the availability of advanced simulation models for a widely used research robot.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enabling Advanced Robotics Research:** By making the TIAGo++ Omni accessible within Isaac Sim, the work facilitates research in areas such as mobile manipulation, reinforcement learning (RL), and perception. Researchers can leverage Isaac Sim's GPU-accelerated capabilities for faster training, larger-scale data generation, and more realistic simulations, which are critical for developing robust robot behaviors.
2. **Dual-Model Utility:** The provision of two distinct simulation models—a physically accurate model and a lightweight velocity-based model—offers flexibility. The physically accurate model supports detailed studies requiring precise dynamic fidelity, while the lightweight model caters to high-throughput applications like RL where computational efficiency is paramount. This dual approach allows researchers to choose the most appropriate model based on their specific research needs.
3. **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The learning-based calibration approach, which approximates the real robot's S-shaped velocity profiles using minimal real-world data, enhances the simulation's fidelity to actual robot behavior. This improved accuracy in replicating the robot's motion dynamics can contribute to reducing the "sim-to-real gap," making it easier to transfer control policies learned in simulation to physical robots.
4. **Insights into Mecanum Wheel Control:** The observation that the real TIAGo robot's controller does not consistently enforce proportional acceleration across all mecanum wheels for complex movements provides a significant insight for future robot design and controller development. This highlights a design consideration for controllers of omnidirectional mecanum-wheeled bases, suggesting that enforcing proportional acceleration is crucial for smooth and predictable motion.
5. **Guidance for Future Integrations:** The detailed methodology, particularly concerning the modeling of mecanum wheels and the learning-based calibration, can serve as a guide for future projects involving the integration of other omnidirectional robots into Isaac Sim or similar physics simulators.
6. **Open-Source Contribution:** The public availability of the integration on GitHub ensures that the research community can directly benefit from and build upon this work, accelerating collective progress in mobile manipulation and simulation.

Future work suggested by the authors includes further validation of the framework for specific tasks like reinforcement learning and trajectory tracking, as well as exploring alternative approaches to improve the modeling of S-shaped velocity profiles, particularly for complex, non-axis-aligned movements.
