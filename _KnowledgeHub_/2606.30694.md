# DSIP: A Dynamic Coordination Planner for Signal-Free Intersections using Diffusion-Model-Based Multi-Agent Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and The University of Texas at Arlington developed DSIP, a diffusion-model-based multi-agent motion planning framework for signal-free intersections. The system coordinates vehicle trajectories to eliminate stop-and-go traffic, achieving higher average speeds and lower time loss compared to traditional signal control and reinforcement learning approaches, with no collisions observed in extensive CAV simulations.

## Problem

- Traditional signalized intersections cause significant delays, fuel consumption, and emissions due to prevalent stop-and-go traffic patterns.
- Current intersection management methods, including advanced RL-based controllers, are limited by discrete temporal phasing and struggle to coordinate complex, non-linear multi-vehicle trajectories, especially under high traffic densities.
- Conventional coordination approaches face difficulties in characterizing complex joint trajectory distributions, leading to sub-optimal or unsafe plans in dynamic multi-agent environments.

## Method

- **DSIP** (Diffusion-model-based Signal-free Intersection Planner) employs a ==neurosymbolic== approach, combining a learned single-agent ==trajectory prior== from a ==diffusion model== with a ==Conflict-Based Search (CBS)== for multi-agent conflict resolution.
- The system formulates intersection coordination as a ==constrained trajectory generation== problem, optimizing continuous vehicle paths instead of discrete signal phases to enable smooth, stop-free passage.
- It integrates pre-intersection trajectory initialization, periodic refreshing, and step-wise execution within a ==dynamic coordination strategy== to ensure real-time, collision-free operation for Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs).

## Results

- DSIP consistently achieved lower average time loss and higher average speeds (maintaining **9-10 m/s** even at maximum traffic density), significantly outperforming fixed-time and state-of-the-art RL-based signal controllers.
- The framework demonstrated robust safety, with **no collision warnings** and only **three abrupt deceleration incidents** observed across more than **2 million vehicle passages** in extensive SUMO simulations.
- It maintained stable performance with narrow confidence intervals under stochastic demand, exhibiting superior robustness compared to signalized methods, and showed real-time computational feasibility with an average inference latency of **7.75 ms** per planning query.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Continuous, trajectory-level coordination offers a fundamental paradigm shift from phase-based signal control, enabling superior traffic efficiency and stability at urban intersections by directly optimizing vehicle movements.
> - Diffusion models, when combined with a search-based conflict resolution layer, provide a robust framework for learning and generating complex multi-agent trajectories while explicitly ensuring collision avoidance.
> - Leveraging idealized Connected and Automated Vehicle (CAV) conditions and perfect V2X communication allows for the effective elimination of traffic signals, unlocking latent intersection capacity and significantly reducing delays and emissions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Urban intersections present a significant challenge to traffic efficiency and environmental sustainability due to the stop-and-go behavior induced by traditional signal control. This behavior leads to increased delays, fuel consumption, and carbon emissions. Research in intersection management has evolved from fixed-time and actuated signal systems to more adaptive approaches, including those based on reinforcement learning (RL). While RL-based controllers have improved performance by optimizing phase sequences, they still operate within the phase-based paradigm, encountering issues like the "curse of dimensionality" and inherent limitations in fully eliminating stop-and-go movements, especially under high traffic densities.

Concurrently, the emergence of connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) and Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication has opened possibilities for signal-free intersection management. Pioneering works in this area, such as VICS and methods developed by Malikopoulos et al. and Lee and Park, have demonstrated the potential of trajectory-level coordination under model predictive control frameworks or distributed conflict-free cooperation. These approaches aim to achieve smooth, stop-free passage for CAVs, showing advantages over conventional signalized control. However, a common challenge for these conventional coordination methods is their difficulty in characterizing the complex, non-linear joint trajectory distributions of multiple interacting agents, which can lead to sub-optimal or unsafe plans in dynamic environments.

This paper addresses these limitations by exploring the application of diffusion-model-based multi-agent motion planning to signal-free intersections. Diffusion models represent a recent advancement in generative models capable of learning complex data distributions, including motion trajectories. While existing data-driven methods like imitation learning and early generative frameworks (e.g., Motion Planning Diffusion) have shown success in single-agent trajectory generation, they often lack explicit mechanisms for multi-agent coordination. More recent works, such as Multi-agent Motion Planning with Diffusion Models (MMD), offer scalable frameworks by hybridizing search strategies with generative models. This research builds upon such diffusion-based multi-agent planning, adapting it to the specific challenges of structured urban intersections by combining learned single-agent trajectory priors with a search-based conflict resolution layer. The work is positioned as a paradigm-level evaluation, assessing whether continuous, trajectory-level planning can serve as an effective alternative to conventional signalized control in future CAV-intensive traffic systems, thereby evaluating the necessity of traffic signals in such environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the environmental and efficiency drawbacks of traditional traffic signal control at urban intersections. Stop-and-go traffic maneuvers lead to significant energy waste, increased fuel consumption (81–107% for conventional vehicles and 12–22% for EVs under deteriorating traffic), and elevated carbon emissions. Current signal control systems, even advanced reinforcement learning-based approaches, are constrained by discrete temporal phasing, which inherently introduces mandatory stops and limits the full utilization of intersection capacity, especially under high traffic demand. The "curse of dimensionality" further hinders the scalability and efficiency of these methods when attempting to incorporate detailed vehicle states or manage multiple intersections.

With the advent of Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) and Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) communication, the possibility of trajectory-level coordination at signal-free intersections has emerged as a potential solution to mitigate these issues. The core objective of this work is to investigate whether a diffusion-model-based multi-agent motion planning approach can serve as a robust and scalable coordination mechanism for signal-free intersections. The authors aim to shift the intersection management paradigm from discrete temporal phasing to continuous multi-vehicle trajectory optimization.

Specifically, the key objectives include:
1. **Develop a Diffusion-Model-Based Multi-Agent Motion Planner:** To create a framework, named DSIP (Diffusion-model-based Signal-free Intersection Planner), that can jointly optimize traffic efficiency through large-scale multi-vehicle coordination at signal-free intersections. This involves combining a learned single-agent trajectory prior with a coordination layer to resolve inter-vehicle conflicts.
2. **Propose a Dynamic Coordination Strategy:** To design an operational strategy that integrates pre-intersection trajectory initialization, periodic trajectory refreshing, and step-wise execution within the intersection to maintain collision-free operation under the specified simulation conditions.
3. **Evaluate Performance under Idealized Conditions:** To assess the theoretical upper-bound performance of this diffusion-driven coordination strategy under idealized assumptions, specifically a traffic stream consisting exclusively of CAVs governed by a centralized framework and perfect V2X communication with negligible latency and packet loss. This approach isolates the core benefits and capabilities of the proposed planning method without confounding factors from real-world communication or mixed traffic scenarios.
4. **Demonstrate Superiority over Baselines:** To provide comprehensive simulation results using the SUMO platform, demonstrating that trajectory-level coordination can achieve superior traffic efficiency (reduced average delay, higher average speed) and safety compared to both traditional fixed-time signal control and state-of-the-art reinforcement learning-based controllers, particularly in medium- to high-density traffic.

The motivation is to unlock latent intersection capacity through software-defined coordination, offering a cost-effective pathway to improve urban traffic flow efficiency and contribute to broader emission reduction goals without requiring physical infrastructure expansion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research proposes DSIP (Diffusion-model-based Signal-free Intersection Planner), a multi-agent motion planning framework for signal-free intersections. The methodology builds on a generative diffusion process, moving away from discrete phase-based management towards continuous trajectory optimization.

**A. Core Assumptions:**
The study operates under two idealized assumptions to isolate and evaluate the core planning capabilities:
1. The traffic stream consists entirely of Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) managed by a centralized coordination framework, ensuring precise control within the intersection conflict zone.
2. V2X communication is assumed to be perfect, with negligible transmission latency and packet loss.

**B. Diffusion-Model-Based Multi-Agent Motion Planning Algorithm:**
The coordination task is formulated as a constrained trajectory generation problem in a 2D road workspace.
- **Trajectory Representation:** For each vehicle *i*, a trajectory **X**_i_ = {*x*_{i,0}, *x*_{i,1}..., *x*_{i,H}} is generated over a planning horizon *H*, where *x*_{i,h} = [*x*_{i,h}, *y*_{i,h}]^⊤ denotes the planned 2D position at time step *h*.
- **Objective Function:** The desired trajectory **X***_i_ is obtained by maximizing the posterior probability *P*_{θ}(**X***_i_ | *Θ*_{i}, **B***_i_), conditioned on task conditions *Θ*_{i} (start/destination, route, map constraints) and constraint set **B***_i_ (lane-following, collision avoidance). This is equivalently framed as minimizing a posterior cost *L*^post^_{i}, which combines a traffic-related trajectory cost *L*_{i} and a learned trajectory prior *P*_{θ}(**X***_i_) from a pre-trained single-agent diffusion model.
- **Traffic-Related Trajectory Cost (*L*_{i}):** This cost function is a weighted sum of four components:
 - *L*^goal^: penalizes deviation from the destination.
 - *L*^lane^: encourages adherence to lane centerlines (soft constraint).
 - *L*^smooth^: suppresses abrupt trajectory changes.
 - *L*^col^: penalizes potential conflicts with other vehicles.
- **Diffusion Prior:** A pre-trained single-agent diffusion planner, based on the framework in, provides a generative prior for 2D trajectory generation. It involves a forward process of progressively perturbing a clean trajectory into a noisy one and a reverse denoising process.
- **Guided Reverse Sampling:** During online planning, the reverse denoising process starts from a noisy trajectory. At each step, a denoised trajectory mean *μ*^{m-1}^_{i} is predicted. This mean is then adjusted by a task-related guidance gradient *g*^{m-1}^_{i} (∇_{**X***_i_} *L*_{i}) to steer the sampled trajectory towards lower task costs, incorporating lane-following, smoothness, and collision avoidance.
- **Lane Constraints:** Lane information from SUMO is used to sample reference points *P*^lane^_{i}. *L*^lane^ is calculated as the sum of squared distances from planned positions to the nearest lane reference point, encouraging route-consistent motion without forcing exact adherence.
- **Collision Avoidance:** Vehicles are modeled as planar positions with a safety radius. A conflict constraint is defined by a conflict region center, safety radius, and active time indices. *L*^col^ penalizes penetration into these conflict regions.
- **Multi-Vehicle Coordination (CBS-DIFFUSION-PLAN):** A Conflict-Based Search (CBS)-based module handles multi-vehicle coordination. It takes active single-agent diffusion planners, detects conflicts among generated trajectories, and adds corresponding spatio-temporal constraints to the affected low-level diffusion planners. These planners then regenerate trajectories under the updated constraints via guided denoising. This combines the generative capacity of diffusion models with search-based conflict resolution.

**C. Control Logic for Vehicle Motion and Trajectory:**
A two-stage coordination strategy is adopted for real-time operation within SUMO’s fixed-route constraints.
- **Pre-calculation (Pre-intersection Stage):** Before a vehicle reaches the stop line, its SUMO-assigned route determines start/destination positions. An individual diffusion planner is initialized, and lane reference points are added as soft constraints. Trajectory validity checks and multi-vehicle planning queries occur every four simulation steps (0.4 seconds).
- **Real-time Fine-tuning (In-intersection Stage):** Vehicles execute the latest planned trajectories at every SUMO step (0.1 seconds). The diffusion-based planning result is refreshed every 0.4 seconds. Newly active vehicles are incorporated into the planning process in subsequent queries. Target positions are clipped to map boundaries, and vehicle heading is derived from adjacent planned positions. TraCI interface moves vehicles.
- **Path Confirmation (Legality & Safety Validation):** A two-stage mechanism ensures trajectory legality and safety: static checks before vehicle departure (start/destination within map bounds, route consistency) and dynamic checks during operation (active vehicles, valid planning states, boundary/conflict checks before execution).

**D. Experiment Setup and Baselines:**
- **Simulation Environment:** SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility) platform.
- **Intersection Configurations:** Three isolated four-leg intersections with 4, 6, and 8 lanes.
- **Traffic Demand:** Five distinct density levels (Minimum Density to Maximum Density) were defined for each configuration, with traffic demand scaled proportionally to lanes. A 1:2:1 (Left:Straight:Right) turning movement distribution was used. Vehicle departure times followed a Gaussian distribution.
- **Safety Constraints:** A maximum speed limit of 50 km/h and a minimum inter-vehicle gap of 1.5 meters were enforced.
- **Metrics:**
 - **Average Time Loss:** Difference between actual travel time and theoretical optimal free-flow travel time.
 - **Average Speed:** Mean instantaneous speed of active vehicles.
 - **Safety:** Monitored by collision warnings (inter-vehicle gaps < 1.5m) and abrupt deceleration incidents (exceeding SUMO’s emergency braking threshold).
- **Baselines:**
 - **Fixed-Time Signal Control:** Traditional four-phase scheme based on Webster’s formula and Highway Capacity Manual (HCM) guidelines.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Based Controllers:** MP-Light (decentralized MARL), CoLight (grid-based, centralized MARL), and AttentionLight (centralized MARL with multi-head attention).
- **Experimental Protocol:** 75 distinct test configurations (3 intersections × 5 density levels × 5 methods). Each configuration involved 30 independent simulation runs, each 1000 seconds long, with different random seeds for statistical robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the DSIP framework was conducted across diverse traffic densities and intersection configurations, benchmarking its performance against traditional signalized control and state-of-the-art reinforcement learning (RL) based controllers. The analysis focused on traffic efficiency, operational robustness, and safety compliance.

**A. Performance Comparison among Different Methods**

1. **Average Time Loss:**
 - Under medium traffic density (MedD), DSIP consistently maintained average vehicle delay below 15 seconds throughout the simulation.
 - DSIP significantly outperformed both traditional fixed-time signal control and the RL-based baselines (MP-Light, CoLight, AttentionLight).
 - Signalized methods showed a monotonic increase in time loss, indicating queue accumulation, and exhibited widening confidence intervals over time, reflecting congestion-induced performance degradation.
 - In contrast, DSIP converged to a stable steady-state within the initial 2000 simulation steps and demonstrated narrower confidence intervals, suggesting lower variance and higher robustness to stochastic demand fluctuations. This stability was attributed to signal-free trajectory coordination through explicit vehicle-level conflict resolution.

2. **Average Speed:**
 - The DSIP framework consistently achieved the highest average speed across all tested traffic densities and lane configurations.
 - It maintained approximately 9–10 m/s (around 32–36 km/h) even under maximum density (MaxD).
 - All signal-based baselines experienced significant performance degradation as traffic density increased, with average speeds dropping below 3 m/s (around 11 km/h) at MaxD.
 - The performance gap between DSIP and baseline methods progressively widened from minimum to maximum densities.
 - Among the baselines, MP-Light exhibited unstable performance at high densities, occasionally underperforming traditional fixed-time control.
 - DSIP's ability to maintain high-speed flow was consistent across two-way four-, six-, and eight-lane scenarios.

3. **Collision Avoidance:**
 - Across all 450 DSIP simulation runs, encompassing three intersection configurations and five traffic density levels, no collision warnings (inter-vehicle gaps below 1.5 meters) were observed. This corresponds to over 2 million planned vehicle passages.
 - Only three instances of abrupt deceleration events (longitudinal deceleration exceeding SUMO’s emergency braking threshold) were recorded throughout the entire evaluation.
 - This near-zero incident rate indicates that trajectory-level coordination via diffusion-based planning can maintain safe multi-agent operation with sufficient safety margins under complex, high-saturation traffic scenarios within the idealized simulation setting.

**B. Traffic Evolution Analysis**
Visualizations of traffic evolution (e.g., at a two-way six-lane intersection under medium density) illustrated dynamic differences:
- Fixed-time signal control led to rapid queue accumulation due to periodic stopping.
- CoLight (an RL-based baseline) initially alleviated congestion but gradually exhibited queue buildup under high traffic demand.
- DSIP, by contrast, maintained a more stable traffic state with continuous vehicle movements and effective mitigation of large-scale congestion. These visual observations corroborated the quantitative results regarding reduced delay and smoother flow.

**C. Discussion**
The consistent performance hierarchy, with DSIP outperforming signal-based paradigms in efficiency and stability, was attributed to a fundamental difference in control abstraction. While RL-based controllers operate at the phase level using aggregated traffic states, inherently limiting their ability to resolve fine-grained vehicle interactions, DSIP directly optimizes continuous vehicle trajectories. This enables explicit vehicle-level conflict resolution, making it effective even at maximum traffic density where phase-based control tends to degrade. The study interprets this comparison as a paradigm-level evaluation between phase-based signal control and trajectory-level intersection management.

Regarding computational efficiency, DSIP achieved an average inference latency of 7.75 ms per planning query on the specified computing platform. This latency is substantially lower than the 400 ms planning refresh interval used in the simulation, indicating its capability to meet real-time update requirements for urban intersection scenarios and potential for deployment on edge computing units.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, DSIP, for signal-free intersection management using a diffusion-model-based multi-agent motion planning approach. The findings demonstrate the viability and advantages of continuous, trajectory-level coordination in future transportation systems dominated by Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs).

**A. Significance of Findings:**
- **Demonstrated Superiority:** The comprehensive evaluations in SUMO show that DSIP significantly outperforms traditional fixed-time signal control and advanced reinforcement learning (RL) based signal controllers across various traffic densities and intersection configurations. This superiority is evident in key performance indicators such as reduced average delay, higher average speed, and robust safety performance, particularly under high-saturation conditions where conventional methods struggle.
- **Paradigm Shift:** The work provides empirical evidence supporting a fundamental shift from discrete, phase-based intersection management to continuous, trajectory-level optimization. This highlights that traffic signals may not be necessary in CAV-intensive environments, offering a path to unlock latent intersection capacity without requiring physical infrastructure expansion.
- **Safety Validation:** The near-absence of collision warnings and abrupt deceleration events in extensive simulations under idealized CAV conditions suggests that diffusion-based planning can maintain safe multi-agent operation, a critical requirement for any intersection control strategy.
- **Real-time Feasibility:** The achieved average inference latency of 7.75 ms per planning query, well below the 400 ms planning refresh interval, indicates the computational efficiency of DSIP. This suggests practical deployability on edge computing units, facilitating hybrid centralized-distributed architectures compatible with existing V2X communication standards.

**B. Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Traffic Efficiency:** By enabling continuous, stop-free passage through intersections, DSIP has the potential to substantially reduce urban traffic congestion, decrease travel times, and improve overall network throughput.
- **Environmental Benefits:** Reduced idling and smoother traffic flow directly translate to decreased fuel consumption and lower greenhouse gas emissions for both conventional and electric vehicles, contributing to global climate crisis mitigation efforts.
- **Cost-Effective Infrastructure Improvement:** The approach offers a software-defined solution to enhance urban traffic flow, which could be more cost-effective than physical infrastructure expansion projects.
- **Foundation for Future ITS:** This research contributes to the vision of next-generation intelligent transportation systems (ITS) where collaborative intelligence, rather than fixed infrastructure, defines intersection efficiency. It paves the way for safer, greener, and more scalable urban mobility solutions.

**C. Future Research Directions:**
The authors identify several areas for future work to transition from idealized conditions to more realistic and complex scenarios:
1. **Incorporating Realism:** Integrate realistic communication delays and control-tracking deviations into the simulation framework to assess robustness under less-than-ideal conditions.
2. **Network-Level Control and Complex Geometries:** Extend the framework from isolated four-leg intersections to network-level coordinated control across multiple intersections and adapt it to more complex traffic geometries, such as roundabouts.
3. **Relaxing Planning Constraints:** Relax the fixed line-following constraint at the intersection center to explore greater planning freedom provided by the diffusion model, potentially maximizing traffic efficiency.
4. **Benchmarking with Other AIM Methods:** Conduct direct comparisons with representative reservation-based and optimization-based Autonomous Intersection Management (AIM) methods under a unified simulation interface for broader algorithmic benchmarking.
