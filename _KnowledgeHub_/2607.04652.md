# KAM-WM: Kinematic Affordance Maps from Latent World Models for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework extracts kinematic affordance maps from a frozen image-to-video model, offering both localization and directional cues for robot manipulation. This method enhances policy learning from limited demonstrations by providing a computationally efficient first-order visual prior that improves success rates on tasks requiring specific kinematic interactions.

## Problem

- Existing visual priors for robot manipulation provide 'zero-order' cues (where to attend) but lack 'first-order' directional information (how to interact), which is crucial for complex contact-rich tasks.
- Integrating generative video models, which inherently contain temporal knowledge, into robot control often incurs high computational costs through multi-step video rollouts or fine-tuning of large model backbones.
- Learning complex manipulation policies from limited demonstrations is challenging without rich, informative visual priors, especially for tasks requiring specific kinematic interactions.

## Method

- **KAM-WM** extracts a ==Kinematic Affordance Map (KAM)== by querying a frozen ==Flow Matching== image-to-video model (Wan 2.2) once at its maximum-noise endpoint (t=1.0), interpreting the single-step latent velocity field as a task-conditioned directional prior.
- A lightweight ==Perceiver cross-attention== bottleneck compresses the high-dimensional KAM tensor into a compact set of 8 dynamic tokens, providing an efficient representation for policy conditioning.
- The compressed KAM tokens, alongside multi-view RGB observations, proprioceptive state, and language instructions, condition a ==1D U-Net Diffusion Policy==, without requiring multi-step video rollout or fine-tuning the large video backbone.

## Results

- KAM-WM achieved an average success rate of **90.6%** on LIBERO, surpassing Diffusion Policy (**72.4%**) and OpenVLA (**76.5%**), with the most substantial gains on tasks requiring longer interaction sequences or recovery from intermediate state changes.
- On RoboTwin 2.0, KAM-WM achieved **65.7%** average success in the Easy setting, outperforming leaderboard baselines like DP3 (**55.2%**), and demonstrated improvements on tasks requiring directional contact or specific object placement, even in low-data scenarios.
- The method maintains high inference efficiency with action policy latency of **99 ms** and amortizes a one-time KAM extraction cost of **895 ms** per episode, while utilizing approximately **25 times** fewer trainable parameters (**120M** total, **2.1M** for Perceiver) compared to some baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A frozen, general-purpose image-to-video generative model can implicitly encode valuable 'first-order' directional kinematic priors in its latent space when queried once at the noise endpoint.
> - Efficiently compressing this dense latent velocity field into a small set of tokens allows for effective integration of this prior into a downstream policy without substantial computational overhead.
> - Leveraging this single-query latent prior provides a computationally efficient method to enhance robot manipulation policies with sophisticated temporal knowledge from foundation models, particularly in low-data regimes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a challenge in robot manipulation learning, specifically the need for effective visual priors when learning from limited demonstrations. The broader research landscape encompasses various approaches to robot learning, including visual representation learning, affordance learning, and the use of generative video and world models.

Previous research in visual representation learning has produced strong spatial and semantic features through methods like language supervision, masked reconstruction, and pre-training on human or embodied video. Affordance-based methods extend this by predicting regions or scores indicating where interaction can occur. While these methods are effective for localizing task-relevant objects ("where to attend"), they primarily provide "zero-order" cues, often lacking information about the required approach direction or post-contact motion ("how to interact"). This limitation can hinder performance on tasks requiring specific kinematic interactions, such as hanging a mug.

Another significant area involves generative video and world models, which have been utilized in robotics for visual planning, subgoal generation, and inverse dynamics. Large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models also learn action-conditioned representations from extensive robot datasets, encoding rich temporal and semantic priors. However, adapting or utilizing these powerful models for low-data manipulation scenarios often incurs substantial costs. These costs typically arise from the necessity of action fine-tuning, co-training the model with actions, or performing multi-step video rollouts at inference time, which can introduce significant computational expense and latency.

A related line of research explores conditioning robot policies on predicted motion, such such as optical flow, point trajectories, or any-point tracking. These methods demonstrate the value of motion priors when static localization is insufficient. However, they often rely on dedicated flow or tracking modules, curated trajectory supervision, or explicit procedures to convert 2D motion into 3D robot actions.

KAM-WM distinguishes itself by proposing a method to extract a "first-order" visual prior that includes directional information directly from a *frozen*, general-purpose image-to-video model. Crucially, this extraction occurs in a *single query* at the noise endpoint, bypassing the computational burdens of multi-step future frame generation, co-training, or fine-tuning the large video backbone. This approach aims to provide both "where" and "how" cues for manipulation tasks more efficiently than prior methods, positioning it as an alternative for leveraging the temporal knowledge within generative models for low-data robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind KAM-WM stems from a recognized gap in existing visual priors for robot manipulation, particularly in low-data learning regimes. While static visual priors, such as segmentation masks, are effective at localizing objects or regions relevant for interaction, they primarily offer "zero-order" cues. These cues inform the robot *where* to attend but do not inherently encode information about *how* the interaction should commence, specifically the desired approach direction or initial motion structure. For manipulation tasks involving intricate contact, placement, or bimanual coordination, merely knowing the target location is insufficient; the kinematic trajectory and orientation of the gripper are often critical for success. For example, hanging a mug on a hook requires not just identifying the mug, but also understanding the directional motion needed to engage with the hook.

Pretrained video models, designed for future-frame prediction, are appealing candidates for providing such directional information because their training objective inherently requires encoding how objects move and interact over time. However, the conventional ways these models are integrated into robotics applications often introduce significant computational overhead. Common practices include rolling out future frames to serve as visual subgoals, which can lead to increased test-time latency due to iterative denoising, or co-training/fine-tuning the large video model backbone alongside the action policy. The latter approach is particularly resource-intensive and impractical in the low-data regimes that this work targets.

This leads to the core research question driving KAM-WM: Is it possible to directly extract useful motion knowledge from a *frozen* video model in a *single query*, thereby circumventing the costs associated with generating future frames or fine-tuning the extensive backbone?

The authors' starting point was an empirical observation: when a frozen text-conditioned image-to-video model (Wan 2.2) is queried at the first denoising step (the high-noise endpoint), its high-response regions often align with task-relevant contact structures and the robot's embodiment. This response not only offered sharper localization than a simple mask but also suggested a coarse interaction cue.

Based on this observation, the key objectives of the KAM-WM framework are:
1. To propose a method for extracting a single-step latent velocity field from a frozen Flow Matching image-to-video model, which is termed a Kinematic Affordance Map (KAM), to serve as a first-order visual prior for low-data manipulation.
2. To design a lightweight interface, specifically a Perceiver-token bottleneck, that can effectively compress this dense latent field into a compact set of tokens. These tokens are then used to condition a diffusion policy, integrating the directional prior without requiring video rollout or fine-tuning of the world model.
3. To evaluate KAM-WM on established robotic manipulation benchmarks, LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0, to quantitatively demonstrate its ability to improve manipulation performance, particularly on tasks where directional interaction cues are significant, and to assess its efficiency compared to existing methods.

Ultimately, KAM-WM aims to provide a computationally efficient means to infuse sophisticated temporal and kinematic knowledge from powerful foundation models into robotic control policies, thereby enhancing their ability to learn complex manipulations from limited demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The KAM-WM framework operates in two main stages: the extraction of a Kinematic Affordance Map (KAM) and the conditioning of a diffusion policy with these maps. The central idea is to derive a single-step latent velocity field from a frozen image-to-video model without engaging in multi-step video rollout.

#### 3.1. Single-Step Latent Velocity Extraction (Kinematic Affordance Map)

The first stage involves acquiring a task-conditioned motion cue from a frozen image-to-video model.
- **Backbone Model:** The official Wan2.2-TI2V-5B model is used as the frozen backbone. This model is trained with a Rectified Flow objective, which implicitly learns a straight path between a noise latent (x_1) and a clean future video latent (x_0).
- **Query Mechanism:** Unlike conventional usage that involves iterative denoising to generate future frames, KAM-WM queries the model *once* at the maximum-noise endpoint, denoted as t=1.0. The input to the model at this point is a pure-noise latent (x_1) conditioned on the current initial observation (o_1) and a language instruction (l), forming the condition c = [o_1, l].
- **Latent Velocity Interpretation:** The model's output at t=1.0, v_θ(x_1, 1.0, c), approximates x_1 - E[x_0 | c]. Since x_1 is fixed, this output primarily reflects the conditional expectation of the clean future video latent E[x_0 | c]. KAM-WM interprets this single-step latent velocity field, V_prior, as the Kinematic Affordance Map (KAM).
- **Nature of KAM:** The KAM (V_prior ∈ R^(C×F×H×W)) remains in the video model’s latent space. It is not decoded into visual frames, nor is it explicitly interpreted as optical flow or a metrically calibrated 3D displacement field. Instead, its magnitude signifies task-conditioned response regions, and its normalized latent response preserves coarse directional structure, collectively providing a "where-and-how" visual prior. For analysis, its magnitude A_kam and normalized response b_V_prior can be separated, but the policy consumes the dense V_prior.

#### 3.2. Dynamic Token Compression

The raw KAM tensor (V_prior) is high-dimensional and cannot be directly fed into the policy. To address this, a lightweight Perceiver cross-attention bottleneck is employed for compression.
- **Compression Process:**
 1. A 1x1x1 Conv3D projection first maps the channel dimension of V_prior (from 48 to 256).
 2. This is followed by a GELU activation and 3D adaptive average pooling to reduce the spatial dimensions, resulting in M' = 1,190 tokens.
 3. These pooled tokens are flattened and augmented with learned positional embeddings (E_pos) to form Z.
 4. Finally, K=8 learnable queries (Q) attend to Z via a pre-norm multi-head cross-attention layer to produce the compressed dynamic tokens, H_dyn ∈ R^(K×d_k).
- **Parameter Efficiency:** This Perceiver bottleneck contains approximately 2.1 million trainable parameters, maintaining a compact representation.

#### 3.3. KAM-Conditioned Diffusion Policy

The compressed KAM tokens are then used to condition a downstream action policy.
- **Policy Architecture:** A 1D U-Net Diffusion Policy is used, a standard architecture for visuomotor policy learning.
- **Multi-Modal Conditioning:** The policy receives multiple inputs at each control step τ:
 - Multi-view RGB observations (o_τ).
 - Proprioceptive state (s_τ), which includes robot joint states and end-effector poses.
 - The pre-computed and compressed KAM tokens (H_dyn).
- **Language Integration:** The RGB observation encoder (Ψ) is modulated using FiLM layers. The scale and shift parameters for FiLM are predicted from the frozen Wan 2.2 text embedding (Φ(l)), ensuring the visual features are conditioned on the language instruction.
- **Global Conditioning Feature:** The language-modulated visual features, flattened KAM tokens, and proprioceptive features are concatenated to form a global conditioning feature g_τ.
- **Policy Training:** The U-Net policy is trained to predict the noise (ε_θ) in a noisy action chunk (ẽ_a_k) based on the diffusion step (k) and the global conditioning feature (g_τ). The loss function is a standard squared error (L_DP).
- **Inference:** During policy execution, the KAM is extracted only *once* per episode from the initial head-camera observation and remains fixed throughout the closed-loop execution. This design choice ensures that the expensive backbone query is amortized over the entire episode, keeping steady-state inference lightweight. KAM functions purely as a conditioning input and is not an action target.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details (from Appendix A)

- The Wan 2.2 5B backbone extracts V_prior of dimensions R^(48x21x50x68).
- The visual encoder consists of ResNet-18 instances per camera view (head and wrist cameras for simulation, head, wrist, and top cameras for real-world feasibility study).
- The 1D U-Net Diffusion Policy has an action chunk horizon H_a=16 and uses 10 denoising steps during inference.
- For training efficiency, KAM tensors are precomputed and cached once per demonstration episode. The Perceiver, visual encoder, FiLM heads, and U-Net policy are the only trainable components, totaling approximately 120 million parameters, with the Perceiver contributing 2.1 million.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of KAM-WM was conducted on two robotic manipulation benchmarks, LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0, focusing on its ability to improve manipulation performance, the nature of its gains (directional vs. spatial), and its efficiency.

#### 4.1. Results on LIBERO

- KAM-WM demonstrated improved performance in the standard 50-demonstration setting on LIBERO. It achieved an average success rate of 90.6% across 40 tasks.
- This performance exceeded reported results for Diffusion Policy (DP) (72.4%) and OpenVLA (76.5%) by 18.2 and 14.1 percentage points, respectively.
- The most substantial gains were observed on the Goal and Long suites, where tasks typically involve longer interaction sequences and require maintaining task intent or recovering from intermediate state changes. For instance, on the Long suite, KAM-WM raised success from 50.5% (DP) and 53.7% (OpenVLA) to 78.8%.
- This pattern of improvement is consistent with KAM functioning as a task-conditioned visual prior that provides more than static object localization.

#### 4.2. Results on RoboTwin 2.0

- KAM-WM achieved the highest 50-task average success among the compared methods under a 50-demonstration protocol.
- **Easy Setting:** KAM-WM achieved 65.7% average success, surpassing leaderboard baselines such as DP3 (55.2%), π_0 (46.4%), RDT (34.5%), ACT (29.7%), and DP (28.0%).
- Significant gains were noted on tasks requiring directional contact, specific object placement, or bimanual coordination, including "Click Alarmclock," "Hanging Mug," "Place Cans Plasticbox," and "Handover Block." For example, "Click Alarmclock" improved from 77% (DP3) to 96% with KAM-WM.
- **Hard Setting:** While generally more challenging for all methods, KAM-WM achieved 22.4% average success, outperforming π_0 (16.3%), RDT (13.7%), DP3 (5.0%), ACT (1.7%), and DP (0.6%). KAM-WM tended to show greater advantages on tasks with asymmetric approach geometries where interaction side is crucial.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Prior Type (KAM vs. Zero-Order Mask):** A controlled comparison against a zero-order mask prior (using SAM 3 masks conditioned on language instruction, passed through the same Perceiver bottleneck) indicated the value of KAM's directional information.
 - KAM increased the Easy average from 67.8% (mask) to 77.0% and the Hard average from 24.0% (mask) to 28.6% on a subset of RoboTwin tasks.
 - The largest gains were on tasks like "Hanging Mug" and "Place Empty Cup," where approach direction is critical.
 - However, KAM did not uniformly outperform masks, showing reduced performance on some tasks (e.g., "Click Bell" Hard, "Place Shoe" Hard). This suggests that in scenarios requiring precise point contact, the latent velocity direction might be more sensitive to texture and lighting variations than mask-based localization.
- **Extraction Timestep:** Evaluating KAM extraction at different Flow Matching timesteps (t=0.0, 0.5, 1.0) showed that the optimal timestep can be task-dependent. While t=0.0 or t=0.5 could sometimes yield better results, t=1.0 was chosen for KAM-WM because it provides a competitive prior from a single, rollout-free query, avoiding multi-step denoising costs.
- **Low-Data Regime:** A diagnostic study comparing DP, DP+Mask, and DP+KAM-WM on six tasks with 50 and 20 demonstrations showed KAM to be particularly effective in lower-data settings. At 20 demonstrations, KAM-WM consistently provided non-negative improvements relative to the backbone policy and increased the subset average from 45.0% (DP) to 66.7%, whereas the mask prior yielded mixed results.

#### 4.4. Efficiency Analysis

- **Inference Efficiency:** KAM-WM avoided multi-step video rollout during policy inference. The action policy latency (for 10 denoising steps) was 99 ms per action chunk, substantially faster than a fine-tuned π_0 implementation (380 ms). The one-time KAM extraction cost 895 ms per episode but was amortized over the rollout. Perceiver compression added less than 1 ms.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** The system kept the 5B video backbone frozen. The trainable components (ResNet-18 encoder, 93.4M 1D U-Net policy, 2.1M Perceiver bottleneck) totaled approximately 120M parameters. The KAM-specific Perceiver module was 2.1M parameters. Contextually, KAM-WM achieved 65.7% Easy success with approximately 25 times fewer trainable parameters than the reported π_0 FT baseline (which achieved 46.4% Easy success).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The KAM-WM framework introduces a method for leveraging large, pre-trained image-to-video models to extract kinematic affordance maps, offering both localization and directional cues for robot manipulation. This approach presents several points of significance and potential impact.

**Significance:**
1. **First-Order Visual Prior:** The work demonstrates that a frozen generative video model can serve as a source of "first-order" visual priors, providing directional interaction cues beyond the "zero-order" spatial localization offered by static masks. This addresses a critical limitation in low-data robot learning where the "how" of interaction is as important as the "where."
2. **Computational Efficiency:** By extracting the Kinematic Affordance Map (KAM) from a single query at the noise endpoint of a Flow Matching model, KAM-WM avoids the computational expense and increased test-time latency associated with multi-step video rollouts or fine-tuning large video backbones. This design choice makes the approach more practical for real-time control applications.
3. **Performance Improvements:** Quantitative results on LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 benchmarks indicate that KAM-WM leads to notable improvements in success rates, particularly for tasks demanding specific approach directions or contact-rich interactions. These gains were achieved while maintaining a comparatively lower number of trainable parameters in the policy loop than some contextual baselines.
4. **Low-Data Learning:** The ablation studies suggest that KAM is beneficial in lower-data regimes, where richer visual priors can compensate for scarcer demonstration data, potentially enhancing the feasibility of deploying robots in scenarios with limited expert demonstrations.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Enhanced Robot Learning from Few Demonstrations:** By providing a more informative visual prior, KAM-WM could enable robots to learn complex manipulation tasks more effectively and reliably from fewer demonstrations, reducing the cost and effort of data collection.
2. **Broader Utility of Foundation Models:** The methodology offers a paradigm for extracting valuable information from large, pre-trained generative foundation models without requiring their full integration or adaptation into the control loop. This could expand the practical applicability of such models in robotics beyond direct planning or subgoal generation.
3. **Development of More Robust Policies:** Policies conditioned on directional cues derived from video models may exhibit greater robustness to variations in object pose or scene configurations, as they are implicitly guided by a notion of plausible interaction kinematics.
4. **Inspiration for Future Prior Design:** This work could inspire further research into novel ways of interpreting and utilizing the latent spaces of generative models to extract kinematic, dynamic, or even semantic priors for various control tasks, moving beyond purely visual or geometric cues.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
1. **Stale Prior:** KAM is currently extracted only once per episode. For long-horizon tasks, significant scene changes, or occlusions, this fixed prior might become "stale," potentially limiting performance.
2. **Model-Specific Nature:** The interpretation of the single-step latent velocity field is tied to the Flow Matching parameterization used by the Wan 2.2 model. Its transferability to other video generative models with different architectures or training objectives would require further examination.
3. **Coarse Representation:** KAM provides a coarse visual-motion prior rather than an explicit, metrically calibrated 3D action plan. Tasks requiring highly precise contact or insertion may still necessitate additional geometric, tactile, or multi-view feedback.
4. **Simulation-Only Evaluation (Primary):** The main experimental results are based solely on simulation environments. While a small-scale real-world feasibility study was conducted, comprehensive real-world validation with controlled comparisons is pending.
5. **Single-Run Success Rates:** The reported success rates are from single training runs, without multi-seed confidence intervals. This leaves the assessment of training variance in low-data regimes for future work.
