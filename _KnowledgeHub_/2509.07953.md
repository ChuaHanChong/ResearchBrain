# RaC: Robot Learning for Long-Horizon Tasks by Scaling Recovery and Correction

> [!summary] Summary
> RaC adds a human-in-the-loop fine-tuning phase after imitation pre-training, where operators intervene on policy rollouts by first rewinding the robot to a familiar in-distribution state, then providing a corrective segment, and the episode terminates immediately. Trained with an unchanged flow-matching objective, the resulting policy retries failures and reaches 78.3% success on bimanual shirt-hanging using roughly 5 hours of data.

## Problem

- Imitation learning on clean teleoperated demonstrations plateaus far below perfect execution on contact-rich, deformable, long-horizon tasks even with thousands of expert episodes.
- Expert-only data omits out-of-distribution and failure states, so compounding errors in long-horizon tasks are never covered and additional demonstrations give diminishing returns.
- Prior intervention methods treat human takeover as optimal expert action pushing the task forward, collecting almost no recovery behavior and continuing rollouts that contaminate later sub-tasks.

## Method

- Rule 1, ==recover-then-correct==: every human intervention first rewinds the robot into a previously visited in-distribution region, then provides a corrective segment completing the current sub-task.
- Rule 2, ==terminate after intervention==: the episode ends once the intervention concludes, preventing later sub-task data from a mixed human-policy state distribution and reallocating budget to earlier sub-tasks.
- A ==VR clutch teleoperation== interface with local-frame relative pose deltas, plus a ==SAM2== gripper-visitation heatmap overlay showing operators which in-distribution regions to recover toward; policy is a 368M ==MM-DiT== trained by ==conditional flow matching==.

## Results

- On shirt-hanging RaC reaches **78.3%** success from about **5 hours** of data, beating ALOHA Unleashed's **75%** from roughly **89 hours** and GR-3's approximately **63.6%** from **116 hours**.
- Test-time scaling is linear in recovery count: success rate versus average recoveries per successful trajectory gives correlation r of **0.714**, **0.803** and **0.877** across the three real tasks.
- Ablations show RaC keeps a **1:1** to **1:2** recovery-to-correction ratio versus **1:3** to **1:10** without Rule 1; on simulated assembly RaC succeeds **61/100** versus **23/100** for full demos and **22/100** for HG-DAgger.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Data composition, not model or objective, is the binding constraint: adding suboptimal recovery segments beats adding more expert demonstrations at one tenth the collection cost.
> - Recovery is cheap to learn because valid past states are broad while goal states are narrow, so retries exponentially decay the chance of never succeeding on a sub-task.
> - Robots can exhibit reasoning-style test-time scaling directly in action space, spending longer rollouts on backtracking rather than more parameters or more data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical and widely recognized challenge in **robot learning**, specifically within the paradigm of **imitation learning (IL)**: the struggle to achieve robust and reliable performance on long-horizon, contact-rich tasks, even with vast amounts of expert demonstration data.

The broader landscape of robot learning has recently witnessed significant advancements through the scaling of data and model architectures. Works like RT-1, RT-2, ALOHA Unleashed, and Open X-Embodiment have demonstrated impressive capabilities on various atomic skills by training expressive policy architectures on large datasets of human teleoperation data. These "generalist" or "specialist" policies have shown promise in diverse manipulation tasks. However, as the authors highlight, this approach is "approaching a performance ceiling well below perfect task completion" for complex scenarios involving deformable objects, intricate contacts, and sequential sub-tasks that characterize long-horizon problems. The core limitation, as argued, is fundamental: current expert data is inherently biased towards clean, successful trajectories and lacks the crucial behaviors needed to handle out-of-distribution states, compounding errors, and environmental stochasticity that inevitably arise in real-world deployment.

**RaC positions itself as a critical evolution in human-in-the-loop (HIL) imitation learning.** While prior HIL methods, notably DAgger and its human-adapted versions like HG-DAgger and RoboCopilot, leverage human interventions to correct policy mistakes, they largely treat these interventions as optimal expert solutions to be imitated, primarily focusing on "correction" or pushing the task forward from failure states. RaC fundamentally departs from this by explicitly emphasizing the collection and learning of "recovery" behaviors—actions that bring the robot back to a familiar, in-distribution state before re-attempting a sub-task. This distinction is crucial because it challenges the conventional wisdom that only "expert" or "optimal" demonstrations are valuable. Instead, RaC argues that the ability to gracefully recover from errors is a distinct and learnable skill, essential for robustness and overall task success in complex environments.

The paper draws a compelling analogy to recent breakthroughs in **Large Language Models (LLMs)**, particularly in their reasoning capabilities through "chain-of-thought" (CoT) and "mid-training" strategies. Just as LLMs improve performance by spending more "tokens" on internal backtracking, trial-and-error, and self-verification, RaC proposes that robots can achieve similar gains by explicitly learning to execute recovery maneuvers in their action space. This novel connection between scaling in LLM reasoning and robotic action sequencing represents a significant conceptual contribution, suggesting a common underlying principle for achieving robust intelligence in different modalities.

Furthermore, RaC builds upon and differentiates itself from other works in "recovery and correction in imitation learning." While some approaches explore "rewind-and-refine" data collection or automatically generate recovery trajectories in simulation, RaC's distinct contribution is in treating recovery as a *learnable skill* from human demonstration rather than an engineered mechanism, and critically, in demonstrating its *scaling properties* and impact on data efficiency in real-world, complex bimanual tasks. This work, therefore, pushes the boundaries of practical and efficient robot learning by tackling the often-overlooked but pervasive issue of failure handling through a novel data collection and learning strategy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind RaC stems from the observed limitations of current imitation learning (IL) systems when applied to complex robotic tasks. Despite significant advancements in policy architectures and the availability of large human demonstration datasets, performance on "contact-rich, deformable-object, and long-horizon tasks" frequently "plateau[s] far below perfect execution." Even with thousands of expert demonstrations, state-of-the-art models struggle, for instance, to reliably hang a t-shirt with bimanual manipulators, often achieving only around 75% success.

The authors pinpoint the fundamental reason for this plateau: the **inefficiency and inherent bias of existing "expert" data collection procedures.** Human demonstrations are typically curated for clean, successful trajectories, which means they implicitly lack examples of how to recover from inevitable errors, handle stochasticity, or navigate out-of-distribution states. In long-horizon tasks, small errors can compound catastrophically, and a policy trained solely on success stories is ill-equipped to adapt when deviations occur. Such policies "fail to generalize to real-world stochasticity and dynamism, and exhibit diminishing returns with additional data."

Against this backdrop, the **key objective of RaC is to introduce an alternative paradigm for training robot policies that directly addresses these limitations.** Specifically, RaC aims to:
1. **Enhance Robustness and Efficiency:** Enable robotic policies to execute long-horizon, complex tasks more robustly and with significantly greater data efficiency.
2. **Explicitly Learn Recovery and Correction:** Imbue policies with the ability to perform recovery and correction behaviors, moving beyond merely mimicking optimal expert actions.
3. **Develop a Scalable Data Collection Protocol:** Design a human-in-the-loop data collection method that naturally generates high-quality, balanced recovery and correction trajectories.
4. **Demonstrate Test-Time Adaptability:** Show that policies trained with RaC can leverage these learned recovery skills at deployment time to increase overall task success, analogous to "chain-of-thought" reasoning in LLMs.

By achieving these objectives, RaC seeks to break the performance ceiling of conventional imitation learning and make real-world deployment of complex robotic skills more feasible and reliable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RaC proposes a novel, two-phase training methodology that extends traditional imitation learning (IL).

**Phase 1: Imitation Learning Pre-training.**
The process begins with an initial phase of standard imitation learning, where a robot policy is pre-trained on a budget of clean, successful human teleoperation data, referred to as "Round 0" data. This establishes a foundational set of "basic" useful skills for the task. This initial dataset can also be drawn from existing, off-the-shelf datasets.

**Phase 2: RaC (Recovery and Correction) Fine-tuning.**
This is the core innovation of RaC, an iterative human-in-the-loop fine-tuning phase after initial pre-training. The central idea is to train the policy on trajectories that explicitly interleave successful task executions with segments demonstrating recovery, retries, and adaptation.

**Human-in-the-Loop Data Collection Protocol:**
RaC introduces a lightweight, standardized human intervention protocol designed to encourage and amplify recovery and correction behaviors:
1. **Intervention Trigger:** Human teleoperators intervene the moment the running policy begins to deviate from the expected course or appears to be failing.
2. **Two-Rule Standardization for Interventions:**
 - **Rule 1: Recover then Correct:** Each human takeover is structured into two distinct phases:
 - **Recovery Segment:** The human operator first guides the robot back to a "familiar, in-distribution state." This teaches the robot how to reset itself to a known good state.
 - **Correction Segment:** Immediately following recovery, the operator provides a corrective sequence of actions that completes the current sub-task. This teaches the robot how to make progress from a stable state.
 - *Motivation:* This pairing ensures the policy learns both how to return to a robust state and how to proceed from it, exploiting a "verification-generation gap" where learning recovery is often easier and more sample-efficient than mastering complex corrections from out-of-distribution states.
 - **Rule 2: Termination After Intervention:** Once an intervention (recovery + correction) is complete, the entire episode is immediately terminated.
 - *Motivation:* This prevents the collection of "contaminated" data on later sub-tasks, which would result from a mixture of learned policy and human expert actions. It ensures that the collected data cleanly reflects targeted recovery-correction behaviors and allows the data collection budget to be more effectively allocated towards improving early sub-tasks.

**Shared Autonomy Interface:**
To facilitate smooth and intuitive human interventions, RaC employs custom hardware and software:
- **VR Joystick Interface:** Utilizes off-the-shelf Oculus Quest VR controllers.
- **"Clutch" Mechanism:** A side button on the controller acts as a "clutch." When pressed, controller motions directly map to end-effector control (human takes over); when released, the robot follows the learned policy.
- **Local-Frame Registration:** Relative pose deltas are used to parameterize end-effector commands, eliminating the need for global posture alignment and reducing operator effort and learning curve.

**Guidance for Teleoperators:**
To help operators adhere to Rule 1, a lightweight software tool is used during data collection:
- **Visitation Frequency Heatmap:** An image segmentation model (SAM2) tracks robot gripper visitation frequency from initial full demonstrations. This is rendered as an overlaid heatmap on the overhead camera display.
- **Visual Aid:** This heatmap visually indicates "in-distribution regions" where the robot grippers have previously been successful, guiding teleoperators on where to recover the robot.

**Policy Architecture and Training:**
- **High-Capacity Model:** A 300 million parameter multi-modal diffusion transformer (MM-DiT) architecture is used.
- **Inputs:** Robot observations (`o_t`) include RGB images from a top-view camera and two wrist cameras (processed by ResNet-50 encoders for real-world, ResNet-18 for simulation) and proprioceptive robot states (end-effector velocities, relative distances).
- **Outputs:** The policy predicts an action chunk (`A_t` = [a_t..., a_{t+H-1}]) with a horizon `H=60` (1 second of actions).
- **Objective Function:** Training is done using a conditional flow matching loss.
- **Data Aggregation:** The dataset (`D`) for training in each iterative round combines the initial full demonstrations, the newly collected human intervention segments (recovery and correction), and any full successful rollouts achieved by the current policy without human intervention.
- **Inference:** During deployment, actions are generated by taking 10 Euler integration steps using the learned vector field, with replanning every 0.5 seconds.

This comprehensive approach allows RaC to systematically collect and learn from diverse behaviors that are crucial for robust, long-horizon task execution, going beyond the limitations of purely expert-driven imitation learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of RaC across three real-world bimanual control tasks (shirt hanging, airtight container lid sealing, takeout box packing) and one simulated assembly task yielded several significant findings:

1. **Order of Magnitude Improvement in Data Efficiency:**
 - RaC achieved comparable or superior success rates with significantly less human data collection time and samples. For instance, on the challenging shirt-hanging task, RaC achieved a 78.3% success rate with only 5 hours of collected data. This contrasts sharply with prior state-of-the-art results (e.g., ALOHA Unleashed), which reported 75% success using approximately 89 hours (over 5000 demonstrations) for a similar task. This represents an order of magnitude improvement in data efficiency.
 - RaC demonstrated a steeper performance scaling curve (higher slope) compared to both batched full demonstration data collection and HG-DAgger baselines, indicating its superior efficiency in leveraging additional data.

2. **Superior Performance Against Baselines:**
 - RaC consistently outperformed both batched full expert demonstrations and HG-DAgger style human-in-the-loop data collection in terms of absolute task success rates and average task progress scores across all evaluated real-world tasks.
 - Even the HG-DAgger baseline, which showed performance trends consistent with prior work (outperforming batched data), was surpassed by RaC.

3. **Robustness of Intermediate Policies:**
 - Analysis of sub-task completion distributions showed that RaC policies, even in intermediate training rounds, rapidly and systematically reduced the fraction of rollouts that made little or no progress. This indicates that RaC effectively teaches policies to overcome early failures and achieve more consistent progress across sub-tasks, addressing the "long tail" of failure modes common in imitation learning. In contrast, simply scaling batched full demonstration data did not show the same consistent improvement across all sub-tasks.

4. **"o1-style" Test-Time Scaling for Robotic Policies:**
 - A remarkable finding was the observation of a strong, *linear relationship* between the number of recovery maneuvers a RaC-trained policy exhibited during test-time deployment and its overall task success rate. Policies that performed more recovery segments were significantly more successful.
 - This phenomenon is termed "test-time scaling" and is analogous to "o1-style" reasoning in Large Language Models (LLMs), where producing longer "chain-of-thought" (CoT) responses involving backtracking and error correction leads to higher performance. This suggests that the ability to adapt and retry (recovery) is a fundamental ingredient for robust intelligence in robotics, similar to how reasoning processes function in LLMs.

5. **Longer and More Successful Rollouts:**
 - Successful rollouts from RaC-trained policies were generally longer in duration compared to those from HG-DAgger or full demonstration policies (especially in simulation). This longer duration is directly attributable to the policy's ability to execute learned recovery behaviors, retry sub-tasks, and ultimately achieve success from situations where other policies would simply fail or terminate prematurely. Longer duration was correlated with higher overall success.

6. **Ablation Studies Validating RaC's Rules:**
 - **Rule 1 (Recover then Correct) Importance:** Ablations showed that the RaC protocol successfully encourages a balanced composition of recovery and corrective frames (roughly 1:1 to 1:2 ratio) in the intervention data. In contrast, conventional intervention data (HG-DAgger) without this explicit guidance skewed heavily towards corrective frames, with recovery segments becoming scarce (1:3 to 1:10 ratio). This confirmed that explicit guidance is necessary to collect diverse, balanced intervention data.
 - **Rule 2 (Termination After Intervention) Importance:** Experiments demonstrated that terminating an episode immediately after an intervention, as prescribed by RaC, led to more effective performance scaling than allowing the rollout to continue. This supports the hypothesis that continuing a rollout after human intervention can contaminate subsequent data with mixed human-policy induced states, hindering efficient learning for later sub-tasks.

In summary, RaC establishes a new state-of-the-art in data-efficient and robust robot learning for long-horizon tasks, validated by substantial empirical gains and key insights into the nature of adaptive robotic intelligence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RaC represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the practical challenges of deploying autonomous systems for complex, real-world tasks. Its potential impact spans both practical applications and fundamental research understanding:

1. **Bridging the Performance Gap in Real-World Robotics:**
 - **Enhanced Robustness:** By explicitly teaching robots how to recover from errors and stochasticity, RaC policies are inherently more robust than those trained purely on successful, expert demonstrations. This is crucial for real-world deployment where perfect execution is rarely achievable, and environments are dynamic and unpredictable.
 - **Dramatic Improvement in Data Efficiency:** The demonstrated 10x reduction in data collection time for complex bimanual tasks is transformative. Data collection is often the most time-consuming and expensive bottleneck in real-robot learning. RaC makes it significantly more feasible and cost-effective to train robots for new, complex skills, accelerating the pace of robotic deployment in various domains (e.g., manufacturing, logistics, domestic assistance).

2. **Challenging and Evolving the Imitation Learning Paradigm:**
 - RaC fundamentally challenges the long-held assumption in imitation learning that only "optimal" or "expert" demonstrations are valuable. It introduces the critical insight that explicitly learning "suboptimal" recovery behaviors is paramount for achieving overall optimal task success in the face of real-world complexities. This could lead to a re-evaluation of data collection strategies across the entire field.
 - The framework's effectiveness through simple, yet powerful, rules for human intervention data composition ("recover then correct" and "terminate after intervention") provides a clear blueprint for future HIL systems.

3. **Conceptual Link to AI Reasoning and Test-Time Scaling:**
 - The discovery of "o1-style" test-time scaling in robotic policies, drawing a direct analogy to Chain-of-Thought reasoning in LLMs, is a profound conceptual contribution. It suggests a unifying principle across different AI modalities: that "thinking longer" or "being more adaptive" (through recovery/re-attempting) can lead to higher performance and generalization. This finding opens new avenues for evaluating and understanding intelligence in robotic systems, moving beyond mere task completion to assessing adaptive problem-solving capabilities. It also encourages a holistic view of intelligence where failures and subsequent recoveries are not just errors but learning opportunities that contribute to robust performance.

4. **Enabling Future Research Directions:**
 - **Foundation for Online Reinforcement Learning:** The authors propose that RaC-trained policies could serve as excellent initializations for online reinforcement learning (RL) on real robots. Unlike typical IL pre-trained policies, which might struggle with exploration after failure, RaC policies, with their learned recovery skills, could provide more structured exploration, naturally returning to known states to re-attempt tasks. This could significantly mitigate the "brittleness" often seen in RL fine-tuning from IL policies.
 - **Integration with Generalist Models:** Applying RaC on top of emerging generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (like Gato, Octo, Gemini Robotics) is a promising future direction. RaC could imbue these broadly capable models with the fine-grained, robust recovery skills necessary for specific, challenging manipulation tasks, thus marrying broad generalization with task-specific resilience.
 - **Rigorous Study of Emergent Behaviors:** The work motivates a deeper study into whether systematic recovery behaviors emerge spontaneously in large VLA models and how to rigorously quantify and scale such behaviors.

In conclusion, RaC is a landmark contribution that moves robot learning beyond merely mimicking success to actively learning from and adapting to failure. By providing a scalable and efficient methodology for instilling recovery and correction behaviors, it propels the field closer to realizing truly autonomous and resilient robotic systems capable of operating reliably in complex, unstructured real-world environments.
