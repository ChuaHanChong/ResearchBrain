# Closing the Loop on Runtime Monitors with Fallback-Safe MPC

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework integrates runtime monitoring of machine learning perception with robust Model Predictive Control and fallback strategies, ensuring safety when perception degrades due to out-of-distribution data. It offers probabilistic end-to-end guarantees through sample-efficient calibration using conformal prediction techniques.

## Problem

- Machine learning perception, particularly deep neural networks, frequently exhibits unreliable performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) data, creating safety hazards for autonomous robotic systems.
- Current runtime monitoring and fault-tolerant control approaches often lack formal safety guarantees or struggle to model arbitrary error behavior of high-dimensional, learned perception systems.
- Unplanned or generic fallback maneuvers, triggered by perception failures, can introduce new safety risks if not carefully designed and integrated into the overall control strategy.

## Method

- The Fallback-Safe Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework integrates a robust control strategy with a perception-aware runtime monitor and explicitly plans for a safety-preserving fallback.
- It introduces the concept of a "safe recovery set" and associated "recovery policy" which can maintain system safety using only reliable, non-ML measurements.
- A runtime monitor is calibrated using conformal prediction to detect perception faults with probabilistic guarantees, specifically targeting the first instance of error beyond specified tolerance for the MPC controller.

## Results

- The framework provides theoretical guarantees for recursive feasibility and system safety, contingent on the runtime monitor's ability to detect perception faults.
- Simulation experiments with a planar quadrotor and a photo-realistic aircraft simulator demonstrated that Fallback-Safe MPC successfully prevented system failures (e.g., crashes, runway excursions) in scenarios with degraded vision, outperforming naive baseline approaches.
- The conformal monitor empirically satisfied its theoretical false negative rate bounds, exhibiting a low false positive rate (near 0% for 5% risk tolerance) and proving sample-efficient, requiring significantly less data than retraining perception models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - End-to-end safety guarantees for perception-enabled systems are achievable by combining robust Model Predictive Control with a calibrated runtime monitor that ensures the feasibility of a non-perception-dependent fallback plan.
> - Conformal prediction can be adapted for sequential data in dynamic systems by focusing on the "stopping time" of the first perception fault, offering distribution-free safety guarantees for the runtime monitor.
> - Modifying nominal control operations to always ensure a feasible path to a predefined "safe recovery set" is crucial for effective fallback strategies, rather than assuming generic safe recovery.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous robotic systems increasingly rely on machine learning (ML), particularly deep neural networks (DNNs), for perception tasks such as extracting task-relevant information from high-dimensional sensor data. A known challenge with modern ML systems is their unreliable performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) data, which corresponds to inputs dissimilar from their training data. These OOD scenarios, representing edge cases and rare anomalous situations, pose a significant safety risk during robotic deployment.

The broader research landscape has seen various approaches to address these challenges:

- **Runtime Monitoring and OOD Detection:** Algorithms have been developed to monitor the performance of ML components at runtime, aiming to detect inference errors caused by OOD inputs. These algorithms often rely on auxiliary DNNs, approximate uncertainty quantification, or reconstruction errors. However, many of these methods provide heuristic correlations rather than formal guarantees, and their integration into an end-to-end perception and control stack has been limited.
- **Fault-Tolerant Control:** Existing work in fault-tolerant control, including approaches like passive-backup, abort-safe, or contingency planning within Model Predictive Control (MPC) frameworks, aims to maintain system feasibility under actuation or sensor failures. These methods often assume perfect fault detection and nominally perfect system function, which is not always applicable to the subtle and often partial failures of ML-based perception.
- **Safety Filters:** Methods like robust MPC, Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), and Hamilton-Jacobi reachability have been developed as safety filters to minimally modify a black-box policy’s actions, ensuring the invariance of a safe state space subset. However, these typically assume perfect state knowledge or the continuous availability of an estimate with known accuracy. When ML perception components fail, full state estimation can become impossible, requiring a different approach. Furthermore, traditional safety filters often adopt a "zero-confidence" view of learned components, which can be overly conservative for tasks where ML is critical.
- **Robust Control from Imperfect Measurements (Output-Feedback MPC):** This field relies on state estimators that persistently satisfy known error bounds, constructed from known measurement models and observability assumptions. Such MPC controllers robustly satisfy state and input constraints. However, for high-dimensional measurements like images, first-principles models are not feasible, necessitating DNNs whose error behavior can be arbitrary on OOD inputs. While some approaches learn error behavior of vision systems, they often assume a fixed environment, failing to account for external changes (e.g., weather) that cause OOD inputs.
- **Conformal Prediction:** Recent advancements in conformal prediction offer distribution-free guarantees on prediction correctness, are sample-efficient, and do not rely on assumptions about the data-generating distribution. However, existing conformal methods primarily address independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data, posing a challenge for sequential predictions along correlated dynamical system trajectories.

This work addresses critical gaps by:
1. Integrating OOD detection into a robust control framework to provide end-to-end safety guarantees.
2. Developing a control strategy that is explicitly aware of the limitations of perception and designs safe fallback mechanisms, rather than assuming their inherent safety or perfect state estimation.
3. Calibrating the runtime monitor to detect specific violations of nominal control assumptions, moving beyond generic OOD detection.
4. Adapting conformal prediction techniques to provide guarantees in a sequential, correlated data setting relevant to robotic trajectories.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent unreliability of deep-learned perception models when deployed in real-world robotic systems. These models, critical for extracting task-relevant information from high-dimensional sensor streams, frequently encounter inputs dissimilar from their training data (OOD inputs). Such encounters can lead to erratic and unsafe behavior, posing a fundamental safety risk to autonomous robots.

Existing solutions often fall short in addressing this challenge comprehensively. Many monitor-in-the-loop systems either assume that perception estimation errors will always satisfy known bounds or that a universally safe fallback strategy exists and can always be triggered. However, naively triggering a fallback, such as a drone landing, can introduce new hazards if the fallback maneuver itself is not carefully designed to be safe within the prevailing environment. Moreover, traditional robust control methods often require modeling the error behavior of sensors, which is challenging for high-dimensional, learned perception systems that can exhibit arbitrary errors on OOD data.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Framework for Safety Certification in Novel Contexts:** To present a framework capable of certifying the safety of a perception-enabled system when deployed in environments or conditions (novel contexts) that may differ from its training data distribution.
2. **Integration of Robust MPC with Perception-Aware Fallbacks:** To leverage robust Model Predictive Control (MPC) to control the system using perception estimates while concurrently ensuring the feasibility of a safety-preserving fallback plan. This fallback plan is explicitly designed not to rely on the potentially unreliable perception system.
3. **Calibrated Runtime Monitoring for Perception Degradation:** To calibrate a runtime monitor, utilizing conformal prediction techniques, to certifiably detect when the perception system's performance degrades beyond the tolerance specified for the MPC controller. This aims to provide an end-to-end safety assurance for the overall system.
4. **Guaranteed Safety of Fallback Strategies:** To ensure the inherent safety of the fallback strategy itself, moving beyond the assumption of a generic, "catch-all" safe fallback. This involves minimally modifying nominal system operation to proactively ensure the robot can transition into a defined safe recovery set.
5. **Quantitative Performance Assessment for Control:** To explicitly quantify the performance of the DNN perception component and the runtime monitor. This information is used to inform control decisions without resorting to overly conservative worst-case assumptions that would limit nominal system utility.
6. **Robustness to Arbitrarily Severe DNN Failures:** To account for the possibility of DNN failures of arbitrary severity through the design and integration of a robust runtime monitor.

These objectives collectively aim to bridge the gap between reliable ML perception and provably safe robotic control, particularly in the face of unexpected environmental changes and OOD data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Fallback-Safe Model Predictive Control (MPC), integrates a robust control strategy with a calibrated runtime monitor to ensure the safety of perception-enabled systems operating in potentially novel or out-of-distribution (OOD) environments.

**Problem Formulation:**
The system is modeled as a discrete-time dynamical system with state $x_t$, control input $u_t$, disturbance $w_t$, and high-dimensional observation $z_t$ (e.g., images). The environment variable $\rho$ is sampled once per episode, leading to a trajectory-dependent joint distribution $p_\rho$ for disturbances and observations. The objective is to ensure safety, defined as satisfying state ($X$) and input ($U$) constraints over a finite duration $t_{lim}$ with a specified risk tolerance $\delta$.
Key assumptions include:
- **Perception:** A black-box system produces state estimates $\hat{x}_t$ from observations.
- **Fallback Measurement:** A reliable, known measurement $y_t = g(x_t)$ is always available, but the system is not observable using $y_t$ alone, meaning perception is nominally necessary.
- **OOD/Anomaly Detection:** An algorithm provides a scalar anomaly signal $a_t$ indicating the quality of $\hat{x}_t$. No assumptions are made on the quality or correlation of $a_t$ with perception error $e_t = x_t - \hat{x}_t$.

**Fallback-Safe MPC Framework:**
The framework operates by controlling the system using perception estimates ($\hat{x}_t$) during nominal, in-distribution operation. A runtime monitor, based on $a_t$, detects when $\hat{x}_t$ becomes unreliable, triggering a transition to a fallback policy $\pi_R$, which relies only on the reliable fallback measurement $y_t$.

1. **Recovery Policies and Recovery Sets:**
 - The concept of a **safe recovery set** $X_R \subseteq X$ is introduced. $X_R$ is defined as a robust positive invariant (RPI) set under a specific **recovery policy** $\pi_R: Y \to U$. This means that if the system's state is within $X_R$, applying $\pi_R$ (which only uses fallback measurements $y_t$) ensures the state remains within $X_R$ despite disturbances. This formalizes the idea of a safe state where full perception is not required (e.g., a landed drone in an open field).

2. **Planning With Fallbacks (Robust MPC):**
 - **Reliability Definition:** A parametric compact state uncertainty set $E_\theta$ (e.g., an infinity-norm bound) defines what constitutes "reliable" perception. Perception is unreliable (a fault) if $e_t \notin E_\theta$. The parameter $\theta$ (e.g., matrix A and bound $\alpha$) allows a trade-off between nominal conservatism and fallback eagerness.
 - **MPC Formulation (Problem 8):** At each time step $t$, the controller solves a finite-time robust optimal control problem online. This MPC simultaneously optimizes two policy sequences over a prediction horizon $T$:
 - A nominal policy sequence $u_{t:t+T|t}$ (state-feedback, assuming perception is reliable).
 - A fallback policy sequence $u^F_{t:t+T|t}$ (only relying on $y_t$).
 - **Constraints:**
 - The k-step reachable sets of the true state $R_k(\hat{x}_t, u^F_{t:t+T|t})$, calculated robustly with respect to perception errors $e_t \in E_\theta$ and disturbances $w_t \in W$, must remain within state constraints $X$.
 - Fallback inputs must satisfy input constraints $U$.
 - A critical terminal constraint: The reachable set at $T+1$ steps, $R_{T+1}$, must be contained within the chosen safe recovery set $X_R$. This ensures that a path to safety always exists.
 - The first nominal and fallback control inputs must be identical ($u_{t|t}(\hat{x}_t) = u^F_{t|t}(y_t)$), allowing a seamless transition if the monitor triggers.
 - **Recursive Feasibility:** An assumption is made that the recovery set $X_R$ is invariant under the estimator dynamics (Equation 6) in nominal conditions, similar to terminal invariant set assumptions in robust MPC.
 - **Algorithm 1 (Fallback-Safe MPC):** The algorithm applies the optimal nominal input $u^\star_{t|t}(\hat{x}_t)$ if the runtime monitor $w(a_t)$ does not trigger. If the monitor triggers at $t_{fail}$, the previously computed fallback policy sequence $u^{F,\star}_{t|t_{fail}-1}$ is applied until the recovery set $X_R$ is reached (within $T$ steps), after which the permanent recovery policy $\pi_R(y_t)$ is engaged.

**Calibrating OOD Detectors with Conformal Inference:**
The objective is to construct a runtime monitor $w: \mathbb{R} \to \{0,1\}$ that triggers with high probability upon a perception fault ($e_t \notin E_\theta$) while minimizing false alarms.

- **Challenge:** Standard conformal prediction methods assume i.i.d. data, which is generally not true for sequential observations in a dynamical system.
- **Insight:** For end-to-end safety, it is sufficient to guarantee that the runtime monitor issues an alarm at the *first* instance of a perception fault with high probability. This allows the use of i.i.d. conformal prediction on "stopping time observables."
- **Stopping Time Definition ($t_{stop}$):** The first time step where a perception fault occurs ($e_t \notin E_\theta$) or the episode time limit is reached.
- **Algorithm 2:** This algorithm adapts a conformal prediction technique from previous work. It requires a calibration dataset $D$ of trajectories sampled i.i.d. from a trajectory distribution $P$. From this, a dataset $D_{stop}$ is generated, consisting of (state, estimate, anomaly score) tuples at each trajectory's $t_{stop}$. The algorithm then uses $D_{stop}$ to compute a quantile $q$, which determines the threshold for the anomaly signal $a_{test}$ to trigger an alarm, based on a desired risk tolerance $\delta$. The core principle is to compare the test anomaly score to the distribution of anomaly scores observed at actual fault times in the calibration data.

**Tractable Reformulations (Appendices):**
- For linear-quadratic systems with polytopic constraints, the robust MPC problem (8) can be reformulated as a convex Quadratic Program (QP) by analytically computing reachable sets using Minkowski sums of error sets and tightening constraints (Appendix V).
- For nonlinear systems, an approximate solution can be found using Particle MPC (PMPC), which samples multiple trajectories to account for uncertainties and solves the problem via Sequential Convex Programming (SCP) (Appendix VI).

This methodology combines robust control principles with data-driven uncertainty quantification to provide a framework for reliable operation of ML-enabled robotic systems under perception uncertainty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical guarantees and empirical validations of the proposed Fallback-Safe MPC framework and its conformal calibration method.

**Theoretical Guarantees:**

1. **Theorem 1 (Fallback Safety):** This theorem establishes the recursive feasibility and safety of the Fallback-Safe MPC (Algorithm 1). It states that if the MPC problem (8) is initially feasible at $t=0$ and the runtime monitor $w(a_0)=0$ (no initial fault), and critically, if the runtime monitor does not miss any perception faults (i.e., $x_t - \hat{x}_t \in E_\theta$ for all $t$ before the monitor triggers, $t < t_{fail}$), then:
 - The MPC problem (8) remains feasible for all time steps prior to a detected fault ($t < t_{fail}$).
 - The closed-loop system satisfies all state and input constraints ($x_t \in X, u_t \in U$) for all $t \geq 0$.
 This theorem underscores that the safety of the system is contingent on the monitor's ability to detect faults without omission.

2. **Lemma 2 (Conformal Calibration):** This lemma provides a bound on the false negative rate (FNR) of Algorithm 2, which calibrates the runtime monitor. When deploying a trajectory $\tau \sim P'$ (potentially different from the calibration distribution $P$), the probability of a false negative (i.e., the monitor fails to trigger when a perception fault occurs) is bounded by $\delta + 1/(|A|+1) + TV(P_{t_{stop}|fault}, P'_{t_{stop}|fault})$. Here, $\delta$ is the user-defined risk tolerance, $|A|$ is the number of fault observations in the calibration data, and $TV(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the total variation distance between the distributions of fault events in calibration and deployment. This bound is notable for its distribution-free nature, with the $TV$ term accounting for distribution shifts between calibration and deployment.

3. **Theorem 2 (End-to-end Guarantee):** Combining Theorem 1 and Lemma 2, this theorem provides a comprehensive end-to-end safety guarantee. It states that, given initial feasibility and no initial fault detection, the probability of the system satisfying all state and input constraints throughout the trajectory is at least $1 - \delta - 1/(|A|+1) - TV(P_{t_{stop}|fault}, P'_{t_{stop}|fault})$.

4. **Corollary 1:** This corollary simplifies the end-to-end guarantee under specific conditions. If the environment distribution $P_\rho$ is consistent between data collection and deployment, and the calibration dataset $D$ is collected by running the Fallback-Safe MPC with a ground-truth supervisor (an oracle monitor), then the $TV$ distance term becomes zero. This implies that safety can be certified with probability at least $1 - \delta - 1/(|A|+1)$, making the framework practically applicable via a two-step procedure: first, collect data with an oracle, then deploy with the calibrated monitor.

**Simulation Results:**

The framework was evaluated on two distinct simulation environments:

1. **Planar Quadrotor (Simplified):**
 - **Necessity of Planning with Fallbacks:** In a vision-based landing scenario, the Fallback-Safe MPC successfully plans to abort the landing and fly to a designated safe recovery set (sufficient altitude) when a vision fault occurs. A comparative "Naive Tube MPC" (which assumes perception is always reliable) crashes in this scenario, demonstrating the importance of proactive fallback planning.
 - **Necessity of Modifying Nominal Operations:** In a scenario requiring navigation through an area containing disjoint recovery sets (e.g., crossing a road by landing on either side), the Fallback-Safe MPC successfully maintains feasibility to both safe recovery regions by adjusting its nominal trajectory (e.g., slowing down before crossing) to ensure a safe fallback path is always available. A baseline "Unsafe Fallback MPC" (which computes fallbacks post-hoc without modifying nominal operations) is forced to crash-land in the unsafe region, illustrating the importance of integrating fallback feasibility into the nominal control design.

2. **X-Plane 11 Aircraft Simulator (Photo-realistic):**
 - **Scenario:** Autonomous aircraft taxiing, using a DNN to estimate heading error (HE) and cross-track error (CTE) from an outboard camera. The DNN was trained on clear, morning weather but deployed in various OOD weather conditions (e.g., night, rain, snow). An autoencoder reconstruction error served as the anomaly signal. The fallback strategy was to brake the aircraft to a stop within the runway boundaries.
 - **Conformal Monitor Validation:** Using 100 training trajectories for calibration and 900 for evaluation, the empirical FNR of Algorithm 2 was found to satisfy the theoretical bound derived in Lemma 2 for various risk tolerance values $\delta$.
 - **False Positive Rate (FPR):** The FPR (unnecessary fallback triggers) was observed to be near 0 even for risk tolerances as low as 5%. This indicates that the calibration procedure is sample-efficient and not overly conservative, achieving effective OOD detection without excessive interruptions.
 - **Effectiveness in Preventing Failures:** In the test trajectories, over 80% of the instances where the fallback was triggered would have resulted in an aircraft failure (running off the runway) if the intervention had not occurred. This demonstrates the practical efficacy of the framework in avoiding hazardous situations.
 - **Sample Efficiency:** The framework achieved its safety guarantees with "orders of magnitude fewer samples" than would be required to retrain the perception system for the novel environments.

In summary, the research provides a robust theoretical foundation for integrating runtime monitoring and fallback strategies with MPC, validated by simulations demonstrating its ability to maintain safety in critical perception-degraded scenarios while remaining practical in terms of data requirements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the field of safe autonomous systems, with potential impacts across various robotic applications.

**Bridging Gaps in Safe AI Deployment:**
The work directly addresses a critical challenge in deploying ML-enabled robots: their unreliability on out-of-distribution (OOD) data. By creating a framework that tightly integrates runtime monitoring for perception performance with robust control and fallback strategies, it provides a method to move beyond heuristic safety assessments. This integration is crucial for transitioning powerful but fallible deep learning models from controlled environments to real-world, uncertain conditions.

**Formal End-to-End Safety Guarantees:**
A key significance is the provision of end-to-end probabilistic safety guarantees for perception-enabled systems. Unlike many existing approaches that either assume perfect sensing or rely on approximate uncertainty quantification, this framework offers certifiable bounds on system safety, even when perception errors can be arbitrarily severe due to OOD inputs. This level of formal assurance is essential for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving, aerial delivery, and industrial robotics.

**Novel Concepts for Fallback Design:**
The introduction of "safe recovery sets" and associated "recovery policies" offers a new conceptual tool for designing effective fallback strategies. By defining state space subsets that can be made invariant without full state knowledge (i.e., solely relying on reliable fallback sensors), the framework provides a principled way to formalize and guarantee the safety of recovery maneuvers. This moves beyond the assumption of generic "catch-all" fallbacks, which might themselves introduce new hazards.

**Practical and Sample-Efficient Calibration:**
The adaptation of conformal prediction for calibrating runtime monitors is highly significant for practical deployment. Conformal methods are inherently distribution-free and sample-efficient. By identifying that it is sufficient to guarantee detection of the *first* perception fault, the authors circumvent the complexities of sequential analysis for correlated data. The simulation results confirm that safety guarantees can be achieved with orders of magnitude fewer samples than required for retraining perception networks, making the framework viable for real-world scenarios where extensive data collection for every new context is impractical.

**Enhanced System Robustness and Performance:**
The framework enables robotic systems to maintain safety even when their primary perception system degrades. By planning for fallbacks and minimally modifying nominal operations to ensure fallback feasibility, robots can continue to leverage the high utility of ML perception for most of the time, only transitioning to a conservative fallback when necessary. This balance between performance and safety is crucial for advanced autonomy.

**Catalyst for Future Research:**
The open-sourcing of the simulation platform (X-Plane 11 aircraft taxiing simulator) provides a valuable benchmark for the research community. This will facilitate further studies on robust perception, runtime monitoring, and safe control, enabling direct comparisons and accelerating progress in the field. The identified limitations, such as the occasional false positive triggers and the current inability to switch back to nominal operations, also serve as clear directions for future work, indicating a vibrant area for ongoing investigation.

In conclusion, this research presents a scientifically sound and practically relevant advancement in ensuring the safety of autonomous systems that rely on machine learning for perception. Its combination of robust control, formal guarantees, and sample-efficient calibration addresses a critical need in the journey towards reliable and certifiably safe autonomous robotics.
