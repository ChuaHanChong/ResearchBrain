# OmniHallu: Unified Hallucination Detection for Cross-Modal Comprehension and Generation in Multimodal Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> OmniHallu is a unified claim-level hallucination detection framework covering comprehension and generation across image, video, and audio. It decomposes outputs into atomic claims, verifies them with modality-specific expert ensembles, and aggregates evidence via a reasoning model. A GRPO-trained compact verifier approximates the ensemble's decision boundary, cutting expert calls by 66%. It also contributes OmniHallu-Bench, 10,000 human-annotated samples over six cross-modal tasks.

## Problem

- Detection methods target one modality or one modality pair, so image, video, and audio hallucination findings stay isolated and offer no cross-modal comparison.
- Most prior work studies comprehension only, neglecting generation tasks even though both share hallucination patterns rooted in weak perception and reasoning.
- Existing benchmarks judge at response level with few instances and no rationales, so they cannot localize which specific claim in an output is hallucinated.

## Method

- **Atomic Claim Decomposition** splits captions (comprehension) or prompts (generation) into self-contained verifiable claims via GPT-4.1, enabling claim-level rather than response-level judgments.
- **Modality-aware expert verification**: ==Grounding DINO== for open-set object checks, three-MLLM ensembles per modality with equal-weight majority voting, plus QA-style temporal decomposition for video.
- A compact Qwen2.5-VL-7B verifier trained by ==GRPO== with label, expert-evidence, and calibration rewards emits SUP/UNSUP/ABS, abstaining to route hard claims to the full ensemble.

## Results

- Macro-F1 beats the strongest per-task baseline by **3.4** to **8.1** points, reaching **82.95** on image-to-text and **73.39** on text-to-audio, significant at p below **0.01**.
- Removing claim decomposition costs **6.6** to **7.9** macro-F1 points versus **4.5** to **5.3** for dropping multi-expert voting; three experts beat one by roughly **5** points.
- Filter mode hits **80.52** macro-F1 with experts called on only **34.2%** of samples, cutting cost from about **$0.12** to **$0.05** and latency from **15s** to **6s**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Claim granularity matters more than ensemble size: decomposition quality is the pipeline bottleneck, so invest there before adding more verifier models.
> - Multi-agent verification helps most where single models are weakest, giving the largest gains on audio, the modality with the least mature expert tooling.
> - Failure modes differ by modality: image is perception-limited, video reasoning-limited, audio tool-limited, and shared expert blind spots defeat majority voting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) increasingly support image, video, audio, and text understanding and generation. Their applications include visual question answering, image and video captioning, text-to-image generation, audio understanding, and multimedia content creation. However, these systems can produce hallucinations: outputs that introduce entities, properties, relationships, or events that are not supported by the input, or that contradict it.

Prior research has generally evaluated hallucinations within a single modality or task direction. For example, POPE and AMBER focus primarily on image-to-text object hallucination, FactVC and related work address video caption factuality, and audio-focused studies assess whether audio-language systems ground their descriptions in acoustic evidence. Separate research has investigated prompt alignment in text-to-image and text-to-video generation. This fragmentation makes it difficult to compare hallucination behavior across modalities and prevents researchers from determining which detection techniques transfer between comprehension and generation tasks.

OmniHallu addresses this gap by placing six tasks under a common claim-level evaluation framework: image-to-text (I2T), video-to-text (V2T), audio-to-text (A2T), text-to-image (T2I), text-to-video (T2V), and text-to-audio (T2A). The paper extends the “decompose, verify, and aggregate” paradigm previously used in image-text hallucination detection. Its central premise is that hallucinations across different modalities can be analyzed using a shared semantic unit—an atomic claim—while the evidence-gathering process must remain modality-specific.

The work therefore contributes both a benchmark and a detection system. It differs from system-ranking approaches, such as cross-model consistency evaluation, by assigning labels to individual claims and providing explanatory rationales. It also broadens the scope of existing benchmarks by including generation tasks, which require checking whether generated media reflects a textual prompt rather than merely whether a caption describes source media accurately.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper has four primary objectives.

First, it seeks to establish a unified definition of hallucination across cross-modal tasks. The authors define an output as hallucinated if it contains at least one semantic claim that is unsupported by, or contradictory to, the input. This definition applies differently depending on task direction. In comprehension, the generated text is checked against the image, video, or audio input. In generation, the textual prompt is treated as the source of intended semantic claims, and the generated image, video, or audio is checked for prompt alignment.

Second, the study aims to create a sufficiently broad benchmark for side-by-side comparison. The authors argue that existing datasets are limited by modality, task direction, response-level evaluation, or lack of explanatory annotations. OmniHallu-Bench is designed to provide claim-level labels, segment-level information, and rationales across six task types.

Third, the authors investigate whether a common detection architecture can work across modalities while incorporating modality-specific evidence. Image verification may rely on object detectors and vision-language models, whereas video verification requires temporal localization and audio verification depends on acoustic analysis. The framework is intended to identify the shared reasoning structure and the modality-specific components that cannot be treated uniformly.

Fourth, the work addresses computational cost. The full system uses several expert models and a reasoning model, which creates latency and API expenses. The authors therefore train a smaller verifier to make confident decisions on easy cases and defer uncertain cases to the full expert pipeline.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. OmniHallu-Bench

The benchmark contains **10,000 samples** distributed across the six tasks. Comprehension accounts for 60 percent of the data and generation for 40 percent. Image, video, and audio samples are allocated in a 5:3:2 ratio. The intended hallucination categories are object, attribute, event, and relation errors, with target proportions of 35, 25, 25, and 15 percent, respectively.

For comprehension, the authors use established datasets such as COCO Captions, Nocaps, Flickr30k, MSVD, MSRVTT, VATEX, AudioCaps, ClothoV2, and AudioSetCaps. Captions are generated using several image-, video-, and audio-language models. For generation, prompts are drawn from T2I-CompBench++, HRS-Bench, T2V-CompBench, FETV, WavText5K, FSD50K, and SoundDescs. These prompts are processed by multiple image, video, and audio generation systems, including DALL-E 3, Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large, Midjourney v6, Open-Sora, CogVideoX, Make-an-Audio, AudioGPT, and AudioLCM. Using several source models is intended to reduce dependence on artifacts associated with one generator.

The annotation procedure begins with atomic claim decomposition using GPT-4.1 with self-reflection. Three trained annotators then validate whether the claims accurately represent the sample and classify each claim as hallucinated or non-hallucinated. A sample is retained only when all three annotators reach consensus. Approximately 24.3 percent of initial samples were discarded because of disagreement. Before consensus filtering, Fleiss’ kappa was 0.89 for image, 0.86 for video, and 0.83 for audio. The final benchmark contains 4,000 human-curated samples and 6,000 model-generated samples that were human-audited.

The overall hallucination rate is 42.8 percent. Generation samples have a higher reported rate than comprehension samples, at 48.1 percent compared with 39.2 percent. The average number of claims varies by task, from 3.1 for T2A to 5.6 for V2T.

#### 3.2. Hallucination Taxonomy

The taxonomy includes four cross-modal categories:

1. **Object hallucinations**, in which nonexistent entities are introduced.
2. **Attribute hallucinations**, in which properties such as color, size, or timbre are incorrect.
3. **Relation hallucinations**, involving incorrect spatial, temporal, or causal relationships.
4. **Event hallucinations**, involving fabricated actions, omitted actions, or incorrect event sequences.

This taxonomy provides a common vocabulary, although it necessarily compresses modality-specific errors. For example, temporal ordering and causal attribution are distinct video problems, while source separation and timbre confusion are different audio problems.

#### 3.3. Multi-Agent Detection Pipeline

The detection system has three stages.

**Atomic claim decomposition** converts the caption or prompt into discrete, self-contained statements. For comprehension, claims are extracted from the model-generated caption. For generation, claims are extracted from the textual prompt and checked against the generated media. A 300-sample comparison with human references reports average claim coverage of 0.92, redundancy of 1.15, and agreement of 0.88.

**Modality-aware verification** uses specialized experts. In image tasks, Grounding DINO 1.5 Pro verifies object presence, while an ensemble of Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5, and GPT-4.1 assesses attributes, relations, and events. For video, claims are converted into targeted question-answering queries, allowing experts to inspect relevant temporal segments and frame-level evidence. The video experts are Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5, and VideoLLaMA3. For audio, Qwen2-Audio, GAMA, and SALMONN provide independent judgments.

Expert outputs are combined through equal-weight majority voting. The paper uses equal voting because confidence scores from different models are not considered sufficiently calibrated for reliable weighted aggregation.

**Reasoning-based aggregation** then receives the claims, expert judgments, evidence traces, and confidence information. GPT-5.2 produces the final claim-level label and an explanatory rationale. GPT-4.1 is also evaluated as a controlled alternative.

#### 3.4. Trainable Verifier

To reduce the number of expert calls, the authors initialize a compact verifier from Qwen2.5-VL-7B. It predicts one of three labels: supported, unsupported, or abstain. Unsupported corresponds to hallucination, supported to non-hallucination, and abstention triggers the full expert pipeline.

The verifier is trained with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Its reward combines agreement with ground-truth labels, agreement with multi-agent consensus, and a calibration penalty for overconfident decisions when expert evidence conflicts. Confident predictions above 0.85 bypass the expert ensemble, while uncertain cases are deferred.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The reported multi-agent framework achieves the following macro-F1 scores:

- **I2T:** 82.95
- **T2I:** 81.81
- **V2T:** 77.14
- **T2V:** 75.63
- **A2T:** 74.11
- **T2A:** 73.39

Compared with the strongest baseline for each task, the improvements range from 3.4 to 8.1 percentage points. The paper reports statistical significance for all tasks using bootstrap testing with 10,000 resamples and a threshold of *p* < 0.01.

Performance follows a modality gradient: image tasks score higher than video tasks, which score higher than audio tasks. The authors attribute this pattern to both modality complexity and differences in available expert capability. Image verification benefits from mature object-detection and vision-language tools, whereas audio verification operates with smaller and less established specialist models.

Within each modality, comprehension outperforms generation. I2T exceeds T2I by 1.14 points, V2T exceeds T2V by 1.51 points, and A2T exceeds T2A by 0.72 points. This difference reflects the asymmetry between checking a claim against an observed source and checking whether generated media realizes a textual specification.

Ablation experiments indicate that atomic claim decomposition is the most influential component. Removing it reduces macro-F1 by 6.58 to 7.93 points across tasks. Removing multi-expert voting reduces performance by 4.47 to 5.31 points. These results suggest that claim granularity contributes more consistently than adding expert diversity, although both components are useful.

The hallucination type analysis reports a difficulty ordering of object, attribute, event, and relation errors. Object errors are comparatively easier to verify because they can often be grounded directly. Relations require reasoning over multiple entities and their interactions, and the framework reports an average improvement of 9.2 points for this category relative to the comparison systems.

The trainable verifier achieves 78.94 macro-F1 without expert calls under GRPO, compared with 71.42 for supervised fine-tuning and 76.85 for direct preference optimization. In hybrid filter mode, it reaches 80.52 macro-F1 while sending only 34.2 percent of samples to the full ensemble. This corresponds to a 66 percent reduction in expert calls, reducing estimated cost from approximately $0.12 to $0.05 per sample and latency from about 15 seconds to 6 seconds.

The error analysis identifies different bottlenecks. Image errors involve small or occluded objects and subtle attributes. Video errors involve temporal misalignment, missing intermediate actions, and incorrect causal interpretations. Audio errors arise mainly from source separation and ambiguous acoustic signals, with correlated weaknesses across all audio experts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

OmniHallu provides a framework for evaluating hallucinations across both multimodal understanding and generation rather than treating them as separate research problems. Its claim-level design can support more precise diagnosis than response-level accuracy, allowing researchers to identify whether a system fails on objects, attributes, events, or relations.

The benchmark may also enable more meaningful comparisons among MLLMs and help researchers study whether improvements in one modality transfer to another. The modular architecture offers a practical deployment pattern: a low-cost verifier can handle clear cases, while ambiguous cases are routed to specialized experts and a reasoning-based aggregator.

Potential applications include quality control for image and video captioning, monitoring of generative media systems, automated evaluation during model development, and risk screening in systems that rely on grounded multimodal outputs. However, the reported performance should be interpreted in light of the paper’s limitations. Results depend on the selected expert models and on GPT-5.2, which is stronger than the baseline reasoning model in the main comparison. Equal-weight voting cannot correct errors shared by all experts. The benchmark is also partly model-generated, and its four-category taxonomy does not cover all modality-specific error types. Finally, the study excludes non-text pairings such as image-to-audio and video-to-image. Human review remains necessary before applying the detector to high-stakes settings.
