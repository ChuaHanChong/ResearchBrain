# Reverse Engineering Human Preferences with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Imperial College London and Cohere introduced Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (RLRE), a method that generates adversarial textual preambles to stealthily inflate evaluation scores from LLM-as-a-judge frameworks. This approach raised judge-LLM scores by up to 0.64 points on MT-Bench while remaining largely undetectable by perplexity analysis and human inspection, revealing a significant vulnerability in current LLM evaluation paradigms.

## Problem

- The LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm is vulnerable to adversarial attacks, but existing methods involve post-hoc modifications to responses that are often detectable.
- A need for a stealthier, upstream adversarial attack was identified to expose deeper vulnerabilities in LLM evaluation and prompt development of more robust safeguards.

## Method

- The authors developed Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (**RLRE**), a pipeline that uses a ==trainable preamble== generator (LLM) to create textual inputs for a frozen candidate LLM.
- A Judge-LLM was employed as a reward model within a reinforcement learning framework, guiding the preamble generator to optimize for maximum evaluation scores.
- The Contrastive Policy Gradient (CoPG) algorithm was adapted to efficiently train the preamble generator, enabling it to produce inputs that bias the Judge-LLM without direct, detectable manipulation of the candidate's output.

## Results

- The preamble-based attack increased MT-Bench scores by **0.64** points for the Command R7B+R7B pipeline, consistently outperforming non-attacked baselines and existing post-hoc adversarial methods.
- The attack demonstrated high stealth, with a False Negative Rate of **0.90** for perplexity-based detection and only **14%** detection by human annotators, in stark contrast to other methods detected in 95-**100%** of cases.
- Despite higher judge-LLM scores, objective accuracy for math and reasoning tasks showed a slight decrease (from **45.9%** to **44.2%**), and human evaluators found only a negligible difference (**0.02** points) in overall quality compared to non-attacked responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial preambles can effectively and stealthily inflate LLM-as-a-judge scores, even when the preambles themselves are non-fluent or seem meaningless, indicating a profound vulnerability.
> - The preamble-based attack demonstrated strong transferability across different candidate LLMs, judge LLMs (including unseen ones like GPT-3.5 and Claude Haiku), and evaluation benchmarks.
> - Higher judge-LLM scores for attacked responses often correlated with improved structural layout and reasoning chains rather than an increase in objective accuracy, particularly for math and reasoning tasks.
