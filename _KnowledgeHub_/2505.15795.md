# Reverse Engineering Human Preferences with Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Imperial College London and Cohere introduced Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (RLRE), a method that generates adversarial textual preambles to stealthily inflate evaluation scores from LLM-as-a-judge frameworks. This approach raised judge-LLM scores by up to 0.64 points on MT-Bench while remaining largely undetectable by perplexity analysis and human inspection, revealing a significant vulnerability in current LLM evaluation paradigms.

## Problem

- The LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm is vulnerable to adversarial attacks, but existing methods involve post-hoc modifications to responses that are often detectable.
- A need for a stealthier, upstream adversarial attack was identified to expose deeper vulnerabilities in LLM evaluation and prompt development of more robust safeguards.

## Method

- The authors developed Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (**RLRE**), a pipeline that uses a ==trainable preamble== generator (LLM) to create textual inputs for a frozen candidate LLM.
- A Judge-LLM was employed as a reward model within a reinforcement learning framework, guiding the preamble generator to optimize for maximum evaluation scores.
- The Contrastive Policy Gradient (CoPG) algorithm was adapted to efficiently train the preamble generator, enabling it to produce inputs that bias the Judge-LLM without direct, detectable manipulation of the candidate's output.

## Results

- The preamble-based attack increased MT-Bench scores by **0.64** points for the Command R7B+R7B pipeline, consistently outperforming non-attacked baselines and existing post-hoc adversarial methods.
- The attack demonstrated high stealth, with a False Negative Rate of **0.90** for perplexity-based detection and only **14%** detection by human annotators, in stark contrast to other methods detected in 95-**100%** of cases.
- Despite higher judge-LLM scores, objective accuracy for math and reasoning tasks showed a slight decrease (from **45.9%** to **44.2%**), and human evaluators found only a negligible difference (**0.02** points) in overall quality compared to non-attacked responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial preambles can effectively and stealthily inflate LLM-as-a-judge scores, even when the preambles themselves are non-fluent or seem meaningless, indicating a profound vulnerability.
> - The preamble-based attack demonstrated strong transferability across different candidate LLMs, judge LLMs (including unseen ones like GPT-3.5 and Claude Haiku), and evaluation benchmarks.
> - Higher judge-LLM scores for attacked responses often correlated with improved structural layout and reasoning chains rather than an increase in objective accuracy, particularly for math and reasoning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has increasingly shifted from human annotation to automated assessment using other LLMs, a paradigm known as "LLM-as-a-judge." This approach offers scalability and cost-efficiency, becoming a standard for numerous benchmarks in LLM performance evaluation. However, the LLM-as-a-judge framework is known to be susceptible to various biases and adversarial manipulations. Previous research has demonstrated that judge-LLMs can be exploited by appending specific text sequences, such as false citations or statements of majority belief, or by manipulating response length, to artificially inflate evaluation scores. These prior adversarial attacks typically involve post-hoc modification of the candidate LLM's generated responses. Such modifications can often be detected through methods like perplexity analysis or human inspection, as they may introduce unusual phrasing or statistical anomalies into the response.

This study positions itself within this landscape by addressing the detectability limitation of existing adversarial attacks. Instead of modifying the candidate LLM's output directly, this work explores an upstream intervention: generating adversarial textual preambles (system prompts) that are fed *into* a frozen candidate LLM. This shift in attack vector aims to achieve similar score inflation while rendering the attack virtually undetectable by current methods, thus highlighting a deeper vulnerability in the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm. Furthermore, the paper introduces a novel approach, Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (RLRE), as a general method for optimizing upstream textual inputs to LLMs, extending its potential applicability beyond adversarial contexts to other objectives like bias mitigation or toxicity reduction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a novel, stealthy adversarial attack against the LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework. This attack aims to artificially boost the scores assigned to a candidate LLM's responses by a judge LLM without direct, detectable manipulation of the candidate's output.

The specific motivation for pursuing this objective and the chosen methodology are multi-faceted:

- **Undetectability:** Previous adversarial attacks, which involve appending or substituting text directly into generated responses, often lead to increased perplexity scores or unnatural text patterns that human evaluators can identify. A key motivation for this study is to devise an attack that bypasses these existing detection mechanisms. By intervening *upstream* through preambles, the goal is for the candidate LLM to generate responses that appear natural and un-attacked while still achieving inflated scores.
- **Novel Attack Vector:** The research aims to be the first to optimize preambles—which are injected into a frozen LLM—specifically for the purpose of deceiving judge-LLMs. This contrasts with prior work that focused on manipulating text appended to pre-generated responses.
- **Efficiency and Resource Management:** The proposed method utilizes a specialized, potentially smaller preamble generator. This design choice is motivated by the computational efficiency it offers, as training a smaller model is generally less resource-intensive than fine-tuning a large candidate LLM directly.
- **Preservation of Candidate LLM Integrity:** By keeping the candidate LLM frozen and only tuning the preambles, the method aims to preserve the original capabilities and stylistic characteristics of the candidate LLM. This makes the attack harder to detect by preventing noticeable shifts in the LLM's output style.
- **Interpretability of Preamble Instructions:** The generated preambles are in natural language. This aspect is motivated by the potential for analyzing and interpreting these instructions to understand what specific directions align a candidate LLM with a judge's preferences.
- **Modularity and Transferability:** Once trained, a preamble generator is envisioned as a "plug-and-play" component. This is motivated by the desire to demonstrate that these generators can be successfully pipelined with different candidate LLMs and potentially generalize to different judge LLMs without retraining, offering broad applicability.
- **Broader Applications of RLRE:** Beyond adversarial attacks, the research is motivated by the introduction of Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (RLRE) as a general framework. This approach, which pipelines LLMs to indirectly optimize upstream textual inputs through reinforcement learning, is presented as a method that could be applied to diverse tasks, including beneficial ones such as reducing toxicity or mitigating bias in LLM outputs, particularly for models that are not directly tunable (e.g., API-accessed models).
- **Reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge:** By exposing a new, stealthy vulnerability, the work is fundamentally motivated by raising critical questions about the reliability and robustness of the widely adopted LLM-as-a-judge evaluation paradigm, prompting a re-evaluation of its design and safeguards.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology introduced in this paper is Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (RLRE). This approach involves pipelining multiple LLMs to indirectly optimize upstream textual preambles using a reinforcement learning (RL) framework.

**Components of the RLRE Pipeline:**

1. **Preamble Generator (π_θ):** This is a trainable LLM responsible for generating textual preambles (system prompts). Given a fixed instruction `i` and a question `q` from a training dataset, the generator produces a preamble `p`.
2. **Candidate-LLM (LLM_C):** This is a frozen LLM that receives the generated preamble `p` concatenated with the original question `q` as input. It then generates a response `c`.
3. **Judge-LLM:** This is another frozen LLM that functions as the reward model. It evaluates the `(q, c)` pair (question and candidate response) and assigns a numerical score (on a 1-10 scale, as in MT-Bench single evaluation) which serves as the reward `R(q, c)` for the RL problem.

**Reinforcement Learning Formulation:**

The objective of the RL problem is to maximize the expected reward `J(π_θ) = E_q~D E_p~π_θ(p|i,q) E_c~LLM_C(c|p,q) [R(q, c)]`. The reward `R(q, c)` is implicitly a function of the preamble `p` because the candidate LLM's generation `c` is conditioned on `p`.

**Optimization Algorithm:**

The authors adapt the Contrastive Policy Gradient (CoPG) algorithm for optimizing the preamble generator. This choice is justified by CoPG's ability to handle arbitrary reward functions (unlike preference-based methods), faster convergence, and higher computational efficiency compared to non-contrastive methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) which require an additional critic model. The CoPG loss function `L(p_j, p'_j; π)` is based on the difference in rewards between two sampled preambles, `p_j` and `p'_j`, for a given question `q_j`, regularized by the KL-divergence between the preamble generator's policy `π` and a reference model `π_ref` (the base LLM of the generator). The hyperparameter `β` controls the importance of this KL-divergence, with a low `β` prioritizing reward over preamble fluency.

**Experimental Setup:**

- **Models:** Three distinct pipelines were trained to test generalizability across model sizes and families:
 - **Command R7B+R7B:** Command R7B (generator) + Command R7B (candidate).
 - **Command R7B+R:** Command R7B (generator) + Command R (35B, candidate).
 - **Llama 8B+70B:** Llama 3.1 8B Instruct (generator) + Llama 3.1 70B Instruct (candidate).
 - **Judge-LLM (for training):** Command R+ (104B parameters) was used consistently across all training pipelines, prompted as in MT-Bench.
- **Hyperparameters:** Tuning was performed on the Command R7B+R7B pipeline and applied to others. Key settings include a batch size of 64, two gradient steps per batch, learning rate of 1e-6 (Adam optimizer), and specific sampling parameters for preamble generation (high temperature `t=4.0` for training diversity, `t=0.5` for inference). The KL-divergence coefficient `β` was set to `0.03` to prioritize reward over preamble fluency. Early stopping was used for training.
- **Datasets:**
 - **Training/Validation:** UltraFeedback (∼60k single-turn questions from diverse sources like ShareGPT, FLAN, Evol-Instruct) was used, with MT-Bench prompts for single evaluation to elicit rewards.
 - **Evaluation:** MT-Bench (160 open-ended, two-turn questions across 8 domains: writing, roleplay, reasoning, math, coding, extraction, STEM, humanities).
 - **Transferability Test:** Arena-Hard (different task distribution and reward metric) was used to assess benchmark transferability.
- **Baselines:** The method was compared against unattacked candidate LLMs and five existing adversarial attacks: Verbosity bias, Bandwagon-effect bias, Authority bias, Refinement-aware bias (all from), and the Universal adversarial attack. All baselines involve post-hoc modification of candidate responses.
- **Attack Detectability Assessment:**
 - **Perplexity Analysis:** A perplexity filter (PPL-W) using a 10-token sliding window was employed to detect attacked responses, with a threshold set to ensure a false positive rate below 0.05 on non-attacked responses.
 - **Human Evaluation:** 16 domain-expert annotators were recruited to label 400 sampled responses (from each attack type and non-attacked) as "attacked" or "not attacked" through visual inspection.
- **Ablation Studies:** To understand the role of conditioning, experiments were conducted where the preamble generator received only a generic instruction (no question) or only special tokens (no instruction).
- **Analysis of Generated Texts:** Post-hoc analysis included examining the fluency and style of generated preambles, evaluating the accuracy of math and reasoning responses (using normalized exact match), and conducting human quality evaluation of attacked versus non-attacked responses across all domains (using 'Poor', 'Fair', 'Good' labels and 1-10 ratings).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings:

- **Effectiveness of Adversarial Preambles:** Responses from candidate-LLMs pipelined with the adversarially tuned preamble generator consistently achieved higher evaluation scores from the Command R+ judge-LLM compared to unattacked responses and all baseline adversarial methods.
 - For the Command R7B+R7B pipeline (where hyperparameters were tuned), the overall MT-Bench score increased by 0.64 points compared to the non-attacked baseline. This represents an improvement of 0.32 points over the refinement-aware attack and 0.52 points over the universal adversarial attack.
 - Command R and Llama 3.1 70B Instruct candidates also showed consistent, though smaller, score increases. Turn 1 responses generally benefited more than Turn 2 responses.
- **Task-Specific Performance:** The preamble-based attack was most effective at raising scores for reasoning and math questions, followed by extraction, STEM, and roleplay tasks.
- **Attack Transferability:**
 - **Candidate-LLMs:** The adversarial preambles demonstrated strong transferability, maintaining higher scores than baselines even when pipelined with candidate-LLMs different from those used during training (e.g., preambles trained for Command R7B+R7B boosting Command R).
 - **Judge-LLMs:** The attack transferred effectively to unseen judge-LLMs. GPT-3.5, GPT-4o-mini, and Claude Haiku consistently assigned the highest scores to responses generated using preambles trained with Command R+, indicating generalizability across different judge model families.
 - **Benchmarks:** The Command R7B pipeline also transferred effectively to Arena-Hard, a benchmark with a different task distribution and reward metric, showing score increases with both the Command R+ judge and the unseen GPT-4 judge.
- **Attack Detectability:**
 - **Perplexity Analysis (PPL-W):** The preamble-based attack was rarely detected by the PPL-W method (False Negative Rate = 0.90), performing similarly to unattacked models or verbosity bias attacks. This is in contrast to the universal adversarial attack, which was almost always detected (FNR = 0.04).
 - **Human Evaluation:** Human annotators identified responses generated via the preamble attack as "attacked" almost as rarely as non-attacked responses (14% vs. 12%). This contrasts sharply with formulaic attacks (e.g., bandwagon, authority, refinement, universal attacks) which were detected in 95-100% of cases.
- **Ablation Study:** The attack's effectiveness remained robust even when the preamble generator was provided with less specific conditioning (e.g., only a generic instruction or only special tokens, removing the question or full instruction), still outperforming all baselines.
- **Analysis of Generated Texts:**
 - **Preamble Fluency:** While Command models generated structured and fluent preambles, Llama preambles often exhibited repetition, lacked fluency, and sometimes devolved into seemingly meaningless character sequences, especially when less conditioned. Despite this, these less fluent preambles were still successful at inflating judge-LLM scores.
 - **Response Accuracy:** For math and reasoning tasks, the preamble-attacked responses did not show an improvement in objective accuracy; in fact, they showed a slight decrease (44.2% vs 45.9% for non-attacked).
 - **Response Layout:** Attacked responses often featured more structured reasoning chains, distinct paragraphs, and clear explanatory headers, suggesting that improved layout, rather than correctness, contributed to higher judge scores.
 - **Human Quality Evaluation:** Human annotators found a negligible difference (0.02 points on a 1-10 scale) in overall quality between attacked and non-attacked responses, despite the significant difference in judge-LLM scores.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Vulnerability of LLM-as-a-Judge:** The study demonstrably exposes a significant, previously unaddressed vulnerability in the widely adopted LLM-as-a-judge evaluation framework. By showing that an adversarial attack can be highly effective at inflating scores while remaining virtually undetectable by current safeguards (perplexity analysis and human inspection), the work calls into serious question the reliability and robustness of this evaluation paradigm. This necessitates a re-evaluation of how LLM performance is assessed and prompts the development of more robust evaluation frameworks and detection mechanisms.
- **Introduction of RLRE:** The paper introduces Reinforcement Learning for Reverse Engineering (RLRE) as a novel and generalizable approach for indirectly optimizing LLMs. This strategy, which tunes upstream textual preambles via reinforcement learning, holds promise for optimizing models that are not amenable to direct fine-tuning (e.g., proprietary models accessed via APIs or those with prohibitive fine-tuning costs).
- **Broad Applications Beyond Adversarial Attacks:** While demonstrated in an adversarial context, RLRE is posited to have diverse beneficial applications. Future research could explore its use in meaningful tasks such as automatically generating preambles to reduce toxicity, mitigate bias, or improve specific aspects of LLM output quality. This highlights a versatile new tool for aligning LLM behavior with desired objectives.
- **Implications for Prompt Engineering and Model Conditioning:** The observation that highly successful preambles can be non-fluent or even appear meaningless (particularly for Llama models under certain ablations) suggests that constraining conditioning tokens (such as preambles or reasoning traces) to natural language might not always be optimal for maximizing model performance. This finding could influence future research into how LLMs are conditioned, potentially leading to less human-readable but more effective prompt designs.
- **Future System Design:** The work envisions future LLM systems that move beyond single token streams to integrate and optimize multiple inputs—from users, tools, and auxiliary models—each contributing to the generation of responses that better align with chosen objectives. RLRE offers a framework for exploring such multi-input optimization.
- **Ethical Considerations:** By demonstrating a powerful and stealthy attack, the research contributes to the scientific understanding of LLM security. While the authors explicitly state they will not release trained preamble generator checkpoints to prevent misuse, the inherent capability revealed by this work underscores the need for continuous research into adversarial machine learning and defensive strategies in the LLM domain.
