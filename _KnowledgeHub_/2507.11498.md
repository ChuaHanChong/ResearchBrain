# Robot Drummer: Learning Rhythmic Skills for Humanoid Drumming

> [!summary] Summary
> This research enables a full humanoid robot to perform complex, expressive drumming by interpreting standard musical scores. The work introduces a reinforcement learning framework that translates MIDI data into a Rhythmic Contact Chain and optimizes control policies using a dense, unified reward function, achieving high-precision drumming with emergent human-like strategies.

## Problem

- Humanoid robot control largely focuses on goal-driven tasks (e.g., manipulation, locomotion) and struggles with expressive, process-driven tasks like music performance, which require precise timing and multi-limb coordination over long durations.
- Current motion imitation-based learning methods for humanoids are limited to short-horizon tasks and struggle to discover novel, temporally precise contact sequences needed for continuous musical expression.
- Robotic musicianship often employs simplified platforms or control paradigms, failing to fully leverage the complex kinematics and dynamic capabilities of a full humanoid body for intricate musical performances.

## Method

- The '**Robot Drummer**' system formulates humanoid drumming as the sequential fulfillment of timed contacts, converting MIDI scores into a '==Rhythmic Contact Chain==' (RCC) representing explicit temporal contact events.
- It employs a reinforcement learning framework using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with temporal decomposition, partitioning the RCC into shorter segments to improve sample efficiency for long-horizon songs.
- The system utilizes a dense, unified reward function that provides positive feedback for correct drum impacts and penalties for wrong strikes, missed strikes, and sticks being far from target drums.

## Results

- Specialist policies achieved high F1 scores (often above **0.9**) for drumming various rock, metal, and jazz tracks, demonstrating high-precision performance across diverse repertoires.
- Rhythmic irregularity (nPVI) and the number of distinct drums used were the primary factors negatively correlating with performance, while tempo and rhythmic diversity had minimal impact.
- Training a single generalist policy on multiple songs led to a noticeable performance degradation compared to specialist policies, indicating challenges with negative transfer for diverse rhythmic skills.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A dense, unified contact reward function, incorporating penalties for wrong and missed strikes alongside rewards for correct ones, is crucial for learning robust rhythmic accuracy.
> - Explicit one-hot encoded 'contact goals' (a lookahead sequence of target drums) in the observation space are essential for the policy to anticipate and plan future drum strikes effectively.
> - Reinforcement learning can enable humanoid robots to learn complex, human-like drumming strategies such as cross-over strikes, fast rolls, and dynamic stick assignments, without explicit programming.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has made significant strides in areas such as dexterity, balance, and locomotion for humanoid robots. However, as the authors note, the application of these advancements to "expressive domains" like music performance remains largely underexplored. This paper positions itself at the intersection of advanced humanoid control, reinforcement learning (RL), and robotic musicianship, aiming to bridge this gap.

**Humanoid Control Landscape:**
Recent progress in humanoid control has been dominated by motion imitation-based learning, where policies are trained to reproduce pre-recorded human motion-capture data or videos. These methods have enabled impressive feats in loco-manipulation (e.g., walking, running, parkour, object manipulation) and whole-body control, often employing techniques like curriculum learning and sim-to-real transfer for robustness. However, these approaches are typically tied to demonstrated motions, optimized for short-horizon tasks (a few seconds), and lack explicit objectives for discovering novel, temporally precise contact sequences crucial for process-driven tasks. This paper explicitly departs from motion imitation, instead focusing on learning from scratch based on musical scores.

**Robotic Musicianship Landscape:**
Robotic musicianship has evolved from trajectory-based systems and rule-based improvisation to more data-driven and learning-based paradigms. Previous works have explored deep RL for dexterous anthropomorphic hands playing the piano, or for simplified, underactuated drumming arms. While these efforts are notable, they often involve less complex robotic platforms or simplified kinematics compared to a full humanoid body. Moreover, the inherent "process-driven" nature of music, demanding intricate timing, fluid motion, and continuous adaptation over long durations, has been largely unexplored by policy-learning methods in the context of whole-body humanoid control.

**Bridging the Gap:**
"Robot Drummer" directly addresses this void by applying a novel RL framework to enable a *full humanoid robot* to perform expressive drumming. Unlike previous works that simplify the robot or the musical task, this research tackles the unique challenges posed by drumming on a humanoid platform: split-second timing, rapid intermittent contacts, and multi-limb coordination over extended performances. By formulating drumming as a "Rhythmic Contact Chain" and employing temporal decomposition and dense contact rewards, the authors present a general framework for learning complex rhythmic skills that can generate novel motions not seen in demonstration data. This work represents a significant step towards endowing humanoids with creative and expressive capabilities, pushing beyond purely functional or goal-driven tasks. It also highlights the potential of RL to discover emergent, human-like strategies in a highly dynamic, real-time environment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable a humanoid robot to perform complex, expressive, and high-precision drumming across a diverse repertoire of songs. This overarching goal is driven by the recognized challenge of extending humanoid robot capabilities beyond traditional goal-driven manipulation and locomotion into more nuanced, expressive, and process-driven domains like music.

The authors identify several specific motivations and key challenges that their "Robot Drummer" system aims to address:

1. **Exploration of Expressive Domains:** While humanoid robots have excelled in dexterity and balance for goal-oriented tasks, their potential in creative fields like music performance remains largely untapped. The paper aims to demonstrate that humanoid robots can master tasks requiring artistic expression, not just functional execution.
2. **Addressing Unique Challenges of Musical Tasks:** Drumming, in particular, presents a unique set of demands that are distinct from typical manipulation tasks:
 - **Split-second Timing:** Each drum strike must occur at a precise moment, with even minor deviations profoundly affecting the musical quality.
 - **Rapid Contacts:** The robot must execute high-frequency, intermittent contacts with various drum surfaces.
 - **Multi-limb Coordination:** Synchronized and coordinated movements of multiple limbs (both arms, and potentially torso) are required to strike different drums, sometimes simultaneously or in rapid succession.
 - **Long-horizon Performance:** Musical pieces last for minutes, requiring the robot to sustain an evolving sequence of actions over a long temporal horizon, a challenge for traditional RL methods.
3. **Process-Driven vs. Goal-Driven Tasks:** Unlike tasks with well-defined end states (e.g., picking up an object), music performance is inherently process-driven. Success is defined by the quality of the continuous sequence of actions rather than reaching a final configuration. This necessitates a different approach to policy learning and reward design.
4. **Autonomous Coordination of a Humanoid Body:** The work specifically focuses on training a *full humanoid body* to drum, rather than simplified arms or hands. This introduces the complexity of whole-body coordination, where the robot must adapt its entire posture and balance to facilitate drumming movements.
5. **Learning from Musical Scores (MIDI):** The objective includes teaching the robot to interpret and perform drum patterns from MIDI transcriptions, replicating the nuanced timing and expressivity of human drummers. This involves translating abstract musical notation into precise physical actions.

By tackling these challenges, the authors aim to push the frontier of humanoid robotics, endowing them with creative and expressive skills, and showcasing the potential of reinforcement learning to achieve human-like musical performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Robot Drummer" system employs a novel reinforcement learning framework designed to handle the long-horizon, high-precision, and contact-rich nature of drumming for a humanoid robot.

**Problem Formulation:**
Humanoid drumming is formulated as the sequential fulfillment of timed contacts. The core idea is to transform standard drum scores (MIDI data) into a "Rhythmic Contact Chain" (RCC), which explicitly represents the temporal sequence of contact events (which drum to strike at what time, and optionally, with which stick).

**Key Methodological Components:**

1. **Music Representation (MIDI to Rhythmic Contact Chain):**
 - Raw MIDI drum tracks are processed by extracting "note-on" events on the percussion channel.
 - Each unique MIDI note number is manually mapped to a specific physical drum in the kit (snare, toms, cymbals, hi-hat). For simplicity, only the most frequent articulation of a drum is used if multiple exist.
 - This data is then converted into a time-indexed drum sequence, where each drum hit is encoded as a one-hot vector indicating the target drums at a given timestep.
 - The **Rhythmic Contact Chain (RCC)**, C = {C_1, C_2..., C_N}, formally defines each contact step C_i as a tuple (D_i, t_i, S_i), where D_i is the set of drums to strike at time t_i, and S_i specifies the stick(s) (left/right) that *can* be used. This allows for flexible stick assignment, a crucial element for adaptive drumming.

2. **Temporal Decomposition:**
 - To address the long-horizon nature of full songs (minutes long, leading to sparse rewards and poor exploration), the RCC is partitioned into multiple shorter, fixed-length "training segments."
 - Each segment C^(m) corresponds to a subset of contact steps. All M segments are trained in parallel. This significantly improves sample efficiency by allowing the RL agent to experience various parts of the song early and frequently.

3. **Policy Training with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):**
 - Drumming is modeled as a finite-horizon Extended Markov Decision Process (MDP).
 - PPO, an on-policy RL algorithm known for stability and efficiency in parallel training, is used to optimize the policy.

4. **State Space (Observations):** The agent's state `s_t` is a comprehensive representation combining three components:
 - **Robot's Proprioception `s_p,t`:** Joint positions (`q_t`), joint velocities (`q_dot_t`), and the action applied at the previous timestep (`a_t-1`). (15 DOF for upper body).
 - **Spatial Information `s_sp,t`:** Positions of the drums and the robot's sticks.
 - **Contact Goals `s_c,t`:** This is a critical component. It comprises a sequence of one-hot encoded target drum strikes for the *next L future timesteps* (`d_t`, `d_t+1`..., `d_t+L`). This lookahead horizon `L` provides the policy with essential rhythmic context, informing it "which" drum(s) to target next, acting as an implicit goal signal for rhythmic progression.

5. **Action Space:** The policy outputs target joint angles (`a_t`) for a Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller, which then generates torques to control the robot's 15 upper-body degrees of freedom (7 per arm, 1 for torso).

6. **Reward Function:** A dense, unified task reward `r_t` is designed to guide the policy effectively:
 - `r_t = r_contact_t + r_reg_t`
 - **`r_contact_t` (Dense Contact Reward):** This is the core of the reward, providing rich feedback on contact events.
 - `r_correct_t`: Positive reward (+1.0) for each drum impact that matches the target drum(s) specified by the RCC.
 - `r_wrong_t`: Penalty (-0.5) for hitting any drum that is not a target.
 - `r_missed_t`: Penalty (-2.0) for failing to hit target drums. (Missing a strike is weighted as more detrimental than hitting a wrong one).
 - `r_prox_t`: Penalty (-1.0) for sticks being far from target drums when hits are expected (encourages efficient movement).
 - **`r_reg_t` (Regularization Rewards):** Penalties to encourage smooth, natural movements.
 - Action-rate penalty: Smooths changes in desired joint positions.
 - DoF acceleration penalty: Reduces jerky movements.

7. **Music State Initialization:** To improve exploration and expose the policy to high-reward states early, training episodes are initialized by randomly sampling contact steps within a segment of the RCC. This effectively randomizes the starting point within the drum track.

**Simulation Environment:**
Experiments are conducted using a Unitree G1 humanoid robot model within the high-performance Isaac Gym simulation environment. The robot's upper body, including 7 DOF per arm and 1 DOF for the torso (total 15 DOF), is controlled.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the "Robot Drummer" across various dimensions, including performance on different song complexities, the individual contributions of methodological components, and the feasibility of learning generalist policies.

**A. Performance Across Songs of Varying Complexity (Specialist Policies):**
- **High F1 Scores:** Specialist policies (trained on individual songs) consistently achieved high F1 scores, often above 0.9, demonstrating the robot's ability to perform high-precision drumming across a diverse repertoire of rock, metal, and jazz tracks.
- **Complexity Factors:**
 - **nPVI (Rhythmic Irregularity):** Showed the strongest negative correlation with F1 score (Spearman's rank correlation), indicating that songs with highly uneven timing between hits are the most challenging. For instance, "Bon Jovi – Livin’ on a Prayer" (high nPVI) had a lower F1 (0.885) compared to "David Bowie - Rebel Rebel" (low nPVI), which achieved near-perfect F1 (0.985).
 - **Number of Drums Used:** Negatively correlated with F1 score. More distinct drums require greater spatial coordination and increase the difficulty of policy learning.
 - **Polyphony:** Imposes a fundamental upper limit on performance, as the physical robot can only strike two drums simultaneously. Songs with high polyphony (e.g., "Nirvana - Smells Like Teen Spirit" with 14.16% polyphony, F1 = 0.712) inevitably lead to missed hits.
 - **Entropy (Rhythmic Diversity) and Tempo (BPM):** Showed minimal impact on performance, suggesting that policies can handle varied drum usage and fast tempos as long as the timing is consistent. Time signature changes also had negligible correlation due to limited occurrences in the dataset.
- **Emergent Human-like Strategies:** The learned behaviors exhibited remarkable emergent strategies not explicitly programmed:
 - **Cross-over Strikes:** The robot performed cross-arm maneuvers (e.g., right stick on cymbal, left stick on snare) to efficiently reach distant drums, as seen in "Bon Jovi - Livin’ on a Prayer."
 - **Fast Drum Rolls and Fills:** The robot could execute rapid successions of hits across multiple drums (e.g., "Linkin Park – In the End" drum fill, "Nirvana - Come As You Are" snare roll), demonstrating precise temporal and spatial coordination.
 - **Adaptive Stick Assignments:** Policies dynamically reassigned sticks based on musical context. For example, the right stick might be used for the snare, but if a cymbal hit immediately followed, the policy might switch to the left stick for the snare to keep the right hand free for the upcoming cymbal.

**B. Ablation Analysis (Contribution of Design Choices):**
- **Reward Components:**
 - **Correct-strike only reward:** Led to significantly degraded F1 scores (e.g., 0.64 for "Rebel Rebel"), indicating insufficient guidance.
 - **Correct + Wrong strikes reward:** Improved performance but still suboptimal (e.g., 0.96 for "Rebel Rebel").
 - **Full Dense Contact Reward (Correct + Wrong + Missed Strikes):** Proved essential for robust rhythmic accuracy, achieving the highest F1 scores across all tested songs (e.g., 0.99 for "Rebel Rebel"). This validates the hypothesis that every contact event provides crucial learning signals.
- **Observation Modalities:**
 - **Without Contact Goals:** Policies collapsed to near-zero F1 scores (e.g., 0.07 for "Rebel Rebel"), demonstrating they lacked the ability to anticipate upcoming drum strikes.
 - **Continuous Time Phase Variable vs. One-Hot Contact Goals:** Policies conditioned on a continuous phase variable (indicating "how far through the segment") also performed poorly. This highlights the necessity of explicit, one-hot encoded "contact goals" (informing "which drum is next") for successful drumming.
- **Temporal Decomposition:** While the final F1 scores remained comparable with or without temporal decomposition, using this technique substantially reduced wall-clock training time (from 8-9 hours to 2-3 hours), indicating significant improvement in sample efficiency.

**C. Generalist Policies:**
- **Performance Degradation:** Training a single "generalist" policy on multiple songs resulted in a noticeable drop in F1 scores compared to specialist policies. Songs with higher rhythmic irregularity or faster tempos (e.g., "Bon Jovi – Livin’ on a Prayer" and "Jimi Hendrix - Fire") experienced the largest performance drops.
- **Negative Transfer:** The difficulty in generalizing suggests that combining widely varied rhythmic patterns (different spatial layouts, temporal structures) in one policy introduces negative transfer, forcing the policy into suboptimal compromises or "safer" strategies (e.g., focusing on frequently used drums and ignoring others, similar to the "Roxanne" specialist policy's behavior).

**D. Listener Study (Qualitative Evaluation):**
- A small-scale listener study (15 participants) provided qualitative insights.
- **Timing Consistency:** Rated highest (3.69/5), reflecting the quantitative F1 scores.
- **Expressiveness:** Rated moderately high (3.38/5).
- **Naturalness:** Rated lower (2.46/5), indicating that while mechanically precise, the robot's movements might still lack the subtle nuances of human drumming.
- **Enjoyment:** Mixed responses (3.08/5), suggesting varied audience perception of robotic musical performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Robot Drummer" project makes significant contributions to robotics and artificial intelligence, showcasing the potential of advanced reinforcement learning to extend humanoid capabilities into complex, expressive domains.

**Significance:**
1. **Breaking into Expressive Domains:** This work is a pioneering effort in demonstrating that full humanoid robots can learn and perform complex, artistic tasks like drumming with high precision and expressivity. It extends the frontier of humanoid control beyond traditional loco-manipulation and object interaction.
2. **Novel RL Framework for Process-Driven Tasks:** The paper introduces a robust RL framework tailored for long-horizon, process-driven tasks. The formulation of drumming as a "Rhythmic Contact Chain," coupled with temporal decomposition and dense, structured contact rewards (correct, wrong, missed, proximity), provides a generalizable approach for learning fine-grained temporal and spatial coordination.
3. **Emergent Complex Behaviors:** A key insight is the emergence of human-like drumming strategies—such as cross-over strikes, fast drum rolls, and dynamic stick assignments—without explicit programming. These behaviors arise organically as the policy optimizes for reward, highlighting the power of RL in discovering efficient and natural solutions in high-dimensional action spaces.
4. **Addressing Core RL Challenges:** The methodology effectively tackles common RL challenges:
 - **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Temporal decomposition significantly improves sample efficiency for minute-long performances.
 - **Sparse Rewards:** Dense contact rewards provide rich learning signals for fine-grained timing and coordination.
 - **High-Dimensional State/Action Space:** The use of structured state representations (contact goals) is shown to be critical for the policy's ability to anticipate and plan future actions.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Advancement in Humanoid Robotics:** This research propels humanoid robotics towards more sophisticated and nuanced interactions with the human world. It suggests future humanoids could engage in a wider array of creative, artistic, and collaborative tasks, not just industrial or assistive roles.
2. **Foundation for Robotic Musicianship:** The "Robot Drummer" provides a strong foundation for future research in robotic musicianship, paving the way for robots that can not only play instruments but also improvise, adapt to different styles, and potentially collaborate with human musicians in dynamic, real-time settings.
3. **Insights into Multi-Task Learning:** The challenges faced by generalist policies (negative transfer) offer valuable insights into the current limitations of multi-task RL for highly diverse and complex motor skills. This highlights the need for more advanced architectural designs or training schemes for true generalization in robotics.
4. **Real-World Applications and Transfer:** The discussion on sim-to-real transfer strategies (domain randomization, compliant dynamics, electronic drum kits with velocity feedback) outlines a practical pathway to deploying these learned skills on physical hardware. This is crucial for validating the research in real-world scenarios and moving beyond simulation.
5. **Human-Robot Interaction:** As robots become capable of artistic expression, it opens new avenues for human-robot collaboration and interaction, potentially leading to novel forms of entertainment, education, and therapy.

In conclusion, "Robot Drummer" stands as a testament to the growing capabilities of reinforcement learning in endowing humanoid robots with complex, expressive, and human-like skills, pushing the boundaries of what is possible in robotic control and artificial intelligence.
