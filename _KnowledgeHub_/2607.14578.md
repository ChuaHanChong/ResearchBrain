# Beyond Implicit Force: Evaluating Explicit Force-Torque Proxies in Action Chunking with Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Australian Institute for Machine Learning at Adelaide University found that Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policies heavily rely on implicit interaction cues from leader-follower teleoperation for contact-rich tasks, and demonstrated that readily available, explicit joint-torque proxies derived from motor currents effectively restore and enhance this force-aware behavior.

## Problem

- Contact-rich robot manipulation policies, particularly Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), often rely on implicit interaction cues from leader-follower teleoperation (positional discrepancies) which are absent in other teaching paradigms like kinesthetic teaching or with high-transparency controllers.
- Many robotic platforms lack expensive 6-axis force/torque sensors, instead providing internal motor current or joint effort estimates, but the systematic evaluation of these explicit torque proxies within ACT-style policies for real-world contact tasks has been limited.
- The true source of force-awareness in ACT, and its generalizability beyond its typical demonstration collection setup, was not fully understood or systematically evaluated.

## Method

- The authors reformulated ==Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT)== into an 'observation-centric' variant (**ACT-o**) by having the policy predict future *follower* robot states instead of *leader* commands, thereby explicitly removing the implicit teleoperation-induced discrepancy signal.
- Simple ==screw-axis torque proxies==, estimated from onboard ==motor current== measurements (joint effort), were introduced as 'first-class' inputs, concatenated directly with ==proprioceptive joint states== before embedding.
- Four ACT model variants (**Base ACT**, **ACT-o**, **ACT+τ**, **ACT-o+τ**) were systematically compared on real-world contact-rich tasks including surface following, precision insertion, stiffness discrimination, and force-based stopping.

## Results

- The ACT-o variant, with implicit interaction cues removed, showed severe performance degradation with significantly lower success rates across all contact-rich tasks (e.g., **15%** for Board Wipe, **25%** for Soft Bottle Press), often resulting in hesitant or incomplete trajectories.
- Torque-augmented policies (ACT+τ and ACT-o+τ) consistently recovered robust contact behavior, matching or surpassing Base ACT performance (e.g., ACT-o+τ achieved **95%** success for Board Wipe) and dramatically improving upon ACT-o's capabilities.
- Explicit torque proxies enabled policies to disambiguate visually similar but mechanically distinct states (e.g., achieving **70-80%** success in high-stiffness Soft Bottle Press compared to **0%** for Base ACT and ACT-o) and exhibited superior force equilibrium convergence in tasks like Foam Stop.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - ACT's ability to perform contact-rich manipulation is critically dependent on latent interaction cues embedded in the leader-follower teleoperation data, as demonstrated by the severe performance degradation of the ACT-o variant when these cues are removed.
> - Readily available, computationally inexpensive explicit joint-torque proxies can effectively recover and enhance force-aware behavior in ACT, providing critical physical grounding for policies to handle diverse contact regimes without external F/T sensors.
> - Integrating explicit torque proxies not only restores performance but can also lead to more precise, adaptive, and stable policy behaviors (e.g., gentle adjustments, consistent normal force) that exceed what kinematic imitation alone achieves.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Robust contact-rich manipulation presents a significant challenge in robotics due to the partial observability of crucial interaction forces, compliance, and friction through vision and kinematics alone. The field of end-to-end imitation learning has seen advances in developing manipulation policies, broadly categorized into large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and more focused Vision-Action (VA) policies. Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) is a prominent VA model recognized for its performance in fine-grained manipulation, efficiency, and ability to operate with limited demonstration data.

A key aspect of ACT's real-world success, particularly with systems like ALOHA, is the prevalent use of leader-follower teleoperation for demonstration collection. In this setup, the low-level controller tracks leader commands using position control, and the resulting tracking discrepancies between the commanded leader motion and the executed follower motion can implicitly encode information about contact onset, resistance, and constraint violation. This "implicit interaction cue" acts as a latent signal aiding policies in distinguishing between free-space motion and high-load contact scenarios.

However, this reliance on an implicit teleoperation cue introduces a limitation: this signal is absent in other common robot teaching paradigms such as kinesthetic teaching (where the robot is physically guided) or when using high-transparency controllers that minimize tracking error. This raises questions about ACT's generalizability and inherent force-awareness beyond its typical deployment context.

Simultaneously, many robotic platforms, both in research and industrial settings, lack expensive and complex six-axis force/torque (F/T) sensors. Instead, these platforms often provide internal measurements like motor currents or joint effort estimates, which can be used to derive simpler, torque- or wrench-like proxies. While there is a growing interest in multimodal sensing, the systematic evaluation of how these explicit torque proxies compare to the teleoperation-induced implicit cues within ACT-style policies, especially under realistic data scales and contact conditions, has been limited.

This paper addresses these gaps by systematically investigating the extent to which ACT depends on the implicit teleoperation cue and whether readily available onboard torque signals can effectively replace or enhance this force-awareness in real-world contact-rich tasks, without the need for external F/T sensors or large-scale data regimes. It builds upon prior work establishing strong VA baselines and demonstrating the utility of explicit interaction sensing, but focuses on the specific context of ACT and the practical constraints of common hardware.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate the origin of force-awareness in Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policies, specifically examining the role of implicit interaction cues derived from leader-follower teleoperation and evaluating the efficacy of explicit torque proxies as a substitute or enhancement.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations and challenges in contact-rich manipulation:

1. **Reliance on Implicit Teleoperation Cues:** ACT has demonstrated strong performance in fine-grained manipulation, often leveraging demonstrations collected through leader-follower teleoperation. In this setup, the tracking error between the commanded leader motion and the executed follower motion implicitly provides signals related to contact, resistance, and constraint violation. The authors hypothesize that ACT's apparent force-awareness might be dependent on this "hidden interaction cue." A primary motivation is to test this hypothesis by isolating and removing this implicit signal.

2. **Absence of Implicit Cues in Other Teaching Paradigms:** The implicit teleoperation cue is not universally present. In kinesthetic teaching or with high-transparency controllers, this discrepancy signal is either reduced or entirely absent. This raises a critical question about ACT's performance and applicability in these common workflows if it heavily relies on the teleoperation-induced cue. The research is motivated to determine if ACT's contact performance would degrade in such scenarios and, if so, how to address it.

3. **Lack of Dedicated F/T Sensors:** Many robotic platforms lack dedicated six-axis force/torque (F/T) sensors due to cost, hardware mismatch, and integration complexity. Instead, these platforms often expose internal measurements like motor currents or joint effort estimates, which can be used to compute simpler torque-like proxies. Despite interest in multimodal sensing, there is limited systematic evidence on the practical effectiveness of these explicit torque proxies in ACT-style policies, especially in comparison to the implicit teleoperation cues.

4. **Need for Systematic Evaluation:** The paper aims to provide a controlled real-world study to fill this knowledge gap. It seeks to systematically compare implicit versus explicit interaction signals in ACT for various contact-rich manipulation tasks, offering quantitative results and diagnostic analyses.

**Specific Contributions and Objectives:**

- **Isolation of implicit interaction cues in ACT:** To formalize and ablate the teleoperation-induced discrepancy signal, the authors introduce an observation-centric ACT variant that predicts future *follower* robot states instead of *leader* commands. This allows for an experimental evaluation of performance degradation when this implicit cue is removed.
- **Explicit torque proxy evaluation:** The study evaluates whether simple joint-torque proxies, derived from onboard motor current or joint effort, can recover contact-aware behavior in the absence of external F/T sensors. This involves integrating these proxies into the ACT architecture and comparing their performance against the baseline ACT and the ablated variant.
- **Task-level analysis under contact:** The research aims to provide quantitative results and diagnostic analyses across diverse contact regimes, including impact-like contacts, sustained frictional interaction, and state-dependent force transitions. This includes characterizing reactivity and stability under contact and reporting practical guidelines for integrating torque proxies.

In summary, the key motivation is to understand the true source of force-awareness in ACT, assess its vulnerability to changes in demonstration collection methods, and develop a practical, hardware-agnostic solution for robust contact-rich manipulation using readily available onboard sensor data.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The research employs a controlled real-world study to systematically evaluate the impact of implicit teleoperation cues versus explicit torque proxies on ACT's performance in contact-rich manipulation tasks. The methodology centers on reformulating the ACT prediction target and integrating onboard torque signals.

**A. Observation-Centric Prediction and Force Decoupling (ACT-o Variant)**

The core approach to decoupling ACT from implicit teleoperation dynamics involves reformulating the policy's prediction target.

- **Standard ACT:** In conventional ACT implementations, the policy typically maps observations to a sequence of *leader* joint positions (A = {q_leader,t..., q_leader,t+n}). This setup inherently captures a "latent interaction cue" via the positional discrepancy (δ = q_leader - q_follower) that arises during contact.
- **Proposed ACT-o (Observation-Centric ACT):** The authors redefine the decoder prediction target as the *follower's* future proprioceptive states (Q ≜ Q_t:t+n = {q_follower,t+1..., q_follower,t+n}). By predicting the actual follower states rather than the leader commands, this variant explicitly removes the teleoperation-induced discrepancy signal. This reformulation allows for isolating the policy's reliance on these implicit cues and evaluating performance in their absence. The reconstruction loss (L_rec) is adjusted to minimize the error between predicted and actually achieved robot configurations.

**B. Explicit Force Proxies via Servo Current**

To recover force-awareness after removing the implicit cues, the study proposes introducing explicit force proxies.

- **Source of Proxy:** The proxies are derived from onboard motor current measurements. For the ViperX Follower actuators, real-time feedback for joint position, velocity, and effort (current) is available. Joint torque (τ) is estimated via the linear relationship between motor current (I) and the manufacturer-defined torque constant (k_t), i.e., τ = k_t * I.
- **Dynamics Consideration:** While classical contact control often requires an analytical dynamics model to compensate for internal arm dynamics (gravity, Coriolis, friction) when mapping joint torques to end-effector wrenches, the authors hypothesize that providing raw, joint-level effort signals directly allows a torque-aware policy to implicitly learn to compensate for external contact forces and gravity.
- **Injection Method:** The torque proxies (screw-axis torque signals τ) are treated as "first-class" inputs. They are directly concatenated with the corresponding proprioceptive joint states *before* projection into the hidden-dimension embedding space. This results in a 14-dimensional input (7 joint positions + 7 joint efforts) projected into the hidden layer dimension (R^14 -> R^d_hidden), allowing the model to utilize a shared joint-state embedding.

**C. CVAE Role and Input Reformulation**

The Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) architecture of ACT is adapted to accommodate these changes:

- **Standard ACT CVAE:** The encoder typically processes current state and target *leader-arm actions*.
- **Modified CVAE (for ACT-o and torque-augmented variants):**
 - For ACT-o, the encoder processes the current state and future *follower states*.
 - For torque-augmented variants (ACT+τ and ACT-o+τ), the encoder produces the latent variable z = E(q_t, Q_t:t+n, τ_t), optionally including the screw-axis torque proxies. This allows the latent variable `z` to explicitly characterize environmental interactions and loading conditions. The CVAE objective includes a reconstruction term and a KL-divergence regularization term, with the latter potentially conditioning on torque data.

**D. Model Variants and Experimental Setup**

Four ACT model variants are compared:
1. **Base ACT:** Standard setup, predicts leader actions, relies on implicit discrepancy.
2. **ACT-o:** Predicts follower states, implicit discrepancy removed.
3. **ACT + τ:** Predicts leader actions, augmented with explicit torque proxies (hybrid force mechanism).
4. **ACT-o + τ:** Predicts follower states, augmented with explicit torque proxies (explicit force mechanism only).

**E. Hardware Setup:**

All experiments are conducted on a physical ALOHA Solo teleoperation system, comprising an Interbotix ViperX-300s Follower arm and a WidowX-250s Leader arm. Visual input is provided by high-angle BEV, low-profile side, and wrist-mounted cameras. Data collection and policy inference are synchronized at 50 Hz, and control is entirely in joint space. Training is distributed across NVIDIA RTX 4090, A6000, and A5000 GPUs, with real-time inference on an RTX 4090.

**F. Contact-Dynamic Task Design:**

A suite of real-world tasks is designed to evaluate force-torque signal behaviors across diverse contact regimes:
- **Board Wipe:** Surface following and maintaining normal force during lateral movement.
- **Plug Insert:** Multi-mode, high-precision manipulation requiring alignment and force control for insertion.
- **Soft Bottle Press:** Stiffness sensitivity, distinguishing between visually similar but mechanically distinct states (high vs. low stiffness).
- **Foam Stop:** Force-equilibrium behavior, stopping upon light contact with a textureless, compliant object at varying heights.

**G. Task Metrics:**

- **Task Success:** Binary completion of a task within a predefined time horizon, verified visually.
- **Latent Observations:** For visually indistinguishable states (e.g., in Foam Stop), end-effector wrench (F_ext) proxy derived from torque values using the analytic Jacobian and quasi-static assumptions is used for diagnostic analysis.

Hyperparameters for model training (e.g., training epochs, chunk size, batch size, learning rate, hidden dimensions, KL weight) are specified to ensure consistency.

### 4. Main findings and results

The study's results indicate a clear dependency of ACT's contact-rich manipulation performance on implicit teleoperation cues and demonstrate the effectiveness of explicit torque proxies in recovering and enhancing this capability.

**A. Impact of Removing Implicit Interaction Cues (ACT-o Performance)**

- **Severe Performance Degradation:** Removing the implicit interaction cue by having ACT predict future *follower* joint states (ACT-o variant) leads to substantial degradation in contact-dependent behaviors. Across all contact-rich tasks, ACT-o exhibits significantly lower success rates compared to the Base ACT policy.
- **Hesitant and Incomplete Trajectories:** In the absence of explicit torque proxies, ACT-o frequently produces hesitant, stalled, or incomplete trajectories. For instance, in "Board Wipe," ACT-o's 15% success rate is attributed to its inability to maintain consistent downward normal force, resulting in "hovering" behavior. In "Plug Insert," ACT-o sometimes produces invalid trajectories characterized by stationary or "hesitant" behaviors, failing to initiate necessary task dynamics.
- **Inability to Differentiate States:** ACT-o struggles to differentiate between visually similar but mechanically distinct states, particularly evident in "Soft Bottle Press." Without leader-follower lag or explicit force feedback, the policy cannot effectively distinguish between different stiffness modes or different phases of vertical downward/exit trajectories, resulting in low overall success rates (25%) and 0% success in the high-stiffness mode.

**B. Effectiveness of Explicit Torque Proxies**

The integration of screw-axis torque proxies derived from onboard motor current significantly restores and enhances force-aware behavior across all evaluated tasks.

- **Restoration of Contact-Aware Behavior:** Torque-augmented policies (ACT+τ and ACT-o+τ) consistently recover robust contact behavior, often matching or surpassing the performance of Base ACT, and dramatically improving upon ACT-o.
- **Improved Surface Following and Normal Force:** In "Board Wipe," while Base ACT achieves 60% success, both ACT+τ (90%) and ACT-o+τ (95%) demonstrate high success rates. This indicates that explicit torque proxies provide the necessary grounding to modulate vertical motion, ensuring consistent surface pressure during wiping, mitigating the "hovering" failure mode observed in ACT-o. Torque acts as a "geometric anchor" in this context.
- **Disambiguation of Latent States:** For "Soft Bottle Press," torque proxies enable the policies to distinguish between visually identical but mechanically distinct states (e.g., high-stiffness vs. low-stiffness bottles). ACT+τ achieves 80% success in the high-stiffness mode (compared to 0% for Base ACT and ACT-o), and ACT-o+τ achieves 70% success in this mode. This demonstrates that torque provides a critical auxiliary grounding to resolve state ambiguity across different stiffness regimes.
- **Enhanced Precision and Multi-Mode Learning:** In "Plug Insert," torque-aware configurations (ACT+τ at 70% overall, ACT-o+τ at 50% overall) exhibit qualitatively superior positional accuracy. They learn "gentle, reactive motions necessary for alignment," often characterized by a series of "adjustment-taps" over two episodes, which contrasts with the stiff adjustment behaviors and freezing failures of Base ACT. This indicates that torque signals enable more expressive and adaptive multi-mode behavior learning.
- **Superior Force Equilibrium Convergence:** For the "Foam Stop" task, where the goal is to stop upon light contact, all models were able to stop without pushing through or floating. However, ACT+τ and ACT-o+τ demonstrated superior convergence towards the mean demonstration F_x (effort) equilibrium value, closely matching the human-demonstrated effort profile. This suggests that while kinematic imitation alone can achieve stopping, integrating a screw-torque proxy provides computationally inexpensive yet powerful physical grounding for more accurate and stable torque-aware object interaction.

**C. Overall Model Capabilities Summary**

The results consistently show that:
- Base ACT relies on implicit cues for contact.
- ACT-o, without these cues, suffers a systemic performance collapse across contact-dependent tasks.
- Augmenting ACT with explicit torque proxies (+Torque models), whether with or without implicit cues, restores and enhances force-aware manipulation, improving policy modeling and stability.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research makes several contributions to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning the deployment and robustness of imitation learning policies in real-world contact-rich scenarios.

**1. Elucidation of ACT's Force-Awareness Mechanism:** The study clearly demonstrates that Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), in its common leader-follower teleoperation setup, heavily relies on *implicit interaction cues*—specifically, the positional discrepancy between leader and follower arms—to achieve its contact-aware behaviors. By isolating and removing this implicit signal through the observation-centric ACT-o variant, the paper shows a severe, even catastrophic, collapse in performance for contact-rich tasks. This finding is significant because it clarifies a previously unstated assumption about ACT's underlying mechanics, highlighting that its apparent force-awareness is not solely derived from visual or kinematic observations, but from a latent signal present in the demonstration collection method.

**2. Validation of Explicit Torque Proxies as a Practical Solution:** The research establishes that readily available, computationally inexpensive *explicit screw-torque proxies*, derived from onboard motor current or joint effort, can effectively substitute for these implicit teleoperation cues. Integrating these simple proxies into the ACT architecture successfully restores robust contact-aware behavior, making the policies capable of handling tasks involving surface following, precision insertion, stiffness discrimination, and force-based stopping. This provides a practical and accessible solution for platforms that lack expensive six-axis force/torque sensors.

**3. Enhancement Beyond Mere Substitution:** Beyond simply recovering performance, the addition of explicit torque proxies is shown to *enhance* fine-grained actions and overall policy robustness. Torque-augmented policies exhibit emergent behaviors like gentle "adjustment-taps" for precise alignment and consistent normal force maintenance, which kinematic imitation alone struggled to achieve. This suggests that explicit torque signals provide valuable physical grounding, leading to more expressive and stable policy modeling in diverse contact regimes.

**4. Broader Applicability and Hardware Democratization:**
 - **Alternative Demonstration Methods:** The findings are crucial for deployment scenarios involving kinesthetic teaching or high-transparency control interfaces, where the implicit teleoperation cue is diminished or absent. The proposed torque-augmentation strategy offers a pathway to enable robust contact manipulation in these settings without redesigning the core imitation learning framework.
 - **Cost-Effective Robotics:** By demonstrating the efficacy of readily available internal motor current signals, the work contributes to the democratization of advanced robotic capabilities. It reduces the reliance on specialized, expensive hardware, making contact-rich manipulation more accessible to researchers and industries using platforms with integrated motor current sensing.

**5. Practical Guidelines for Policy Integration:** The study provides quantitative results and diagnostic analyses that characterize reactivity and stability under contact, offering practical insights into how to integrate torque proxies effectively into ACT-style policies. For instance, the method of concatenating raw effort values directly with joint states before hidden-dimension projection is empirically validated as effective.

In summary, this research not only uncovers a critical dependency in a widely adopted imitation learning framework but also offers a robust, practical, and hardware-agnostic solution that broadens the applicability and enhances the capabilities of ACT-style policies in contact-rich manipulation tasks. The potential impact lies in fostering more robust, adaptable, and accessible robotic systems capable of performing intricate physical interactions.
