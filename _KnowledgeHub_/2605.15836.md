# GAP: Geometric Anchor Pre-training for Data-Efficient Visuomotor Learning of Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Polytechnic University of Turin developed Geometric Anchor Pre-training (GAP), a method that enhances data-efficient visuomotor learning for robotic manipulation by regularizing spatial adapters with object-centric geometric priors. This approach mitigates issues like "bottleneck collapse" and yields improved policy robustness and success rates, especially in low-data scenarios.

## Problem

- Visuomotor policies for robotic manipulation struggle with data scarcity, requiring effective extraction of task-relevant geometric information from high-dimensional observations without overfitting.
- Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) often lack the fine-grained geometric precision needed for contact-rich manipulation, as their semantic invariance objectives can suppress critical spatial details.
- In low-data regimes, lightweight spatial bottlenecks in VFM-based pipelines are prone to "collapse," latching onto task-irrelevant visual shortcuts and producing poorly localized, brittle keypoints.

## Method

- **GAP** is an action-free warm-up stage pre-training the visual spatial adapter using a simple simulated proxy task, decoupling geometric feature learning from action-mapping.
- The pre-training phase leverages ground-truth object segmentation masks and a multi-objective spatial loss (LGAPL_{GAP}LGAP) comprising centroid alignment, geometric spread, and keypoint diversity.
- After this geometric pre-training, the regularized adapter and frozen VFM encoder are transferred and fine-tuned on downstream manipulation tasks with limited expert demonstrations.

## Results

- GAP consistently achieved higher success rates across diverse manipulation tasks and data scarcity levels, outperforming baselines such as AFA and end-to-end fine-tuning. For example, it reached **62%** success on `Can` with 15 demonstrations, significantly higher than AFA's **46%**.
- The geometric priors learned by GAP demonstrated invariance to the proxy task or simulator, indicating their generalizability, with average success rates of 56-**59%** on `StackCube` across different pre-training conditions.
- GAP effectively enhanced performance for all tested Vision Foundation Model backbones (R3M, DINOv2, VC-1), illustrating its broad compatibility as a spatial regularizer in data-scarce settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision Foundation Models, while semantically rich, benefit from explicit geometric regularization to achieve fine-grained precision necessary for contact-rich manipulation, especially with scarce data.
> - A lightweight, action-free pre-training strategy can effectively prevent "bottleneck collapse" by injecting robust, object-centric geometric priors into the visual representation.
> - The combination of centroid alignment, geometric spread, and keypoint diversity losses synergistically guides the spatial adapter to learn stable, well-distributed, and non-redundant keypoints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has made advancements through Imitation Learning (IL), enabling robots to acquire complex behaviors from expert demonstrations. Recent developments, such as diffusion-based policies, have further improved performance by modeling multimodal action distributions. However, a significant challenge in practical settings is learning reliable visuomotor policies when expert demonstrations are scarce. This scarcity necessitates that the robotic agent effectively extract task-relevant geometric information from high-dimensional RGB observations without overfitting to irrelevant visual cues.

A common approach to enhance data efficiency involves utilizing frozen, pre-trained Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) as visual backbones (e.g., VC-1, DINOv2, R3M). These VFMs provide rich semantic features learned from large-scale datasets, improving generalization across various object categories and environments. Following the VFM, a lightweight spatial bottleneck, often implemented with Spatial Softmax, is typically employed to compress dense feature maps into a compact representation suitable for control. More advanced alternatives include attention-based pooling mechanisms like TokenLearner and Attentive Feature Aggregation (AFA), designed to identify and aggregate task-relevant visual cues.

Despite the benefits of VFMs in providing semantic knowledge, a fundamental trade-off exists: pre-training objectives focused on semantic invariance often suppress high-frequency spatial and geometric details critical for contact-rich manipulation. This limitation means VFMs may excel at identifying *what* an object is but struggle with precisely locating its *extremities* or overall spatial extent. This can lead to two main issues: (1) fine-grained tasks requiring sub-centimeter precision are difficult to resolve, and (2) VFM-based policies can be susceptible to visual distribution shifts, relying on spurious correlations (e.g., background textures, lighting) rather than invariant geometric structures.

In low-data regimes (e.g., 15-50 demonstrations), the lightweight spatial bottleneck, despite its small parameter count, becomes a primary point of failure. Without sufficient data, it can "collapse," latching onto task-irrelevant visual shortcuts (e.g., background features) rather than stable, object-centric geometry. This results in poorly localized and brittle keypoints, rendering the downstream policy unreliable under minor scene perturbations or distribution shifts.

Keypoint-based and structured representations have a history in robotics for condensing visual states into sparse coordinate representations. While earlier methods like Transporter Nets and KeypointNet were sample-efficient, they often relied on reconstruction objectives that did not guarantee control-relevant disentanglement. More recent strategies include leveraging large generative models for feature extraction or semantic correspondence, or employing structured geometric pipelines for category-level planning. These approaches confirm the benefit of explicit spatial structure for visuomotor control but often entail heavy computational overhead, external model dependencies, or dense manual supervision. The research presented here aims to address these identified gaps by providing a lightweight and effective method to inject robust geometric priors into the visual representation for data-efficient visuomotor learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core challenge addressed by this research is the difficulty of learning robust visuomotor policies from a limited number of expert demonstrations in robotic manipulation. This problem is exacerbated by the need to effectively distill high-dimensional RGB observations into control-relevant geometric representations without overfitting, especially when data is scarce.

The key objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

1. **Address "Bottleneck Collapse" in Low-Data Regimes:** A primary motivation is to mitigate the phenomenon of "bottleneck collapse," where lightweight spatial pooling modules, when fine-tuned with few data samples, latch onto task-irrelevant visual shortcuts (e.g., background textures) rather than learning stable, object-centric geometry. This results in poorly localized and brittle keypoints, rendering the downstream policy unreliable even under minor scene perturbations. The paper aims to prevent this by regularizing the spatial adapter.

2. **Enhance Geometric Grounding for VFMs:** Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) are powerful for semantic feature extraction but often lack the fine-grained geometric precision required for contact-rich manipulation. Their pre-training objectives, designed for semantic invariance, can suppress high-frequency spatial details. The objective is to provide a mechanism that injects geometric priors into the VFM-based visual pipeline, ensuring that the visual representations accurately locate object extremities and cover their spatial extent.

3. **Improve Robustness to Scene Perturbations and Domain Shift:** Pre-trained visual representations used for policy learning have been observed to struggle under minor scene perturbations. This highlights a need for robustness-oriented inductive biases. The motivation is to develop a method that yields stable geometric anchors, which can provide a reliable coordinate interface for policy learning and maintain performance despite changes in background, lighting, or simulator environments.

4. **Increase Data Efficiency for Visuomotor Learning:** With limited expert demonstrations (e.g., 15-50 trajectories), current methods often underperform or fail to generalize. The objective is to significantly improve the data efficiency of visuomotor learning, allowing policies to achieve higher success rates with fewer demonstrations by providing better-initialized and more robust visual features.

5. **Develop a Lightweight and Reusable Pre-training Strategy:** The proposed solution, Geometric Anchor Pre-training (GAP), aims to be an action-free warm-up stage that is computationally inexpensive and decoupled from specific downstream tasks. This allows the pre-trained spatial adapter to be reused across various environments and manipulation skills, thereby increasing practicality and reducing the overall training burden. The motivation is to create a solution that is simple to implement and integrate into existing imitation learning frameworks, without requiring extensive additional data or complex supervision for each new task.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to bridge the gap between the semantic richness of VFMs and the precise geometric requirements of robotic manipulation, particularly in data-scarce scenarios, through a targeted and efficient pre-training strategy for the visual bottleneck.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces Geometric Anchor Pre-training (GAP), an action-free warm-up stage designed to regularize the spatial adapter before downstream imitation learning. The approach consists of two distinct phases: a pre-training phase using GAP loss on a proxy task, and a subsequent few-shot adaptation phase for downstream visuomotor policy learning.

**A. Imitation Learning with Diffusion Policies**
The framework adopts Diffusion Policies for visuomotor policy learning. In this setting, an agent learns control behaviors from a dataset of expert demonstrations, each comprising visual observations and corresponding actions. The policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|o_t)$ is parameterized as a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model, with the training objective minimizing the noise prediction error. GAP focuses on improving the visual embedding $E(o_t)$ used to condition this denoising network, ensuring a robust, geometry-aware signal.

**B. The Spatial Adapter**
To extract visual features, a frozen, pre-trained Vision Foundation Model (VFM) $f_\phi$ (e.g., ResNet-50, ViT-S, ViT-B) is employed as the visual backbone. Following this backbone is a lightweight adapter module, $f_A$, which maps the VFM features into precise spatial embeddings.
The adapter $f_A$ first applies a shallow convolutional network (a 3x3 followed by a 1x1 convolution) to project the high-dimensional backbone features into $K$ spatial activation maps, $\Phi_t \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times h \times w}$. Subsequently, $f_A$ applies a Spatial Softmax (SS) module to convert these maps into $K$ 2D spatial coordinates, defined as candidate keypoints $P_t = \{p_{k,t}\}^K_{k=1}$.
This process compresses dense feature maps into $K$ sparse 2D coordinates. While this method can learn semantic trackers with abundant data, the paper notes that in extremely low-data regimes, these keypoints tend to latch onto spurious visual cues rather than geometrically meaningful locations due to the weak, indirect spatial supervision provided by the downstream action regression loss. This leads to the "bottleneck collapse."

**C. Geometric Anchor Pre-training (GAP)**
GAP addresses bottleneck collapse by pre-training the spatial bottleneck $f_A$ on a simple, simulated proxy task, explicitly decoupling geometric feature learning from action-mapping.

1. **Proxy Task:**
 The pre-training aims to align geometric keypoints with task-relevant objects without requiring task-specific knowledge or expert actions. For this, any simple manipulation task or contact-rich motor babbling can be used. The paper experiments with the `LiftCube` task from RoboMimic (a Franka Emika Panda robot grasping and lifting a cube) and `PlaceSphere` from ManiSkill. Trajectories are generated automatically using a scripted controller. Crucially, 100 demonstrations of this proxy task are used, leveraging ground-truth object segmentation masks $M_t$ provided by the simulator. No expert action labels are required during this phase.

2. **GAP Spatial Objectives:**
 The adapter $f_A$ is supervised using a multi-objective spatial loss that encourages object-centric, spatially distributed, and non-redundant keypoints on the robot gripper and interacting objects. The loss comprises three components:
- **Centroid Alignment ($L_{center}$):** Ensures keypoints are grounded on the target object by minimizing the squared Euclidean distance between the predicted keypoint centroid $\bar{p}_t$ and the ground-truth mask centroid $c_t$.
 $\bar{p}_t = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K p_{k,t}$
- **Geometric Spread ($L_{spread}$):** Prevents all keypoints from collapsing to a single location (e.g., the centroid) by enforcing the spatial variance of the keypoints $\sigma_p$ to match a normalized object scale $\sigma_{target}$. $\sigma_{target}$ is approximated from the mask area.
 $\sigma_p = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \|p_{k,t} - \bar{p}_t\|^2$
- **Keypoint Diversity ($L_{div}$):** Maximizes the structural information captured by penalizing redundancy. It enforces a minimum separation margin $\delta_{min}$ between any pair of keypoints. This encourages the network to discover distinct geometric extremities of the object.
 $L_{div} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \left( \max \left( 0, \delta_{min} - \min_{j \neq k} \|p_{k,t} - p_{j,t}\|^2 \right) \right)^2$

 The final pre-training objective, $L_{GAP}$, combines these terms:
 $L_{GAP} = \lambda_c L_{center} + \lambda_s L_{spread} + \lambda_d L_{div}$
 The weighting coefficients used are $\lambda_c = 0.3$, $\lambda_s = 0.5$, and $\lambda_d = 2.0$, with a diversity margin $\delta_{min} = 0.15$.

3. **Object-Centric Keypoint Allocation:**
 For scenes with multiple semantic entities, the $K$ keypoints are partitioned into $M$ disjoint subsets, $P_t = \bigcup_{m=1}^M P_{t,m}$. The $L_{GAP}$ objectives are then applied independently to each subset using its corresponding mask. This promotes entity-centric keypoint tracking, allowing the pre-trained subsets to deploy as independent semantic trackers in novel multi-object environments, requiring minimal adaptation during downstream fine-tuning.

**D. Downstream Policy Adaptation**
After GAP pre-training, the regularized adapter $f_A$ and the frozen VFM encoder $f_\phi$ are transferred to the downstream tasks. The segmentation masks $M_t$ are discarded. The VFM backbone remains frozen, and only the lightweight adapter $f_A$ is fine-tuned alongside the diffusion head using the action-prediction objective $L_{diff}$. This process aims to adapt the geometrically pre-trained keypoints to the specific objects and actions of the new task with minimal expert demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of GAP was conducted on two simulation benchmarks, RoboMimic and ManiSkill3, across four tasks of varying difficulty: `PickAndPlace Can`, `Square Nut Assembly`, `Tool Hang`, and `StackCube`. Experiments were performed under severe data scarcity (15, 20, 30, and 50 expert demonstrations) and tested for domain shift.

1. **Impact of Pretraining on Downstream Task Learning (Table I):**
- **Consistent Outperformance:** GAP consistently achieved the highest success rates across all evaluated tasks and data scarcity levels compared to baselines.
 - On `Can` (RoboMimic), with 15 demonstrations, GAP achieved 62% success, outperforming AFA (46%) and end-to-end fine-tuning (55%). At 50 demonstrations, GAP reached 96%.
 - On the challenging `Square Nut Assembly`, GAP scored 53% with 50 demonstrations, compared to 43% for AFA and 38% for end-to-end.
 - For the long-horizon `Tool Hang`, GAP achieved 63% with 50 demonstrations, significantly higher than the best competitor (R3M + Spatial Softmax at 50%). It also showed a reasonable 27% success with just 15 demos, while end-to-end failed.
 - On `StackCube` (ManiSkill), with 30 demonstrations, GAP achieved 61% success, substantially outperforming full fine-tuning (50%) and AFA (25%).
- **Semantic Attention Limitations:** Methods employing semantic attention mechanisms like AFA exhibited overfitting in low-data regimes. For instance, on `StackCube` with 15 demonstrations, AFA achieved 9% success, which was even lower than a simpler VC-1 + Spatial Softmax (4%). This suggests that larger pooling capacity can be detrimental when data is scarce.
- **Unregularized Spatial Softmax Inconsistency:** Unregularized Spatial Softmax yielded inconsistent results, performing adequately on simpler tasks but struggling to anchor to object geometry on more complex ones.
- **End-to-End Fine-tuning Underperformance:** Despite updating a larger number of parameters, end-to-end fine-tuning generally underperformed GAP across tasks, particularly on more complex skills.
- **High-Data Regime:** At 100 demonstrations on the `Square Nut` task, the performance gap between GAP and unregularized baselines narrowed (VC-1 + SS: 64%, VC-1 + AFA: 65%, GAP: 68%). This confirms that GAP's geometric prior is most critical and provides the highest relative gains in data-scarce environments.

2. **Proxy Task Invariance (Table II):**
 An ablation study on `StackCube` demonstrated that GAP learns general geometric priors rather than simulator-specific textures or visual cues. Performance remained consistent across three pre-training conditions: `Same Table` (proxy task `PlaceSphere` in ManiSkill, same table), `Cross Table` (modified `PlaceSphere` with a different background), and `Cross-Sim` (`LiftCube` in RoboMimic). With 30 demonstrations, the average success rates were 56%, 54%, and 59%, respectively, with no statistically significant difference, all significantly outperforming the VC-1 + SS baseline (28%). This also indicates GAP's task-agnosticism regarding the choice of proxy task.

3. **GAP Impact on Different Backbones (Figure 4):**
 GAP's effectiveness as a spatial regularizer was demonstrated across various VFMs (R3M, DINOv2, VC-1) on the `Square Nut` and `StackCube` tasks with 30 demonstrations.
- GAP consistently improved performance for all backbones, demonstrating its compatibility. For example, it improved DINOv2 performance from 23% to 29% on `Square Nut` and pushed VC-1 to 37%, outperforming all other methods.
- Semantic pooling (AFA) occasionally degraded performance compared to standard Spatial Softmax (e.g., AFA with DINOv2 yielded 19% vs. 23% for DINOv2 + SS on `Square Nut`), reinforcing that high-capacity semantic mechanisms can be detrimental in low-data settings.

4. **Ablation on GAP Objective (Table III):**
 An ablation study on the three components of the GAP loss ($L_{center}$, $L_{spread}$, $L_{div}$) for `Square Nut` with 30 demonstrations showed that all terms are necessary. Removing any individual term or combination degraded performance:
- Removing $L_{div}$ caused keypoints to collapse into the centroid, losing orientation and boundary information.
- Removing $L_{spread}$ prevented keypoints from reaching object boundaries, leading to a tightly clustered, brittle representation.
 This demonstrates the synergistic contribution of all three loss components to the GAP objective.

5. **Pre-training Impact (Table IV):**
 The study compared GAP with baselines pre-trained on the same proxy demonstrations but using action-supervised imitation. Baselines (unregularized Spatial Softmax and AFA) pre-trained with action supervision on the trivial proxy task degraded their downstream performance when transferred. In contrast, GAP, using its geometric supervision, yielded substantial improvements (e.g., 14% improvement over VC-1 + SS baseline). This highlights that the *type* of pre-training (geometric regularization) is crucial, not just exposure to additional proxy data.

6. **Efficiency and Qualitative Observations (Figure 3 & 5):**
 The proxy pre-training stage is computationally lightweight (approximately 40 minutes on a single consumer GPU) and fully decoupled, making it practical for reuse. Qualitative analysis showed that GAP learns transferable geometric anchors, maintaining favorable geometric grounding even in zero-shot applications to novel tasks, simulators, and real-world videos, indicating good sim-to-real transfer potential.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper introduces Geometric Anchor Pre-training (GAP), a method with implications for the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in scenarios characterized by data scarcity.

One significant aspect of GAP is its **direct addressal of "bottleneck collapse,"** a identified failure mode in data-efficient visuomotor learning pipelines that utilize frozen Vision Foundation Models (VFMs). By regularizing the spatial pooling layer, GAP prevents this layer from overfitting to irrelevant visual features and losing object-centric geometric grounding when fine-tuned with limited demonstrations. This directly contributes to the robustness and reliability of robotic policies.

The method demonstrates **substantial improvements in data efficiency**. In imitation learning, acquiring large datasets of expert demonstrations is often costly and time-consuming. GAP enables higher success rates with significantly fewer demonstrations (e.g., 15-50), making it more practical to deploy visuomotor learning solutions in real-world environments where data collection is constrained.

GAP offers a **lightweight, action-free, and decoupled pre-training strategy**. The pre-training phase is computationally efficient, requiring approximately 40 minutes on a single consumer GPU, and does not require expert action labels. This decoupling means the geometric anchor pre-training can be performed once and reused across different downstream tasks and environments, enhancing scalability and reducing the development overhead for new robotic skills.

The work highlights the **complementarity between semantic and geometric representations**. While VFMs provide rich semantic understanding, GAP augments this with precise geometric grounding. This suggests a pathway for developing policies that are simultaneously robust to semantic variations and accurate in their spatial reasoning, which is crucial for contact-rich manipulation tasks.

Furthermore, the demonstrated **transferability of geometric priors across different simulators, visual domains, and tasks** is a notable achievement. The ability of GAP to learn domain-agnostic geometric anchors reduces the need for domain-specific pre-training, facilitating sim-to-real transfer and generalization to novel situations without requiring extensive fine-tuning.

The empirical findings underscore the **importance of explicit geometric regularization** over purely semantic attention mechanisms or unregularized pooling, especially in low-data regimes. This provides valuable insights into the inductive biases necessary for robust visuomotor learning and informs future architectural designs for robotic control systems.

In conclusion, GAP provides a practical and effective solution to a core challenge in robot learning, with the potential to accelerate the development and deployment of visuomotor policies for a wider range of manipulation tasks, particularly those where expert demonstrations are inherently limited. Its impact extends to making robotic learning more accessible, efficient, and robust in diverse operational settings.
