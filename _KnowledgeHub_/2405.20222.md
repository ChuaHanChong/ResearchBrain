# MOFA-Video: Controllable Image Animation via Generative Motion Field Adaptions in Frozen Image-to-Video Diffusion Model

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Tokyo and Tencent AI Lab developed MOFA-Video, a framework that integrates Generative Motion Field Adapters into a frozen Image-to-Video diffusion model to enable precise animation. This method achieves superior control precision and visual quality for diverse image animation tasks, supporting controlled motion via trajectories, facial landmarks, or reference videos, and allows for longer, temporally consistent video generation.

## Problem

- Existing domain-specific image animation methods offer high control but lack generalizability to diverse, "in-the-wild" images.
- Open-domain Image-to-Video (I2V) diffusion models generalize well but often produce content that deviates from the input image, offer weak motion control, or generate only simple idle animations.
- There was a need for a unified framework that combines open-domain generalizability with precise, fine-grained control using various external signals like trajectories or key-points.

## Method

- Introduced Generative Motion Field Adapters (MOFA-Adapters) as an additional network branch to condition a frozen, pre-trained Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) model, allowing for diverse motion control.
- MOFA-Adapters utilize a Sparse-to-Dense (S2D) Motion Generator to convert sparse motion hints (from ==optical flow== or human key-points) into dense motion fields for explicit frame-wise motion modeling.
- Implemented a training strategy that optimizes only the MOFA-Adapter parameters while keeping the base SVD model frozen, efficiently leveraging its generative priors.

## Results

- Achieved superior control precision and visual quality for trajectory-based image animation, demonstrating improved LPIPS (0.2274 vs. 0.2705 for DragNUWA) and higher Control Precision in user studies (3.58 vs. 2.76).
- Demonstrated enhanced performance for portrait image animation, producing more natural expressions and higher resolution results (e.g., CPBD 0.4075 vs. 0.3218 for SadTalker, ID 0.9293 vs. 0.9188).
- Successfully enabled the generation of longer, temporally coherent videos and supported zero-shot combination of multiple control signals (e.g., facial landmarks and trajectories) using a mask-aware approach.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling frame-wise motion through generative motion field adapters is crucial for achieving fine-grained, domain-aware control within a frozen I2V diffusion model.
> - Converting sparse motion hints into dense motion fields allows for a balanced integration of precise control signals with the generative capabilities of the base video diffusion model.
> - A periodic sampling strategy with overlapping frames and noise averaging effectively mitigates error accumulation and flickering, enabling the generation of longer, temporally consistent video sequences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses the domain of image animation, which involves generating a video sequence from a single input image. This field has traditionally faced a dichotomy between highly controllable, domain-specific methods and more general, but less controllable, approaches.

Earlier image animation methods often specialized in limited categories of objects or phenomena, such as fluids, human hair, or specific human body parts (e.g., face or pose). These methods typically achieved high control abilities by leveraging detailed domain knowledge, often through self-supervised decomposition of video and subsequent animation via new driving signals. Examples include methods like SadTalker for facial animation or Text2Cinemagraph for water animation. However, their primary limitation is a lack of generalizability to diverse, "in-the-wild" images, as they depend heavily on domain-specific priors.

More recently, the advent of large-scale generative models, particularly diffusion-based Image-to-Video (I2V) models, has opened new avenues for open-domain image animation. Leveraging priors from text-to-image models like Stable Diffusion, these I2V models have demonstrated the capability to generate videos from arbitrary images. Despite this generalizability, current I2V models often suffer from several drawbacks: they may generate content that deviates from the input image, typically produce simple motions based on text descriptions, or create only idle animations. These limitations restrict their applicability in scenarios requiring precise and nuanced motion control.

The current work, MOFA-Video, positions itself at the intersection of these two paradigms. It aims to bridge the gap by developing a general image animation framework that supports meticulous control abilities over in-the-wild images, combining the open-domain generation capabilities of I2V diffusion models with the fine-grained control associated with domain-specific methods. The authors frame animation as the motion propagation of sparse key-points or key-frames, seeking to integrate diverse motion control signals into a pre-trained video diffusion model, specifically Stable Video Diffusion (SVD). This approach distinguishes itself from concurrent works like DragNUWA, which integrates sparse motion without explicit flow and warping, or MotionCtrl, which focuses on object and camera motions in text-to-video models. MOFA-Video's differentiation lies in its explicit modeling of frame-wise motion through generative motion field adapters, allowing for domain-aware control in a frozen I2V model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an advanced, controllable image animation method capable of generating video from a given image using a variety of external control signals. These signals include human landmarks, manual trajectories, or even reference videos, and can be used individually or in combination.

The motivation for this work stems from observed limitations in existing image animation techniques:
1. **Limited Generalizability of Domain-Specific Methods**: While prior methods achieve high control for specific object categories (e.g., human faces, fluids, hair), their reliance on domain-specific knowledge prevents their application to general, open-domain images. The goal is to extend precise control beyond these narrow categories to "in-the-wild" scenes.
2. **Weak Control in Open-Domain I2V Diffusion Models**: Contemporary diffusion-based I2V models offer the advantage of open-domain image animation. However, they frequently exhibit drawbacks such as:
 - Generating video content that may diverge significantly from the initial input image.
 - Providing only rudimentary motion control, often limited to simple text descriptions or idle animations.
 - Lacking the precise and fine-grained control often required for practical applications.
 The authors identified a need for enhanced control capabilities within these powerful generative frameworks.
3. **Desire for Unified and Meticulous Control**: The researchers were motivated to ascertain if a general image animation framework could be developed that supports meticulous control over diverse images, akin to the precise control seen in domain-specific animations. This involved exploring whether all animations could be conceptualized as the motion propagation of sparse control points or key-frames.
4. **Leveraging Pre-trained Models Efficiently**: Inspired by ControlNet, the motivation was to add additional motion control abilities to a powerful pre-trained generative video diffusion model (Stable Video Diffusion in this case) without extensive retraining of the base model. This approach aims to efficiently adapt existing models to new control tasks.
5. **Multi-Domain Control and Combinability**: Recognizing that motion control can originate from various sources (e.g., hand-drawn paths, facial key-points), the objective was to design a system that could not only handle these diverse domains individually but also combine different control signals in a zero-shot manner for more complex animations.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the gap between domain-specific precision and open-domain generality by introducing a flexible framework that integrates diverse, sparse motion control signals into a frozen video diffusion model through novel domain-aware adapters.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MOFA-Video proposes a framework for controllable image animation built upon a frozen, pre-trained Image-to-Video (I2V) diffusion model, specifically Stable Video Diffusion (SVD). The core of the methodology involves introducing **Generative Motion Field Adapters (MOFA-Adapters)**, which are designed to interpret and apply sparse motion control signals to guide the video generation process.

#### 3.1. Generative Motion Field Adapter (MOFA-Adapter) Structure

Inspired by ControlNet, the MOFA-Adapter operates as an additional network branch that conditions the frozen denoising UNet of the SVD model. Its structure comprises three main components:
- **Reference Encoder**: This is a multi-scale convolutional feature encoder that extracts hierarchical features from the input reference image (the first frame). Each stage of this encoder uses basic blocks consisting of Conv-SiLU-ZeroConv layers. These extracted features are crucial for subsequent warping operations.
- **Sparse-to-Dense (S2D) Motion Generator**: This network, conceptually similar to CMP, is a convolutional neural network designed to take the first frame image and sparse motion hints as input. Its function is to convert these sparse hints into dense motion fields for the entire video sequence. This dense motion field explicitly models frame-wise motion, providing a balance between the sparse guidance and the generative process.
- **Feature Fusion Encoder**: This component is a trainable copy of the SVD encoder. It receives the multi-scale features from the Reference Encoder, which are then spatially warped according to the dense motion fields produced by the S2D network. These warped features are then merged and added to the corresponding feature maps within the decoder of the pre-trained SVD model, akin to how ControlNet injects control signals.

#### 3.2. Training MOFA-Adapters on Stable Video Diffusion

The training process focuses solely on optimizing the parameters of the MOFA-Adapter, leaving the base SVD model frozen. The SVD model itself is a latent diffusion model that first encodes the input image into a latent space and then generates video by denoising sampled Gaussian noise conditioned on the image.

The training pipeline involves generating sparse motion vectors from video clips, which then serve as input to the S2D network:
- **Sparse Motion Vectors from Dense Optical Flow**: For general open-domain motions, dense optical flow is considered a universal motion representation. The Unimatch algorithm is used to extract forward optical flow between video frames. From this dense flow, sparse motion vectors are sampled using a watershed sampling strategy. A sparse mask identifies the sampled spatial points, and the flow values at these points constitute the sparse motion vectors.
- **Sparse Motion Vectors from Structural Human Key-Points**: For human-specific motions, such as facial animation, structural key-points provide semantic guidance. For a portrait video, 2D facial landmarks are extracted. The motion hints are generated by calculating the point-wise difference between the landmarks of the reference (first) frame and subsequent frames. This converts key-point movements into a sparse motion vector representation compatible with the S2D network.

The training objective is a standard diffusion loss, where the MOFA-Adapter (M) assists the SVD model (S) in reconstructing the video clip (V) from its noisy latent representation (V_t), conditioning on the first frame (I) and the sparse motion vectors (F_s). The loss function L aims to minimize the difference between the SVD's output (conditioned by MOFA-Adapter) and the ground-truth video latent.

#### 3.3. Inference and Applications

After training, MOFA-Video supports various controlled animation tasks:
- **Hand-Crafted Trajectories**: Users can draw trajectories on an image. These trajectories are interpolated to a specified number of points, and sparse motion hints are calculated based on the displacement from the starting point. This enables control over object movement or simulated "camera motion" (e.g., panning, zooming).
- **Regional Motion Brushes**: Users can define a binary mask to specify the animation region. Trajectories are categorized as inside or outside the mask. Separate dense optical flows are generated for each group via the S2D network, masked, and then combined to control specific regions.
- **Portrait Image Animation**: An audio-driven talking face generation method (e.g., SadTalker) is used to generate a sequence of facial landmarks from an audio input and a single image. These landmarks are then converted into sparse motion hints, which drive the facial MOFA-Adapter to produce animated portraits.
- **Longer Animation (Periodic Sampling)**: To overcome the SVD's inherent limitation on video length, a periodic sampling strategy is introduced. In each diffusion step, 14 frames are sampled periodically with a 7-frame overlap between consecutive groups. The predicted noise for overlapping frames is averaged, promoting temporal smoothness and consistency over longer sequences.
- **Plugin Combinations**: The modular design of MOFA-Adapters allows for combining multiple adapters from different motion domains (e.g., facial landmarks and hand-crafted trajectories) in a zero-shot manner. A mask-aware strategy can be employed to define specific control areas for each adapter, enabling complex animations where different parts of the image are controlled by distinct motion signals.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both quantitative and qualitative evaluations of MOFA-Video, comparing it against state-of-the-art methods and conducting ablation studies to validate its architectural components.

#### 4.1. Comparison with Other State-of-the-Art Methods

- **Trajectory-based Image Animation**:
 - **Qualitative Comparison**: When compared with DragNUWA, MOFA-Video exhibited superior control precision, localizing motion influence more accurately and avoiding unintended background movement (e.g., controlling a ship without moving the beach). DragNUWA was observed to incorrectly extend user annotations to the background, particularly when motion priors for specific parts were strong.
 - **Quantitative Comparison**: MOFA-Video achieved better performance across multiple metrics for trajectory-based image animation, including LPIPS (↓0.2274 vs. 0.2705), FID (↓16.82 vs. 19.66), FVD (↓86.76 vs. 91.38), Frame Consistency (↑0.9390 vs. 0.9302), Control Precision (↑3.58 vs. 2.76), and Visual Quality (↑3.42 vs. 3.18) in user studies (Table 1).
 - **Camera Motion Control**: For camera motion tasks (e.g., zooming, panning, rotating), MOFA-Video produced results comparable to MotionCtrl, with fewer visual artifacts and the ability to handle larger motion magnitudes. This was achieved by directly using fixed optical flow patterns to control the video diffusion model.

- **Portrait Image Animation**:
 - **Qualitative Comparison**:
 - Against StyleHEAT, MOFA-Video adapted to unaligned input images, avoiding issues of overly smooth outcomes and identity preservation that arose from StyleHEAT's latent inversion process.
 - Against SadTalker, MOFA-Video processed unaligned images without the noticeable "paste-back" artifacts generated by SadTalker. It also demonstrated synchronized body movements with head motion and enabled higher-resolution synthesis (up to 1024x1024), addressing the resolution discrepancy observed in SadTalker's 256x256 outputs.
 - **Quantitative Comparison**: MOFA-Video achieved superior results across metrics for portrait image animation: CPBD (↑0.4075 vs. 0.3218 for SadTalker and 0.2577 for StyleHEAT), ID (↑0.9293 vs. 0.9188 and 0.7993), Fidelity (↑4.8 vs. 4.15 and 3.26), Naturalness (↑3.97 vs. 3.12 and 3.65), and Visual Quality (↑4.52 vs. 3.97 and 3.7) in user studies (Table 2). It also showed better visual quality (LPIPS 0.2099 vs. SadTalker 0.2308) and comparable lip synchronization.

- **User Studies**: User studies consistently indicated a preference for MOFA-Video's results in both trajectory-based and facial animation tasks, particularly regarding visual quality, control precision, fidelity, and naturalness of motion.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Network Structure of MOFA-Adapter**:
 - **Sparse-conditioning model (w/o warping)**: This variant, which directly used sparse trajectories as conditions without spatial warping, was unable to precisely manipulate the target object's trajectory, leading to background shifts.
 - **Sparse-warping model (w/o S2D network)**: This model, which directly used sparse optical flow for warping without generating a dense flow, could manage trajectories but produced significant artifacts due to a lack of dense feature control.
 - **Non-tuning model (w/o tuning)**: Directly using a flow-based reconstruction model without finetuning resulted in "over-control" from the dense optical flow, preventing suitable content generation with SVD's priors.
 - The full MOFA-Video model, integrating the S2D network and spatial warping, achieved optimal results in both controllability and synthesis quality, as supported by both qualitative (Fig. 10) and quantitative (Table 3) evaluations.

- **Longer Video Inference (Periodic Sampling)**:
 - **Dynamic-conditional Naive Separation**: Generating groups of frames by conditioning on the last frame of the preceding group led to significant error accumulation and unsatisfactory results in later stages.
 - **Zero-conditional Naive Separation**: Conditioning each group solely on the first frame mitigated error accumulation but introduced noticeable flickering and temporal inconsistency between inference clips.
 - The proposed periodic sampling strategy, with overlapping frames and averaging predicted noise, successfully addressed both error accumulation and temporal inconsistency, enabling the generation of longer, coherent video sequences (Fig. 11).

- **Effectiveness of Domain-Aware MOFA-Adapter**: Directly applying the trajectory-based MOFA-Adapter (trained on open-domain videos) to landmark-based portrait animation resulted in unnatural facial expressions. This demonstrated the necessity and effectiveness of training domain-specific MOFA-Adapters to handle distinct motion domains (Fig. 12).

#### 4.3. Limitations

The paper acknowledges two primary limitations:
1. The method struggles to control or generate novel content significantly different from the input image, a constraint attributed to the base video diffusion model being trained on limited video clips.
2. Visual artifacts, such as blurriness or structural loss, may occur under extensive motion guidance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MOFA-Video framework represents a notable advancement in the field of controllable image animation, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Unified and Flexible Control Framework**: The introduction of MOFA-Video provides a unified architecture that can accommodate a diverse range of control signals, including manual trajectories, human facial landmarks, and even dense motion flows. This flexibility contrasts with previous methods that were often restricted to specific motion domains, thereby expanding the applicability of controllable image animation to a broader array of "in-the-wild" scenarios.

- **Enhanced Fine-Grained Control**: By explicitly modeling frame-wise motion through the Sparse-to-Dense (S2D) network and spatial warping within the MOFA-Adapter, the method achieves a level of fine-grained control that addresses a key limitation of many open-domain Image-to-Video (I2V) diffusion models. This allows users to precisely dictate object movements or camera motions, moving beyond simple text-based or idle animations.

- **Efficient Leveraging of Pre-trained Models**: The design choice to build MOFA-Video on top of a frozen Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) model, with only the MOFA-Adapters being trained, represents an efficient paradigm. This approach capitalizes on the extensive generative priors of large-scale, pre-trained models while enabling specialized control without the prohibitive computational cost of full model retraining. This efficiency can accelerate research and development in controllable video generation.

- **Diverse Application Potential**: The framework's ability to handle multiple control modalities individually and in combination opens up new possibilities across various applications. This includes customized video creation for artistic and design purposes, realistic animation of still portraits (e.g., for virtual avatars or historical reenactments), and complex scene animations where different elements require distinct types of control. The "motion brush" and "camera motion" capabilities further augment its utility in creative workflows.

- **Zero-Shot Combinability of Controls**: The demonstrated ability to combine different MOFA-Adapters in a zero-shot manner (e.g., animating a face with landmarks while simultaneously moving background objects with trajectories) is a significant architectural advantage. This modularity allows for the creation of intricate and highly customized animations without requiring specific multi-modal retraining, enhancing practical utility and creative freedom.

- **Methodological Contributions to Temporal Consistency**: The proposed periodic sampling strategy for generating longer videos addresses a critical challenge in video diffusion models: maintaining temporal consistency and mitigating error accumulation over extended sequences. This technique offers a practical solution for producing more coherent and longer animations, which is vital for many real-world video applications.

In summary, MOFA-Video's significance lies in its methodological innovation for integrating precise, multi-domain control into robust I2V diffusion models, thereby expanding the capabilities of controllable image animation and potentially impacting creative industries, virtual content generation, and broader computer vision research.
