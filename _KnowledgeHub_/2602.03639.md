# Variance-Reduced Model Predictive Path Integral via Quadratic Model Approximation

> [!summary] Summary
> A variance-reduced Model Predictive Path Integral framework enhances sample efficiency and robustness in control tasks by integrating local objective function models into the sampling process. This approach achieves consistent, high-precision convergence with significantly fewer samples across diverse problems, including those with non-smooth dynamics.

## Problem

- Sampling-based controllers like MPPI suffer from high estimator variance and low sample efficiency, often requiring thousands of parallel simulations for stable performance.
- Traditional MPPI struggles with real-time, high-frequency control loops due to the computational cost of generating numerous samples, especially in high-dimensional and low-sample scenarios.
- Existing methods for incorporating curvature information into MPPI still operate in high-sample regimes and lack robust performance for non-smooth or black-box objectives.

## Method

- A hybrid variance-reduced ==MPPI== framework is proposed, which decomposes the objective function into an approximate model term and a residual term, and samples from a model-guided prior distribution.
- A closed-form Gaussian model-guided prior is derived using a quadratic approximation of the objective function, allowing samples to be concentrated in informative regions.
- The framework flexibly incorporates curvature information from various sources, including analytical derivatives, Gauss-Newton approximations, Quasi-Newton methods, and gradient-free Randomized Smoothing for non-smooth objectives.

## Results

- Model-Guided MPPI consistently converged faster and with lower variance on static optimization benchmarks compared to vanilla MPPI and CMA-ES using 100 samples.
- On a cart-pole swing-up task, the framework achieved consistent high-precision convergence with negligible variance, even with as few as N=2 samples, demonstrating 'sample invariance' across different sample budgets.
- For contact-rich manipulation with non-smooth dynamics, Model-Guided MPPI using Randomized Smoothing reliably achieved low optimality gaps across 200 diverse problem instances, outperforming vanilla MPPI and CMA-ES in consistency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing the objective and sampling from a model-guided prior significantly reduces estimator variance and improves sample efficiency by focusing samples on the residual.
> - The proposed method exhibits 'sample invariance' in continuous control tasks, achieving high-precision convergence with as few as two samples, demonstrating robustness to low-sample budgets.
> - Randomized Smoothing allows the framework to extract useful geometric information and maintain robust performance in problems with non-smooth or black-box dynamics, such as contact-rich manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses fundamental challenges in formulating control, estimation, and motion planning as optimization problems, particularly within the paradigm of sampling-based controllers. Traditionally, these problems are solved by finding a single optimal configuration or trajectory using local, first- or second-order methods like gradient descent or Newton's method. While efficient for smooth and convex objectives, these methods can converge to local minima in non-convex landscapes and struggle with non-smooth functions.

An alternative perspective involves optimizing over probability measures, aiming to minimize the expected cost under a distribution. This measure-theoretic approach unifies several frameworks in global optimization, stochastic control, and sampling-based motion planning. Within this approach, two complementary families of methods exist:

1. **Convex Relaxations:** Methods like Lasserre’s Moment–Sum-of-Squares (SOS) hierarchy and KernelSOS provide systematic sequences of semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations that can theoretically converge to global optima. These methods offer strong theoretical guarantees but suffer from rapid growth in computational complexity and size of SDPs with problem dimensionality, making them generally impractical for real-time, high-frequency control loops.

2. **Information-Theoretic / KL-Regularized Formulations:** These methods, exemplified by Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, express optimal control distributions as Boltzmann distributions by introducing an entropy or KL penalty. MPPI offers practical advantages, including being derivative-free, accommodating non-smooth costs (e.g., contact events), and being amenable to parallel computation. However, MPPI approximates the optimal distribution via Monte Carlo sampling, which introduces significant challenges: high estimator variance and low sample efficiency, often requiring thousands of parallel simulations for stable performance. Existing attempts to incorporate curvature information, such as reconstructing local Jacobians and performing Gauss-Newton updates, have been noted to still operate in high-sample regimes.

The broader field of stochastic optimization has extensively studied variance reduction techniques, including CMA-ES, the Cross-Entropy Method, importance sampling, control variates, and variance-reduced gradient methods. These techniques consistently demonstrate that incorporating structural information improves the efficiency of noisy estimators. However, applying these in real-time control is constrained by strict computational budgets, necessitating effective performance in low-sample regimes.

This research paper positions itself within this landscape by seeking to bridge the gap between the flexibility of sampling-based control (like MPPI) and the need for higher sample efficiency and faster convergence, particularly in scenarios with limited computational resources. It aims to integrate structural information into MPPI to reduce variance and improve performance in low-sample settings, thereby making these methods more practical for real-time applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the efficiency and robustness of sampling-based controllers, specifically Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) methods, by reducing their inherent variance and improving sample efficiency. This is motivated by the observation that while MPPI offers substantial flexibility in handling complex, non-smooth dynamics and is amenable to parallelization, its reliance on Monte Carlo sampling often leads to high estimator variance and slow convergence, especially in high-dimensional and low-sample scenarios. Current state-of-the-art MPPI implementations frequently require thousands of parallel rollouts to achieve stable performance, which is often impractical for real-time control with stringent computational budgets.

The key question driving this work is whether it is possible to retain the flexibility of sampling-based control while achieving faster convergence with significantly fewer samples. To address this, the authors propose a hybrid variance-reduced MPPI framework. The specific objectives and motivations are:

- **Decomposition of the Objective Function:** To formally derive a variance-reduced MPPI framework by decomposing the objective function into an approximate model term and a residual term. The motivation is that the residual, representing the discrepancy between the model and the true objective, is expected to have a smaller magnitude and lower variance, making it more amenable to efficient sampling.
- **Development of a Closed-Form Model-Guided Prior:** To propose a specific instantiation of this framework using a quadratic approximation of the objective function. This choice is motivated by its analytical tractability, as it allows for the derivation of a closed-form, model-guided Gaussian proposal distribution. This proposal distribution is designed to effectively concentrate samples in informative regions, thereby guiding the sampling process more efficiently than isotropic or fixed priors.
- **Flexibility in Curvature Information Acquisition:** To demonstrate that the quadratic model can be constructed from various sources of geometric information, including exact analytical derivatives, structural approximations (such as Gauss-Newton or Quasi-Newton methods for specific problem structures), or gradient-free randomized smoothing for non-smooth or black-box objectives. This objective aims to ensure the framework's broad applicability across different problem types and computational contexts, from fully differentiable systems to those with non-smooth dynamics.
- **Validation of Performance in Low-Sample Regimes:** To numerically validate the proposed approach on standard optimization benchmarks, a nonlinear underactuated control task (cart-pole), and a contact-rich manipulation problem (single-finger sphere manipulation) with non-smooth dynamics. The motivation for this validation is to quantitatively demonstrate superior convergence speed and performance, particularly in low-sample regimes, compared to standard MPPI baselines. The goal is to show that the method can make sample-based control strategies more practical in real-world scenarios where obtaining numerous samples is computationally expensive or otherwise limited.

In summary, the motivation is to overcome the sample efficiency limitations of existing MPPI methods by intelligently incorporating local geometric information, thereby enabling more reliable and faster control in resource-constrained environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this work is a hybrid variance-reduced MPPI framework that integrates a prior model into the sampling process by decomposing the objective function. This approach fundamentally alters how samples are generated and weighted, aiming to concentrate them in informative regions and reduce the variance of the overall estimation.

**A. Model-Guided Distribution Update**

The approach begins by reformulating the information-theoretic optimal Boltzmann update for a distribution `p*(x)` at iteration `k+1`:
`p*(x) ∝ p_θ_k(x) exp(-f(x)/λ)`
where `p_θ_k(x)` is the prior estimate, `f(x)` is the objective function, and `λ` is the temperature parameter. The key insight is to decompose `f(x)` into a known model `m_k(x)` and a residual term `r_k(x)`, such that `f(x) = m_k(x) + r_k(x)`. Substituting this into the Boltzmann update yields:
`p*(x) ∝ [p_θ_k(x) exp(-m_k(x)/λ)] exp(-r_k(x)/λ)`

The term in brackets is defined as the **model-guided prior** `~p_θ_k(x)`. Instead of sampling from the original prior `p_θ_k(x)` and weighting by the full objective `f(x)`, the method samples from this structurally informed `~p_θ_k(x)` and weights only by the residual `r_k(x)`. This formulation is flexible to any choice of model `m_k`, provided it allows `~p_θ_k(x)` to remain analytically tractable for sampling.

**B. Closed-Form Model-Guided Prior via Quadratic Model Approximation**

To achieve an efficient, closed-form algorithm, the authors instantiate this general framework using Gaussian approximations:
1. The current estimate `p_θ_k(x)` is assumed to be a Gaussian distribution `N(x | x̄_k, Σ_k)`.
2. The model `m_k(x)` is chosen as a quadratic expansion of the cost around the current mean `x̄_k`:
 `m_k(x) = f(x̄_k) + (g_k)^T(x - x̄_k) + 1/2 (x - x̄_k)^T H_k (x - x̄_k)`
 where `g_k` is the gradient and `H_k` is the Hessian at `x̄_k`.

Since the product of a Gaussian PDF and the exponential of a quadratic function results in another Gaussian, the model-guided prior `~p_θ_k(x)` also remains Gaussian, `N(x | ~x_k, ~Σ_k)`. Its parameters are derived analytically:
- **Guided Covariance:** `~Σ_k = ((Σ_k)^-1 + (1/λ)H_k)^-1`
- **Guided Mean:** `~x_k = ~Σ_k ((Σ_k)^-1 x̄_k + (1/λ)(H_k x̄_k - g_k))`

A crucial step involves regularizing or convexifying `H_k` if it is indefinite to ensure `~Σ_k` is a valid positive-definite covariance matrix. The final update `x̄_k+1` is then obtained by sampling `N` trajectories `{x^(i)}` from `N(~x_k, ~Σ_k)` and applying Monte Carlo estimation with self-normalized importance weights `~w^(i)` based on the residual `r_k(x^(i))`:
`x̄_k+1 ≈ Σ ~w^(i) x^(i)`, where `~w^(i) = exp(-r_k(x^(i))/λ) / Σ exp(-r_k(x^(j))/λ)`

**C. Incremental Update Formulation**

The method can also be expressed in terms of incremental steps `ϵ = x - x̄_k`. This leads to a model-guided incremental update step mean `~δ_k` and covariance `~Σ_k` for the perturbations `ϵ`. The Monte Carlo approximation for `∆x̄_k = E[ϵ]` is performed by sampling `{ϵ_i}` from `N(~δ_k, ~Σ_k)` and weighting them by `w_i` based on `r_k(x̄_k + ϵ_i)`. The mean parameter is then updated as `x̄_k+1 ← x̄_k + ∆x̄_k`. This formulation exposes the prior covariance `Σ_0k` as a tunable parameter, which is used for variance control.

**D. Quadratic Model Approximations (Sources of `g_k` and `H_k`)**

The framework's strength lies in its ability to decouple the sampling mechanism from the method used to obtain `g_k` and `H_k`:
- **Analytical Second-Order Approximations:** If `f` is differentiable and smooth, `g_k` and `H_k` can be exact analytical derivatives, leading to precise Newton-like steps.
- **Gauss-Newton Approximations:** For non-linear least-squares objectives `f(x) = 1/2 ||R(x)||^2`, the Hessian can be approximated as `H_k ≈ J(x)^T J(x)`, where `J(x)` is the Jacobian of `R(x)`. This avoids full Hessian computation and ensures a positive-semidefinite approximation.
- **Quasi-Newton Approximations:** Methods like BFGS or L-BFGS can be employed when second-order information is expensive or unavailable. They iteratively build a curvature estimate `H_k` from gradient updates history.
- **Randomized Smoothing (RS) Approximations:** For non-smooth or black-box objectives, RS is used to estimate `g_k` and `H_k` using only function evaluations. This involves defining a smoothed surrogate `f_σ(x)` via Gaussian convolution and using Monte Carlo estimators based on Stein's identity. A larger smoothing scale `σ` yields a "coarse" model, capturing global geometry and filtering noise, while a smaller `σ` yields a "fine" model, capturing local curvature for precise convergence.

**E. Stability and Variance Control**

To prevent variance collapse and ensure adequate exploration, two regularization strategies are employed:
- **Polyak/Juditsky Averaging:** Exponential moving averages are applied to the model-guided parameters (`~δ_k`, `~Σ_k`) to smooth their evolution, preventing abrupt changes due to noisy estimates.
- **Variance Control:** A scaling strategy regulates the magnitude of the distribution while preserving its anisotropic geometry. By analyzing the covariance update along the principal directions of the Hessian, a lower bound on the input noise `σ^2_0k` is enforced. This ensures that the variance in the most constrained direction (associated with the maximum eigenvalue of `H_k`) remains above a target threshold `σ^2_target`, maintaining a minimum exploration width.

The overall algorithm proceeds iteratively, constructing the quadratic model, computing the guided prior, sampling from it, evaluating the residual, and updating the mean parameter, incorporating the stability mechanisms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validates the proposed variance-reduced Model-Guided MPPI framework across static optimization benchmarks, continuous control tasks, and contact-rich manipulation problems, highlighting its performance benefits, particularly in low-sample regimes.

**A. Illustration of Quadratic Model Guidance**
On a 2D narrow-valley objective (Figure 1), the Model-Guided MPPI demonstrably reshapes the sampling distribution to align with the cost geometry by performing a Newton-like update for the distribution mean. This results in samples being concentrated in high-probability regions. Quantitatively, the method achieved an Effective Sample Size (ESS) of 23.4, indicating efficient coverage and lower weight degeneracy, compared to vanilla MPPI, which exhibited an ESS of 1.1, suggesting severe weight degeneracy.

**B. Performance with Analytical Gradients**
- **Static Benchmarks:** On standard optimization problems (Rosenbrock, Styblinski–Tang, Rastrigin, Ackley) using 100 samples, the Model-Guided MPPI consistently converged faster and exhibited lower variance across 100 random seeds compared to vanilla MPPI and CMA-ES. For instance, on the Rastrigin function, Model-Guided MPPI converged in 3.0 ± 1.1 iterations, whereas vanilla MPPI required 6.9 ± 6.4 iterations and CMA-ES 5.5 ± 4.6 iterations, indicating higher stability and precision.
- **Continuous Control (Cart-Pole Swing-Up):** Evaluating the method on a nonlinear, underactuated cart-pole swing-up task with exact analytical gradients showed distinct performance characteristics across varying sample budgets `N` (from 2 to 1024).
 - In the high-sample regime (N = 1024), both Model-Guided MPPI and CMA-ES converged rapidly to a low error (< 10^-4), while vanilla MPPI required more iterations.
 - In the low-sample regime (N < 64), the baselines were sensitive: vanilla MPPI showed slower convergence and high variance, and CMA-ES became unstable. In contrast, Model-Guided MPPI exhibited "sample invariance," with convergence trajectories for N=2 to N=1024 collapsing onto a single curve (Figure 3a). This robustness was further quantified by maintaining negligible variance and consistent high-precision convergence even with as few as N=2 samples (Figure 3b).

**C. Hessian Approximations**
Investigation into the impact of Hessian approximation quality on the cart-pole task (using 8 samples) revealed that the fidelity of curvature information is critical.
- Methods capturing dense curvature information (Analytical Hessian, Gauss-Newton, BFGS) achieved high-precision convergence. The Gauss-Newton approximation, exploiting the least-squares structure of the cost, closely matched the performance of the exact analytical Hessian.
- The BFGS variant, despite using only iterative gradient updates, maintained a similar convergence trajectory to the exact methods, with a minor performance gap emerging in the higher-precision regime (< 10^-3).
- Adam-based model-guidance, utilizing a diagonal Hessian approximation, improved upon the vanilla MPPI baseline but proved insufficient for high-precision convergence (Figure 4), underscoring the importance of dense curvature information.

**D. Coarse and Fine Quadratic Model Approximations**
Experiments on nonconvex objectives, such as the Rastrigin function, demonstrated the complementary roles of coarse and fine quadratic models, controlled by the smoothing scale `σ` in Randomized Smoothing:
- A coarse model (large `σ = 1.5`) initially filtered out high-frequency perturbations, enabling the optimizer to escape local minima and locate the global basin of attraction.
- Subsequently, a fine model (small `σ = 0.1`) resolved accurate local curvature, leading to high-precision optimization in a few iterations.

**E. Randomized Smoothing on Non-Smooth Dynamics**
To evaluate robustness to non-smooth dynamics, the framework was applied to a challenging single-finger sphere manipulation task, where contact events introduce discontinuities. Randomized smoothing was used to extract quadratic model guidance for a black-box physics simulator.
- A performance profile across 200 diverse problem instances (using 64 trajectory samples and 128 auxiliary samples for RS per iteration) showed that Model-Guided MPPI consistently achieved low normalized optimality gaps, indicating reliable convergence over a large fraction of tasks.
- Vanilla MPPI exhibited higher variability in performance, and CMA-ES, while competitive in many instances, showed slightly reduced consistency across the full task distribution (Figure 5). These results confirm the framework's versatility in handling hybrid dynamics in contact-rich manipulation scenarios.

In summary, the main findings indicate that the proposed Model-Guided MPPI framework significantly improves sample efficiency, convergence speed, and robustness in low-sample regimes by incorporating local geometric information, making it a viable approach for a wide range of control and optimization problems, including those with non-smooth dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces a variance-reduced Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) framework that integrates a local objective function model into the sampling process, which has several significant implications and potential impacts:

**Improved Sample Efficiency and Robustness:** A primary impact is the significant improvement in sample efficiency and robustness of sampling-based controllers, particularly MPPI. By leveraging a model-guided prior, the method effectively concentrates samples in informative regions, leading to faster convergence and reduced variance, especially in low-sample regimes. This addresses a critical limitation of traditional MPPI, which often requires a large number of parallel rollouts, making it computationally expensive and sometimes impractical for real-time applications. The demonstrated "sample invariance" (performance largely independent of sample count down to N=2 for some tasks) highlights a substantial gain in efficiency.

**Enhanced Practicality for Real-time Control:** The ability to achieve high performance with fewer samples makes sampling-based control strategies more practical for real-time control scenarios where computational budgets are strict, or obtaining samples is expensive. This is particularly relevant in robotics and autonomous systems, where quick decision-making under uncertainty is crucial.

**Flexibility in Handling Diverse Problem Structures:** The framework's agnostic nature regarding the source of curvature information (exact derivatives, Gauss-Newton, Quasi-Newton, or gradient-free Randomized Smoothing) broadens its applicability. This flexibility allows it to be employed across a wide spectrum of problems:
- **Smooth, Differentiable Systems:** Utilizing analytical derivatives for optimal convergence near local optima.
- **Structured Problems (e.g., Least-Squares):** Employing Gauss-Newton approximations for efficient Hessian estimation.
- **First-Order Only Systems:** Integrating Quasi-Newton methods to build curvature estimates.
- **Non-smooth or Black-Box Systems:** Utilizing Randomized Smoothing to extract geometric information, as demonstrated in contact-rich manipulation tasks. This capability is particularly impactful for problems with hybrid dynamics where analytical gradients are ill-defined or simulators are not differentiable.

**Advancement in Stochastic Optimization:** The model-residual decomposition represents an advancement in integrating structural information into Monte Carlo estimators, drawing parallels with control variates techniques in broader stochastic optimization. The analytical derivation of a closed-form Gaussian guided prior based on a quadratic model provides a principled way to inject first- and second-order information into the sampling process, akin to a Newton-like update at the distribution level.

**Potential for Future Research and Applications:** The work opens several avenues for future research:
- **Adaptive Parameter Tuning:** The identified need for adaptive smoothing strategies (e.g., for `σ` and `λ`) suggests future work on meta-learning or online adaptation algorithms to remove manual heuristic tuning.
- **Integration with Learned Models:** Combining the model-guided MPPI with learned models and data-driven priors could further enhance sample efficiency in complex, high-dimensional domains.
- **Scaling to Complex Robotic Tasks:** The demonstrated robustness in contact-rich manipulation implies a promising path toward applying this variance-reduced MPPI to full-scale dexterous manipulation and other complex robotic tasks, where the cost of exploration is prohibitively high.
- **Leveraging Differentiable Physics Engines:** The discussion on computational overhead reduction with fast differentiable physics engines indicates a synergistic relationship, where such tools could further mitigate the cost of obtaining geometric information, making the proposed method even more efficient.

While the method introduces an additional computational cost for constructing the quadratic model, especially with Randomized Smoothing, its benefits in terms of sample efficiency and robustness, coupled with ongoing advancements in differentiable physics, suggest a net positive impact on the feasibility and performance of sampling-based control in challenging applications.
