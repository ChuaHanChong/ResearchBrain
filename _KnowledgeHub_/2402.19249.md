# Mirage: Cross-Embodiment Zero-Shot Policy Transfer with Cross-Painting

> [!summary] Summary
> Mirage, developed by UC Berkeley and Google DeepMind, introduces a method for zero-shot policy transfer between different robot embodiments by visually adapting observations through "cross-painting" and aligning action spaces with a blocking controller. The approach achieved 30-90% success rates on various real-world manipulation tasks when transferring policies from a Franka to an unseen UR5 robot, outperforming generalist policies in zero-shot settings.

## Problem

- Extensive data collection and re-training are typically required to deploy a robot policy on a new, different robot embodiment.
- Existing policy transfer methods often struggle with zero-shot generalization to robot hardware not encountered during training.
- Decoupling and independently addressing the visual and kinematic disparities between source and target robots remains a challenge for generalizable manipulation.

## Method

- A "cross-painting" technique, performed in real-time, generates a synthetic image for the source policy by masking the target robot, inpainting the background, and overlaying a rendered image of the source robot at the target's end-effector pose.
- A simple forward dynamics model combined with a high-gain blocking controller translates actions from the source policy (derived from the cross-painted image) into desired end-effector poses for the target robot.
- An initial state-based transfer study using coordinate frame transformations and a blocking controller established the feasibility of kinematic transfer between common robot arms, isolating control challenges from visual ones.

## Results

- Mirage demonstrated 30-90% success rates on real-world manipulation tasks (e.g., Open Drawer, Stack Cup) when transferring policies from a Franka to an unseen UR5 robot, significantly outperforming a state-of-the-art generalist policy (Octo) in zero-shot scenarios.
- State-based transfer experiments in simulation achieved 94-100% success rates for tasks when using similar 2-jaw grippers, highlighting that the primary challenge for zero-shot transfer is often the visual domain gap.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the cross-painting for visual inputs and the state alignment with a blocking controller for control inputs are critical components for effective zero-shot policy transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Kinematic differences between common robotic arms are largely manageable for zero-shot transfer when end-effector poses are aligned and a robust blocking controller is used to execute actions.
> - Bridging the visual gap between robots with the real-time cross-painting technique is crucial for the success of vision-based zero-shot policy transfer, allowing the source policy to operate as if on its familiar robot.
> - Explicitly addressing the visual and control domain gaps through model-based transformations can enable more robust zero-shot generalization to unseen embodiments compared to relying solely on large, pre-trained generalist policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The ability to transfer learned policies between different robot embodiments is a critical challenge in robotics. The conventional paradigm requires extensive data collection and re-training or fine-tuning for each new robot, which is time-consuming and expensive. This paper directly addresses the problem of **zero-shot policy transfer** – the ability to transfer a policy trained on a *source* robot to an *unseen target* robot without any target robot-specific data, demonstrations, or fine-tuning.

This work builds upon and contrasts with several existing approaches:
- **Transfer Learning and Fine-tuning:** Many methods use pre-training on a source robot or diverse datasets, followed by fine-tuning on the target robot (e.g.,). Mirage differentiates itself by eliminating the need for any target robot data for adaptation.
- **Multi-task and Multi-robot Training:** Approaches like kinematic domain randomization or robot-conditioned policies aim to train a single policy robust to a *distribution* of robots seen during training. Mirage, conversely, focuses on transferring a *specialized policy* from a single source robot to an *unseen* target robot.
- **Generalist Policies from Large Datasets:** Recent advancements in foundation models for robotics, such as RT-X and Octo, leverage massive datasets to create highly generalizable policies. While these models show impressive in-distribution performance and robustness, they often struggle with zero-shot transfer to *new* robot setups without explicit action space alignment or conditioning on robot kinematics. The paper directly compares Mirage with Octo, demonstrating Mirage's superior zero-shot performance in these specific cross-embodiment scenarios.
- **Domain Adaptation and Sim-to-Real:** Methods using visual domain randomization or image editing/inpainting for sim-to-real gaps are related. Mirage's "cross-painting" technique uses image manipulation for domain adaptation, specifically to bridge the visual gap *between different robots* in the real world, which is distinct from typical sim-to-real transfer.
- **Robot-Aware Visual Foresight:** This related work uses robot models to mask pixels and train video prediction models. Mirage extends this by not only masking but also *inpainting* the source robot, allowing it to use a broader class of pre-trained policies without modification and achieve manipulation tasks beyond pushing.
- **MimicGen:** This system generates target robot demonstration data by spatially transforming human demonstrations. Mirage, in contrast, applies a real-time visual transformation (cross-painting) to the *trained policy's input*, removing the need for new demonstrations on the target robot altogether.

Mirage occupies a unique niche by tackling the zero-shot aspect for visual manipulation skills, specifically for common robotic arms and 2-jaw grippers, without requiring prior exposure of the policy to the target robot's embodiment or its data. It focuses on addressing the fundamental kinematic and visual disparities between robots in a principled, model-based way (using URDFs and renderers), allowing policies trained for one robot to be directly reused.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable **zero-shot policy transfer across different robot embodiments** without requiring any data collection or fine-tuning on the target robot. This goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Alleviate Data Collection Burden:** Training robust robot policies, especially vision-based ones, requires substantial amounts of demonstration data. If a policy trained on one robot (e.g., a Franka arm) could be immediately deployed on another (e.g., a UR5) without new data, it would significantly reduce the time, cost, and effort associated with robot deployment and scaling.
2. **Address Limitations of Existing Transfer Methods:** Current methods, such as pretraining and fine-tuning or co-training across diverse datasets, often fail to generalize to robots *unseen* during the training phase. These methods typically require some form of data or adaptation in the target domain. The authors aim to overcome this "unseen embodiment" challenge.
3. **Exploit Commonalities in Robot Hardware:** The paper focuses on common robot arms with similar workspaces and parallel 2-jaw grippers, acknowledging that despite superficial differences, many industrial and research robots share fundamental functional similarities. This suggests that a direct transfer might be feasible if domain gaps are appropriately addressed.
4. **Decouple Vision and Control Challenges:** The authors identify that policy transfer faces challenges in both kinematic/control differences and visual disparities. A key motivation is to decouple and address these two gaps separately, simplifying the overall problem.
5. **Achieve Real-World Zero-Shot Manipulation:** While some zero-shot or highly generalizable policies exist, real-world demonstrations of zero-shot *visual manipulation skills* (beyond simpler tasks like pushing) across different physical robots and grippers are still scarce. The paper explicitly aims to fill this gap.

In summary, the authors seek to answer the question: "Is it possible to achieve policy transfer without any target robot data?" by developing a method that effectively creates an "illusion" for the source policy, making it believe it is operating its familiar robot, while the target robot executes the translated actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Mirage's methodology addresses the dual challenges of visual and control discrepancies between source and target robots through a two-pronged approach: an initial state-based transfer study to isolate control issues, followed by the full "cross-painting" strategy for vision-based policies.

#### 3.1. Initial State-Based Transfer Study

Before tackling vision, the authors conducted a systematic simulation study to understand the feasibility and challenges of state-based (proprioceptive) policy transfer.
- **Setup:** 8 manipulation tasks across 3 simulators (Robosuite, ORBIT, RLBench) were used. Policies (Imitation Learning for Robosuite, Reinforcement Learning for ORBIT/RLBench) were trained on a Franka robot (source).
- **Transfer Mechanism:** At test time, the target robot's end-effector and object poses ($p_T, p_o$) were transformed into the source robot's coordinate frame ($p_S = T_S^T p_T$) using known rigid transforms. The source policy $\pi_S(p_S)$ then predicted a source action. This action was conceptually executed on the source robot to get a *desired next pose* $p'_S$. Finally, the target robot used a high-gain or **blocking controller** to reach the equivalent target pose $p'_T = T_T^S p'_S$.
- **Goal:** To determine if kinematic differences alone (with aligned state spaces and a robust controller) prevent zero-shot transfer, and to what extent gripper morphology impacts performance. This step was crucial to isolate the control gap from the visual gap.

#### 3.2. Mirage: Cross-Embodiment Transfer for Vision-Based Policies

Motivated by the success of state-based transfer, Mirage extends this concept to vision-based policies by bridging the visual gap using **cross-painting** and refining the control gap strategy.

**Assumptions:**
1. Knowledge of robot URDFs and coordinate frames.
2. Knowledge of camera parameters for both domains.
3. Target robot has a high-gain/blocking controller.
4. Background and lighting conditions are similar or the policy is robust to background changes.
5. Tasks are quasi-static, within both robots' workspaces, and performable by both.

##### 3.2.1. Bridging the Visual Gap (Cross-Painting)

The core of Mirage lies in generating a synthetic image for the source policy that makes it "believe" the source robot is performing the task. This process occurs in real-time during execution:
1. **Optional Camera Reprojection:** If there's a significant camera angle change between source and target, the target image $o_T$ can be reprojected to the source camera's viewpoint, provided depth sensing is available. This helps align the visual perspective.
2. **Target Robot Masking:** Using the target robot's known URDF and joint angles, a renderer (Gazebo in this work) generates a segmentation mask for the target robot in the observed image $o_T$. These pixels are then masked out.
3. **Background Inpainting:** The masked-out region is filled using an image inpainting algorithm (Fast Marching was used for simplicity and speed). This creates a background image as if the target robot were not present.
4. **Source Robot Overlay:** The source robot's URDF is used to render the source robot at the *same end-effector pose* as the current target robot. This rendered image of the source robot is then overlaid onto the inpainted background. Gripper width is also matched.
5. **Output:** The resulting "cross-painted" image $o_{T \rightarrow S}$ is fed into the source policy $\pi_S$.

##### 3.2.2. Bridging the Control Gap

To account for differences in robot dynamics and control:
1. **Forward Dynamics Model:** A simple forward dynamics model $f(p^S_{r,t}, a^S_t) = p^S_{r,t+1}$ is fitted to the source robot's training trajectories. This model predicts the *next end-effector pose* of the source robot given its current pose and the action from the source policy.
2. **Blocking Controller Execution:** The action predicted by the source policy (using the cross-painted image) is used by the forward dynamics model to obtain a desired source end-effector pose $p^S_{r,t+1}$. This pose is then transformed into the target robot's frame $p^T_{r,t+1} = T^T_S p^S_{r,t+1}$. The target robot then executes this desired pose using a **high-gain or blocking controller**, which ensures it accurately reaches the specified position, effectively abstracting away the nuanced dynamics differences.

This decoupling allows Mirage to leverage existing, well-trained policies without modification, by essentially "tricking" them into thinking they are interacting with their familiar source robot, while the underlying control system ensures the target robot physically matches the desired state.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides strong evidence through extensive simulation and physical experiments that Mirage can successfully achieve zero-shot policy transfer across different robot embodiments.

#### 4.1. State-Based Transfer Experiments (Simulation)

- **High Success with Similar Grippers:** When target robots (UR5e, IIWA, Kinova Gen3, Sawyer) used the *same gripper* as the Franka source robot, most tasks achieved very high success rates (94-100%). This indicated that kinematic differences between common robot arms are largely manageable when the action space is aligned (Cartesian end-effector pose) and a robust blocking controller is used.
- **Mild Drop with Different 2-Jaw Grippers:** Using the target robots' *default 2-jaw grippers* resulted in a mild performance drop (10-25%) but still high success rates for many tasks. This suggests that small variations in gripper appearance and properties can be tolerated.
- **Significant Drop with 3-Jaw Gripper:** The Jaco robot with its 3-jaw gripper showed significantly lower success rates, especially on challenging tasks. This highlights that fundamental differences in end-effector morphology that require different grasping strategies are difficult to overcome with simple pose alignment.
- **Importance of Blocking Controller:** Experiments showed a significant drop in performance when using a non-blocking controller to execute delta actions, emphasizing that the blocking controller is crucial for mitigating robot dynamic differences.

#### 4.2. Vision-Based Policy Transfer Experiments (Simulation)

- **Effectiveness of Cross-Painting:** For both state-and-vision-based and vision-only policies, Mirage consistently showed a substantial performance improvement over the "Mirage – CP" baseline (no cross-painting), which directly fed the target robot's visual observation to the policy. This clearly demonstrated that cross-painting effectively bridges the visual gap.
- **Near-Oracle Performance:** Mirage's performance was generally within a 25% margin of the "Oracle" baseline (which had access to ground-truth renderings of the source robot), indicating that the cross-painting was successful in creating a visually convincing illusion for the source policy.
- **Robustness to Gripper Changes:** Mirage successfully transferred policies between robots with different 2-jaw grippers, validating its ability to handle visual changes in end-effector morphology.

#### 4.3. Physical Experiments (Real Robots)

- **Strong Zero-Shot Performance:** On 4 manipulation tasks (Tiger in Bowl, Open Drawer, Stack Cup, Toaster), Mirage achieved strong zero-shot performance when transferring policies from a Franka source to a Franka with a different gripper (Robotiq 2F-85) and to a UR5 with a Robotiq gripper.
- **Outperforming Baselines:** Mirage significantly outperformed direct zero-shot application of the source Diffusion Policy and a fine-tuned state-of-the-art generalist policy (Octo). Octo, despite pre-training on diverse datasets including Franka and UR5, struggled in the zero-shot setting without specific alignment, achieving 0% success on more difficult tasks for the UR5. Mirage, in contrast, achieved 30-90% success rates, depending on the task and transfer setting.
- **Crucial Role of State Alignment and Cross-Painting:** Ablation studies confirmed that both state alignment (for proprioceptive inputs) and cross-painting (for visual inputs) are crucial components for successful transfer, especially for policies taking both state and image inputs.
- **Flexibility with Camera Views:** Mirage was shown to work effectively with first-person (wrist) camera views, as well as combinations of wrist and third-person cameras, demonstrating its adaptability to different sensor configurations.

#### 4.4. Sensitivity Analysis

- **Visual Calibration Error:** Moderate camera calibration errors (e.g., ~30 pixels offset) leading to inaccurate masks and residual target robot parts in cross-painted images significantly degraded performance. Dilating masks was proposed as a mitigation.
- **Luminance:** Policies were relatively robust to changes in the luminance of the inpainted source robot.
- **Control Gain/Offset:** Inaccuracies in the target robot's controller (e.g., large control gain errors or proprioceptive offsets) drastically reduced performance, underscoring the importance of accurate control and state information.
- **Camera Reprojection:** For medium to large differences in camera poses (e.g., 5-10 cm translation, 15° rotation), the optional camera reprojection step in Mirage provided a 20-30% improvement in success rates, by minimizing visual distribution shifts due to viewpoint changes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Mirage makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of robotic manipulation:

1. **Pioneering Zero-Shot Visual Policy Transfer:** Mirage is presented as the first demonstration of zero-shot transfer of *visual manipulation skills* on real robots, moving beyond simpler tasks like pushing. This marks a significant step towards truly generalizable robotic systems that can operate on new hardware without costly re-training.
2. **Alleviating Data Scarcity:** By enabling zero-shot transfer, Mirage dramatically reduces the data collection and annotation burden for new robot deployments. A single policy trained on one robot can be immediately reused across a fleet of compatible, but unseen, robots, accelerating development and deployment cycles.
3. **Enhancing Generalization of Specialist Policies:** Instead of requiring large, robot-agnostic datasets and complex multi-robot training schemes, Mirage allows a specialized policy trained on a single robot to be effectively transferred. This highlights that careful domain alignment can be a powerful alternative or complement to massive-scale data collection for generalization.
4. **Principled Decoupling of Domain Gaps:** The explicit separation and targeted bridging of visual and control domain gaps (via cross-painting and blocking controllers) provide a clean and effective framework. This modularity makes the approach robust and potentially adaptable to other types of robots or sensors.
5. **Practical Applicability:** The method leverages readily available information (robot URDFs, camera parameters) and off-the-shelf components (renderers, inpainting algorithms, blocking controllers), making it practical for real-world implementation. The real-robot experiments with Franka and UR5 arms, common in research and industry, underscore its direct utility.
6. **Insights for Foundation Models:** The comparison with Octo, a state-of-the-art generalist policy, reveals that even highly capable foundation models can benefit from explicit action and observation space alignment for robust zero-shot generalization to unseen embodiments. This suggests avenues for future research in combining the strengths of large pre-trained models with the precise alignment offered by methods like Mirage.

While Mirage relies on known robot parameters and is most suitable for quasi-static tasks and environments with similar backgrounds, its core idea of "cross-painting" opens new pathways for robust, efficient, and flexible robot policy deployment, pushing the boundaries of what's achievable with zero-shot learning in robotics. Future work could integrate Mirage with advanced background augmentation techniques and extend it to more dynamic tasks or unknown robot morphologies, further amplifying its impact.
