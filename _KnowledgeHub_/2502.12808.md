# Exceeding the Maximum Speed Limit of the Joint Angle for the Redundant Tendon-driven Structures of Musculoskeletal Humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Tokyo developed two control methods to enable musculoskeletal humanoids to exceed their perceived maximum joint angle velocity in redundant tendon-driven structures. These methods increased actual robot joint angle velocity by up to 1.4 times and reduced peak muscle tension by over 37% compared to a baseline movement, facilitating more dynamic movements.

## Problem

- The maximum joint angle velocity in redundant tendon-driven musculoskeletal humanoids is limited by the slowest muscle among the actuators, particularly antagonist muscles with large moment arms.
- This velocity constraint prevents the robot from fully utilizing its dynamic potential and external forces like gravity during fast movements.
- Existing research primarily addressed issues like high internal muscle tension or slack, but not the specific limitation on overall joint velocity imposed by individual muscle speed limits.

## Method

- Method 1: Inhibits the current of identified velocity-limiting antagonist muscles, leveraging actuator backdrivability to allow them to elongate passively without imposing a velocity limit.
- Method 2: Actively elongates selected antagonist muscles in advance of a rapid movement to provide sufficient slack, preventing them from reaching their velocity limits during the fast motion.
- A critical index 'q' is used to identify muscles most prone to reaching their maximum length velocity limit, guiding the selection of muscles to manage.

## Results

- Method-1 increased the maximum joint angle velocity on an actual robot by approximately **42%** (from **2.6** rad/s to **3.7** rad/s) compared to the basic movement.
- Method-2 increased the maximum joint angle velocity on an actual robot by approximately **31%** (from **2.6** rad/s to **3.4** rad/s) compared to the basic movement.
- Both methods reduced peak muscle tension, with Method-1 showing a **37%** reduction (from 290 N to 180 N) and Method-2 achieving a **50%** reduction (to 140 N).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inherent redundancy and backdrivability of musculoskeletal actuators can be leveraged to overcome joint velocity limitations imposed by individual muscle speed limits.
> - Strategically managing antagonist muscles, either by inhibiting their current or pre-elongating them, significantly increases the achievable joint angular velocity.
> - These control strategies also lead to a substantial reduction in maximum muscle tension during high-speed movements, potentially increasing operational lifespan and energy efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Musculoskeletal humanoids are a field of robotics focused on developing robots that mimic the biological structures and mechanisms of the human body. This approach offers several biomimetic benefits, including anatomically inspired limb structures (e.g., radioulnar forearms, flexible spines, wide-range scapula structures). A key characteristic of these systems is the use of redundant muscle arrangements, where multiple tendons actuate a single joint or set of joints. This redundancy is known to provide advantages such as fail-safe actuation, allowing continuous operation even if some tendons are compromised, and enabling variable stiffness control through the coordinated use of multiple actuators and elastic elements.

However, the complexity of these redundant, tendon-driven systems also introduces challenges. Previous research has addressed issues such as high internal muscle tension or slack in antagonistic muscles, which can arise due to model inaccuracies. Solutions developed for these problems include antagonist inhibition control, dynamic modification of antagonistic relationships, and muscle relaxation control.

This current work identifies and addresses a distinct problem within this context: the limitation of maximum joint angle velocity. In redundant tendon-driven structures, the overall joint velocity can be restricted by the slowest individual muscle among the entire set, particularly by antagonist muscles with large moment arms. This limitation can prevent the robot from achieving its full dynamic potential, especially in movements that could otherwise benefit from external forces like gravity and body inertia.

Traditional approaches to increasing robot velocity often involve software-based optimization methods. For axis-driven robots with variable stiffness mechanisms, hardware-centric velocity maximization techniques have also been explored. This study distinguishes itself by focusing on simple methods that leverage the inherent characteristics of *redundant tendon-driven* musculoskeletal humanoids to overcome this velocity limitation. By addressing this specific aspect, the research aims to enhance the dynamic capabilities of musculoskeletal humanoids beyond previously perceived hardware limits, building upon the foundational benefits of biomimetic design while mitigating an identified constraint.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from an identified problem in musculoskeletal humanoids equipped with redundant tendon-driven structures: the maximum joint angle velocity is limited by the slowest muscle among the redundant actuators. Specifically, antagonist muscles, particularly those with large moment arms, are often the limiting factor. In movements that exploit gravity and body inertia (e.g., swinging an arm down), antagonist muscles tend to have larger moment arms than agonist muscles, necessitating faster muscle length velocities for the same joint angular velocity. If these antagonist muscles reach their maximum length velocity limit (`˙l_limit`), they constrain the overall joint movement, even if agonist muscles possess the capacity for faster actuation. This scenario prevents the robot from fully utilizing its available power and the assistance of external forces.

The key objective of this study is to propose and experimentally verify methods that enable musculoskeletal humanoids to *exceed* this inherent maximum joint angle velocity. The aim is to transform this limitation into a beneficial characteristic, allowing the robot to move faster than previously thought possible. This involves:

1. **Identifying the limiting factors:** Systematically characterizing how antagonist muscles, especially those with large moment arms, impose velocity constraints during fast, dynamic movements.
2. **Developing novel control strategies:** Designing two distinct, yet simple, methods that specifically manage these velocity-limiting antagonist muscles.
 - The first method seeks to inhibit the current of identified antagonist muscles, leveraging the backdrivability of the actuators to allow them to elongate passively without imposing a velocity limit.
 - The second method focuses on actively elongating selected antagonist muscles *in advance* of a rapid movement. This pre-elongation provides sufficient slack to prevent these muscles from reaching their velocity limits during the fast motion, thereby allowing other muscles and external forces to drive the joint faster.
3. **Experimental validation:** Conducting experiments on an actual musculoskeletal humanoid robot to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed methods in achieving higher joint angular velocities and to analyze their effects on muscle length velocities and tensions.
4. **Comparative analysis:** Comparing the performance, advantages, and disadvantages of the two proposed methods against a baseline movement to provide insights into their applicability and efficiency based on hardware characteristics like backdrivability.

Ultimately, the research is motivated by the desire to enhance the dynamic performance of musculoskeletal humanoids, enabling them to execute faster and more agile movements, which is crucial for interacting with dynamic environments and performing human-like tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study proposes two methods to exceed the maximum joint angle velocity in redundant tendon-driven musculoskeletal humanoids. The methodologies are generalized to apply to complex humanoids with non-constant moment arms and simple tendon-driven robots, primarily assuming electric motors but noting applicability to pneumatic systems.

**A. Problem Characterization**

The paper focuses on fast joint angle velocity movements, exemplified by actions like swinging an arm down. The relationship between joint angle (`θ`) and muscle length (`l`) is defined by `l = h(θ)`, with muscle Jacobian `G(θ) = ∂h/∂θ`. The moment arm `r` for a muscle is derived from `G(θ)˙θ/||˙θ||₂`. Muscles with positive `r` are antagonists, and those with negative `r` are agonists.

A critical index `q` is introduced, calculated as `q = r((θ_start + θ_end)/2, θ_end - θ_start) ⊘ ˙l_limit`, where `˙l_limit` is the maximum muscle length velocity of the actuator, and `⊘` denotes element-wise division. A larger `q` indicates a muscle is more prone to reaching its `˙l_limit`. The study highlights that in gravity-assisted movements, antagonist muscles often have larger moment arms than agonist muscles, making them the primary limitation to joint velocity. The core strategy is to manage these high-`q` antagonist muscles to prevent them from reaching `˙l_limit`.

**B. Proposed Methods**

1. **Method Inhibiting Antagonist Muscles (Method 1)**
 - **Principle:** This method aims to prevent antagonist muscles from restricting movement by inhibiting their motor current to zero.
 - **Implementation:** For muscles where `q[i] > C` (where `C` is a constant, set to 0 in experiments), the motor current `o[i]` is set to 0.
 - **Mechanism:** This method relies on the *backdrivability* of the muscle actuators. When an inhibited muscle is pulled by the joint movement, it spontaneously elongates without active resistance, effectively removing its velocity constraint. This control runs at a high frequency due to the dynamic nature of `q`.

2. **Method Elongating Antagonist Muscles in Advance (Method 2)**
 - **Principle:** This method manages muscle velocity by proactively elongating specific antagonist muscles before the fast movement begins, providing sufficient slack.
 - **Muscle Selection:** The selection of which muscles to elongate (`m`, a mask vector where `0` means elongate, `1` means don't) involves a two-step process:
 - **Achievability of `θ_start`:** A quadratic programming problem is solved to determine if the `θ_start` posture can still be maintained after elongating chosen muscles. This ensures that the necessary joint torque (`τ_nec`) can be generated by the remaining active muscles (`m ⊗ f`), utilizing the muscle redundancy.
 - **Maximizing Joint Velocity (Simulated `t_cost`):** A simulation is conducted to estimate the time cost (`t_cost`) to move from `θ_start` to `θ_end` for different `m` masks. This simulation minimizes `(θ_end - θ - Δθ)` subject to muscle length velocity constraints (`−m ⊗ ˙l_limit Δt ≤ m ⊗ (G(θ)Δθ) ≤ m ⊗ ˙l_limit Δt`). The search strategy involves iteratively setting `m` to 0 for antagonist muscles in decreasing order of `q` until `θ_start` is no longer achievable.
 - **Elongation Amount:** Once the optimal `m` is determined, `Δl_elongate` is calculated. This represents the minimum amount of muscle length the chosen muscles should be elongated in advance at `θ_start` to prevent them from limiting the movement velocity.

**C. Experimental Setup**

Experiments were conducted on the left arm of the musculoskeletal humanoid Musashi, which features five DOFs (shoulder pitch, roll, yaw; elbow pitch, yaw) actuated by ten muscles, including one polyarticular muscle. The actuators are 90W Maxon BLDC Motors with a 29:1 gear ratio.
- **Actuator Model Refinement:** The study acknowledges that the physical current control of the motor drivers cannot instantly achieve `˙l_limit`. Therefore, `˙l_max` and `˙l_min` in the simulation (Eq. 7) were modified to model a gradual increase/decrease in muscle length velocity, with a constant acceleration `α = 0.46 m/s²` and `˙l_limit = 0.30 m/s`. This refined model better represents the actual muscle module behavior.
- **Experimental Movement:** The movement chosen was swinging down the left arm.
- **Parameters:** `C = 0` (for Method 1), `Δt = 0.03` s (simulation time interval), `f_min = 10 N`, and `f_max = 200 N` (minimum/maximum muscle tension).
- **Measurements:** Joint angles were measured using equipped joint modules, while muscle length and tension were obtained from encoders and tension measurement units, respectively.

**D. Experimental Procedure**

- **Basic Experiment:** A baseline movement was executed without any proposed controls, both in simulation and on the actual robot. The goal was to reach `θ_end` from `θ_start` in 0 seconds (i.e., immediately command the target posture).
- **Method 1 Experiment:** The target movement was executed with antagonist muscles (`q[i] > C`) inhibited (current set to 0). Simulation assumed full backdrivability, while the actual robot experiment directly applied current inhibition.
- **Method 2 Experiment:** The mask `m` was calculated (only muscle #9 was selected for elongation). The movement was then performed in simulation and on the actual robot, with the chosen antagonist muscles pre-elongated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study compared the performance of three scenarios: a basic movement without specific control (Basic), a movement utilizing the method of inhibiting antagonist muscles (Method-1), and a movement employing the method of elongating antagonist muscles in advance (Method-2).

**A. Basic Experiment (Without Proposed Controls)**
- **Simulation:** The maximum joint angle velocity observed was 2.4 rad/s (at the elbow pitch, E-P), and the time cost (`t_cost`) to complete the movement was 0.99 seconds.
- **Actual Robot:** The maximum joint angle velocity reached 2.6 rad/s (E-P), showing agreement with the simulation. During this movement, the muscle length velocities of the biceps brachii (#9) and brachialis (#10) reached their `˙l_limit`. The maximum observed muscle tension was approximately 290 N, predominantly on the agonist muscles of the shoulder (#1) and elbow (#6). The polyarticular biceps brachii (#9), acting as an antagonist, maintained approximately 50 N of tension.

**B. Experiment with Method Inhibiting Antagonist Muscles (Method-1)**
- **Simulation:** By assuming backdrivability and setting the mask `m` to zero for muscles #2, #3, #9, and #10 (antagonists with `q[i] > C`), the maximum joint angle velocity significantly increased to 4.4 rad/s (E-P), with a `t_cost` of 0.6 seconds.
- **Actual Robot:** The maximum joint angle velocity reached 3.7 rad/s (E-P). While lower than the simulation, this represented a substantial increase compared to the basic movement. Notably, the muscle length velocities of the biceps brachii (#9) and brachialis (#10) were observed to *exceed* `˙l_limit`, indicating that the actuators partially exhibited backdrivability. The maximum muscle tension was reduced to about 180 N, primarily on the agonist muscles (#1 and #6). Antagonist muscles showed minimal tension.

**C. Experiment with Method Elongating Antagonist Muscles (Method-2)**
- **Muscle Selection:** The calculation for `m` indicated that elongating both #9 and #10 would compromise the ability to maintain `θ_start`. Therefore, only muscle #9 (biceps brachii) was selected for pre-elongation.
- **Simulation:** The maximum joint angle velocity was 3.3 rad/s (E-P), with a `t_cost` of 0.78 seconds.
- **Actual Robot:** The maximum joint angle velocity was 3.4 rad/s (E-P), closely matching the simulation result. In this scenario, the muscle length velocities of the deltoid (front) (#3) and brachialis (#10) achieved `˙l_limit`. The maximum muscle tension was approximately 140 N, primarily on the agonist muscles of the elbow (#6 and #8). The brachialis (#10), acting as an antagonist, maintained about 60 N of tension.

**D. Comparative Summary (Table I)**
The comparison table summarizes the key results:
- **Maximum Joint Angle Velocity (˙θ):**
 - Simulation: Basic (2.4 rad/s) < Method-2 (3.3 rad/s) < Method-1 (4.4 rad/s).
 - Actual Robot: Basic (2.6 rad/s) < Method-2 (3.4 rad/s) < Method-1 (3.7 rad/s).
 - Both proposed methods demonstrated an increase in maximum joint angle velocity compared to the basic movement, with Method-1 showing the highest potential, particularly in simulation.
- **Maximum Muscle Length Velocity (˙l):**
 - Basic and Method-2 showed `˙l ≤ ˙l_limit`.
 - Method-1 resulted in `˙l > ˙l_limit` in the actual robot, confirming its reliance on backdrivability.
- **Maximum Muscle Tension (T):** Both proposed methods reduced maximum muscle tension compared to the basic movement.
 - Basic: 290 N
 - Method-1: 180 N
 - Method-2: 140 N

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a specific limitation in the dynamic performance of musculoskeletal humanoids: the constraint on maximum joint angle velocity imposed by the slowest muscle in a redundant tendon-driven system. By proposing and experimentally verifying two distinct methods, the study contributes to enhancing the capabilities of these biomimetic robots.

The primary significance lies in the demonstrated ability to *exceed* the conventionally defined maximum joint angle velocity. The experiments showed that both the "Method Inhibiting Antagonist Muscles" (Method-1) and the "Method Elongating Antagonist Muscles in Advance" (Method-2) successfully increased the peak joint angular velocity compared to a baseline movement. Method-1 achieved a velocity up to approximately 1.4 times higher (3.7 rad/s vs. 2.6 rad/s) in actual robot experiments, while Method-2 resulted in approximately 1.3 times higher velocity (3.4 rad/s vs. 2.6 rad/s). This indicates that the identified problem can be mitigated, allowing musculoskeletal humanoids to achieve more dynamic and potentially human-like movements.

Furthermore, both proposed methods also led to a significant reduction in maximum muscle tension during the rapid movements. Method-1 reduced tension by over 37% (from 290 N to 180 N), and Method-2 by over 50% (to 140 N). Lower muscle tension can contribute to reduced wear and tear on actuators and tendons, potentially increasing the robot's operational lifespan and energy efficiency during dynamic tasks.

The study provides a comparative analysis of the two methods, highlighting their respective dependencies and performance characteristics. Method-1, while offering higher theoretical velocity gains, relies on the backdrivability of the actuators. Its performance in real-world scenarios is directly influenced by how effectively the motors can passively disengage. Method-2, in contrast, demonstrates robustness by not relying on backdrivability, making it potentially more applicable across a wider range of hardware specifications. However, Method-2 requires pre-tensioning to achieve the starting posture, which may temporarily increase muscle tension during the setup phase.

The findings have potential implications for the design and control of future musculoskeletal humanoids. Enabling faster joint movements is crucial for applications requiring high agility, such as sports robotics, dexterous manipulation, or human-robot interaction in dynamic environments. The methodologies provide concrete strategies for leveraging the inherent redundancy of these complex systems to improve performance. The research also identifies areas for future investigation, such as improving the accuracy of modeling factors like friction, hysteresis, and dynamics for more detailed performance analysis, and developing methods for precise trajectory realization at high velocities. This work lays a foundation for developing more capable and agile musculoskeletal robots that can better exploit their biomimetic advantages.
