# Exceeding the Maximum Speed Limit of the Joint Angle for the Redundant Tendon-driven Structures of Musculoskeletal Humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The University of Tokyo developed two control methods to enable musculoskeletal humanoids to exceed their perceived maximum joint angle velocity in redundant tendon-driven structures. These methods increased actual robot joint angle velocity by up to 1.4 times and reduced peak muscle tension by over 37% compared to a baseline movement, facilitating more dynamic movements.

## Problem

- The maximum joint angle velocity in redundant tendon-driven musculoskeletal humanoids is limited by the slowest muscle among the actuators, particularly antagonist muscles with large moment arms.
- This velocity constraint prevents the robot from fully utilizing its dynamic potential and external forces like gravity during fast movements.
- Existing research primarily addressed issues like high internal muscle tension or slack, but not the specific limitation on overall joint velocity imposed by individual muscle speed limits.

## Method

- Method 1: Inhibits the current of identified velocity-limiting antagonist muscles, leveraging actuator backdrivability to allow them to elongate passively without imposing a velocity limit.
- Method 2: Actively elongates selected antagonist muscles in advance of a rapid movement to provide sufficient slack, preventing them from reaching their velocity limits during the fast motion.
- A critical index 'q' is used to identify muscles most prone to reaching their maximum length velocity limit, guiding the selection of muscles to manage.

## Results

- Method-1 increased the maximum joint angle velocity on an actual robot by approximately **42%** (from **2.6** rad/s to **3.7** rad/s) compared to the basic movement.
- Method-2 increased the maximum joint angle velocity on an actual robot by approximately **31%** (from **2.6** rad/s to **3.4** rad/s) compared to the basic movement.
- Both methods reduced peak muscle tension, with Method-1 showing a **37%** reduction (from 290 N to 180 N) and Method-2 achieving a **50%** reduction (to 140 N).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The inherent redundancy and backdrivability of musculoskeletal actuators can be leveraged to overcome joint velocity limitations imposed by individual muscle speed limits.
> - Strategically managing antagonist muscles, either by inhibiting their current or pre-elongating them, significantly increases the achievable joint angular velocity.
> - These control strategies also lead to a substantial reduction in maximum muscle tension during high-speed movements, potentially increasing operational lifespan and energy efficiency.
