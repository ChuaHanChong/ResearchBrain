# SpringGrasp: Synthesizing Compliant, Dexterous Grasps under Shape Uncertainty

> [!summary] Summary
> SpringGrasp plans compliant dexterous grasps under object-shape uncertainty by modeling grasping as a spring-damper process from pregrasp to equilibrium, using a differentiable SpringGrasp metric plus GPIS surface uncertainty to jointly optimize pregrasp pose, target contacts, and per-finger impedance gains, reaching 89% real-robot success from two views.

## Problem

- Prior optimization-based grasp planners assume precisely known object geometry, but real sensors give noisy, partial point clouds with shape uncertainty.
- Force-closure and min-weight metrics require slow bi-level optimization and fixed manually-tuned controller gains that do not adapt to uncertainty.
- Existing shape-uncertainty methods either need expensive tactile re-planning or only pick fixed grasp candidates without jointly optimizing contact, gains, and stability.

## Method

- **SpringGrasp** metric: analytical, differentiable energy modeling each fingertip as a virtual ==spring-damper== reaching an equilibrium object pose via a closed-form ==Wahba/Kabsch solve==, avoiding forward simulation.
- Uncertainty-aware pregrasp formulation: augments decision variables with a joint-space pregrasp pose and an ==E_uncer term== built from ==GPIS== probability density to place fingertips just outside the true surface.
- Joint ==gradient-based optimization== over pregrasp pose, target contact locations, and per-finger ==impedance gains==, combining spring energy, distance, collision, regularization, and force terms into one differentiable objective.

## Results

- Real-robot Allegro-hand experiments on 14 objects: **89%** success with two-view point clouds and **84%** with a single view, both at least **18%** above the strongest force-closure bilevel baseline.
- Bilevel baselines with high, low, or heuristic fixed gains reached only **62%**, **65%**, and **71%** respectively, showing no single gain setting works across object shapes.
- Ablations show removing the uncertainty term drops success from **84%** to **75%**, and removing the optimized pregrasp (c=1.0) drops it from **84%** to **78%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating grasp acquisition as a dynamic compliant process rather than a static contact-point problem lets gradient optimization sidestep costly forward simulation while still capturing stability.
> - Jointly optimizing controller gains with contact geometry matters more than the geometry alone; fixed gains fail differently depending on object shape and size.
> - Allowing deliberate object movement during grasping can rescue grasps that are otherwise infeasible under fixed kinematic and torque limits, an underused degree of freedom in grasp planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Generating stable and robust grasps for dexterous robotic hands is a fundamental challenge in robotics, crucial for enabling robots to interact with diverse, irregularly shaped objects in unstructured environments. The existing body of work on dexterous grasp planning primarily falls into two categories: data-driven methods and optimization-based methods.

Data-driven approaches, such as those relying on deep learning, have shown promise in handling partial and noisy observations. However, many of these methods often focus on selecting from a limited set of fixed grasp candidates and may not inherently optimize for object and grasp stability.

Optimization-based methods, on the other hand, typically employ differentiable grasp criteria, such as force closure or min-weight metrics, to synthesize precision grasps. These approaches often require a precise and complete geometric model of the object, which is usually represented as an explicit mesh or an implicit Signed Distance Function (SDF). A significant limitation of these methods arises in real-world scenarios where sensor noise and partial observability lead to ubiquitous shape uncertainty. Obtaining detailed and accurate object models under such conditions is challenging. While some works attempt to approximate force closure or use bi-level optimization, these can be computationally intensive or lack strong guarantees.

The concept of compliance is recognized as vital for robustly grasping objects with varying and uncertain geometries. Research has explored both passive compliance (built into mechanical design) and active compliance (simulated through control). Active compliance, achieved via impedance control, offers advantages in maintaining degrees of freedom and kinematic modeling for precision grasps. Some prior work has adapted joint-level compliance based on surface variance or focused on compliant control *after* a grasp is established.

Modeling objects with shape uncertainty has evolved from probabilistic occupancy maps and sample-based representations to more memory-efficient probabilistic implicit functions, such as Gaussian Process Implicit Surfaces (GPIS) or Probabilistic Signed Distance Functions (pSDF). These models can quantify uncertainty at various points on the object surface. Building upon these models, some grasp planning approaches have attempted to plan grasps based on completed object shapes or explicitly by penalizing contact in high-variance regions. However, achieving precise contact at planned locations under uncertainty remains difficult. Some methods resort to refining contacts or replanning using tactile feedback, which requires additional sensor modalities. The use of pre-grasp hand poses has also been explored to prevent accidental contact, but often without explicit planning or optimization for uncertainty.

This work, SpringGrasp, situates itself at the intersection of these challenges by proposing an optimization-based framework for synthesizing compliant dexterous grasps specifically under shape uncertainty. It addresses the gap where existing optimization methods struggle with imprecise object geometry and where data-driven methods may not fully optimize for stability. By introducing a novel differentiable metric that models the *dynamic process* of grasping with optimized impedance controls and integrating uncertainty directly into the planning pipeline, SpringGrasp aims to provide a more robust and adaptable solution for real-world dexterous manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to advance dexterous robotic manipulation by enabling robots to generate stable and robust grasps on arbitrary objects despite ubiquitous shape uncertainty in real-world observations. The authors articulate several specific objectives and motivations for their proposed SpringGrasp planner:

**Primary Objectives:**

1. **Develop a planner that explicitly considers shape uncertainty:** The core motivation is to overcome the limitations of previous optimization-based methods that assume precisely known object geometry. Real-world perception, often relying on noisy sensors and limited viewpoints, invariably produces uncertain and partial shape observations. The planner aims to leverage this uncertain object information to make informed decisions.
2. **Synthesize compliant dexterous grasps:** The work seeks to generate grasps that inherently minimize the impact of unexpected contact with the object, which is a common occurrence when object shape is uncertain. This involves optimizing not only the pre-grasp hand pose but also per-finger impedance controls, allowing the robot to adapt its interaction forces dynamically.
3. **Introduce an analytical and differentiable metric for dynamic grasping:** A critical objective is to create a computationally efficient and gradient-friendly metric, termed "SpringGrasp metric," that can evaluate the entire compliant grasping process. This metric is designed to determine if the dynamic interaction, starting from a pre-grasp pose and engaging fingers with impedance control, will lead to a stable, force-closure grasp at equilibrium. This distinguishes it from methods that only evaluate static force closure.
4. **Enable optimization of impedance parameters and pre-grasp poses:** Unlike baselines where controller gains are manually tuned or fixed, this work aims to optimize these parameters directly. This allows the system to adapt its compliance based on the specific object's geometry and perceived uncertainty, leading to more robust grasps. Furthermore, the pre-grasp pose is optimized to facilitate compliant contact acquisition.

**Motivation:**

- **Addressing the "Uncertainty Gap":** The authors argue that every component of the grasping pipeline – from perception to planning and control – must account for shape uncertainty. Existing methods often fail to integrate uncertainty explicitly across these stages. SpringGrasp addresses this by using probabilistic object models (GPIS) and incorporating uncertainty metrics directly into the optimization.
- **Enhancing Grasp Stability and Robustness:** By synthesizing compliant grasps, the system aims to tolerate deviations between the perceived and actual object shape, reducing the likelihood of grasp failures due to unexpected contacts or object perturbations. The dynamic modeling of the grasping process, including object movement, is motivated by the need to ensure a stable equilibrium even if the object shifts during contact.
- **Improving Efficiency of Optimization:** The introduction of an analytical and differentiable metric, as opposed to computationally expensive forward simulations or bi-level optimizations common in force closure methods, is motivated by the desire for a more efficient planning process suitable for real-time applications.
- **Expanding Manipulation Capabilities:** Achieving robust dexterous grasping under uncertainty is a significant step toward advanced manipulation capabilities for robots in complex, real-world environments, such as service robotics or industrial tasks involving novel or poorly modeled objects.

In essence, the research is motivated by the practical need for robots to reliably grasp objects whose exact geometry is unknown or imprecise, and it proposes a comprehensive, optimization-based solution that integrates compliance, dynamic modeling, and uncertainty consideration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SpringGrasp planner proposes an optimization-based approach for synthesizing compliant dexterous grasps under shape uncertainty, taking partial point cloud observations as input. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

**A. Compliant Grasp Formulation**
A compliant grasp is defined by a set of initial fingertip contact locations on the object surface, `p_i(t0)`, a set of target locations for each fingertip, `o_i`, and a set of per-finger impedance controller gains, `k_i`. The grasping process is modeled as a dynamic interaction where each fingertip, starting from `p_i(t0)`, moves towards its target `o_i` under Cartesian-space impedance control with gain `k_i` and a damping coefficient of `2*sqrt(k_i)`. The object's pose is expected to change during this process until a stable equilibrium (`t_eq`) is reached. A crucial assumption is that fingertips move with the object without slippage, meaning contact points in the object frame remain fixed.

To avoid computationally expensive numerical forward simulation, the authors leverage an analytical solution for the equilibrium state. The fingertip positions at equilibrium, `p_i(t_eq)`, are expressed as a function of the object's rigid transformation (`R_eq`, `t_eq`). This transformation is determined by solving a modified Wahba's problem (using a modified Kabsch algorithm) that minimizes the total kinetic energy stored in the virtual springs connecting fingertips to their targets. This analytical approach makes the equilibrium state differentiable with respect to the grasp parameters.

**B. SpringGrasp Metric**
With `p_i(t_eq)` analytically determined, a metric is defined to assess if the fingertips at equilibrium can form a force-closure grasp. This "SpringGrasp metric" evaluates the contact margin `ϵ_i(t)`, which is the angular difference between the virtual spring force `f_i(t)` (modeled as `k_i(o_i - p_i(t)) - 2*sqrt(k_i)*p_dot_i(t)`) and the surface normal `n_i(t)` at the contact point. A non-negative margin (`ϵ_i(t) ≥ 0`) indicates force closure. The authors hypothesize that if object rotation during the compliant grasping process is small, non-negative contact margins at both the initial (`t0`) and equilibrium (`t_eq`) states are sufficient to guarantee feasibility throughout the process.

The SpringGrasp metric energy (`E_sp`) is formulated as the sum of negative logarithms of (`ϵ_i(t)+1`) for both `t0` and `t_eq`. This logarithmic formulation encourages the optimizer to prioritize improving fingertips with negative margins.

**C. Optimizing Compliant Grasp with Known Object Surface**
Initially, for objects with precisely known surfaces (represented by a Signed Distance Function, SDF `d(x)`), the optimization aims to minimize a combination of `E_sp` and `E_dist`, where `E_dist` is the sum of absolute SDF values at initial fingertip contact points, penalizing distances from the surface. The decision variables are `p_i(t0)`, `o_i`, and `k_i`.

**D. Pregrasp with Uncertain Object Surface**
To address shape uncertainty, the method employs Gaussian Process Implicit Surfaces (GPIS) to model the object. GPIS provides both a mean `d_μ(x)` and variance `d_σ(x)` for the signed distance at any query point `x`, allowing the calculation of a probability density `p(d(x)=0|x)` of `x` being on the object surface.

The grasping process is extended backward in time to include a "pregrasp" (`q_init`), which is a full hand pose (wrist 6D pose and joint angles) where fingertips are initially outside the object. The initial contact locations `p_i(t0)` are then linearly extrapolated from `q_init` and `o_i` using a coefficient `c`.

An "uncertainty objective" (`E_uncer`) is introduced. It encourages initial contact locations (`p_i(t0)`) to have a high probability of being on the object surface and to be local maxima of this probability along a line segment from the pregrasp fingertip position to `o_i`. This term is approximated by summing probability densities at `K` sample points along these segments.

**E. Full Optimization with Uncertain Object Surface**
The final optimization problem includes `q_init`, `{o_i}`, and `{k_i}` as decision variables (with `p_i(t0)` derived from `q_init` and `o_i`). The full objective function (Equation 18) is a weighted sum of several energy terms:
- `E_sp`: SpringGrasp metric energy.
- `E_dist`: Fingertip-to-surface distance.
- `E_uncer`: Uncertainty consideration for contact locations.
- `E_gain`: Penalizes high controller gains (`sum(k_i^2)`) to encourage compliance.
- `E_tar`: Encourages target locations `o_i` to be inside the object (`sum(d(o_i))`).
- `E_col`: Collision avoidance penalties for self-collision, hand-object, and hand-table collisions, approximated using spheres.
- `E_reg`: Regularization terms to keep `q_init` close to a neutral reference pose and to minimize object movement between `t0` and `t_eq`.
- `E_force`: Encourages a minimal force exerted by each fingertip at equilibrium.

This composite objective function is fully differentiable, enabling optimization using gradient-based solvers like RMSProp. The weights for each energy term are tuned and provided in Table I.

**F. Implementation Details and Experiment Setup**
The system uses a Kuka iiwa arm with an Allegro hand and Realsense RGB-D cameras. Object shapes are reconstructed using GPIS from point clouds. Motion planning for the arm is handled by `curobo`. The planner initializes with multiple wrist poses and neutral finger joint angles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the SpringGrasp planner through real-robot experiments on 14 common objects, measuring grasp success rates and conducting ablation studies.

**A. Evaluation Metric**
Grasp success was determined by two criteria: whether the object was lifted 5cm and whether there was any slippage between fingertips and the object. Success rates were calculated as (`#successful + 0.5 * #partial`) / 5 trials per object, and then averaged across all objects.

**B. Comparison with Baseline (Bilevel Optimization)**
The SpringGrasp method was compared against a baseline that uses bilevel optimization with a differentiable force closure criterion, similar to prior work. This baseline did not optimize controller gains but used manually tuned (Bilevel (high), Bilevel (low)) or heuristically chosen (Bilevel (heuristic)) gains. Both SpringGrasp and the baseline utilized point clouds from two camera viewpoints for object shape estimation.
- **Results**: SpringGrasp achieved an overall grasp success rate of 89%. In contrast, Bilevel (low) achieved 65%, Bilevel (high) achieved 62%, and Bilevel (heuristic) achieved 71%. This demonstrates that SpringGrasp achieved 18-27% higher success rates compared to the baselines.
- **Observations on Baselines**: The results indicated that no single set of fixed gains performed universally well. Low gains could lead to unstable equilibria, causing objects to spin out of grasp, while high gains could result in target locations being far from the actual object surface, leading to missed contacts. SpringGrasp's ability to optimize controller gains for each specific object and uncertainty level was identified as a key factor in its superior performance.

**C. Robustness to Varying Levels of Shape Uncertainty**
The method's robustness to varying levels of shape uncertainty was assessed by using point clouds from three, two, or one camera viewpoint.
- **Results**: Grasp success rates were 93% for three viewpoints, 89% for two viewpoints, and 84% for a single viewpoint.
- **Observations**: The performance degraded gradually, indicating that SpringGrasp maintains a high success rate even under significant shape uncertainty (single-view input). For larger objects, GPIS could estimate the true shape sufficiently well even from a single viewpoint, preserving necessary geometry for successful grasping.

**D. Ablation Study**
Two ablation studies were performed to evaluate the contribution of specific components of the SpringGrasp framework, using a single-viewpoint point cloud for input.
- **Without Uncertainty Term (`E_uncer`)**: Removing the `E_uncer` term from the objective function caused the grasp success rate to drop from 84% to 75%. Failure modes often involved making contact with regions of high uncertainty on the GPIS surface, which were distant from the actual object surface, leading to missing or unexpected contacts.
- **Without Pregrasp Optimization (setting `c=1.0`)**: If the pregrasp coefficient `c` was set to 1.0 (meaning fingertips are initially in contact with the estimated object surface rather than slightly offset), the success rate decreased from 84% to 78%. This indicated that optimizing for a pregrasp that places fingertips slightly away from the estimated surface helps prevent premature and unexpected contacts, especially for objects in unstable initial poses.

**E. Hyperparameter Analysis**
A sensitivity analysis on the weights of different energy terms in the optimization objective (Eq. 18) showed that moderately scaling weights (by factors of 0.5 or 2) generally did not cause significant changes in the overall grasp success rate, though some parameters, such as `w_tar` and `w_reg`, exhibited higher sensitivity.

**F. Computation Time**
SpringGrasp demonstrated faster optimization times compared to the bilevel optimization baseline. For optimizing 7 initial guesses, SpringGrasp took 15.58 seconds on CPU, while the baseline took 34.97 seconds. The forward kinematics computation was identified as a bottleneck for scaling up the number of seeds.

**G. Benefit of Modeling Object Movement during Grasp Acquisition**
A 2D simulated experiment showed that allowing object movement during grasping, a property of the SpringGrasp metric, could help overcome kinematic and joint torque limits that would otherwise prevent a stable force-closure grasp. Approximately 15% of feasible grasps in the simulation required object movement to achieve stability, indicating that this feature expands the range of achievable grasps. The method also enables deliberate control of object movement (e.g., lifting by a specific amount) by modifying the regularization term.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SpringGrasp research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a method for generating compliant, dexterous grasps that explicitly addresses the challenge of shape uncertainty in real-world environments.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

1. **Robustness to Real-World Uncertainty:** By integrating Gaussian Process Implicit Surfaces (GPIS) for object modeling and a dedicated uncertainty objective term, the system demonstrates notable robustness to noisy and partial observations from single or multiple viewpoints. This is a significant step towards practical applications where precise CAD models are unavailable.
2. **Novel Differentiable Grasp Metric:** The SpringGrasp metric offers an analytical and differentiable approach to evaluate the *dynamic process* of compliant grasping, from pre-grasp to equilibrium. This allows for efficient, gradient-based optimization, avoiding the computational overhead and numerical instability of forward simulations or complex bi-level optimization strategies common in traditional force closure methods.
3. **Adaptive Compliance through Optimized Gains:** The ability to optimize per-finger impedance controller gains (`k_i`) is a crucial advancement. This allows the robot to dynamically adjust its interaction stiffness based on the specific object geometry and perceived uncertainty, mitigating common failure modes observed with fixed-gain approaches (e.g., unstable equilibria with low gains, missed contacts with high gains).
4. **Integrated Pre-Grasp Planning:** The optimization of a pre-grasp pose that places fingertips slightly offset from the estimated object surface (`c < 1.0`) helps prevent premature and unexpected contacts. This enhances stability, especially for objects in unstable initial configurations, where unintended perturbations can lead to grasp failure.
5. **Handling Kinematic and Torque Limits:** The explicit modeling of object movement during grasp acquisition allows the system to find feasible grasps that might otherwise be unreachable due to the robot's kinematic or joint torque limits. By allowing the object to shift into a more favorable pose, the method expands the space of achievable stable grasps.
6. **Potential for Broader Applications:** The real-robot validation on a diverse set of objects demonstrates the practical utility of SpringGrasp. This robust grasp planning capability could be impactful in various domains, including service robotics (handling household items), logistics (manipulating diverse packages), and manufacturing (adaptive assembly tasks).
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework provides a strong foundation for future work, such as integrating gravity and object mass uncertainty, addressing challenges with thin-walled object geometries (e.g., through part decomposition), or using the generated data to train learning-based models for even faster, direct compliant grasp prediction.

In summary, SpringGrasp addresses a critical gap in dexterous manipulation by providing a holistic, differentiable, and robust solution for grasping objects under shape uncertainty, marking progress towards more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems.
