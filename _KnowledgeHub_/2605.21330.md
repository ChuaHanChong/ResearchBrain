# Learning Robust Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation from Joint Sensors with Proprioceptive Transformer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zürich developed a Proprioceptive Transformer that enables tendon-driven robotic hands to achieve dexterous in-hand manipulation using only joint sensor feedback. This approach demonstrated a 3.1x improvement in cube rotation speed over proprioceptive PPO baselines and maintained 100% rotation accuracy on a real ORCA hand.

## Problem

- External vision systems for dexterous manipulation suffer from occlusions, high computational demands, and large sim-to-real gaps, limiting their practical deployment.
- Tendon-driven robotic hands present challenges due to transmission nonlinearities like cable stretch and backlash, which complicate precise control with motor-side sensing.
- Extracting sufficient exteroceptive information (e.g., object state) solely from internal robot (proprioceptive) sensors for complex in-hand manipulation tasks remains a challenge.

## Method

- A teacher-student learning framework is employed, where a teacher policy trained with privileged information in simulation distills its knowledge into a student policy.
- The student policy, implemented as a Proprioceptive Transformer (PT), learns from only noisy joint position and velocity histories, incorporating auxiliary reconstruction objectives for object pose and clean joint states.
- The trained PT policy is deployed on a real tendon-driven ORCA hand using direct joint angle sensors, enabling zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for continuous cube reorientation.

## Results

- The Proprioceptive Transformer with direct joint sensing (PT-Joint) achieved 11.83 Rotations Per Minute (RPM) for a medium cube on the real ORCA hand, a 3.1x improvement over a proprioceptive PPO baseline.
- PT-Joint maintained 100% Rotation Accuracy and Drop-Free Success Rate across both medium and large cubes, demonstrating robust and stable performance.
- The Transformer architecture significantly reduced object position reconstruction RMSE to 13.70 mm from proprioceptive history alone, a 23.4% reduction compared to an MLP baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct joint sensing provides superior proprioceptive feedback compared to motor encoder readings for tendon-driven hands, effectively mitigating issues from transmission nonlinearities.
> - Temporal sequences of joint measurements implicitly encode sufficient information about object presence, size, and state, allowing dexterous manipulation without explicit external vision or tactile sensing.
> - Transformer architectures effectively leverage long-range temporal dependencies in proprioceptive data to implicitly estimate object states and denoise sensor readings, outperforming MLPs and LSTMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous in-hand manipulation, which involves reorienting and repositioning an object within a robotic hand, is a fundamental and complex capability in robotics. Historically, approaches to this challenge relied on analytical models and trajectory optimization, requiring precise knowledge of object geometry and contact mechanics. These model-based methods faced limitations due to the inherent uncertainties of contact dynamics and the extensive manual engineering required for task adaptation.

The advent of deep reinforcement learning (RL) significantly advanced the field. Landmark work demonstrated that RL agents could learn to reorient objects, such as a cube, to arbitrary target orientations. These successes often leveraged large-scale parallel simulation environments, domain randomization for transferring policies from simulation to reality (sim-to-real transfer), and sophisticated vision-based perception systems for real-time object pose tracking. Examples include the OpenAI work on cube reorientation and subsequent extensions to tasks like solving the Rubik's cube or achieving more agile manipulation.

However, a reliance on external vision systems introduces several practical constraints. These include the need for specific camera placements, susceptibility to occlusions during manipulation, high computational demands for real-time pose estimation, and a substantial sim-to-real gap in visual perception due to the difficulty of rendering realistic images in simulation. These factors have motivated the exploration of alternative sensing modalities.

Proprioceptive sensing, which refers to the use of internal sensors to perceive the robot's own state, offers advantages such as inherent availability, immunity to visual occlusions, computational efficiency, and a minimal sim-to-real gap. While proprioceptive feedback, sometimes combined with tactile sensing, has been explored for complex manipulation behaviors, its potential for dexterous in-hand manipulation, particularly with tendon-driven hands, remains underexplored. A critical distinction exists between using motor encoder readings (motor-side measurements) and direct joint sensor measurements. Motor-side readings can be affected by transmission effects like gear backlash, cable stretch, and joint compliance, leading to discrepancies between commanded and actual joint positions, especially in compliant or tendon-driven mechanisms. Direct joint sensing, which measures positions and velocities at the joint output, offers more accurate kinematic state information.

Teacher-student learning, where a teacher policy trained with privileged information in simulation distills its knowledge into a student policy using only real-world observations, has emerged as a strategy for robust robot control and sim-to-real transfer. Transformer architectures, known for their ability to model long-range temporal dependencies through self-attention, have shown promise in sequence modeling for RL, enabling policies to infer hidden states from historical observations. This research aims to integrate these concepts to develop a proprioception-only approach for dexterous manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work is to explore the extent to which dexterous in-hand object manipulation can be achieved using only joint sensing, without reliance on external vision or tactile feedback, especially for tendon-driven robotic hands where joint sensing has been largely overlooked. The paper specifically addresses three key questions:

1. **Extraction of Exteroceptive Information from Joint Measurements**: The research seeks to determine how much information about the environment (e.g., object state) can be implicitly inferred solely from temporal sequences of joint measurements, and which policy architecture is most effective at capturing this information. This is motivated by the desire to reduce dependence on external perception systems, which suffer from issues such as occlusion, computational cost, and sim-to-real gaps.
2. **Comparison of Motor Encoders vs. Direct Joint Sensing**: For tendon-driven hands, the study investigates whether motor encoder signals or direct joint sensing (measuring at the joint output) provides superior proprioceptive feedback. This is crucial because tendon transmission effects like cable stretch, friction, and backlash can introduce significant nonlinearities and discrepancies between motor commands and actual joint positions, affecting control precision and sim-to-real transfer.
3. **Real-World Performance of Joint-Only Control**: The paper aims to evaluate whether a controller trained exclusively on joint feedback can achieve competitive real-world in-hand manipulation performance, specifically continuous cube reorientation, without any external vision or tactile sensing. This addresses the practical challenge of deploying robust manipulators in scenarios where external sensing is impractical or unreliable.

By answering these questions, the authors aim to develop a robust and efficient approach to dexterous manipulation that is less susceptible to the limitations of external sensing modalities and the complexities of tendon-driven mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves a three-stage approach: teacher policy training in simulation, student policy distillation using a Proprioceptive Transformer (PT) architecture, and real-world deployment on a tendon-driven hand.

**A. Overview**
1. **Teacher Policy Training**: A teacher policy is trained in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with access to privileged information, including the ground-truth object pose.
2. **Student Policy Distillation**: A student policy, implemented as a Proprioceptive Transformer, is trained to imitate the teacher's actions. The student's input is restricted to noisy joint sensor readings and its output includes both actions and auxiliary reconstructions of object and clean joint states.
3. **Real-World Deployment**: The trained Proprioceptive Transformer student policy is deployed directly on the real ORCA hand without further fine-tuning (zero-shot sim-to-real transfer).

**B. Training Environment**
- **Platform**: Isaac Lab simulation platform, utilizing 8192 parallel environments of the ORCA dexterous hand.
- **Frequency**: Simulation runs at 120 Hz; policy operates at 20 Hz (decimation factor of 6) to match real hardware control.
- **Task**: Continuous rotation of a 55mm cube around its z-axis, with a commanded angular velocity magnitude up to 1.5 rad/s.
- **Domain Randomization**: Applied to both robot and object properties to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Robot**: Friction coefficients (0.8–2.0×), link masses (0.95–1.05×), and log-uniform distributions for joint stiffness/damping.
 - **Object**: Friction (0.3–1.0), mass (0.5–1.5× scale), and initial pose with full SO(3) rotation.

**C. Teacher Policy Training**
- **Algorithm**: Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Observations**: An 81-dimensional noise-free vector including normalized joint positions (17-dim), joint velocities (17-dim), ground-truth object position (3-dim) and quaternion (4-dim) (privileged information), goal command (6-dim), previous action (17-dim), and previous joint position command (17-dim).
- **Actions**: 17-dimensional joint position targets, scaled to actuator limits. Exponential moving average (EMA) filtering is applied to raw policy outputs for smooth motions.
- **Reward Function**: A composite reward function including:
 - `r_pos`: Exponential kernel for object position tracking.
 - `r_mag`, `r_dir`: Angular velocity tracking, decomposed into magnitude and direction.
 - Regularization terms: Penalizing angular acceleration (`r_smooth`), joint velocities (`r_vel`), action magnitude (`r_act`), and action rate (`r_rate`). The action rate penalty was crucial for smooth hardware transfer.

**D. Student Policy Training**
- **Observations**: The student policy receives only noisy joint positions and velocities over a history window of T steps, previous action and joint position command, and goal command. Joint positions are corrupted with episode-specific bias and per-step Gaussian noise, and velocities with per-step Gaussian noise. Crucially, no privileged object information is provided.
- **Network Architecture**: Proprioceptive Transformer (PT).
 - **Input Processing**: Each observation group is projected to a 256-dimensional token embedding via a linear layer. This generates 10 tokens for proprioceptive history, 1 for action context, and 1 for command group (12 tokens total).
 - **Positional Embeddings**: Sinusoidal positional embeddings are added across the sequence.
 - **Transformer Encoder**: A 3-layer, 4-head Transformer encoder with a feedforward dimension of 1024 processes the sequence. Learnable `[CLS]` query tokens are appended.
 - **Output Heads**: Separate linear heads map the `[CLS]` tokens to predict actions (`â_t`) and auxiliary reconstructions of object position (`p̂_obj,t`), clean joint positions (`q̂_t`), and clean joint velocities (`q̇̂_t`).
- **Training Losses**:
 - **Behavior Cloning Loss (`L_BC`)**: L2 distance between the student's predicted action and the teacher's action.
 - **Auxiliary Reconstruction Loss (`L_recon`)**: Encourages the Transformer to encode implicit object state and denoise joint measurements. It consists of L2 distances between predicted and ground-truth object position, clean joint positions, and clean joint velocities.
 - **Total Loss**: `L = L_BC + L_recon`.

**E. Experimental Setup**
- **Hardware**: ORCA hand, a tendon-driven manipulator with 17 actuated degrees of freedom (16 finger joints, 1 wrist joint).
- **Sensing**: 16 AS5600 magnetic angle sensors integrated at the finger joints for direct joint angle measurement; wrist joint fixed. Joint velocities are derived from sensor readings.
- **Control**: ROS2-based control system at 20 Hz, sending joint position targets to a low-level PD controller.
- **Evaluation Objects**: Two cube sizes: 55 mm (medium) and 65 mm (large).
- **Implementation Details**: Training on NVIDIA RTX 4070 Ti SUPER GPU. Teacher policy: 4-layer MLP, ELU activations, 10,000 PPO iterations. Student policy (PT): 1200 epochs, batch size 256. Reward and loss weights are specified.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Rotations Per Minute (RPM), Rotation Accuracy (RA, ratio of correct-direction rotations), Drop-Free Success Rate (DFSR), and Drop Count (DC). Each trial is 60 seconds, averaged over 3 trials.
- **Baselines**:
 - **Proprio-PPO**: PPO policy using only joint positions and velocities, without object information.
 - **Extero-PPO**: PPO policy trained with ground-truth object pose in simulation, and in real-world deployment, object pose is estimated from camera images.
 - **PT-Motor**: Proprioceptive Transformer using motor encoder readings.
 - **PT-Joint**: Proprioceptive Transformer using direct joint sensor feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Proprioceptive Transformer vs. PPO Baselines**
The Proprioceptive Transformer variants (PT-Joint and PT-Motor) demonstrated superior performance over baseline PPO policies on the real ORCA hand for continuous cube rotation:
- **Medium Cube (55 mm)**: PT-Joint achieved 11.83 RPM, a 3.1× improvement over Proprio-PPO (3.83 RPM) and 3.8× over Extero-PPO (3.08 RPM). Both PT-Joint and PT-Motor achieved 100% Rotation Accuracy (RA) and Drop-Free Success Rate (DFSR) with zero drops, while Proprio-PPO exhibited variability in RA (92.59±10.47%) and DFSR (89.74±14.51%).
- **Large Cube (65 mm)**: PT-Joint achieved 11.33 RPM, a 2.2× improvement over Proprio-PPO (5.17 RPM) and 2.3× over Extero-PPO (4.83 RPM). Similar to the medium cube, PT-Joint maintained 100% RA and DFSR, demonstrating robust transfer across object sizes.
- **Comparison of Sensor Types**: PT-Joint consistently outperformed PT-Motor, achieving a 26.8% higher RPM (11.83 vs. 9.33 for medium cube). This suggests that direct joint sensing more accurately reflects the kinematic state, mitigating issues from tendon transmission nonlinearities.

**B. Implicit Object Detection via Joint Sensors**
Analysis of joint command vs. actual position in simulation revealed that direct joint sensors implicitly encode information about object presence and size:
- **Volume Effect**: Larger cubes physically obstruct finger movement, preventing fingers from reaching commanded positions and creating distinct position errors, especially in MCP (metacarpophalangeal) joints. These errors correlated with the object's physical volume.
- **Weight Effect**: Heavier cubes induce larger steady-state position errors in supporting joints (e.g., thumb joints) due to increased load under the same PD controller gains.
These effects generated discernible patterns in joint position data that differentiated between no cube, small, medium, and large cubes, indicating that joint sensors can provide implicit information about object properties without external perception.

**C. Implicit State Estimation via Transformer**
Comparison of encoder architectures (Transformer, MLP, LSTM) for reconstructing object position and clean joint states from proprioceptive observations:
- **Object Position Reconstruction**:
 - The Transformer achieved the lowest position RMSE of 13.70 ± 4.62 mm, outperforming MLP (17.87 ± 4.60 mm, a 23.4% reduction) and LSTM (15.64 ± 4.46 mm, a 12.2% reduction).
 - The Transformer demonstrated superior tracking of complex object dynamics, including rapid position transitions and high-frequency oscillations, where MLP exhibited systematic offsets and LSTM diverged during rapid state changes.
 - Statistical analysis confirmed these differences were significant (p < 0.001) with large effect sizes.
- **Joint State Reconstruction**:
 - The Transformer achieved the lowest mean position RMSE (0.098 rad) and velocity RMSE (0.070 rad/s), surpassing LSTM (0.110 rad, 0.112 rad/s) and MLP (0.146 rad, 0.168 rad/s).
 - The Transformer effectively denoised joint measurements, particularly for abduction joints where MLP struggled, indicating that temporal context is critical for disentangling contact-induced deflections from voluntary motion.

**D. Ablation Study**
Ablation experiments on the real robot confirmed the importance of direct joint sensing, observation window size, and the Transformer architecture with the reconstruction objective:
- **Effect of Sensor Feedback**: Policies using direct joint sensors (PT-Joint) consistently achieved 100% RA and 100% DFSR across all window sizes, whereas policies without (PT-Motor) showed reduced RA (averaging 84.75%) and DFSR (averaging 94.08%), particularly at smaller window sizes. PT-Joint also achieved a 6.7% higher average RPM (9.27 vs. 8.69).
- **Effect of Window Size**: Increasing the observation window size from 1 to 10 steps improved average RPM by 31% (from 8.08 to 10.58 RPM, averaged across sensor configurations), indicating that greater temporal context aids in implicit state estimation.
- **Effect of Encoder Architecture and Reconstruction**:
 - The Transformer architecture achieved the highest RPM (9.33 ± 0.63) with strong RA (96.19%) and DFSR (98.10%), significantly outperforming LSTM (7.00 ± 0.25 RPM) and MLP (5.08 ± 0.38 RPM) by 33% and 84%, respectively. This supports the effectiveness of self-attention for extracting implicit object state.
 - Removing the auxiliary reconstruction objective ("No Recon") led to severe performance degradation, with DFSR dropping to 35.0 ± 20.9% and an average of 5 drops per trial, demonstrating the critical role of this objective in learning robust latent representations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates that high-performance in-hand manipulation can be achieved using solely proprioceptive feedback, specifically joint position and velocity histories. This finding represents a shift from previous reliance on external vision or tactile systems, addressing several practical limitations associated with these modalities, such as susceptibility to occlusion, computational demands, and significant sim-to-real gaps.

The introduction of the Proprioceptive Transformer (PT) within a teacher-student distillation framework offers a method for transferring privileged simulation information into a deployable policy that operates on inherently available and robust sensory data. The empirical evidence showing that direct joint sensing (via magnetic angle sensors) provides superior feedback compared to motor-side sensing for tendon-driven hands is significant. This directly addresses the challenges posed by transmission nonlinearities, which are common in compliant and tendon-driven robotic mechanisms.

The achieved performance, including a 3.1× speed gain over baselines and 100% rotation accuracy on real-world benchmarks with the ORCA hand, indicates the practical viability of this approach. The capacity of the Transformer to implicitly extract object state information and denoise joint measurements from temporal proprioceptive patterns, as evidenced by lower object-state reconstruction error compared to MLP and LSTM baselines, suggests a robust mechanism for internal state estimation.

The ability to implicitly detect object presence and size through joint position tracking errors further highlights the richness of proprioceptive data when processed by an appropriate architecture. This work contributes to the development of more autonomous, robust, and efficient dexterous manipulators by reducing their dependence on complex external perception stacks, which can be prone to failure in varied real-world conditions.

Potential impacts include:
- **Increased Robot Autonomy**: Robots could operate in environments with poor visibility or occlusions, such as cluttered industrial settings or delicate assembly tasks.
- **Reduced System Complexity and Cost**: Eliminating the need for external cameras and associated processing pipelines can simplify hardware design and reduce computational overhead.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Leveraging a sensing modality with a minimal sim-to-real gap facilitates more seamless deployment of policies trained in simulation to physical robots.
- **Enhanced Dexterity in Tendon-Driven Hands**: Providing a practical method for precisely controlling complex tendon-driven hands by accounting for their unique mechanical properties.

Future research directions suggested by this work include extending the framework to multi-axis reorientation and more diverse object geometries, as well as investigating the synergistic combination of proprioceptive latent-state learning with sparse exteroceptive cues, such as tactile information, for even broader real-world generalization.
