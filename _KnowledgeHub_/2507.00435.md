# RoboEval: Where Robotic Manipulation Meets Structured and Scalable Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Washington introduced ROBOEVAL, a framework for evaluating robotic manipulation policies that expands beyond binary success to include multi-dimensional behavioral and outcome metrics. This framework revealed significant differences in execution quality, efficiency, and failure modes among policies with similar success rates, offering a more comprehensive assessment of robotic capabilities.

## Problem

- Current robotic manipulation policy evaluation relies primarily on binary success metrics, which provide an incomplete assessment of execution quality, trajectory smoothness, or grasp stability.
- The lack of granular insight impedes the diagnosis of specific failure modes, the understanding of learned skills, and the effective comparison between different policies.
- Existing bimanual manipulation benchmarks, while advancing data collection and environments, often lack a unified framework for systematically capturing task progression and diverse failure types across varied tasks.

## Method

- Introduces ROBOEVAL, a structured evaluation framework for bimanual manipulation policies, built on principles of Diversity (8 base tasks with variations), Interpretability (multi-dimensional metrics), and Extensibility (modular design).
- Integrates four classes of metrics: Efficiency (temporal and spatial path lengths), Safety/Stability (jerk, collisions, slips), Coordination (bimanual gripper/arm differences), and Task Progression/Outcome (stage-wise success and overall success rate).
- Provides a benchmark environment with eight bimanual manipulation tasks, each with 3-5 systematic spatial variations, and a dataset of 3,000 high-quality human expert demonstrations collected via VR teleoperation.

## Results

- Policies with similar success rates showed significant differences in behavioral metrics; for example, in 'Pack Box', ACT achieved 4x lower Cartesian jerk and 2.7x shorter paths than Pi-0.5, despite comparable success rates.
- Task progression metrics differentiated policies even at 0% overall success, showing varied stage-wise completion (e.g., 35-48% progression in 'Lift Tray' for policies with 0% success).
- Policies consistently underperformed human demonstrations in efficiency (3-10 times slower, 2-9 times longer trajectories) but often exhibited 'smoother' motions (lower jerk) than human teleoperation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - ROBOEVAL's multi-dimensional metrics offer complementary insights that go beyond binary success rates, revealing qualitative aspects of policy execution and distinct failure modes.
> - The framework's metrics effectively differentiate between policies that achieve similar overall success rates by highlighting variations in execution quality, efficiency, and underlying strategies.
> - The discriminative power of the various metrics varies across task domains and complexity levels, underscoring the necessity of a comprehensive metric suite to fully capture policy differences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation often evaluates policy performance primarily through binary success metrics. While useful for determining task completion, this approach has limitations as it does not capture the qualitative aspects of execution, such as trajectory smoothness, grasp stability, or bimanual coordination. Policies achieving identical success rates may exhibit significant differences in their underlying behavior and robustness, which binary evaluation methods cannot differentiate. This lack of granular insight impedes the diagnosis of failure modes, the understanding of learned skills, and the effective comparison of policies.

Prior research has established numerous benchmarks for robotic manipulation, including single-arm settings (e.g., RLBench, Robosuite, ManiSkill2, Colosseum) and more recently, bimanual scenarios (e.g., Peract2, BiGym, RoboTwin, HumanoidBench, DexMimicGen). These benchmarks have advanced the scalability of data collection and evaluation environments, yet many still rely on limited outcome-based metrics. Although some benchmarks evaluate generalization under environmental variation, a unified framework that systematically captures task progression, execution quality, and diverse failure modes across varied bimanual tasks remains less common.

The current work addresses this gap by introducing ROBOEVAL, a framework that augments traditional binary success with a multi-dimensional suite of behavioral and outcome metrics. This approach aligns with evaluation methodologies in other domains, such as BLEU in natural language processing and FID in computer vision, which moved beyond simple accuracy to assess generation quality more comprehensively. It also draws inspiration from classical robotics literature that characterizes performance across axes like accuracy, efficiency, stability, and safety. ROBOEVAL aims to provide a structured, fine-grained evaluation platform that unifies diverse bimanual tasks within a common framework, thereby enabling a deeper understanding of robotic manipulation policy capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is the observation that evaluating robotic manipulation policies solely on binary task success provides an incomplete and potentially misleading assessment of their true capabilities. Two policies might achieve the same success rate, yet one could operate fluently and robustly, while the other is brittle and erratic. This conventional evaluation method masks critical differences in execution quality and obscures the underlying structure of failures. As robotic systems operate in increasingly complex and dynamic environments, a richer set of evaluation signals is necessary to understand policy behavior, diagnose failures, and identify acquired skills.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a structured evaluation framework and benchmark:** To move beyond binary success, the authors aimed to create ROBOEVAL, an evaluation framework that integrates principled behavioral and outcome metrics. This framework is designed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of robotic manipulation policies.
2. **Quantify execution quality through multi-dimensional metrics:** The research sought to instrument tasks with standardized metrics that quantify efficiency (e.g., time and resource usage), safety/stability (e.g., robustness to collisions or slips), and coordination (e.g., temporal and spatial coupling between arms). These behavioral metrics are complemented by outcome measures that trace stagewise task progress and localize specific failure modes.
3. **Provide a robust and reproducible benchmark environment:** ROBOEVAL includes eight bimanual manipulation tasks, each with systematically controlled variations, allowing for a structured testbed. To support learning and analysis, the benchmark provides over three thousand expert demonstrations collected via VR teleoperation and a modular simulation platform for reproducible experimentation.
4. **Validate the utility and informativeness of the proposed metrics:** A central objective was to experimentally confirm that the new behavioral and outcome metrics offer reliable and meaningful insights into policy performance. This validation was conducted by assessing the metrics' stability under task variation, their discriminative power among policies with similar success rates, and their correlation with overall task success.
5. **Enable fine-grained policy diagnosis and continuous improvement:** By revealing execution aspects that contribute to robustness, distinguishing policies with similar success, and exposing structured failure modes, the framework aims to facilitate more precise diagnosis of policy limitations and guide future research toward more capable and robust robotic systems.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to shift robotic manipulation evaluation from a superficial measure of *whether* a task is completed to a detailed analysis of *how* and *why* it is completed (or fails to be completed), thereby fostering more interpretable and effective robot learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ROBOEVAL is conceived as a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating learning-based manipulation policies, structured around three design principles: Diversity, Interpretability, and Extensibility.

**Diversity:** The benchmark includes eight base bimanual tasks encompassing service, warehouse, and industrial domains. These tasks, such as lifting a tray, closing a box, and rotating a hand-wheel, require varying levels of temporal complexity, coordination demands, and semantic content. Each base task is accompanied by 3-5 systematic variations in spatial configuration (static, position, rotation, or combined position-rotation perturbations), allowing for controlled analysis of robustness and generalization.

**Interpretability:** Beyond binary success, ROBOEVAL offers multi-dimensional behavioral and outcome metrics for fine-grained analysis. This allows for detailed diagnosis of failure modes, sensitivity to task variations, and qualitative comparisons between policies.

**Extensibility:** The framework is designed with modularity, separating task definitions, variation generation, and evaluation protocols. This allows for the seamless integration of new tasks, variations, and metrics while maintaining comparability across different studies.

**Task Definition and Dataset:**
Each task in ROBOEVAL is formally defined by a tuple `T = (S, A, P, G, ρ0, Ssuccess)`.
- `S` represents the state space (robot joint states, object poses, environmental context).
- `A` is the action space (continuous control inputs like joint positions or end-effector delta displacements).
- `P` denotes the transition dynamics, governed by a physics simulator.
- `G` specifies the intended task outcome.
- `Ssuccess` defines binary completion based on geometric conditions.
- `ρ0` is the initial state distribution.
Tasks are instantiated with a parameterized family of variants `Tθ`, where `θ` controls scene layout or content.

To facilitate learning and provide baselines, ROBOEVAL includes 3,000 high-quality human expert demonstrations across all tasks and variations. These demonstrations were collected using a VR-based teleoperation system, capturing proprioceptive, visual, and scene-annotated interaction states. This dataset exhibits natural variability in execution strategies, motion trajectories, and coordination styles.

**Evaluation Scoring (Metrics):**
ROBOEVAL introduces four classes of metrics:

1. **Efficiency-Based Metrics:**
 - **Temporal Efficiency:** Trajectory length (number of control steps) and completion time (total duration in seconds).
 - **Spatial Efficiency:**
 - **Joint Path Length (Ljoint):** Cumulative displacement in joint space.
 - **Cartesian Path Length (Lcart):** Cumulative displacement of the end-effector in Cartesian space.
 - **Orientation Path Length (OPL):** Cumulative angular displacement of the end-effector orientation.

2. **Safety/Stability Metrics:**
 - **Motion Smoothness:**
 - **Joint Jerk (Jerkjoint):** Average norm of the third-order finite difference of the joint trajectory.
 - **Cartesian Jerk (Jerkcart):** Average norm of the third-order finite difference of the end-effector Cartesian trajectory.
 - **Contact-based Indicators:**
 - **Self-Collisions (SCC):** Number of contacts between robot links.
 - **Environment Collisions (ECC):** Number of contacts with scene elements.
 - **Object Slips (SC):** Instances where a grasped object loses contact.

3. **Coordination and Bimanual Metrics:**
 - **Height Discrepancy (BGVD - Bimanual Gripper Vertical Difference):** Mean absolute difference in vertical (z-axis) positions of the left and right end-effectors.
 - **Velocity Divergence (BAVD - Bimanual Arm Velocity Difference):** Mean norm of the difference between left and right end-effector velocities.

4. **Task Progression and Outcome Metrics:**
 - **Task Progression (TP):** Stagewise success indicators, logged as binary flags for discrete task phases.
 - **Success Rate (Success):** Proportion of episodes achieving overall task completion.

**Experimental Setup:**
The authors evaluated five state-of-the-art visuomotor policies: ACT, Diffusion Policy, GR00T N1.6, X-VLA, and π0.5. These models included single-task specialists (ACT, Diffusion Policy) and vision-language-action (VLA) models (GR00T, X-VLA, π0.5), with some models fine-tuned in full or LoRA settings. Human teleoperation demonstrations served as an upper-bound reference. Experiments focused on the 8 tasks with their 3-4 spatial variations (static, position, orientation, combined). The evaluation aimed to answer three research questions regarding the insights provided by the metrics beyond binary success, conditions under which metrics are informative, and how their patterns vary across tasks and complexities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using ROBOEVAL validated the utility of its multi-dimensional metrics, providing insights beyond traditional binary success rates. The findings are organized according to the research questions posed by the authors.

**RQ1: Do behavioral and outcome metrics provide insights beyond binary success rates?**

The analysis demonstrated that behavioral and outcome metrics offer complementary insights not captured by binary success.
- **Dissociation of Success and Quality:** Across eight tasks, the model achieving the highest success rate led in only 33% of the 104 other metric comparisons. For example, in the "Rotate Valve" task, ACT achieved a Cartesian jerk up to 3 times lower than the next-best policy (1.0 vs. 3.0 m/s³) despite not consistently leading in success across tasks. In "Pack Box," Pi-0.5 and ACT had similar success rates (0.54 vs. 0.48), but ACT exhibited 4 times lower jerk and 2.7 times shorter paths, indicating substantial differences in execution efficiency and smoothness.
- **Distinct Failure Modes:** Task progression metrics could differentiate policies even when overall success was zero. For instance, in "Lift Tray," policies with 0% success still showed task progression ranging from 35% to 48%. Safety metrics highlighted distinct failure mechanisms; in "Stack Two Blocks," GR00T and Diffusion Policy had similar low success rates (4% and 6%), but GR00T incurred significantly more self-collisions (23.6 vs. 2.0 per episode).
- **Metric Correlation Structure:** A Spearman correlation analysis showed that metrics cluster into interpretable families (e.g., path length metrics, jerk metrics, temporal metrics) that are tightly correlated within their groups. However, the overall suite does not collapse into a single factor, indicating that different metric families capture distinct aspects of policy behavior. Temporal metrics showed a negative correlation with success, clustering with outcome measures. Coordination metrics fell into separate branches, suggesting they capture complementary dimensions.
- **Comparison to Human Performance:** Policies consistently underperformed human demonstrations in efficiency, being 3–10 times slower and having 2–9 times longer trajectories. Humans also exhibited near-zero self-collisions and slips, indicating higher reliability. Interestingly, policies often appeared "smoother" (lower Cartesian and joint jerk) than human teleoperation, which the authors attribute to the smoothing effects of learned action generation compared to the discrete corrections inherent in human teleoperation.

**RQ2: Under what task or policy conditions are these metrics most informative?**

Behavioral and outcome metrics proved informative under conditions where binary success alone was insufficient.
- **Differentiating Policies with Similar Success:** In scenarios where policies achieved comparable success rates, behavioral metrics revealed meaningful differences in execution quality. For example, in "Pack Box," Pi-0.5 and ACT had overlapping success rates, but ACT demonstrated significantly lower Cartesian jerk and shorter Cartesian path lengths. Similar observations were made in "Lift Tray." This indicates that these metrics can distinguish policies based on their execution strategies and efficiency, even when their final outcomes are similar.
- **Diagnosing Skill Gaps at Low Success Rates:** When overall success rates were low, outcome metrics, particularly task progression and stage-wise failure distributions, provided granular insights into where policies encountered difficulties. In "Pack Box," policies with low success (≤12%) were differentiated by their task progression, with some policies stalling at early stages (e.g., GR00T and xVLA making no measurable progress in 66% and 58% of episodes, respectively) while others distributed failures more gradually across intermediate stages (e.g., ACT). This stage-wise breakdown helps pinpoint specific bottlenecks in policy execution.

**RQ3: How do the patterns and discriminative power of metrics vary across task domains and levels of complexity?**

The discriminative power of the metrics varied across tasks and complexity levels.
- **Strongest Discriminators:** The coefficient of variation (CV) analysis revealed that success rate was consistently the most discriminating metric across all conditions (CV ≥ 1.0). Task progression (TP) and self-collision count (SCC) also showed high discriminative power.
- **Weakest Discriminators:** Trajectory length (TL) and slip count (SC) consistently exhibited low CV, suggesting that these behaviors were more uniform across different policies, regardless of the task or variation.
- **Stability Across Complexity:** The discriminative power of metrics remained largely stable as task complexity increased from static to combined position-and-orientation perturbations. This indicates that the metrics are robust and continue to provide informative distinctions even in challenging environments.
- **Task-Dependent Discrimination:** The effectiveness of individual metrics varied significantly across different tasks. For instance, SCC was highly discriminating in "RotateValve" and "StackTwoBlocks," reflecting diverse collision-avoidance behaviors. End-effector collision count (ECC) showed a high CV specifically in "LiftPot." Coordination metrics (BAVD, BGVD) were most discriminating in "RotateValve" and "StackTwoBlocks." This task-dependence underscores the necessity of a comprehensive metric suite to fully capture policy differences across a range of manipulation challenges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ROBOEVAL represents a significant contribution to the evaluation of robotic manipulation policies by challenging the adequacy of binary success as the sole performance indicator. The framework systematically integrates a diverse set of behavioral and outcome metrics, moving toward a more nuanced and comprehensive assessment of robotic capabilities. This approach is significant for several reasons:

1. **Deeper Understanding of Policy Behavior:** By quantifying aspects like efficiency, safety, stability, and bimanual coordination, ROBOEVAL enables researchers to gain a deeper understanding of *how* policies operate. This moves beyond simply knowing *if* a task was completed to understanding the quality and characteristics of the execution, which is crucial for developing more sophisticated and human-like robotic skills.
2. **Enhanced Policy Diagnosis:** The detailed metrics, particularly task progression and stage-wise failure analysis, offer a powerful diagnostic tool. They allow researchers to pinpoint specific failure modes and skill gaps, even when overall success rates are low or similar across policies. This capability is critical for identifying areas for improvement and guiding the iterative development of robust manipulation policies.
3. **Improved Benchmark Comparability and Reproducibility:** The modular design, standardized metric instrumentation, and comprehensive dataset of human demonstrations contribute to higher comparability and reproducibility across research efforts. This facilitates a more objective evaluation landscape, allowing for fair comparisons between different learning algorithms and architectures.
4. **Advancement in Robust and Interpretable Robot Learning:** The validation study demonstrates that the proposed metrics are stable, discriminative, and complementary to binary success. This empirical evidence supports the utility of richer evaluation signals in revealing distinctions hidden by simple success rates, thereby fostering the development of policies that are not only successful but also efficient, safe, and coordinated.
5. **Potential for New Research Directions:** The authors suggest future extensions, such as expanding variation modalities and utilizing these metrics as auxiliary objectives or reward-shaping signals during policy training. This indicates that ROBOEVAL can serve not just as an evaluation tool but also as a catalyst for developing more interpretable, robust, and data-efficient robot learning algorithms. By providing a clearer signal of desirable and undesirable behaviors, the framework can inform the design of reward functions and learning objectives, ultimately leading to more capable and generalized robotic systems.

In conclusion, ROBOEVAL offers a foundational framework for advancing robotic manipulation research by enabling fine-grained analysis of policy performance, thereby contributing to the development of robots that can perform complex tasks with greater proficiency and adaptability.
