# GemNav: Discrete-Token Visual Robot Navigation using a Multimodal Large Language Model

> [!summary] Summary
> GemNav adapts a frozen Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) for visual robot navigation by converting continuous actions into discrete tokens and applying LoRA to the language tower. This approach enables robust navigation in unseen real-world environments, achieving 5/0/0 success rates in pose-goal trials across four diverse environments using only 8.7 hours of training data.

## Problem

- Traditional robot navigation systems often lack generalization, being optimized for specific robot configurations or environments.
- Current large vision-language-action (VLA) models demand thousands of hours of training data and require specialized visual encoders.
- The visual encoder is a significant bottleneck in VLA performance, necessitating continuous scaling and retraining.

## Method

- Adapted a frozen, pretrained MLLM (Gemma 4) by applying ==Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)== exclusively to its language tower, without modifying its visual backbone.
- Introduced a ==discrete-token action representation== to unify the prediction of continuous metric waypoints and categorical navigation signals like `<goal reached>` or `<goal unreachable>`.
- Developed a ==soft-decoded auxiliary loss== function to recover the underlying metric structure of continuous waypoints, which is otherwise discarded by pure cross-entropy training on discrete bins.

## Results

- Achieved a **5/0/0** success rate (stop/partial/miss) in all pose-goal trials across four diverse, unseen real-world environments on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot (**20 trials** total).
- Consistently stopped within **0.25–0.42 m** of target goals, substantially outperforming OmniVLA which failed all comparable trials with final displacements ranging from **10.44–29.18 m**.
- Demonstrated robust navigation and obstacle avoidance in cluttered real-world settings, with performance in terms of time and path length approaching human experts in some environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-quality visual representations from off-the-shelf MLLMs are sufficient for real-world robot navigation when effectively coupled with appropriate action representations.
> - Data efficiency for VLA models can be dramatically improved, achieving successful deployment with less than 9 hours of training data compared to thousands of hours for baseline methods.
> - Discrete tokenization of continuous actions, augmented with an auxiliary metric loss, effectively bridges the gap between MLLM language outputs and precise robot control.
> - Increasing visual history can be counterproductive for closed-loop robot deployment, despite improving offline metrics, due to detrimental time-lag effects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot navigation has traditionally relied on modular systems comprising perception, state estimation, and control, typically optimized for specific robot configurations, sensors, or environments. More recently, the field has seen a shift towards large pretrained vision-language-action (VLA) models, which frame navigation as a cross-embodiment learning problem. These VLA policies are trained on extensive datasets, often accumulating hundreds to thousands of hours of trajectories from diverse robots, environments, and sensor setups.

The prevailing methodology for constructing these large-scale visual navigation policies involves a dedicated visual encoder, a bespoke continuous-action regression head, and training on vast, cross-embodiment datasets. While this approach has demonstrated broad transferability, it also necessitates increasingly large volumes of demonstration data and specialized components, particularly visual encoders trained from scratch on robotic data. Recent evaluations have identified the visual encoder as a significant bottleneck for downstream VLA performance, suggesting a trajectory where visual scale is the primary driver for improvement.

GemNav, as presented in this paper, proposes an alternative perspective. It interrogates whether the robust and broad visual representations already present in modern multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can be directly leveraged for robot navigation. Instead of developing or retraining visual encoders, GemNav focuses on re-evaluating the action representation. This approach contrasts with existing methods like OmniVLA, which uses an L1 regression head trained on 9,500 hours of data, or JanusVLN, which couples an MLLM backbone with a 3D-geometry encoder for continuous vision-language navigation. Other works, such as VLM2VLA, also represent continuous actions as natural language, but GemNav employs a compact, binned token alphabet with explicit stop signals. The work also distinguishes itself from methods that augment action-free video with VLM-generated language or learn from web-scale video data, by focusing on pose and image goals with cross-trajectory negatives for visually disconnected targets. By doing so, GemNav aims to address the data efficiency and architectural complexity challenges associated with scaling current VLA models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether the visual representations from frozen, pretrained Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are sufficient for robot navigation, and whether the operational gap between a general-purpose MLLM and a deployable navigation policy can be bridged by innovating in action representation rather than through further scaling of perceptual components.

Specifically, the paper outlines several key objectives:

- **Bypassing the Vision-Encoder Bottleneck:** To test if the identified vision-encoder bottleneck in current VLA models can be circumvented for short-to-medium-horizon waypoint navigation. This involves relying exclusively on the pretrained MLLM's inherent visual grounding, without retraining its vision tower or introducing any auxiliary visual encoders. The aim is to determine if a frozen visual backbone can be made actionable for robot control.
- **Novel Discrete-Token Action Representation:** To introduce a discrete value tokenization scheme that unifies the prediction of continuous metric waypoints and categorical navigation signals (e.g., `<goal reached>`, `<goal unreachable>`). This approach aims to integrate these diverse outputs into a single, shared discrete token vocabulary generated directly by the MLLM's language-model head, thereby leveraging the MLLM's native interface.
- **Restoring Metric Structure with an Auxiliary Loss:** To develop a soft-decoded auxiliary loss function that can recover the underlying metric structure of continuous waypoints, which is otherwise discarded by pure cross-entropy training on discrete bins. This loss is designed to penalize errors based on metric distance between bins, thereby improving prediction accuracy for continuous values.
- **Assessing Temporal Context:** To systematically evaluate the contribution of short image histories (e.g., two or four past frames) to navigation performance, both in terms of offline validation metrics and real-robot deployment benefits. This objective aims to understand the utility of temporal context when strong pretrained vision features are already in place.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several challenges in the broader field of robot learning:

- **Scalability of VLA Models:** Current VLA policies necessitate increasingly large training datasets and specialized architectures, raising questions about the true source of their generalization capabilities and their long-term scalability.
- **Underutilization of MLLM Capabilities:** Modern MLLMs possess highly robust and general visual representations, yet their direct application to robot control without significant architectural modification or retraining of visual components remains largely unexplored.
- **Data Efficiency:** The reliance on thousands of hours of demonstration data makes VLA training resource-intensive and often prohibitive. There is a need for more data-efficient learning paradigms for robot navigation.
- **Architectural Simplification:** By integrating action generation directly into the MLLM's language tower and freezing the vision tower, the research aims to simplify the overall policy architecture, reducing the need for bespoke components.
- **Practical Deployment:** The focus on short-to-medium-horizon waypoint navigation addresses a common and practical requirement for structured robot deployments, where policies predict local trajectories for higher-level planners.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GemNav proposes a visual navigation policy that adapts a frozen Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) for short-to-medium horizon waypoint navigation. The core methodology involves using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) exclusively on the MLLM's language tower, without any auxiliary visual encoder or a continuous regression head. Navigation is framed as a multimodal sequence prediction task.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

At each control step, the policy receives a goal specification `g`, which can be a goal image (`I_g`), a 2D goal coordinate (`p_g` in the robot-local frame), or both. The current view (`I_c`) is a sequence of `h+1` egocentric frames, including the current frame and an optional history. Given `I_c` and `g`, the policy outputs either an eight-waypoint trajectory `τ` in the body frame or a stop signal (`<goal reached>` or `<goal unreachable>`). The stop signals indicate either successful goal arrival or refusal to plan for visually disconnected targets.

#### 3.2. Trajectory Tokenization and Prompt Construction

Rather than using a regression head, GemNav extends the MLLM's vocabulary to represent trajectories as token sequences. Sixty-nine slots from Gemma 4’s reserved token range are allocated: 64 for value-bin tokens and 5 for role tokens (`<bot traj>`, `<eot traj>`, `<goal reached>`, `<goal coord>`, `<goal unreachable>`). Each waypoint coordinate (x, y) is independently quantized into one of the 64 uniform bins within a range of [-15m, 15m]. This results in a half-bin reconstruction error of 0.234m per axis.

A trajectory sample is encoded as an 18-token sequence: `<bot traj>` followed by 8 pairs of (x, y) value-bin tokens, and then `<eot traj>`. Stop samples output a 3-token sequence: `[<bot traj>, s, <eot traj>]`. The 2D goal pose is also encoded using these value tokens, prefixed by `<goal coord>`, ensuring a consistent representational interface for both conditioning and generation.

The input to the MLLM is constructed as a Gemma-4 user-turn prompt, which includes a task description, the current and historical image frames, the optional goal view, and the optional goal-coordinate token sequence. This is followed by a "Trajectory:" marker to trigger the assistant's turn. Image inputs are resized to 224x224 pixels and processed by Gemma 4's native image tokenizer, with a cap of 256 visual tokens per image.

#### 3.3. Model Training and Adaptation

GemNav adapts the public Gemma-4-E2B-it model using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (r=32, α=16, dropout 0) applied to all linear layers in the language tower. The vision tower of the MLLM remains frozen, meaning no LoRA targets, unfrozen parameters, or auxiliary encoders are used for visual processing. Trajectories are predicted directly through the stock language model head.

The policy is supervised using token-level cross-entropy (`L_CE`) on the target sequence. This loss also applies to the stop and unreachable tokens, enabling the model to refuse planning when appropriate. An auxiliary soft-decoded regression term (`L_aux`) is introduced to reincorporate the ordinal structure inherent in metric value bins, which pure cross-entropy disregards. This term computes the squared Euclidean distance between the soft-decoded waypoint prediction (derived from a softmax expectation over bin centers) and the ground-truth waypoint. The total training loss is `L = L_CE + λ_aux * L_aux`, with `λ_aux = 0.1`.

#### 3.4. Data Pipeline

Training utilizes the SCAND dataset, specifically its image and pose information, totaling approximately 8.7 hours.
- **Waypoint Extraction:** Eight target waypoints are extracted at fixed intervals ahead of the current frame and transformed into the robot's body frame.
- **Goal Sampling:** A distance-based sampler selects goal indices `t_g` such that the world-frame displacement from the current position `p_t` falls within `Uniform(0.5, 14) m`. This ensures training covers a deployment-relevant distance range.
- **Filters and Augmentation:** Samples are filtered to reject goals behind the robot (x < -0.3m) and samples requiring pure in-place rotation (>30 degrees yaw difference). Left-right flip augmentation is applied.
- **Negatives:** For ~10% of image-bearing samples, the goal image is replaced with one from a different trajectory, and the target is set to `<goal unreachable>` to train a "refuse-to-plan" response.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform:** Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped equipped with an Intel RealSense color camera.
- **Deployment Environments:** Four real-world environments unseen during training: Carpark (open), Obstacle Carpark (with obstacles), Outdoor Chemical Yard (long, complex), and Indoor Warehouse (long, cluttered).
- **Goal Modalities:** Ego (image-only), Pose (coordinate-only), and Ego+Pose (both).
- **Baselines:** OmniVLA (a published cross-embodiment VLA) and human teleoperator reference (for complex environments).
- **Metrics:**
 - **Real-world:** Success rate (SR) (stop/partial/miss), elapsed time (t), path length (d), and final displacement (∆).
 - **Offline Validation:** Average Displacement Error (ADE) and Final Displacement Error (FDE) for trajectories, and stop accuracy.

#### 3.6. Ablation Studies

The methodology includes several ablation studies to evaluate component contributions:
- **Decode Mode:** Comparison between continuous decoding (softmax expectation) and bin-snapped decoding (greedy argmax).
- **History Depth:** Evaluation of single-frame (h=0), two-frame (h=2), and four-frame (h=4) history variants.
- **Goal Sampling:** Comparison between distance-based and legacy frame-aligned goal sampling.
- **LoRA Capacity, Crop Mode, Model Size, and Auxiliary Loss** are also ablated in the supplementary material.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the performance of GemNav, both in offline validation and real-world robot deployment, as well as insights from ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Validation Performance

Offline evaluation was conducted on two distinct validation subsets: an in-distribution split from SCAND and an out-of-distribution (OOD) split from TokenWalker (TW), a newly collected dataset.
- **In-distribution (SCAND):** GemNav achieved a combined Average Displacement Error (ADE) of 0.49 m and demonstrated 100% stop accuracy (50/50 samples correctly identified as `<goal reached>`).
- **Out-of-distribution (TokenWalker):** The combined ADE increased to 1.19 m, and stop accuracy was 80/108, indicating a moderate performance degradation under distribution shift rather than a complete failure.
- **Modality-Specific Performance:** The ego-only (image-goal) modality was the most challenging across both splits.
- **Auxiliary Loss Impact:** The auxiliary soft-decoded regression term was shown to reduce ego-only validation ADE by 23% (from 1.05 m to 0.81 m) compared to using pure cross-entropy loss.

#### 4.2. Real-world Deployment Performance

GemNav was deployed on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot in four distinct, unseen real-world environments.
- **Cross-Environment Success:** GemNav achieved a 5/0/0 (stop/partial/miss) success rate in every pose-goal trial across all four environments: Carpark, Obstacle Carpark, Outdoor Chemical Yard, and Indoor Warehouse (20 trials in total).
- **Goal-Reaching Accuracy:** The policy consistently stopped within 0.25–0.42 m of the target goal across both short and medium-horizon settings.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **OmniVLA:** In contrast, OmniVLA failed to complete any pose-goal trials (0/0/5 in each environment), often moving away from the target or failing to re-acquire the goal direction after required turns. Its final displacements were substantially larger, ranging from 10.44 m to 29.18 m.
 - **Human Teleoperator:** In the two medium-horizon environments (Chemical Yard and Warehouse), GemNav's performance in terms of time and path length approached that of a human expert, while achieving lower final displacement (e.g., GemNav 0.25 m vs. Human 0.92 m in Chemical Yard).
- **Modality Breakdown (Carpark):** Pure image-goal navigation proved the weakest setting, with GemNav achieving only 1/5 success and OmniVLA 0/5. The inclusion of a goal coordinate significantly improved GemNav's stopping behavior (4/5 for ego+pose, 5/5 for pose).
- **Obstacle Avoidance:** GemNav successfully routed around obstacles in the Obstacle Carpark, Chemical Yard, and Warehouse environments, demonstrating the model's ability to generate appropriate waypoint outputs for navigation in cluttered spaces.

#### 4.3. Ablation Results

- **Decode Mode:** Continuous decoding (softmax expectation over bin centers) improved validation ADE by approximately 5 cm and enhanced closed-loop performance in the complex ego+pose setting, increasing Warehouse ego+pose success from 3/5 to 5/5, compared to bin-snapped decoding.
- **History Depth:** Offline validation metrics showed a monotonic improvement in combined ADE with increased history depth (from 1.45 m for h=0 to 1.15 m for h=4). However, real-robot deployment showed an inverse trend: the single-frame policy (h=0) was fastest and most reliable, while policies with two or four frames of history were slower and less reliable, with increased final displacement and reduced success rates in some modalities. This discrepancy is attributed to a time-lagged trajectory effect in closed-loop settings.
- **Goal Sampling:** The distance-based goal sampler, despite yielding a higher in-distribution combined ADE (0.49 m) than the legacy frame-based sampler (0.26 m), was crucial for deployability. The frame-based sampler failed entirely in real-robot deployment due to a fixed training horizon that did not extend to deployment-time goal distances. This indicates that in-distribution ADE alone is not a reliable indicator of deployability.
- **LoRA Capacity:** An LoRA rank of r=32 with α=16 was found to be optimal. Increasing the rank to r=64 led to worse performance across all metrics, which was attributed to an effective reduction in the per-update scaling factor (α/r).
- **Data Mix (SCAND vs. SCAND+TokenWalker):** Unexpectedly, adding the TokenWalker corpus to the SCAND training mix did not improve deployment performance and instead regressed image-bearing modalities on robot tests.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GemNav research introduces an alternative paradigm for visual robot navigation that challenges established practices and offers several significant implications for the field:

- **Re-evaluation of Vision Encoder Necessity:** The core finding that a frozen, pretrained Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) visual backbone, without additional training or auxiliary encoders, can be effectively adapted for real-world robot navigation is significant. This directly contradicts the prevailing notion that dedicated, often robot-specific, visual encoders are a bottleneck requiring continuous scaling. GemNav demonstrates that high-quality visual representations already exist within general-purpose MLLMs and can be made actionable.
- **Data Efficiency and Scalability:** GemNav achieves successful deployment across diverse, unseen real-world environments using a comparatively small training dataset of approximately 8.7 hours. This is orders of magnitude smaller than the thousands of hours typically required by state-of-the-art cross-embodiment VLA models. This data efficiency addresses a major barrier to entry in robot learning, making advanced navigation capabilities more accessible and reducing the computational and data collection resources needed.
- **Simplified Architectural Design:** By integrating continuous waypoint prediction and categorical navigation signals into a shared discrete-token vocabulary and decoding them through the MLLM's native language-model head, GemNav offers a simpler and more unified architectural approach. This eliminates the need for bespoke continuous regression heads and dedicated visual encoders, streamlining the policy design and potentially reducing implementation complexity.
- **Leveraging MLLMs for Embodied AI:** The work highlights the potential of adapting foundation models like MLLMs for embodied AI tasks by focusing on their language tower and discrete token generation. It suggests that these models possess a rich internal representation that can be repurposed for control tasks, opening new avenues for research into how general-purpose AI can be specialized for physical interaction.
- **Practical Deployability:** The robust performance in real-world, unseen environments, including obstacle-rich and long-horizon scenarios, demonstrates GemNav's practical utility. The consistent goal-reaching accuracy and successful obstacle avoidance indicate its potential for deployment in various structured and semi-structured settings.
- **Insights into Action Representation:** The research emphasizes that action representation, rather than solely visual scaling, is a critical design axis for adapting MLLMs to robot navigation. The effectiveness of discrete-token generation combined with a soft-decoded auxiliary loss provides a model for how continuous robot actions can be mapped to and from MLLM interfaces.
- **Guidance on Temporal Context:** The concrete negative result regarding the diminishing returns of visual history in closed-loop deployment, despite improving offline metrics, offers valuable guidance for future architectural decisions. It suggests that once strong pretrained visual features are present, adding more temporal context might not always yield benefits and can even introduce detrimental time-lag effects.

Overall, GemNav provides a new, data-efficient, and deployable approach to visual robot navigation that leverages the power of frozen MLLM representations. Its findings suggest a re-evaluation of current VLA model design principles, paving the way for more accessible and resource-efficient robot learning.
