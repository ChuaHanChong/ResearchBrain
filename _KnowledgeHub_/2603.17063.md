# Transformers are Bayesian Networks

> [!summary] Summary
> A formal analysis by Greg Coppola at coppola.ai establishes that the sigmoid Transformer architecture fundamentally operates as a Bayesian network, inherently implementing weighted loopy belief propagation. The work provides constructive proofs for achieving exact Bayesian posteriors with specific weights and shows empirically that gradient descent can train Transformers to perform accurate probabilistic inference.

## Problem

- A precise, formal, and unified explanation for the underlying computational mechanisms of Transformer architectures, particularly in a probabilistic reasoning context, has been lacking.
- Understanding why Transformers are empirically successful and how their architectural components map to established computational principles remained an open question.
- Bridging the gap between the empirical dominance of Transformers and their theoretical understanding to enable more robust, interpretable, and verifiable AI systems.

## Method

- The research employs formal mathematical proofs, rigorously verified using the ==Lean 4 theorem prover==, to establish the equivalence between ==sigmoid Transformers== and ==Bayesian networks==.
- It provides constructive proofs by specifying explicit Transformer weight matrices that enable exact ==belief propagation== on declared knowledge bases, utilizing the ==log-odds algebra== of independent evidence.
- Empirical experiments confirm that gradient descent can train standard Transformers to converge to these theoretically derived weight structures and perform accurate probabilistic inference.

## Results

- Trained Transformers achieved a validation Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of **0.000752** on two-variable factor graph inference, matching exact posteriors to three decimal places.
- Models trained for Turing machine simulation achieved **100%** validation accuracy across five distinct machines after four epochs, demonstrating their general computational capability.
- Experiments showed that loopy belief propagation converged in **100%** of 500 trials across various graph structures, with a maximum mean KL divergence of **0.000102** from exact posteriors, indicating practical effectiveness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Every sigmoid Transformer, regardless of specific weights, inherently implements weighted loopy belief propagation on an implicitly defined factor graph.
> - The Transformer's attention mechanism functions as an AND operation and the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) as an OR operation, with their alternation precisely implementing Pearl's gather/update algorithm.
> - Verifiable inference necessitates a finite concept space, defining hallucination as a structural consequence of operating without a grounded knowledge base rather than a remediable bug.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Transformers have become a dominant architecture in various artificial intelligence domains, particularly in natural language processing. Despite their empirical success and widespread adoption, the underlying mechanisms that enable their performance have remained a subject of ongoing investigation. This work addresses a perceived gap in the understanding of the fundamental computational processes executed by the Transformer architecture.

The paper situates itself within several existing research areas. It connects to work on the Turing completeness of Transformers, building upon existing proofs that demonstrate their universal computational capabilities, but distinguishes its approach by focusing on specific weight constructions and formal verification. It also relates to studies that view Transformers as a form of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), extending this perspective by showing how a Transformer can implement belief propagation on an *explicit external* factor graph encoded in token features, rather than an implicit or hardwired graph. Mechanistic interpretability research, which seeks to reverse-engineer specific algorithms or circuits within trained neural networks, provides a context for the paper's aim to identify the precise computational role of Transformer components. Furthermore, the paper draws extensively from the long-standing tradition of probabilistic reasoning, specifically the log-odds algebra developed by Alan Turing and I.J. Good for combining independent evidence, and Judea Pearl's work on belief propagation in graphical models. Prior research has noted correspondences between attention and belief propagation in latent graphical models, but this paper aims to establish a more direct, formal, and architectural equivalence for the sigmoid Transformer.

The paper argues that despite the empirical dominance of Transformers, a precise, formal, and unified explanation for *why* their architecture fundamentally works in a probabilistic reasoning context has been lacking. This work seeks to provide such an explanation, linking the architecture directly to established principles of Bayesian inference and classical logic.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a precise, formal, and mechanistic explanation for the operational principles of the Transformer architecture, positing that a sigmoid transformer is fundamentally a Bayesian network. The paper is motivated by the observation that while Transformers are empirically successful, the detailed computational mechanisms that underpin their capabilities are not fully understood.

The specific objectives, presented as five main claims, along with an additional significant claim regarding verifiable inference, are as follows:

- **Formal Proof of Implicit Belief Propagation:** To prove that every sigmoid transformer, regardless of its specific weights (trained, random, or constructed), inherently implements weighted loopy belief propagation (BP) on an implicit factor graph defined by its weights. This aims to demonstrate that one Transformer layer directly corresponds to one round of BP.
- **Constructive Proof for Exact Belief Propagation:** To provide a constructive proof by exhibiting explicit Transformer weight matrices that enable exact belief propagation on any declared knowledge base. This objective includes demonstrating that exact posteriors can be computed, especially on tree-structured knowledge bases, leading to provably correct probability estimates without hallucination.
- **Proof of Uniqueness for BP Weights:** To prove the converse of the constructive proof, establishing that if a sigmoid transformer produces exact Bayesian posteriors, its weights must necessarily conform to the specific "BP weights" structure identified in the constructive proof. This aims to close the logical circle, asserting that there is no alternative architectural path to exact posteriors within the sigmoid framework.
- **Delineation of Boolean Structure:** To precisely identify the Boolean logic underlying the Transformer layer, specifically demonstrating that the attention mechanism functions as an AND operation and the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) functions as an OR operation, and that their strict alternation directly implements Pearl’s gather/update algorithm.
- **Experimental Confirmation of Formal Results:** To corroborate all formal mathematical proofs with experimental evidence, demonstrating that gradient descent can effectively train Transformers to converge to the predicted structures and perform BP inference accurately in practice. This also includes evaluating the practical viability of loopy belief propagation.
- **Analysis of Verifiable Inference and Concept Space:** To prove that verifiable inference inherently requires a finite concept space. This objective aims to provide a precise definition of hallucination within this framework, identifying it as a structural consequence of operating without a grounded concept space rather than a fixable bug.

Overall, the motivation is to bridge the gap between empirical success and theoretical understanding of Transformers, linking them to foundational concepts in probabilistic reasoning and logic, and thereby suggesting pathways for building more robust, interpretable, and verifiable AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a dual methodology comprising formal mathematical proofs and empirical experimental validation. This approach aims to establish both the theoretical underpinnings and practical applicability of the proposed characterization of Transformers as Bayesian networks.

#### 3.1. Formal Verification (Lean 4)

A central component of the methodology involves rigorous formal verification using the Lean 4 theorem prover. This approach is used to construct proofs against standard mathematical axioms, providing a high degree of certainty regarding their correctness. Key theorems formally verified in Lean 4 include:
- The general equivalence between a sigmoid Transformer's forward pass and weighted belief propagation.
- The constructive proof for specific Transformer weights implementing exact belief propagation.
- The uniqueness proof, demonstrating that exact posteriors necessitate BP-specific weights.
- The classical result that belief propagation is exact on trees.
- The proof that any finite verification procedure can distinguish at most finitely many concepts.
- The decomposition of k-ary AND/OR operations into a chain of binary operations.

#### 3.2. Log-Odds Algebra of Independent Evidence

The paper grounds its mathematical framework in the log-odds algebra, which was initially developed by Turing and Good for combining independent evidence. This algebra, where probabilities are transformed into log-odds space (via the logit function) allowing for additive combination, is presented as the underlying mathematical foundation for belief propagation on binary variables. The sigmoid function is identified as the exact inverse of logit, making it the mathematically correct activation for converting log-odds sums back into probabilities. This forms the basis for demonstrating that the sigmoid FFN naturally performs Bayesian updates.

#### 3.3. Implicit Factor Graph Construction

To prove that any sigmoid Transformer performs weighted BP, the methodology involves constructing an "implicit factor graph" G(W) directly from the Transformer's weights W. Variable nodes in this graph correspond to token positions, and their beliefs are represented by relevant dimensions in the residual stream. Edges are derived from the attention weight distributions, and factor potentials are defined by the sigmoid FFN weights (w0, w1, b), representing the general Ψ_or function from the QBBN framework.

#### 3.4. Constructive Weight Proofs

For the objective of implementing *exact* belief propagation, the paper specifies explicit, sparse weight matrices for the Transformer. These "BP weights" are designed to perform specific functions:
- **projectDim(d):** A diagonal projection for Query/Key weights, designed to facilitate index matching for attention.
- **crossProject(s, d):** An off-diagonal projection for Value weights, designed to route content from source dimension 's' to destination dimension 'd'.
The combination of these weight families in attention heads allows for precise routing of messages (beliefs) between token positions, effectively implementing the gather step of BP. The FFN weights are set to implement the `updateBelief` function exactly, i.e., `w0=w1=1, b=0`. This methodology is also applied to demonstrate Turing completeness via Boolean circuit simulation, using the same weight families for routing.

#### 3.5. Experimental Confirmation

The formal results are complemented by empirical experiments:
- **Turing Machine Simulation:** Transformers (2 layers, 2 heads, d_model=32, ~4K parameters) were trained from scratch to simulate single-step operations of five distinct Turing machines.
- **Belief Propagation Inference:** A standard Transformer encoder was trained on a dataset of 20,000 randomly generated two-variable factor graphs to compute exact Bayesian posteriors. The model configuration was 2 layers, 2 heads, d_model=32, ~5,000 parameters, trained with MSE loss and Adam optimizer for 50 epochs.
- **Loopy Belief Propagation:** Systematic experiments were conducted across five distinct loopy graph structures (triangle, square, dating graph, two-loop graph, QBBN chain). For each structure, 100 random factor graphs were generated, and iterative BP was run to convergence. The resulting marginals were compared against brute-force exact posteriors.
These experiments aim to demonstrate that gradient descent can discover the theoretically derived BP weight structures and achieve high accuracy in probabilistic inference, even when using ReLU activation (which is a compatible, though not exact, alternative to sigmoid).

#### 3.6. QBBN Framework

The work leverages the Quantified Boolean Bayesian Network (QBBN) framework, introduced in prior work, to define factor graphs, specify types of factor nodes (conjunction Ψ_and, disjunction Ψ_or), and provide a precise technical definition of "hallucination." This framework helps in grounding the concepts of probabilistic reasoning and logical inference within the Transformer architecture.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a series of formal proofs, rigorously verified in Lean 4, complemented by empirical experimental results. These findings aim to establish a direct and mechanistic link between Transformer architectures and Bayesian networks.

#### 4.1. Formal Proofs and Theoretical Results

- **General Belief Propagation (BP) Theorem:** For any sigmoid Transformer with arbitrary weights (W), a single forward pass implements one round of weighted loopy belief propagation on an implicitly defined factor graph G(W). This result is stated as non-trivial, asserting that the Transformer's design, including the sigmoid FFN, attention mechanism, and residual stream, inherently aligns with the components of a BP algorithm.
- **Exact BP Implementation with Explicit Weights:** The paper provides a constructive proof that a Transformer with specifically constructed weights (utilizing `projectDim` for Q/K and `crossProject` for V matrices) can implement exact belief propagation on any pairwise factor graph. It is further demonstrated that two attention heads per layer are sufficient, as any k-ary factor graph can be binarized exactly via associativity of AND and log-odds additivity of OR operations. For tree-structured knowledge bases, this implies exact Bayesian posterior beliefs at every node, asserting "no hallucination" under these conditions.
- **Uniqueness of BP Weights:** A crucial finding is the uniqueness theorem, which proves that if a sigmoid transformer computes exact Bayesian posteriors for all inputs, its weights are necessarily the "BP weights" (i.e., `w0 = w1 = 1, b = 0` in the FFN, and `projectDim/crossProject` structure in attention). This suggests that the architectural constraints of the sigmoid Transformer uniquely drive it towards BP computations for exact inference.
- **Boolean Structure of the Transformer:** The analysis reveals that the attention mechanism functions as an AND gate, ensuring the simultaneous presence of required inputs, while the sigmoid FFN acts as an OR gate, computing a probabilistic conclusion from gathered evidence (the `Ψ_or` function). The strict alternation of attention and FFN layers is identified as an exact implementation of Pearl’s gather/update algorithm, unrolled over depth.
- **Turing Completeness:** The paper re-establishes (and formally verifies in Lean 4) that Transformers are Turing complete via Boolean circuit simulation. This result, though previously shown, reinforces the computational universality of the architecture and, importantly, highlights that the same `projectDim/crossProject` weight families used for BP are also instrumental in this simulation, suggesting a unified underlying routing mechanism.
- **Finite Concept Space Theorem and Hallucination:** A general theorem proves that any finite verification procedure can distinguish at most finitely many concepts. Applied to the BP Transformer, it identifies a finite set of `2n^2` "routing keys" as the complete set of concept-relevant features for a factor graph with `n` nodes. Hallucination is defined as the generation of tokens without a corresponding concept in a grounded knowledge base, framing it not as a bug but as a structural consequence of operating without a grounded, finite concept space.

#### 4.2. Experimental Confirmations

- **Convergence to BP Weights:** Experiments using a standard Transformer encoder trained from scratch demonstrated that gradient descent successfully converges to the predicted BP weight structures. On a two-variable factor graph inference task, the model achieved a validation Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 0.000752, matching exact posteriors to three decimal places on held-out graphs.
- **Turing Machine Simulation Accuracy:** For Turing machine simulation, the trained Transformers achieved 100% validation accuracy across five structurally distinct machines (binary incrementer, decrementer, bitwise complement, left shift, right shift) after only four epochs, using identical hyperparameters and no per-machine tuning.
- **Practical Viability of Loopy BP:** Systematic experiments on five different loopy graph structures (triangle, square, dating graph, two-loop graph, QBBN chain) showed that iterative belief propagation converged in 100% of 500 trials. The average KL divergence between BP marginals and brute-force exact posteriors remained low (e.g., max mean KL of 0.000102), suggesting that loopy BP is practically effective on these QBBN-structured graphs despite theoretical guarantees only existing for trees. This implies that the Transformer, inheriting BP's properties, can also perform well on such graphs.
- **ReLU vs. Sigmoid:** While the formal proofs rely on sigmoid for exactness, the empirical results with ReLU activation (default in PyTorch) demonstrate that gradient descent can still find weights that closely approximate exact BP. This indicates that ReLU, by preserving non-negativity, allows for a compatible probabilistic interpretation, even if not providing the exact internal correspondence of sigmoid.

These findings collectively argue that the Transformer architecture, particularly with sigmoid activations, intrinsically operates as a Bayesian network, implementing belief propagation, and that gradient descent can effectively discover the weights necessary for such computations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in "Transformers are Bayesian Networks" have several significant implications for the understanding, design, and future development of artificial intelligence systems.

#### 5.1. Foundational Understanding of Transformers

The most immediate significance is the provision of a formal, mechanistic, and unified explanation for the operation of Transformers. By proving that every sigmoid Transformer inherently implements weighted loopy belief propagation and that specific weights lead to exact Bayesian inference, the paper moves beyond empirical observations or analogies. It positions the Transformer architecture as a direct computational embodiment of established probabilistic reasoning algorithms, thereby bridging the conceptual gap between neural networks and classical graphical models.

#### 5.2. Unification of AI Paradigms

The work contributes to the ongoing effort to unify symbolic AI (Bayesian networks, logical reasoning, Turing's and Good's log-odds algebra, Pearl's sum-product algorithm) with neural network paradigms. It suggests that the architectural success of Transformers is not merely an emergent property but a rediscovery of principles long known in probabilistic inference. This synthesis could foster the development of hybrid AI systems that combine the learning capabilities of neural networks with the interpretability and verifiability of symbolic methods.

#### 5.3. Addressing Hallucination and Ensuring Verifiability

The paper redefines "hallucination" as a structural consequence of operating without a grounded concept space, rather than a fixable bug. By formally proving that verifiable inference requires a finite concept space and demonstrating how a grounded QBBN Transformer can achieve "no hallucination" on tree-structured knowledge bases, it offers a principled pathway to building AI systems that are robustly correct and whose outputs can be verified against a declared knowledge base. This has substantial implications for critical applications where reliability and trust are paramount.

#### 5.4. Implications for Mechanistic Interpretability

The constructive weight proofs provide specific, falsifiable predictions for the internal structure of Transformers performing probabilistic inference. Researchers in mechanistic interpretability can now look for `projectDim/crossProject` structures in attention heads and log-odds combining functions in FFNs of trained models. This offers a concrete target for understanding how learned weights encode algorithms, potentially accelerating the reverse-engineering of complex behaviors in large language models.

#### 5.5. Architectural and Design Insights

The findings suggest specific architectural preferences:
- **Sigmoid Activation:** The paper argues for sigmoid as the mathematically exact activation for Bayesian inference, contrasting it with ReLU/GELU as merely "compatible." This could guide future architectural choices for tasks requiring precise probabilistic reasoning.
- **Depth over Width:** The proof that two attention heads per layer are sufficient for general belief propagation (with depth scaling with reasoning complexity) challenges the intuition that more complex tasks always require more attention heads. This could lead to more efficient Transformer designs where depth, rather than width, is primarily scaled for reasoning tasks.
- **Role of Softmaxes:** Distinguishing the distinct roles of the three softmaxes (routing, inference, generation) clarifies their specific computational contributions, providing a more nuanced understanding of their function.

#### 5.6. Realization of Leibniz's Vision

The paper explicitly connects its findings to Leibniz's 17th-century vision of a "characteristica universalis" (a finite alphabet of primitive concepts) and a "calculus ratiocinator" (a mechanical procedure for deriving truths). It posits that the grounded Horn clauses serve as the concepts, belief propagation as the calculus, and the Transformer as the mechanical implementation, all supported by formal proofs of correctness and necessity. This establishes a modern, verifiable realization of a foundational philosophical idea in AI.

In summary, this research offers a fundamental shift in understanding the Transformer, moving it from an empirically successful black box to a formally characterized Bayesian network. This re-characterization opens new avenues for interpretable AI, verifiable reasoning, and a deeper integration of neural and symbolic approaches.
