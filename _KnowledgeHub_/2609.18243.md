# Acting in Meters: Learning Metric Interactions for Precise Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A two-stage plug-in framework that makes VLA and world-action-model policies reason about manipulation in meters. Interaction-Centric Tokens encode end-effector poses relative to manipulated objects and are jointly denoised with actions; a Metric Action Interaction Field then cross-attends predicted trajectories to metric point clouds to learn gated flow corrections. Across five backbones it lifts RoboTwin 2.0 success by 3.59 points on average and real-world success by 6.8 points.

## Problem

- VLAs and world-action models acting in joint space learn metric relations among end-effector, objects, and scene only implicitly, from action supervision alone.
- Geometry-aware policies add depth, point maps, foundation-model features or 3D alignment objectives, but richer perception does not ensure object-relative end-effector states are ever supervised.
- Existing spatial cues are rarely expressed in a shared metric frame with the action itself, so candidate trajectories are not grounded in surrounding geometry at a consistent physical scale.

## Method

- **Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICTs)**, adapted from ==HumanEgo==: 9D object-relative end-effector poses (translation in meters plus ==rot6d==) jointly flow-denoised with action tokens, supervised by flow loss plus a low-noise robust pose loss.
- **Metric Action Interaction Field (MAIF)**: a roughly 200M-parameter residual adapter whose action and ICT queries cross-attend to frozen ==Sonata== point-cloud tokens in robot-base-frame meters, plus temporal and bimanual attention.
- **Gated flow correction** with two-stage training: Stage 1 adds ICT to the action expert; Stage 2 freezes it and trains MAIF's ==zero-initialized==, ==(1-σ)² noise-gated== velocity corrections with flow, metric-position and ICT losses, preserving native samplers.

## Results

- RoboTwin 2.0, 50 tasks, five backbones: average gains of **2.24** to **5.94** points (mean **3.59**); Fast-WAM randomized success rises from **1.92%** to **11.48%**, and X-WAM reaches **82.01%** average.
- Real world, dual-arm Piper-H, five tasks: π0.5 improves from **69.4%** to **77.0%** and Fast-WAM from **40.8%** to **46.8%**; OOD averages rise **7.4** and **7.5** points; LIBERO gains are small (**+0.55**, **+1.05**).
- Fast-WAM ablations: full method **45.78%** versus **40.63%** for step-matched extra training, **40.36%** flow-only MAIF, **39.80-40.51%** with separate frames or rescaled clouds; ADE **0.24** versus **0.31 cm** flow-only.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adding geometry is not enough; the gain vanishes when scene points and action waypoints sit in separate frames or rescaled units, so shared metric scale is load-bearing.
> - Velocity-space flow loss alone is a weak signal for refining actions; decoding corrections into physical positions and object-relative poses gives the supervision that actually improves trajectories.
> - Metric grounding pays off most off-distribution: gains are typically larger under randomization and on precision-critical insertion and bimanual tasks, while near-saturated LIBERO leaves little headroom.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot lifts an ear of corn over a 15 cm barrier and places it in a bowl. Completing the task is only part of the test. The robot should also clear the obstacle without lifting farther than needed. Both π0.5 and π0.5 + Ours achieved 100% success on the broader barrier-crossing task. Across five trials with the same barrier, π0.5 + Ours had a lower mean peak end-effector height: 282.621 mm versus 307.151 mm. This shows a more economical trajectory in this example. It does not show higher task success, and it does not prove that the proposed mechanism alone caused the difference.

The method first teaches the policy how its end effector relates to the object it is manipulating. It then adjusts a proposed path against measured scene geometry, using a shared physical scale.

Barrier-crossing corn placement. Left: setup with 10, 15, and 20 cm barriers. Middle: the baseline trajectory crosses the 15 cm barrier at 32 cm. Right: π0.5 + Ours crosses it at 27 cm.

### 2. From joints to relationships

Policies often learn spatial relations implicitly from action supervision. A policy can perceive an object and still lack explicit supervision for the end effector's pose relative to it. The paper's analogy is human task-space reasoning: people adjust the hand relative to a cup and its surroundings rather than describing the action as a sequence of joint angles.

The first step adapts Interaction-Centric Tokens (ICTs) from [[2605.24934|HumanEgo]]. At each trajectory step, an ICT represents the end effector's position and orientation relative to the manipulated object. It is supervised from synchronized object and end-effector poses. The policy jointly denoises ICT and action tokens, so the object-relative trajectory can inform action prediction. Ground-truth object poses serve as training supervision.

The second step is MAIF. The frozen ICT-adapted policy proposes action and ICT trajectories. MAIF uses these as queries that attend to point-cloud features made from calibrated depth. The points and end-effector motion are represented in the robot-base frame, in meters, without per-scene centering or rescaling. At inference, MAIF corrects action velocities only, and the backbone's native scheduler remains in place.

Stage 1 jointly trains action and ICT streams in the action expert, with ground-truth object poses used only in training. Stage 2 freezes the adapted policy and trains MAIF. In MAIF, calibrated RGB-D becomes base-frame points, and action and ICT queries cross-attend to metric scene features. A gated action-flow correction returns to the native scheduler. At inference, only the action branch is used.

### 3. Does the added correction matter?

The main test comes from the RoboTwin 2.0 simulation benchmark. There, "Avg." is the mean of task-averaged success rates in clean and randomized scenes, with 100 episodes per setting for each of 50 tasks.

With the Fast-WAM backbone, ICT alone reached 41.25% average and 4.10% randomized success. The full method reached 45.78% average and 11.48% randomized success. Two controls fell short of the full method:

- **Flow Only:** a MAIF variant trained with only action-velocity supervision averaged 40.36%.
- **Further training:** a continuation control with 5,000 additional clean-data steps averaged 40.63%.

These results suggest that action-flow supervision alone and extra training alone do not explain the gain. They do not isolate metric geometry as the only cause. The full objective combines physical position supervision with ICT supervision, and other design choices differ from the controls.

A metric-consistency control offers supporting evidence. Variants with separate coordinate frames or altered or normalized point-cloud scales averaged between 39.80% and 40.51%.

The approach was also tested on real robots under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions: five real-world tasks with scene/object or background changes and with lighting changes. Under these perturbations, π0.5's average success rose from 50.0% to 57.4% with Ours.

### 4. The scale is useful, and conditional

The paper's contribution is to pair object-relative supervision with scene-relative correction on a shared metric scale. That pairing improved success in the tested settings. It also produced a lower trajectory in the barrier-crossing example.

The method has practical boundaries. MAIF has about 200M trainable parameters. The paper does not establish reduced computation. The method depends on useful point clouds and accurate coordinate alignment, and the reported tasks use grippers rather than dexterous hands.
