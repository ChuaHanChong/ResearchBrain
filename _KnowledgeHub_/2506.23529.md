# When Test-Time Adaptation Meets Self-Supervised Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Korea University and NAVER AI Lab researchers developed a Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA) protocol and an 'Adapt Without Source pretraining' (AWS) framework that enables effective online adaptation of self-supervised models. This approach eliminates the need for expensive labeled source pretraining, improving DINO's performance on ImageNetC by 16.2% and iBOT's by 19.9% while also achieving competitive results with supervised baselines.

## Problem

- Traditional Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) methods depend heavily on costly, label-intensive source-pretrained models, posing significant computational and data acquisition challenges.
- The computational expense of source pretraining, often overlooked, makes TTA less practical for real-world deployments.
- Existing TTA techniques struggle when directly applied to self-supervised models due to their lower initial accuracy and the unreliability of generated pseudo-labels.

## Method

- Introduced the ==Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA)== protocol, which leverages a self-supervised model and an efficient ==prototype classifier== instead of labeled source pretraining.
- Proposed 'Adapt Without Source pretraining' (AWS), a collaborative learning framework consisting of ==Contrastive Learning==, Knowledge Distillation, and Mutual Learning components.
- Designed a prototype classifier that computes class prototypes using only forward passes of the self-supervised model, allowing for efficient adaptation without backpropagation or high memory usage.

## Results

- SSTTA significantly reduces pretraining computational cost; for ImageNet, classifier construction takes **36m25s** compared to **1h8m23s x 300 epochs** for conventional pretraining.
- AWS improved DINO's error rate on ImageNetC by **16.2%** (from **6**9.2%** to 53.0%**) and iBOT's error rate by **19.9%** (from **6**8.0%** to 48.1%**), demonstrating strong adaptation performance for SSL models.
- AWS achieves state-of-the-art results even on traditionally source-pretrained models, recording a mean error of **39.4%** on ImageNet-to-ImageNetC, outperforming the previous best of **42.5%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The substantial computational cost of traditional source pretraining in TTA necessitates a shift towards label-free pretraining paradigms.
> - Self-supervised models, while powerful, require a tailored adaptation strategy due to their initial accuracy and pseudo-label characteristics that differ from supervised models.
> - A multi-component collaborative learning framework that includes robust contrastive learning, knowledge preservation through distillation, and mutual information maximization can effectively adapt self-supervised models to dynamic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable success across various computer vision tasks. However, their practical applicability is often hampered by "domain shift," where a model trained on a source data distribution performs poorly when deployed on a target distribution with different characteristics. This necessitates adaptive mechanisms to maintain performance in dynamic environments.

The broader research landscape for addressing domain shift includes several paradigms:
- **Domain Adaptation (DA):** Methods that adapt a model to a target domain, often requiring access to both source and target data during the adaptation phase.
- **Test-Time Training (TTT):** A paradigm where models are further trained at test time on unlabeled target data, often with access to source data or a source-trained proxy model.
- **Test-Time Adaptation (TTA):** An evolution of TTT, focusing on adapting a *pretrained model* to the test domain *without requiring access to the original source data*. This is crucial for privacy, memory efficiency, and real-world deployment scenarios where source data might be unavailable. Prominent TTA methods include Entropy Minimization (EM) and techniques leveraging consistency regularization (CR).
- **Continual Test-Time Adaptation (CTTA):** Extends TTA by addressing scenarios where the test distribution continuously evolves over time, requiring models to adapt sequentially and avoid catastrophic forgetting.

The current work critically examines a significant limitation within the existing TTA/CTTA landscape: the heavy reliance on a *source-pretrained model that was trained with labeled data*. As the authors highlight, this source pretraining is computationally expensive (Table 1), limits generality (a new pretraining might be needed for each target domain, Fig. 1), and assumes the availability of labeled source data, which is often not the case in real-world applications due to cost or privacy concerns.

This paper proposes a paradigm shift by integrating **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)** into Test-Time Adaptation. SSL has emerged as a powerful technique for learning robust visual representations from large amounts of *unlabeled data*, reducing the reliance on human annotations. However, directly applying existing TTA methods to SSL models is challenging because SSL models might exhibit lower initial accuracy or unstable pseudo-labels, which traditional TTA methods (like EM and CR) depend upon (Fig. 2a, 2b).

Therefore, this work positions itself at the intersection of Test-Time Adaptation and Self-Supervised Learning. It seeks to develop a new protocol and framework, **Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA)**, that can effectively leverage the generalization capabilities of SSL models while overcoming the limitations of conventional TTA approaches and the inherent challenges of adapting SSL models. By doing so, it aims to make test-time adaptation more computationally efficient, generalizable, and practical for real-world scenarios where labeled source data is scarce or unavailable.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical limitations observed in conventional Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) and Continual Test-Time Adaptation (CTTA) protocols. While TTA offers an appealing solution for adapting models to unseen target domains without source data access, it still heavily depends on a robust, pre-trained source model, which typically requires:
1. **Extensive Labeled Source Data:** Acquiring and annotating large datasets is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming.
2. **Significant Computational Cost:** Pretraining these models is resource-intensive, a cost often overlooked when evaluating TTA methods (Table 1 demonstrates this hidden cost).
3. **Lack of Generality:** A source model pre-trained for one domain might perform poorly on another, necessitating separate pretraining for each new target environment (Fig. 1).

The authors pose a simple yet profound question: "Is the computational cost of pretraining the source model negligible compared to the adaptation process for unlabeled target data in TTA?" Their analysis clearly shows this is not the case, underscoring the need for a more efficient pretraining paradigm.

Given the rise of Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) as a powerful method for learning robust representations from unlabeled data, a natural next step is to explore its potential for TTA. However, the authors empirically observe that existing TTA approaches (e.g., Entropy Minimization and Consistency Regularization) struggle when directly applied to SSL models (Fig. 2a, 2b). This struggle arises because SSL models, especially in their raw form, might have lower initial accuracy, leading to unreliable pseudo-labels and unstable entropy profiles that traditional TTA methods rely on. Low entropy in an SSL model does not necessarily guarantee correct predictions, and inaccurate pseudo-labels can propagate errors, hindering adaptation.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this paper are:
- To empirically investigate the effectiveness of existing TTA approaches when applied to self-supervised models.
- To formally introduce a **Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA) protocol** that eliminates the need for labeled source pretraining.
- To propose a novel collaborative learning framework, **Adapt Without Source pretraining (AWS)**, specifically designed to enable continuous adaptation of self-supervised models to dynamic target environments.
- To develop a *prototype classifier* that can align SSL model representations with classes using only forward passes, circumventing the need for backpropagation or large memory footprints.
- To thoroughly validate the proposed AWS framework across diverse self-supervised models (DINO, MoCo, iBOT) and standard TTA benchmarks, demonstrating its effectiveness in achieving competitive performance even without source pretraining.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this paper's methodology revolves around establishing a new protocol for Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA) and proposing a novel framework, AWS, to facilitate it.

#### 3.1. Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA) Protocol

The authors redefine the TTA protocol to remove dependency on labeled source pretraining:
- **Source Model:** Instead of a supervised `g_s ◦ f_s` trained on `(x_s, y_s)`, the SSTTA protocol utilizes a self-supervised model `f_ssl` trained on unlabeled data `x_u`. This `f_ssl` serves as the initial feature extractor.
- **Prototype Classifier:** A crucial challenge for SSL models in TTA is the absence of a task-specific classifier. The paper addresses this by proposing a *distance-based prototype classifier*, `g_ssl`. Class prototypes (`μ_c`) are computed as the mean of features for each class `c` from a (potentially small) source dataset `{X_s, Y_s}` using only *forward passes* of `f_ssl`. This avoids the computational overhead of backpropagation (as in linear probing) or large memory requirements (as in k-NN). Predictions are then made using cosine similarity between input features and these prototypes. This makes the classifier highly efficient and adaptable.
- **Target Adaptation:** Following the Continual Test-Time Adaptation (CTTA) protocol, the target model (`g_t ◦ f_t`), initialized from `g_ssl ◦ f_ssl`, adapts sequentially to an online stream of unlabeled target data `x_t` while minimizing error as the domain shifts.

#### 3.2. Adapt Without Source pretraining (AWS) Framework

The AWS framework is a collaborative learning approach designed to enable stable and effective adaptation of SSL models. It consists of three complementary components:

1. **Contrastive Learning (CL):**
 - **Purpose:** To enhance the representational capability of the target model by refining feature embeddings based on relationships between samples in the target domain.
 - **Approach:** Addresses the unreliability of pseudo-labels in low-accuracy SSL models by introducing an "approximately correct contrastive learning" method. Instead of relying on a single pseudo-label, it identifies "positive" samples as those sharing a common pseudo-label within the *top-k* predictions (`T_k`). "Negative" samples are those with no common prediction among the *top-n* predictions (`T_n`, where `n > k`). Ambiguous samples that don't fit either category are ignored. This robust strategy allows for leveraging initial classification capabilities while ensuring stability against noisy pseudo-labels. The contrastive loss (`L_cl`) maximizes similarity for positive pairs and minimizes for negative pairs.

2. **Knowledge Distillation (KD):**
 - **Purpose:** To preserve the generalization ability of the initial (frozen) self-supervised model and prevent the adapting target model from overfitting to the continually shifting target domain.
 - **Approach:** Transfers knowledge from the feature extractor of the initial SSL model (`f_ssl`) to the adapting target model (`f_t`). This is achieved through a knowledge distillation loss (`L_kd`) that aligns the *normalized feature vectors* of `f_t(x_i)` and `f_ssl(x_i)`. Aligning normalized features helps preserve the geometric structure of the feature space, ensuring stable knowledge transfer and maintaining the foundational representations learned during self-supervised pretraining.

3. **Mutual Learning (ML):**
 - **Purpose:** To collaboratively leverage the strengths of both the generalized SSL model and the domain-specific adapting target model, particularly in the presence of noisy pseudo-labels.
 - **Approach:** This component involves a mutual information maximization and a pseudo-label-based refinement strategy.
 - The *SSL model's classifier* (`g_ssl`) is refined by updating it using pseudo-labels generated by the more accurate *target model* (`g_t ◦ f_t`). This ensures `g_ssl` adapts to the target domain while `f_ssl` (the feature extractor) remains fixed to maintain generalization.
 - Additionally, the method maximizes the *mutual information* between the predicted probability distributions of the target model (`p_t`) and the SSL model (`p_ssl`). This encourages both models to learn complementary information and align their predictions, enhancing overall robustness. The mutual learning loss (`L_ml`) combines cross-entropy for `g_ssl` updates and mutual information for `f_t` updates.

#### 3.3. Total Loss Function

The overall objective function for AWS is a weighted sum of these three components:
`L_aws = L_cl + λ_kd L_kd + λ_ml L_ml`
where `λ_kd` and `λ_ml` are hyperparameters controlling the influence of knowledge distillation and mutual learning, respectively.

This holistic approach allows AWS to effectively adapt self-supervised models by refining representations, preserving generalization, and fostering collaborative learning, all without relying on expensive and label-dependent source pretraining.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation validates the efficacy of the proposed Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA) protocol and the AWS framework across various benchmarks and SSL models.

#### 4.1. Computational Efficiency Validation

- Table 1 vividly illustrates the significant computational savings of SSTTA. Conventional source pretraining takes "1h8m23s × 300 epochs" for ImageNet and "9m7s × 200 epochs" for CIFAR100. In contrast, the "SSL w/ Prototype" approach for classifier construction takes only "36m25s" for ImageNet and "1m25s" for CIFAR100 (for full-shot setup), demonstrating a drastic reduction in pretraining cost.

#### 4.2. Empirical Failure of Conventional TTA on SSL Models

- Figures 2a and 2b provide critical insights into why existing TTA methods struggle with SSL models.
 - **Entropy Minimization (EM):** SSL models, compared to source-pretrained ones, often exhibit higher loss at the same entropy level, meaning low entropy does not reliably indicate correct predictions. They can even decrease entropy for incorrect predictions, increasing "true risk."
 - **Consistency Regularization (CR):** SSL models tend to generate pseudo-labels with lower accuracy. Since CR methods rely on these pseudo-labels for consistency, their inaccuracy hinders effective performance improvement.

#### 4.3. Superior Performance of AWS

- **On Source Pretrained Models (Tables 3 & 4):** Even when applied to traditionally source-pretrained models, AWS achieves state-of-the-art results. On ImageNet-to-ImageNetC, AWS records a mean error of 39.4%, surpassing the previous best (Continual-MAE at 42.5%) by a substantial margin. Similar improvements are observed on CIFAR10C (10.8% error vs. 12.6% for Continual-MAE) and CIFAR100C (20.4% error vs. 26.4% for Continual-MAE). This demonstrates the robustness and general applicability of the AWS framework beyond the self-supervised setting.
- **On Self-Supervised Models (DINO, MoCo, iBOT - Tables 3 & 4):** This is the core contribution. While conventional TTA methods show minimal or even negative gains for SSL models, AWS achieves significant performance improvements:
 - For **DINO**, AWS improves by 16.2% on ImageNetC (from 69.2% to 53.0% error), 17.5% on CIFAR10C (from 44.3% to 26.8% error), and 13.5% on CIFAR100C (from 64.1% to 50.6% error). Notably, the adapted DINO model (53.0%) even outperforms the *source-pretrained baseline* (55.8%) on ImageNetC after adaptation.
 - For **MoCo**, AWS provides gains of 7.0% on ImageNetC (from 76.5% to 69.5% error), 1.5% on CIFAR10C (from 42.2% to 40.7% error), and 2.1% on CIFAR100C (from 64.2% to 62.1% error).
 - For **iBOT**, AWS shows impressive gains of 19.9% on ImageNetC (from 68.0% to 48.1% error), 17.9% on CIFAR10C (from 48.0% to 30.1% error), and 15.4% on CIFAR100C (from 65.6% to 50.2% error).
These results firmly establish AWS as an effective method for adapting SSL models, making them competitive with or superior to source-pretrained models in TTA scenarios.

#### 4.4. Few-Shot Classifier Evaluation (AWS-FS)

- The `AWS-FS` variant, using only 30 samples per class to construct the prototype classifier, still yields substantial improvements (e.g., 14.8% gain for DINO on ImageNetC). This highlights the efficiency and robustness of the prototype classifier, demonstrating its viability even with very limited labeled source data. Appendix B further confirms that performance remains close to full-shot with a small number of samples.

#### 4.5. Feature Visualization and Hyperparameter Analysis

- **t-SNE visualization (Fig. 5):** Shows that AWS leads to improved decision boundaries and better separation of classes in the feature space for both source-pretrained and SSL models, contrasting with more conservative existing TTA methods that largely preserve initial representations.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity (Table 5):** The ablation studies demonstrate that AWS is relatively insensitive to the choice of hyperparameters (`k, n, λ_kd, λ_ml`), indicating its robustness and ease of deployment.

#### 4.6. Domain Generalization and Component Effectiveness

- **Domain Generalization (Table 6):** AWS improves performance on *unseen* corruption types, suggesting that the adaptation process not only enhances performance on observed shifts but also imbues the model with more generalized knowledge.
- **Ablation Study (Table 7):** Each of the three components—Contrastive Learning (CL), Knowledge Distillation (KD), and Mutual Learning (ML)—contributes positively to the overall performance, with the full combination yielding the best results. CL provides initial strong gains, KD helps mitigate forgetting, and ML further refines adaptation through collaborative learning.

#### 4.7. Natural Domain Shift Scenarios

- Appendix C, Table 8 further extends the validation to natural domain shift datasets (ImageNet-R/V2/Sketch), where AWS consistently outperforms baselines and other TTA methods, showcasing its effectiveness in real-world scenarios.

In summary, the main findings conclusively show that conventional TTA methods are ill-suited for SSL models, but the proposed AWS framework successfully bridges this gap. It enables SSL models to achieve competitive, and often superior, adaptation performance compared to supervised baselines, all while eliminating the heavy burden of labeled source pretraining.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper presents a highly significant advancement in the field of Test-Time Adaptation and its intersection with Self-Supervised Learning, with substantial potential impact across both academic research and practical applications.

1. **Addressing a Critical Bottleneck in TTA:** The most immediate impact is the successful decoupling of TTA from expensive, label-dependent source pretraining. Historically, the hidden cost and impracticality of preparing a new labeled source model for every potential target domain limited the scalability and universality of TTA. By validating the "Self-Supervised Test-Time Adaptation (SSTTA)" protocol, the authors effectively remove this bottleneck, making TTA a more viable and attractive solution for real-world deployment.

2. **Unlocking the Potential of Self-Supervised Models:** The work demonstrates that the powerful, generalizable representations learned by modern self-supervised models (like DINO, MoCo, iBOT) can indeed be leveraged for robust online adaptation, even when their initial classification accuracy is lower than supervised counterparts. This bridges two critical areas of AI research, showing how SSL can extend its utility beyond initial representation learning to dynamic adaptation challenges.

3. **Novel and Robust Framework (AWS):** The proposed Adapt Without Source pretraining (AWS) framework, comprising contrastive learning, knowledge distillation, and mutual learning, is a robust and principled approach. Its ability to achieve state-of-the-art performance even in conventional supervised TTA settings, alongside its exceptional gains in the self-supervised context, highlights its versatility and effectiveness. The inclusion of a highly efficient prototype classifier further enhances its practical appeal.

4. **Enhanced Model Robustness and Generalization:** The improved performance of AWS on various corruption types, including unseen ones, indicates that the framework fosters models that are not only adaptable but also more robust to diverse distribution shifts. This contributes directly to building more reliable and trustworthy AI systems in unpredictable real-world environments.

5. **Democratization of Adaptive AI:** By reducing the reliance on vast labeled datasets, this work lowers the barrier to entry for developing and deploying adaptive AI systems. This is particularly impactful for resource-constrained organizations, emerging applications, or domains where data annotation is exceptionally challenging (e.g., rare diseases in medical imaging, specialized industrial tasks).

6. **Catalyst for Future Research:** This paper lays a strong foundation for future research in several directions:
 - Further optimization of SSL pretraining specifically for adaptation tasks.
 - Development of more sophisticated "approximately correct" pseudo-labeling strategies robust to very low initial accuracy.
 - Exploration of the interplay between different SSL architectures and TTA components.
 - Application of SSTTA to other modalities beyond computer vision and to more complex tasks like object detection or segmentation under domain shift.
 - Investigating theoretical guarantees for the stability and convergence of SSTTA.

In conclusion, "When Test-Time Adaptation Meets Self-Supervised Models" represents a significant step forward in making adaptive deep learning more practical, efficient, and widely accessible. By pioneering a self-supervised approach to test-time adaptation, the authors pave the way for a new generation of AI models that can autonomously and continuously learn and adapt in dynamic, label-scarce environments, promising profound implications for the deployment of intelligent systems across various industries.
