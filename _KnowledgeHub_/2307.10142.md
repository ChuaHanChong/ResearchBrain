# Benchmarking Potential Based Rewards for Learning Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> This research empirically evaluates Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) against Direct Reward Shaping (DRS) in learning dynamic locomotion for an MIT Humanoid robot. The study demonstrates that PBRS offers significantly greater robustness to reward weight tuning and enhances training stability, achieving comparable or slightly improved performance over DRS.

## Problem

- Designing and tuning effective reward functions for reinforcement learning (RL) in complex robotic systems is a time-consuming and challenging task.
- Standard reward shaping (DRS) can inadvertently alter the optimal policy, potentially leading to undesired behaviors or overfitting to the shaping terms.
- The practical benefits and robustness of theoretically policy-invariant Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) have not been extensively validated in high-dimensional, continuous control tasks like humanoid locomotion.

## Method

- An empirical case study compared PBRS and DRS for learning running locomotion with an 18-degree-of-freedom ==MIT Humanoid== robot in a 3D simulation environment.
- Identical shaping terms (orientation, height, joint regularization) were applied in both DRS and PBRS formulations, alongside baseline task rewards, using the PPO algorithm on NVIDIA IsaacGym.
- A sensitivity analysis was conducted by sweeping individual shaping reward weights across a wide range to assess the robustness of each shaping method.

## Results

- PBRS policies showed slightly faster convergence and marginally higher final baseline rewards, while demonstrating substantially lower variance across independent training runs (**0.946** for PBRS vs. **1.905** for DRS).
- Both DRS and PBRS successfully mitigated undesirable emergent gaits (e.g., inward-turned legs) observed with baseline-only rewards, leading to natural-looking locomotion.
- The sensitivity analysis revealed that PBRS maintained high-quality policies across a 100-fold range of shaping weights, whereas DRS frequently resulted in overfitting or poor performance when weights were varied from nominal values.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - PBRS provides substantially greater robustness to reward weight scaling compared to DRS, simplifying the reward design and tuning process in complex robotic applications.
> - PBRS leads to significantly lower variance across multiple training runs, indicating enhanced learning stability and reliability in high-dimensional continuous control.
> - Setting the discount factor to 1 within the PBRS potential function calculation was crucial for stable and faster convergence, pragmatically improving performance despite a theoretical deviation from strict policy invariance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of effective reinforcement learning (RL) agents, particularly in complex domains such as robotics, frequently encounters challenges in the design and tuning of reward functions. Reward functions serve as the primary signal guiding an agent's learning process. In robotics, naive reward formulations can lead to slow convergence, local optima, or behaviors that diverge from the engineer's intent. To address these issues, reward shaping, which involves adding supplementary reward terms to provide more informative signals, has become a common practice. However, standard reward shaping (referred to as Direct Reward Shaping, DRS, in this paper) can fundamentally alter the underlying Markov Decision Process (MDP), potentially leading to unintended behaviors or policies that overfit to the shaping terms rather than the primary task. Furthermore, tuning these shaping rewards is an iterative, labor-intensive process sensitive to hyperparameters and weights.

A theoretically distinct approach to reward shaping is Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS), introduced by Ng, Harada, and Russell in 1999. PBRS offers a theoretical guarantee of policy invariance, meaning that, under ideal conditions, it should accelerate learning convergence without altering the optimal policy of the original MDP. This property makes PBRS attractive, as it could decouple the process of defining desired behavior from the process of guiding learning. While prior research has explored PBRS, often demonstrating accelerated convergence, these studies have predominantly been confined to simpler environments like grid-worlds or low-dimensional control systems (e.g., cartpole). In contrast, most successful applications of RL in high-dimensional robotics have continued to rely on standard, direct reward shaping methods.

This paper addresses this gap by empirically evaluating the efficacy of PBRS in a high-dimensional, continuous control task: learning dynamic locomotion for a humanoid robot. By systematically comparing PBRS with standard DRS in such a complex setting, the authors aim to provide insights into its practical benefits and limitations, particularly regarding convergence speed and the robustness of reward tuning, areas where prior research in robotics has been limited.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are outlined as follows:

1. **Address the Challenge of Reward Function Design and Tuning in Robotics RL:** The authors emphasize that designing effective reward functions is a significant bottleneck in RL pipelines for optimal control, both in model-based and model-free methods. Simple, task-based rewards often lead to slow convergence or undesired behaviors. Reward shaping is a common solution, but its implementation is typically an iterative, time-consuming process of tuning weights to balance convergence speed with the fidelity of the desired behavior. The sensitivity of the training process to these hyperparameters obscures the true impact of individual reward terms.

2. **Empirically Benchmark PBRS Against Standard Reward Shaping in a High-Dimensional Robotics Task:** While PBRS theoretically offers policy invariance and has shown promise in simpler environments, its practical application and benefits in complex, continuous, and high-dimensional robotic systems, such as humanoid locomotion, have not been extensively studied or confirmed. The paper aims to fill this empirical gap by systematically comparing PBRS with standard DRS for a humanoid robot learning to run in a 3D simulation.

3. **Evaluate the Impact of PBRS on Convergence Speed:** Previous studies in grid-worlds and low-dimensional systems often reported that PBRS significantly accelerates learning convergence. A key objective is to determine if this benefit translates to high-dimensional robotic control.

4. **Assess the Robustness and Ease of Tuning of PBRS:** The theoretical property of policy invariance in PBRS suggests it might be less sensitive to the specific scaling or weighting of shaping terms compared to DRS, which can fundamentally alter the optimal policy. The authors hypothesize that PBRS terms might be more robust to tuning, thereby simplifying the reward design process. This robustness would represent a practical advantage for engineers and researchers.

5. **Provide Practical Guidance for Reward Shaping in Robotics RL:** By conducting a rigorous empirical case study, the authors intend to offer practical recommendations on whether and how to utilize PBRS in real-world robotics applications, especially given the common reliance on direct reward shaping in the field. They seek to understand if the theoretical advantages of PBRS manifest as practical benefits in terms of training stability, policy quality, and ease of deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an empirical case study approach to compare Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) and Direct Reward Shaping (DRS) in the context of learning humanoid locomotion via deep reinforcement learning.

**A. Robotic System and Task**
The target robotic platform is the MIT Humanoid, an 18-degree-of-freedom (DoF) bipedal robot. For this study, the arm joints were fixed, reducing the controllable degrees of freedom to the 10 joints of the legs. The task is to learn running locomotion, encompassing forward velocity tracking and yaw rate tracking.

**B. Control Architecture and Observations**
The policy network is a single neural network that outputs joint position targets (a ∈ R^10^) to the robot's leg joints. These targets are translated into torques using a proportional-derivative (PD) control law: $\tau = K_p(a - q) + K_d(\dot{q})$, with fixed gains $K_p = 30$ Nm/rad and $K_d = 5$ Nms/rad.

The agent's observations (s ∈ R^38^) are listed in Table I and include:
- Joint positions and velocities (10 each)
- Body height (1)
- Body linear velocity (3)
- Body angular velocity (3)
- Body frame gravity vector (3)
- Binary foot contact state (2)
- Commanded velocities (forward, lateral, yaw) (3)
- Clock phase (3, sinusoidal components to encourage periodicity)

Uniformly sampled noise is applied to most observations to enhance robustness.

**C. Reward Functions**
The reward structure is composed of baseline rewards and shaping rewards, applied in three distinct cases:

1. **Baseline Rewards Only:** A minimal set of rewards designed for general locomotion (Table II, "Baseline" section). These include:
 - **Task-related:** Linear and angular velocity tracking (exponentially decaying with error).
 - **Regularization:** First and second-order action rates (to encourage smoothness), torques (for efficiency), torque limits, and joint limits (to avoid hardware constraints).
 - **Termination Penalty:** A large negative reward if the robot's velocity or angular velocity exceeds limits, gravity vector indicates falling, or self-collision occurs.

2. **Direct Reward Shaping (DRS):** The baseline rewards are augmented with additional shaping terms (Table II, "Direct Shaping" section). These shaping terms are chosen for their common use in humanoid locomotion literature:
 - **Orientation (R_ori):** Rewards maintaining an upright body orientation (exponentially decaying with deviation from zero gravity components in body x,y).
 - **Height (R_h):** Rewards maintaining a desired body height (exponentially decaying with deviation from 0.6 m).
 - **Joint Regularization (R_j):** Rewards symmetrical abduction/adduction and pitch joints, and zero yaw joints.
 These DRS terms are added directly to the total reward function: $\hat{r}(s_k, a_k, s_{k+1}) = r(s_k, a_k, s_{k+1}) + R(s_k)$.

3. **Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS):** The same three shaping terms (Orientation, Height, Joint Regularization) are reformulated into their potential-based form (Table II, "Potential Shaping" section) and added to the baseline rewards. A PBRS term is calculated as $P_s(s_k, s_{k+1}) = \gamma R_s(s_{k+1}) - R_s(s_k)$, where $R_s$ is the scalar shaping reward function. This formulation measures the change in the potential function value between consecutive states, discounted by $\gamma$.

**D. Implementation Details and Training Setup**
- **Simulation Environment:** NVIDIA IsaacGym, leveraging its massively parallel simulation capabilities.
- **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO-Clip).
- **Hyperparameters:** Standard PPO hyperparameters (Table III), with a discount factor $\gamma = 0.99$ for the PPO algorithm itself.
- **Hardware:** Intel i9-10850K CPU and NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU.
- **Simulation/Control Frequency:** Simulation runs at 1000 Hz, control at 100 Hz.
- **Training Runs:** Each training run involves 4096 parallel agents and lasts for 1000 policy iterations, converging within approximately 30 minutes of wall-clock time. Ten independent runs are conducted for each case (baseline, DRS, PBRS) with nominal weights to assess consistency.
- **Reward Weight Tuning:** Baseline reward weights are tuned first for reasonable performance and then fixed. DRS and PBRS shaping reward weights are then tuned until acceptable locomotion is achieved.
- **Comparison Metric:** Policies are primarily compared based on the accumulated *baseline* rewards, to ensure that the comparison is not biased by the potentially different magnitudes of the shaping reward terms themselves.
- **Sensitivity Analysis:** To assess robustness, the authors sweep the weights of individual shaping rewards (for both DRS and PBRS) from 0.1x to 10x their nominal values, while keeping other reward weights fixed.

**E. Modification to PBRS Discounting**
A notable modification to the standard PBRS formulation is made: the discount factor $\gamma$ within the PBRS calculation $P(s_k, s_{k+1}) = \gamma\Phi(s_{k+1}) - \Phi(s_k)$ is set to $\gamma=1$. The authors state this decision was made due to observed learning instability with a discounted potential function, acknowledging that this technically sacrifices the theoretical policy invariance property of PBRS, but significantly improves training stability and speed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings regarding the performance and characteristics of PBRS compared to DRS in humanoid locomotion:

1. **Convergence Speed and Stability:**
 - All three cases (baseline, DRS, PBRS) converged to comparable performance levels within 1000 iterations.
 - PBRS exhibited a slightly faster convergence and achieved a marginally higher final return in terms of baseline rewards, as shown in Fig. 3.
 - A more significant finding was the substantially lower variance across ten independent training runs for PBRS (0.946) compared to DRS (1.905) and baseline rewards only (2.025). This indicates greater training stability and reliability for PBRS.

2. **Policy Quality and Behavior:**
 - Baseline-only policies tended to develop abnormal gaits, such as inward-turned legs, likely for mediolateral stability. This behavior persisted and was detrimental during high-velocity turns.
 - Both DRS and PBRS successfully rectified these undesirable gaits by regularizing the yaw joints around zero.
 - **Reward Distribution Analysis (Fig. 4):** During training, DRS terms were quickly maximized by the agent, indicating a strong prioritization of these shaping rewards. This suggests that the agent might overfit to the shaping terms, potentially conflicting with maximizing the baseline task reward. In contrast, PBRS terms remained centered around zero with a relatively wide distribution throughout training. This observation empirically supports the theoretical policy invariance property of PBRS, where its influence on the optimizer decreases as training progresses, allowing the agent to focus on baseline rewards.
 - **Bias from Shaping Rewards:** While DRS policies achieved an average body height of 0.596 m (close to the desired 0.60 m), PBRS policies resulted in an average height of 0.639 m. This difference of almost 5% of the robot's total height suggests that DRS terms can bias the policy more strongly towards specific states dictated by the shaping function, even if it might slightly conflict with other task objectives, whereas PBRS allows for more flexibility.
 - **Emergent Behaviors:** The trained policies, regardless of the shaping method, exhibited natural heel-toe transitions, a behavior not explicitly specified in the reward functions.

3. **Command Tracking Performance (Fig. 5):**
 - No significant difference in average linear or angular velocity tracking performance was observed between DRS and PBRS policies at commanded limits.
 - The baseline policy frequently terminated during turns, contributing to a large standard deviation in angular velocity tracking.

4. **Robustness to Reward Weight Scaling (Sensitivity Analysis) (Fig. 6):**
 - This was identified as the main advantage of PBRS. Learning with PBRS terms was substantially more consistent and robust across a wide range of weights (from 0.1x to 10x the nominal value) and for individual shaping rewards (height, orientation, joint regularization).
 - When DRS term weights were significantly increased, the policy often overfit to the specified shaping reward, leading to a substantial sacrifice in baseline reward performance. For example, over-weighting the upright orientation reward (R_ori) with DRS was detrimental, as it conflicted with the natural banking and oscillations required for running motions.
 - PBRS allowed for a much larger range of acceptable weights that still produced desirable behavior, significantly simplifying the tuning process for reward shaping functions.

5. **Impact of PBRS Discount Factor:**
 - Empirically, setting the discount factor $\gamma=1$ within the PBRS potential function calculation (i.e., $P(s_k, s_{k+1}) = R_s(s_{k+1}) - R_s(s_k)$) was found to be crucial for stable and faster convergence. While this technically deviates from the theoretical policy invariance guarantee of PBRS (which assumes $\gamma < 1$), the practical benefits in terms of stability outweighed this theoretical compromise. This finding aligns with observations from other studies regarding the discount factor's effect on potential-based rewards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning in robotics by providing an empirical validation of Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) in a complex, high-dimensional continuous control task – humanoid locomotion. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Practical Guidance for Reward Design in Robotics:** The most significant finding is that PBRS terms, when applied to a legged robot, offer considerable robustness to reward weight tuning compared to direct reward shaping (DRS). This directly addresses a major practical challenge in robotics RL: the time-consuming and often unpredictable process of iterating on reward weights. By simplifying the tuning process, PBRS can accelerate research and development cycles for robotic control policies.

2. **Increased Reliability of Training:** The observation of lower variance across multiple training runs for PBRS indicates greater stability and reliability of the learning process. This is valuable in experimental settings where consistent policy convergence is desired, reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter searches or multiple retraining attempts.

3. **Empirical Support for PBRS in High-Dimensional Systems:** Prior work on PBRS primarily focused on simpler, low-dimensional tasks. This study demonstrates that the benefits of PBRS, particularly its robustness, extend to complex, high-dimensional, continuous control problems characteristic of realistic robotics. This encourages broader adoption and further investigation of PBRS within the robotics community.

4. **Mitigation of Overfitting to Shaping Rewards:** The analysis of reward distributions revealed that PBRS terms remain centered around zero, suggesting they primarily guide the learning process without significantly biasing the final policy towards maximizing the shaping rewards themselves. This contrasts with DRS, where agents tended to strongly prioritize shaping rewards, potentially leading to suboptimal task performance if shaping rewards conflict with baseline objectives. This property helps maintain the fidelity of the desired behavior as defined by the core task rewards.

5. **Implications for Reward Function Formulation:** The authors advocate for using the PBRS form for any shaping rewards that can be trivially converted from $R(s)$ to $\gamma\Phi(s_{k+1}) - \Phi(s_k)$, given the observed advantages. This offers a straightforward methodological improvement for practitioners.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper also opens avenues for future research, particularly concerning the interaction between PBRS and control timescales in hierarchical RL. The idea of automatically assigning rewards to different hierarchy levels based on their relative scaling, or using value functions from simplified world models as PBRS terms, offers promising pathways for more sophisticated and efficient learning architectures in robotics.

In essence, this research provides a strong empirical case for the practical utility of PBRS in complex robotic systems, shifting the perceived main benefit from solely accelerating convergence to significantly enhancing the robustness and ease of tuning reward functions, thereby addressing a critical bottleneck in the application of reinforcement learning to real-world robotics.
