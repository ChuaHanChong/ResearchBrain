# Benchmarking Potential Based Rewards for Learning Humanoid Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> This research empirically evaluates Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) against Direct Reward Shaping (DRS) in learning dynamic locomotion for an MIT Humanoid robot. The study demonstrates that PBRS offers significantly greater robustness to reward weight tuning and enhances training stability, achieving comparable or slightly improved performance over DRS.

## Problem

- Designing and tuning effective reward functions for reinforcement learning (RL) in complex robotic systems is a time-consuming and challenging task.
- Standard reward shaping (DRS) can inadvertently alter the optimal policy, potentially leading to undesired behaviors or overfitting to the shaping terms.
- The practical benefits and robustness of theoretically policy-invariant Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) have not been extensively validated in high-dimensional, continuous control tasks like humanoid locomotion.

## Method

- An empirical case study compared PBRS and DRS for learning running locomotion with an 18-degree-of-freedom ==MIT Humanoid== robot in a 3D simulation environment.
- Identical shaping terms (orientation, height, joint regularization) were applied in both DRS and PBRS formulations, alongside baseline task rewards, using the PPO algorithm on NVIDIA IsaacGym.
- A sensitivity analysis was conducted by sweeping individual shaping reward weights across a wide range to assess the robustness of each shaping method.

## Results

- PBRS policies showed slightly faster convergence and marginally higher final baseline rewards, while demonstrating substantially lower variance across independent training runs (**0.946** for PBRS vs. **1.905** for DRS).
- Both DRS and PBRS successfully mitigated undesirable emergent gaits (e.g., inward-turned legs) observed with baseline-only rewards, leading to natural-looking locomotion.
- The sensitivity analysis revealed that PBRS maintained high-quality policies across a 100-fold range of shaping weights, whereas DRS frequently resulted in overfitting or poor performance when weights were varied from nominal values.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - PBRS provides substantially greater robustness to reward weight scaling compared to DRS, simplifying the reward design and tuning process in complex robotic applications.
> - PBRS leads to significantly lower variance across multiple training runs, indicating enhanced learning stability and reliability in high-dimensional continuous control.
> - Setting the discount factor to 1 within the PBRS potential function calculation was crucial for stable and faster convergence, pragmatically improving performance despite a theoretical deviation from strict policy invariance.
