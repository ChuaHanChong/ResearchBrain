# SplineWAM: Adaptive Action Horizons for World Action Models via B-Spline Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> SplineWAM replaces a world action model's fixed-length action chunk with a fixed-size window of cubic B-spline knot times and control points, fitted with adaptive knots so resolution and horizon follow motion complexity. Video supervision is aligned to knot times, and Jacobian-pullback real-time chunking enables asynchronous execution. It gains 8.2 and 4.4 success points on LIBERO-Plus and RoboCasa with 22-26% fewer policy calls.

## Problem

- Nearly all world action models inherit fixed-length uniform action chunks, coupling parameter count, executed control steps, and temporal resolution that need not be tied together.
- Fixed chunks waste inference on smooth free-space motion yet replan too slowly during contact-rich grasping and insertion, bottlenecking throughput when large video-backbone policies are cloud-served.
- Prior spline tokenizers like BEAST fix each block's duration, and real-time chunking constrains generated variables directly, which fails when actions decode nonlinearly from spline parameters.

## Method

- ==B-spline action window==: the action branch predicts a fixed number of knot times and control points, fitted with ==FITPACK-style error-driven knot insertion== under a single tolerance epsilon.
- ==Knot-grid video-action alignment==: video targets are sampled at the demonstration's fitted knot times rather than a uniform grid, concentrating world-model supervision where trajectories are complex.
- **Jacobian-Pullback Real-Time Chunking (JP-RTC)**: continuity residuals are formed on decoded actions and pulled back through the decoder's regularized Jacobian, strictly generalizing RTC for asynchronous deployment.

## Results

- On a shared Fast-WAM backbone, success rises from **66.3 to 74.5** on LIBERO-Plus and **69.1 to 73.5** on RoboCasa, with calls per episode dropping **33.6 to 26.2** and **21.9 to 16.3**.
- Fixed-duration BEAST splines fall **4.1 points** below baseline on RoboCasa; knot-grid alignment beats time-window alignment by **1.2 and 1.6 points**; removing video costs **10.7 and 14.5 points**.
- Matching baseline best accuracy needs **2.2-2.5x** fewer calls; on three real bimanual tasks JP-RTC leads or matches chunking while decoding **1.2-1.6x** more motion per call, whereas naive RTC degrades.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Letting the action representation carry its own horizon turns replanning frequency into a model prediction, yielding cheaper inference without auxiliary entropy or visual-discrepancy triggers.
> - Spline gains come from adaptive duration and smooth parameterization, not compression itself; overly loose fitting tolerance discards contact knots and drops performance below fixed chunking.
> - When outputs decode nonlinearly into executed actions, impose consistency constraints in execution space and pull them back through the decoder Jacobian, not on raw parameters.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

World action models (WAMs) predict both future video and robot actions. Their usual fixed-length action chunks apply one temporal rhythm to every phase of a task. During smooth travel, that rhythm creates many short replanning intervals. During precise contact, it can leave too few opportunities to adjust. Each call runs a large model, so unnecessary calls consume inference capacity. The authors frame this as a potential cloud-fleet throughput bottleneck; it is not a throughput result they directly measure.

SplineWAM keeps the amount the model must predict fixed. That prediction can describe a longer span when motion is smooth and a shorter, finer-grained span when motion is difficult. In synchronous simulation, this gave higher success with fewer policy calls per episode.

### 2. Let the trajectory set the clock

SplineWAM represents a trajectory with a cubic B-spline, a smooth curve described by knot times and control points. The predicted knot times set both the action resolution and when to call the policy again.

Training targets come from demonstrations. The demonstration actions are normalized and fitted with a cubic spline. A knot is inserted where the reconstruction error is largest. Insertion stops when the largest elementwise error falls below a chosen tolerance. Smooth stretches therefore need fewer knots, while hard-to-approximate stretches attract more. The authors associate dense knots with complex or contact-rich motion. Placement, however, is driven by the fitted error, not by a semantic contact detector.

At run time, the policy predicts a fixed-size window of knot times and control points. The decoder then samples executable actions at control steps. The knot times determine the temporal span, so the same parameter budget can yield a longer or shorter action horizon.

Video supervision follows the same clock. Future frames are supervised at fitted knot times instead of on a uniform time grid. The comparison uses the same number of frames and the same subsampling ratio. Under those conditions, knot-grid alignment exceeds time-window alignment by 1.2 points on LIBERO-Plus and 1.6 on RoboCasa.

### 3. Fewer calls in simulation

Results at the benchmarks' default settings:

- **LIBERO-Plus:** SplineWAM-Knot reaches 74.5% versus 66.3% episode-weighted success. It makes 26.2 versus 33.6 policy calls per episode.
- **RoboCasa:** SplineWAM-Knot reaches 73.5% versus 69.1% success. It makes 16.3 versus 21.9 calls per episode.

LIBERO-Plus is a zero-shot evaluation, under seven perturbation factors, of models trained on LIBERO. RoboCasa evaluates 24 kitchen tasks.

Calls/episode counts model invocations. It does not measure elapsed time, generated tokens, or attempts. The comparisons are synchronous and use each suite's default tolerance. Both methods share a Fast-WAM backbone, demonstrations, and training budget. All results are single-run point estimates.

The fitting tolerance also matters. Loose fitting that removes contact-resolving knots sharply reduces accuracy.

### 4. Efficiency has a precision price

On the robot, execution is asynchronous. The robot keeps moving on an existing plan while a new prediction is computed, so the arriving prediction must agree with actions already committed. Spline parameters decode nonlinearly into overlapping actions. To handle this, a method called JP-RTC forms its continuity residual on the decoded actions. It then uses the decoder's local Jacobian to pull a correction back into parameter space.

The robot evaluation covered three bimanual real-robot tasks. SplineWAM with JP-RTC leads or matches the fixed-chunk baseline on five of six success/progress measures. It executes 1.2–1.6 times as many control steps per call. This means more executed motion per call; it does not demonstrate a wall-clock speedup.

- **Clean Whiteboard:** success is 60.0% versus 50.0%.
- **Charge Earphone:** this precision-focused task ties at 26.7% success, but progress is 57.5% versus the baseline's 73.3%.

Each method ran 30 trials per task. The result is not evidence that spline actions are equally reliable in all contact-heavy tasks.

### 5. A horizon the policy predicts

In SplineWAM, one predicted timing structure guides action resolution, video supervision, and the next policy call. The commitment can be shorter for difficult motion and longer for smooth motion. The evidence covers fewer calls in simulation and more steps per call on a robot. It does not cover measured fleet throughput or uniform reliability across contact-heavy tasks.
