# Causal Confusion in Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper identifies and addresses 'causal misidentification' in imitation learning, a problem where policies learn non-causal correlations from demonstrations that lead to failures when deployed. It proposes a targeted interventional framework that combines graph-parameterized policy learning with efficient expert queries or policy execution to identify and rely on true causal factors, resulting in significantly improved robustness and sample efficiency.

## Problem

- Standard behavioral cloning (BC) learns policies by mapping observations to actions, often picking up non-causal correlations (effects or confounders) from demonstration data.
- This reliance on non-causal correlations becomes problematic in imitation learning due to distributional shift, as these correlations may not hold in new states encountered by the learned policy, leading to failures.
- A counter-intuitive symptom is that access to more seemingly relevant information in the state can lead to worse performance if it is a non-causal correlate (e.g., brake light as a cause for braking).

## Method

- Proposes a two-step pipeline: first, training a single neural network as a ==graph-parameterized policy== capable of representing multiple ==causal graph== hypotheses.
- Second, performing ==targeted interventions== (via efficient ==expert queries== or direct ==policy execution== in the environment) to identify the true causal model by evaluating each graph hypothesis.
- Utilizes ==disentangled latent representations== of observations (e.g., from a ==β-VAE==) to enable meaningful masking and identification of individual causal state variables.

## Results

- Empirically demonstrated that policies trained with non-causal information achieved low training error but yielded significantly lower rewards when deployed, validating the existence and severity of causal misidentification.
- The proposed intervention framework, using both policy execution and expert queries, significantly improved rewards on various control tasks, often closing the performance gap with original, non-confounded baselines.
- Achieved high performance with substantially fewer intervention episodes/queries compared to DAgger or GAIL, and successfully recovered the true causal graph for tasks like MountainCar and Hopper, and disentangled causal factors in Atari games.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Behavioral cloning's discriminative nature makes it susceptible to learning non-causal correlations (causal misidentification) from expert demonstrations.
> - This causal misidentification, exacerbated by distributional shift during policy deployment, leads to compounding errors and poor performance.
> - Targeted active interventions, where the agent interacts with the environment or queries an expert, can efficiently identify the true causal factors needed for robust imitation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical and often overlooked challenge in **Imitation Learning (IL)**, a field focused on teaching agents to perform tasks by observing expert demonstrations. Behavioral cloning (BC), a foundational IL technique, reduces policy learning to a supervised learning problem, training a discriminative model to predict expert actions from observations.

The core problem in IL, widely acknowledged since its early days, is **distributional shift** (or "covariate shift"). This occurs because the learned policy, when deployed, inevitably encounters states slightly different from those seen in the expert demonstrations, leading to compounding errors. Prior work, such as DAgger, has attempted to mitigate this by iteratively querying the expert for labels on states encountered by the learner's evolving policy.

This paper makes a significant contribution by identifying a specific, insidious form of distributional shift: **"causal misidentification."** It argues that the discriminative nature of behavioral cloning, which is unaware of the underlying causal structure of the expert's interaction with the environment, makes it susceptible to learning non-causal correlations. In standard supervised learning, if training and test distributions are identical, such correlations might not be problematic. However, in IL, the agent's actions *change* the state distribution, breaking these non-causal correlations and leading to catastrophic failures. The paper highlights that this is exacerbated when the state observations include information that is an *effect* of the expert's action (e.g., a brake light indicator) or a *confounder* (e.g., historical actions).

By bringing the principles of **Causal Inference**—specifically, the concepts of causal graphs, interventions, and the do-calculus—into the domain of imitation learning, this work offers a novel perspective and solution. While causal inference has typically focused on discovering relationships from observational data or through controlled experiments in static settings, this paper adapts interventional causal discovery to the dynamic, sequential decision-making context of IL. This is a relatively underexplored intersection, as previous work observing similar symptoms (e.g., recurrent policies hurting performance in driving) did not explicitly attribute them to causal misidentification or propose causally-motivated solutions.

Furthermore, the paper subtly bridges IL with **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** by proposing policy execution as an intervention mechanism. Instead of relying solely on expert queries, the agent can interact with the environment and learn from environmental rewards, demonstrating a pragmatic blend of IL and RL principles to solve a causal problem. This distinguishes it from pure imitation learning methods like GAIL, which also interact with the environment but through an adversarial training scheme rather than targeted causal discovery. In essence, this research pushes the boundaries of robust imitation learning by grounding it in causal principles, offering a more fundamental understanding of why certain IL approaches fail and providing a principled way to overcome these failures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this paper is to identify, analyze, and propose a solution for a novel and highly problematic phenomenon in imitation learning called **"causal misidentification."**

The **motivation** stems from a critical vulnerability of standard behavioral cloning:
1. **Non-causal learning:** Behavioral cloning trains a policy by simply mapping observations to expert actions, treating it as a supervised learning problem. This discriminative approach is inherently "non-causal" because it does not distinguish between true causes of expert actions and mere correlates (effects or confounders) that might appear in the demonstration data.
2. **Interaction with distributional shift:** While ignored causality might not be an issue in static supervised learning, it becomes "particularly damaging" in imitation learning due to the ubiquitous problem of distributional shift. When a learned policy takes actions that differ from the expert's, it leads to novel state distributions. If the policy relies on non-causal correlates, these correlations may no longer hold in the new state distributions, leading to compounding errors and poor performance.
3. **Counter-intuitive failure mode:** The authors highlight a "counter-intuitive" symptom of causal misidentification: *access to more information can yield worse performance*. They illustrate this with a compelling driving example where a brake light indicator (an *effect* of braking) is wrongly identified as a *cause* for braking. Providing the policy with this extra, seemingly useful information leads it to brake only when the light is on, causing it to crash when the light isn't already on (e.g., when approaching a pedestrian). This phenomenon motivated the core investigation.

Therefore, the **key objectives** are:
- To formally point out and deeply investigate the "causal misidentification" problem in imitation learning, demonstrating its prevalence in both simplified benchmarks and realistic settings.
- To propose a principled, causally-motivated solution that allows deep neural network policies to learn the *correct causal model* of expert actions.
- To validate the proposed solution against existing imitation learning baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness in overcoming causal misidentification and improving generalization performance.
- Ultimately, the overarching goal is to make imitation learning more robust and reliable for deployment in real-world sequential decision-making tasks, especially where distributional shifts are inevitable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a two-step pipeline to resolve causal misidentification: first, jointly learning policies conditioned on various causal graph hypotheses, and second, performing targeted interventions to efficiently identify the correct causal model.

#### 3.1. Causal Graph-Parameterized Policy Learning

The core idea here is to train a single neural network that can represent multiple policies, each corresponding to a different hypothesized causal graph.
- **Assumption:** The expert's action depends on an unknown subset of the available state variables (the "causes"). Other variables are "nuisance variables."
- **Graph Parameterization:** A causal graph `G` is represented as a binary vector where each element `G_k` indicates whether the `k`-th state variable `X_k` is a cause of the action `A`. With `n` state variables, there are `2^n` possible causal graphs.
- **Policy Architecture:** A single neural network `f_φ` is trained to be a "graph-parameterized policy," `π_G(X) = f_φ([X ⋅ G, G])`. The `X ⋅ G` term performs element-wise multiplication, effectively masking out nuisance variables (where `G_k = 0`). The `G` vector is also concatenated to the input, allowing the network to condition its behavior on the current graph hypothesis.
- **Training Objective:** The network parameters `φ` are trained via gradient descent to minimize a loss (MSE for continuous actions, cross-entropy for discrete actions) over demonstrations `(X_i, A_i)`. During training, the graph `G` is sampled. Two variants are explored:
 - **UNIF-INTERVENTION:** `G` is sampled uniformly at random, encouraging the network to learn a mixture of policies that can adapt to different input subsets.
 - **DISC-INTERVENTION:** `G` is sampled preferentially from a distribution learned via a variational causal discovery approach (detailed in Appendix D). This approach infers a distribution `q_ψ(G)` over graphs that best fit the demonstration data, effectively providing a prior for the subsequent intervention phase.

#### 3.2. Targeted Intervention

Once the graph-parameterized policy `f_φ` is trained, the next step is to determine which hypothesized graph `G` is the true causal model. This is achieved through "interventions," where the agent actively interacts with either an expert or the environment to gather information. The goal is to estimate the likelihood `L(G)` of each `G` and then select the `G` with the highest likelihood (or its mode). The authors frame this process as learning a linear energy model `E(G) = <w, G> + b` for the graph distribution `p(G) ∝ exp(<w, G>) / τ`. `w` is updated via linear regression based on collected likelihoods.

Two intervention modes are proposed:
- **Expert Query Mode (Algorithm 1):**
 - Similar to DAgger, but more efficient.
 - The agent executes a "mixture policy" (averaging actions from all `π_G`).
 - It collects states where the different `π_G` policies disagree most (using KL divergence), identifying "interesting" states.
 - The expert is queried for optimal actions on these selected states.
 - The likelihood `L(G)` for each graph `G` is estimated based on the loss of `π_G` on these expert-queried samples.
- **Policy Execution Mode (Algorithm 2):**
 - This mode does not require an interactive expert.
 - The agent directly executes individual policies `π_G` (sampled based on the current `p(G)`).
 - The total episode return `R_G` (sum of rewards) from the environment is collected.
 - The likelihood `L(G)` for each graph `G` is proportional to `exp(R_G)`, assuming higher rewards imply a more causally correct policy. This mode is more flexible as it only requires episodic returns, not per-timestep rewards.

#### 3.3. Disentangling Observations

The intervention methods assume access to "disentangled observations," meaning that each dimension `X_k` truly represents an independent causal factor. This is often not the case with raw observations like images. To address this:
- A **β-VAE** is trained to learn a disentangled latent representation of the observations.
- The VAE is trained on a mix of expert and random trajectory states to ensure it captures states beyond just expert demonstrations.
- The policy then operates on this disentangled latent representation `X_t` (mean of the latent distribution from the VAE encoder).
- **CoordConv** is used in the VAE architecture to aid in learning spatially consistent representations.

This multi-faceted approach combines elements of supervised learning, variational inference, active learning, and reinforcement learning within a causal framework to tackle a fundamental problem in imitation learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling empirical evidence for both the existence of causal misidentification and the effectiveness of their proposed solution across several benchmark control domains and by drawing connections to real-world driving scenarios.

#### 4.1. Diagnosis of Causal Misidentification (Sec 3.2)

- **Benchmark Control Tasks:** The authors demonstrate causal misidentification by introducing a "confounded" scenario to standard environments (MountainCar, MuJoCo Hopper, Atari games like Pong, Enduro, UpNDown). In this scenario, they explicitly add information about the *previous action* to the state observation, which correlates with the current action but is not a direct cause.
 - **Key Result:** Fig 4 (and expanded Fig 11) clearly shows that in "CONFOUNDED" settings, policies achieve low training and validation error on expert data but yield significantly *lower rewards* when deployed, often failing entirely. In contrast, "ORIGINAL" policies (trained without the previous action in the state) achieve much higher rewards. This counter-intuitive outcome—more information leading to worse performance—validates the existence and severity of causal misidentification.
- **Real-World Driving:** The paper cites prior work (Wang et al., Bansal et al.) that observed similar symptoms: incorporating "history" information into driving policies sometimes *degraded* performance, despite improving validation metrics. This observation provides strong external validation that causal misidentification is not just a synthetic artifact but a pervasive problem in practical imitation learning systems.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of the Proposed Solution (Sec 5)

The core experimental question was whether their targeted intervention-based solution could bridge the performance gap caused by causal misidentification.

- **Intervention by Policy Execution (Fig 6, Fig 7):**
 - **Performance Improvement:** Both UNIF-INTERVENTION (uniform sampling of graphs during initial policy learning) and DISC-INTERVENTION (variational causal discovery for graph sampling) significantly improved rewards on MountainCar, Hopper, Pong, Enduro, and UpNDown. They successfully closed most of the performance gap between the "CONFOUNDED" and "ORIGINAL" baselines, often approaching the "ORIGINAL" performance.
 - **Sample Efficiency:** Their methods achieved high performance with relatively *few intervention episodes* (tens of episodes for MountainCar and Hopper). In comparison, GAIL required orders of magnitude more episodes (1.5k for Hopper) to achieve similar performance, and failed to converge on Atari games.
 - **Baseline Comparison:** Their approaches consistently outperformed the DROPOUT baseline, which attempts to mitigate the issue by applying dropout regularization.
 - **UNIF vs. DISC:** While DISC-INTERVENTION showed slight early advantages in MountainCar, UNIF-INTERVENTION performed equally well or better overall, suggesting that simple uniform sampling of causal graphs during the initial phase is often sufficient, emphasizing the importance of the intervention step itself.

- **Intervention by Expert Queries (Fig 8):**
 - **Performance Improvement:** Similar to policy execution, both UNIF-INTERVENTION and DISC-INTERVENTION successfully improved rewards over CONFOUNDED policies within a small number of expert queries.
 - **Comparison to DAgger:** Crucially, their methods significantly outperformed DAgger, requiring far fewer expert queries (tens vs. hundreds or thousands) to reach comparable or superior rewards (Fig 12). This highlights the efficiency gained by active learning (querying states of high disagreement) and focusing interventions on causal model identification rather than just data collection.
 - **Overall Comparison:** Policy execution generally led to better final rewards than expert queries, especially for Atari environments, suggesting that direct interaction with environmental rewards provides a clearer signal than expert agreement, which might be an imperfect proxy for optimality.

#### 4.3. Recovery of True Causal Graph (Sec 5, Fig 9)

- The authors verified that their approach not only improved performance but also successfully identified the *true causal model*. For MountainCar and Hopper, the method correctly selected the actual causes and excluded nuisance variables.
- For Atari (Pong), analysis of the learned β-VAE latent space (Fig 9) demonstrated that the method effectively disentangled causal variables (e.g., ball, paddles) from nuisance variables (e.g., overlaid digit representing previous action). When nuisance dimensions were "zeroed out" (replaced with prior samples), the critical causal elements remained, while the nuisance elements changed or disappeared, validating that the method correctly learned which latent dimensions were causally relevant.

#### 4.4. Necessity of Disentanglement (Sec 5, Table 2)

- Empirical tests on a "rotated" (entangled) MountainCar state demonstrated a significant drop in performance for their intervention methods compared to the disentangled state. This confirms the theoretical assumption that a disentangled representation of the state is crucial for the effectiveness of their approach, as it allows for meaningful masking and identification of individual causal factors.

In summary, the results convincingly diagnose causal misidentification as a distinct and problematic issue in IL and provide strong evidence that the proposed targeted intervention framework effectively mitigates this problem, leading to more robust and higher-performing policies with remarkable sample efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper presents a highly significant contribution to the fields of imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and causality, with substantial potential impact:

1. **Fundamental Problem Identification:** The paper meticulously identifies "causal misidentification" as a distinct and critical failure mode of behavioral cloning, separate from the general problem of distributional shift, even though it is exacerbated by it. By providing a clear diagnosis and demonstrating its prevalence in both synthetic and real-world scenarios, the authors highlight a previously underexplored theoretical and practical challenge in imitation learning. This reframing of a known problem (distributional shift) through a causal lens is highly insightful.

2. **Principled Causal Approach:** The proposed solution—learning graph-parameterized policies combined with targeted interventions—offers a principled, causally-motivated framework to address this problem. This represents a significant step towards bringing rigorous causal inference methods into the dynamic, high-dimensional domain of deep imitation learning and control. Unlike heuristic fixes, this approach grounds the solution in the fundamental nature of cause-effect relationships.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The ability to identify and rely solely on the true causes of expert actions makes the learned policies significantly more robust to the inevitable distributional shifts encountered during deployment. This directly translates to better generalization performance in real-world applications where data might not perfectly match the training distribution.

4. **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The empirical results clearly demonstrate that targeted interventions, particularly the policy execution mode, can achieve high performance with remarkable sample efficiency compared to existing methods like DAgger or GAIL. This is crucial for real-world robotic or autonomous systems where collecting vast amounts of interaction data can be costly, time-consuming, or dangerous.

5. **Implications for Real-World Systems:** The prevalence of causal misidentification, as evidenced by issues with history-based policies in autonomous driving, underscores the practical relevance of this work. A more robust imitation learning framework is essential for deploying reliable AI agents in complex environments like self-driving cars, robotics, and other control systems.

6. **Broader Applicability to Machine Learning:** The authors explicitly note that "general machine learning systems deployed in the real world also encounter 'feedback', which opens the door to causal misidentification." This suggests that the insights and methodology developed in this paper could have wider implications for improving the robustness and interpretability of many other machine learning systems beyond imitation learning, particularly those that interact with their environment or where their outputs feed back as inputs.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge that while they demonstrated the phenomenon and validated their solution in synthetic settings designed to mimic real-world problems, extending the solution to complex, truly realistic scenarios (e.g., end-to-end learning from raw sensor data without disentangled latent spaces) remains an exciting direction for future work. The need for disentangled representations, while addressed with VAEs, could still be a bottleneck for very complex visual inputs.

In conclusion, "Causal Confusion in Imitation Learning" provides a profound theoretical contribution by illuminating a critical failure mode of behavioral cloning through a causal lens. Its proposed interventional framework offers a practical and sample-efficient pathway to build more robust and reliable autonomous systems, opening new avenues for research at the intersection of deep learning, control, and causal inference.
