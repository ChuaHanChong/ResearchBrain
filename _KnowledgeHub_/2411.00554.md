# Differentiable Physics-based System Identification for Robotic Manipulation of Elastoplastic Materials

> [!summary] Summary
> A Differentiable Physics-based System Identification (DPSI) framework was developed to infer physics parameters of volumetric elastoplastic materials for robotic manipulation. It achieves accurate simulation-to-real alignment of material deformations and generalizes to unseen manipulation motions using minimal real-world interaction data and noisy 3D point clouds.

## Problem

- Manipulating deformable materials presents challenges due to their infinite degrees of freedom and complex, unpredictable dynamics, making precise physics modeling difficult.
- Conventional robotic motion planning struggles without explicit, accurate physics models, while learning-based approaches require extensive and often unavailable realistic simulation data.
- Accurate physics parameters are hard to obtain from real-world observations, often involving incomplete 3D point clouds and lacking differentiable simulators for efficient, gradient-based optimization.

## Method

- A **DPSI** (==Differentiable Physics-based System Identification==) framework was developed that iteratively minimizes a loss function between real-world observations and physics simulations.
- The framework employs the ==mean-least-square Material Point Method (MLS-MPM)== for high-fidelity simulation of elastoplastic materials, incorporating ==von Mises plasticity== and frictional contacts.
- It leverages ==DiffTaiChi==, a GPU-accelerated programming language with ==automatic differentiation==, to enable efficient gradient-based optimization of key physics parameters (Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, yield stress, density, friction coefficients) from noisy 3D point cloud data.

## Results

- The optimization process demonstrated convergence within **100 gradient updates**, with identified Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, and yield stress values aligning with reported ranges for clay-like materials.
- DPSI successfully recovered parameters that enabled accurate simulation of unseen, longer, and more complex manipulation motions, maintaining visual similarity between real and simulated deformed shapes.
- A typical optimization run (**100 iterations, 1 datapoint**) completed in approximately **10 minutes** on an Nvidia GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, with potential reductions to **2-5 minutes** with a good initial guess, indicating practical computational efficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - DPSI achieved accurate sim-to-real alignment and robust generalization to unseen manipulation motions with minimal real-world interaction data, demonstrating comparable performance with single-datapoint datasets.
> - Optimization converged effectively, especially with physically plausible initial parameter guesses, and the identified parameters offered transparent, physically intuitive interpretations.
> - The Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) loss, comparing a filled simulated particle system to a reconstructed target, proved most suitable for capturing correct spatial differences and achieving better sim2real alignment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation of deformable materials, particularly volumetric elastoplastic substances such as dough or clay, presents challenges due to the materials' infinite degrees of freedom (DoFs) and complex deformation dynamics. This complexity impedes the direct application of conventional robotic motion planning methods that rely on explicit, precise physics models.

Current approaches to deformable object manipulation generally fall into two categories: model-free and model-based. Model-free methods, often utilizing reinforcement learning, typically require extensive trial-and-error in simulation, which can be computationally intensive and may lack the precision necessary for intricate tasks. Model-based approaches, while aiming for higher accuracy by employing physics models, frequently encounter difficulties in aligning simulated dynamics with real-world behavior and often resort to simplified geometric representations for computational efficiency, potentially sacrificing manipulation precision.

System identification for deformable objects is notably more complex than for rigid bodies, primarily due to the difficulty in accurately measuring material properties (e.g., elasticity, plasticity) and friction coefficients. Prior research in this area has largely concentrated on linear or planar deformable objects. Efforts for three-dimensional volumetric materials often involved simpler assumptions, such as identifying a single parameter or using reduced-DoF representations. More recent advancements in differentiable physics have shown promise in identifying material parameters from synthetic data or via differentiable rendering from images, but these approaches often focus on simulated environments or complete, ideal observations.

This work addresses a gap by developing a framework for system identification specifically for *real-world* volumetric elastoplastic materials. It distinguishes itself by utilizing robotic interactions and operating with noisy, incomplete 3D point cloud data from optical cameras, which often suffer from occlusions. The research integrates high-fidelity physics simulation with differentiable programming, aiming to provide a method that can infer accurate physics parameters from minimal real-world interaction, thereby bridging the simulation-to-real gap for precise robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a Differentiable Physics-based System Identification (DPSI) framework. This framework aims to enable a robot arm to infer the physics parameters of volumetric elastoplastic materials and their environment. The overarching goal is to align simulation dynamics with real-world behavior, which is crucial for achieving high-precision robotic manipulation of such materials.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in manipulating deformable objects:
- **Modeling and Perception Difficulty:** Elastoplastic materials possess infinite degrees of freedom, making their dynamics highly unpredictable and difficult to model and perceive accurately in high-dimensional spaces.
- **Computational Cost:** Simulating the complex dynamics of these materials is computationally expensive, further exacerbated by inaccuracies in estimated physics parameters.
- **Data Scarcity and Quality:** Obtaining accurate physics parameters from real-world observations is problematic. Optical cameras often capture incomplete 3D point clouds with heavy occlusions, and collecting extensive, diverse real-world deformation data is time-consuming and costly.
- **Limitations of Existing Methods:** Conventional robotic motion planning struggles due to the lack of explicit, accurate physics models. Learning-based approaches, such as reinforcement learning, typically require extensive data from realistic physics simulations, which are often unavailable or inaccurate for deformable materials.
- **Lack of Differentiability:** Identifying physics parameters using gradient-based optimization is challenging because most physics simulators do not natively support derivative computations, limiting the efficiency of parameter search.

To address these issues, the DPSI framework is designed with specific objectives:
- To estimate key physics parameters, including Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, yield stress, and friction coefficients, using minimal and simple manipulation motions (demonstrated to be achievable in under 5 minutes with a reasonable initial guess) and incomplete 3D point clouds.
- To enable accurate material simulation that can predict visually and physically realistic deformation behaviors induced by unseen and long-horizon manipulation motions.
- To inherently provide physically intuitive interpretations for the identified parameters, offering transparency in contrast to "black-box" machine learning approaches.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Differentiable Physics-based System Identification (DPSI) framework is designed to infer material properties and environmental parameters by iteratively minimizing a loss function between real-world observations and physics simulations. The framework comprises modules for differentiable dynamics modeling, real-to-simulation object and trajectory reconstruction, and loss function computation.

**Differentiable Dynamics Modeling:**
- **Material Point Method (MPM):** The work employs the mean-least-square material point method (MLS-MPM) to simulate deformable object manipulation dynamics. MLS-MPM is a meshless, hybrid Lagrangian-Eulerian scheme that combines particle-based (Lagrangian) material representation with a background grid (Eulerian) for efficient computation of motion, deformation gradients, and frictional contacts. This approach is selected for its efficiency and high physical fidelity, particularly for elastoplastic materials undergoing large deformations.
- **Elastoplasticity:** The simulation incorporates strain elastoplasticity. The material's strain-stress relationship is described using a deformation gradient decomposed into elastic and plastic parts. The fixed corotated elastic energy model is used for robustness under large deformation, defining Lamé parameters (λ, μ) based on Young’s modulus (E) and Poisson’s ratio (ν). The von Mises plasticity model, with an associative plastic flow assumption, determines plastic response when the yield criterion is violated, governed by the yield stress (σy).
- **Frictional Contacts:** Collision detection is performed using pre-computed Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) for rigid objects (table and end-effectors). Dynamic friction with a sticky impulse model is applied, with separate friction coefficients for the table (μt) and manipulators (μm).
- **Differentiable Programming:** The simulator is built using DiffTaiChi, a programming language tailored for GPU-accelerated parallel computation and automatic differentiation. DiffTaiChi generates derivative functions for simulation steps via source code transformation, allowing efficient gradient computation for the physics parameters. The parameters jointly optimized are Young’s modulus (E), Poisson’s ratio (ν), yield stress (σy), material density (ρ), table friction coefficient (μt), and manipulator friction coefficient (μm).

**Real-to-Sim Object Reconstruction:**
- **Real-world Platform:** Experiments utilized a Kuka IIWA LBR 14 industrial arm equipped with a Zivid One+ medium 3D camera and three types of 3D-printed end-effectors (rectangular cuboid, cylinder roller, bullet-shaped). Plasticine was the primary material, with cloud slime and soil used for generalization tests.
- **Perception:** Multi-view 3D point clouds of the object are captured before and after manipulation. These are fused and cropped to represent the object. Challenges include camera calibration error (~±3 mm discrepancy), occlusions (especially the bottom of the object), and the use of only pre- and post-manipulation observations. To mitigate occlusion, an assumption is made that the angle between the object boundary and table is ≥ 90°, allowing points to be projected to form a closed bottom surface.
- **Reconstruction Pipeline:** The fused point cloud is down-sampled, converted into a watertight mesh using the ball-pivoting algorithm, and then uniformly filled with particles at a specified density (e.g., 4 × 10^6 particles/m^3) to create the initial particle system for simulation. End-effectors are treated as rigid bodies with pre-computed SDFs.

**Real-to-Sim Trajectory Reconstruction:**
- **Manipulation Motions:** Short, simple motions were designed for parameter optimization, categorized into two contact complexity levels: Level 1 (poking motions) focused on deformation parameters, and Level 2 (poking-shifting motions) for deformation and friction. Three longer, more complex motions were designed for out-of-distribution validation, each with a different end-effector.
- **Trajectory Conversion:** Real-world robot trajectories, generated by ROS and MoveIt!, have uneven time intervals. For simulation, these are reconstructed into constant-velocity segments with a fixed time step (`dt = 0.01` seconds) to maintain stability.
- **Sticking Prevention:** To prevent the object from sticking to the end-effector (which would introduce large, uncertain dynamics), end-effectors are lightly coated with flour before each manipulation.

**Loss Functions:**
- Four loss functions are used to quantify the difference between the simulated and real deformed object states: Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover's Distance (EMD), each with two variants:
 - `d_PCD-CD` and `d_PCD-EMD`: comparing simulated particles to the raw (downsampled) real-world point cloud.
 - `d_PRT-CD` and `d_PRT-EMD`: comparing simulated particles to a reconstructed particle system from the real-world point cloud.
- The non-averaged versions of CD and EMD are used for gradient computation.
- A pixel-wise heightmap distance (`d_Heightmap`) is used for evaluation purposes, not for optimization, due to its visual interpretability and limitations in gradient information.

**Optimization:**
- Gradient descent is performed using the Adam algorithm. Experiments involved 100 iterations (gradient updates) with three random seeds for various loss functions and five different datasets (ranging from 1 to 12 interaction datapoints). Validation is performed after each update using a separate dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents an examination of DPSI's performance through loss landscape analysis, in-distribution validation, and out-of-distribution generalization, alongside a discussion of its computational efficiency and limitations.

**Loss Landscape Analysis:**
- Analysis of loss landscapes (Figure 3) across parameters (E, ν, σy, ρ, μt, μm) revealed that Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) losses could exhibit different directional sensitivities, particularly along the yield stress (σy) axis at lower contact complexities. EMD losses generally presented less flat landscapes, suggesting higher sensitivity and potential for more effective optimization.
- The distributional patterns of losses were largely similar across datasets with varying numbers of datapoints, indicating that the optimization process is robust to the quantity of interaction data.
- The presence of large flat regions in many loss distributions suggested multiple local minima and potentially plausible parameter combinations describing similar physical characteristics.

**In-Distribution Performances:**
- **Convergence:** The optimization process demonstrated convergence, with validation loss curves stabilizing over 100 gradient updates (Figures 4, 5). Parameters, while converging to different solutions across runs (aligned with local minima observations), showed greater convergence for E, ν, and σy values with higher contact complexity motions (Level 2) compared to simpler motions (Level 1). Material density (ρ) remained relatively dispersed.
- **Physical Plausibility:** Recovered E, ρ, and ν values from Level 2 experiments were observed to be closer to reported ranges for clay-like materials. Yield stress (σy) values were higher than literature values, attributed to numerical stability constraints in the simulation. Hand-picked parameters, derived from geotechnical literature, yielded reasonable but not superior performance compared to DPSI-identified parameters.
- **Loss Function Behavior:** CD and EMD losses often exhibited a negative correlation in their optimization tendencies, particularly at Level 1, suggesting they focus on different spatial aspects of the point sets. EMD losses generally resulted in smaller heightmap distances, indicating better sim2real alignment.
- **Data Efficiency:** The framework achieved comparable performance with single-datapoint datasets as with larger datasets (Table 3), demonstrating that DPSI is not data-hungry in the presence of real-world perception challenges.
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 8):** Visualizations of simulated deformations were largely plausible and consistent with ground truths. CD optimization tended to produce taller, darker heightmaps (emphasizing z-direction matching), while EMD optimization led to wider, lighter heightmaps (emphasizing x-y direction matching), correlating with identified yield stress values. Physically intuitive relationships were observed between parameter changes (e.g., friction, density) and the object's deformation or displacement, supporting the interpretability of the identified parameters.

**Generalization (Out-of-Distribution):**
- **Accuracy for Unseen Motions:** DPSI successfully recovered parameters that enabled accurate simulation of unseen, longer, and more complex manipulation motions (e.g., triple-poking, flattening, poking-180-rotating) (Figures 9, 12). Visual similarity between real and simulated trajectories and deformed shapes was maintained, exhibiting robustness against perception and data challenges.
- **Single-Datapoint Efficacy:** Single-datapoint optimization cases contributed significantly to the best generalization results (11 out of 18 top-three lowest heightmap losses for unseen motions), further supporting data efficiency.
- **Parameter Interpretability:** The physical meanings and interpretability of the identified parameters persisted for unseen motions, showing consistent correlations with deformation outcomes.
- **Limitations:** The study noted that the identified solutions were not globally optimal, as the best in-distribution parameters did not consistently yield the best generalization performance. Visual discrepancies, such as insufficient elastic return in contact areas, floating displaced particles, and localized force impact, indicated modeling limitations and the need for further model diversification or observational noise reduction.

**Cloud Slime & Soil Materials:**
- DPSI could identify parameters that produced visually similar simulations for cloud slime and soil, even though these materials might not be perfectly represented by the elastoplastic physics model (Figure 13, Table 5). This suggests the framework attempts to fit observed deformations regardless of perfect model-material alignment, underscoring the importance of appropriate physics model selection.

**Cause and Treatment to Local Minima:**
- Experiments revealed that DPSI's convergence to different local minima was influenced by random initial parameter values (Figure 14). With a manually provided, physically plausible initial guess, DPSI converged rapidly (within 20 iterations) and with minimal variance across multiple random seeds, indicating deterministic differentiation behavior and the benefit of good initialization.
- The PRT EMD loss, comparing a filled simulation particle system to a non-hollow target point cloud, was identified as the most suitable for capturing correct spatial differences due to better point correspondences compared to hollow targets or CD.

**Computation Cost:**
- The framework's computational costs (Table 7), run on an Nvidia GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, indicated practicality. A typical optimization run (100 iterations, 1 datapoint) could be completed in approximately 10 minutes, potentially reduced to 2-5 minutes with a good initial parameter guess. Memory consumption remained within 3 GB, making it suitable for portable GPUs and suggesting real-world deployment viability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a differentiable physics-based system identification framework (DPSI) that addresses a key challenge in robotics: bridging the gap between simulated and real-world manipulation of elastoplastic materials. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

- **Advancing Deformable Object Manipulation:** The DPSI framework provides a foundation for more accurate and efficient real-world deployment of robotic manipulation tasks involving deformable materials. By enabling precise parameter estimation, it can improve motion planning and control strategies for complex operations such as surgery, soft object assembly, or handling food and construction materials.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** The demonstrated ability to achieve accurate simulation-to-real alignment with minimal real-world interaction data (even a single interaction datapoint) significantly reduces the costly and time-consuming burden of data collection. This makes the application of advanced robotic manipulation to novel deformable materials more practical and accessible.
- **Online Calibration and Adaptability:** The framework's computational efficiency, allowing parameter identification within minutes, suggests the potential for online recalibration of simulations. This capability is critical for addressing time-varying material properties (e.g., changes due to humidity, temperature, or dryness), enabling robots to adapt dynamically to their environment without relying on static or previously stored material properties.
- **Physically Meaningful Interpretations:** Unlike purely data-driven, "black-box" approaches, DPSI yields physically interpretable parameters. This transparency offers insights into the underlying material dynamics, helps in diagnosing discrepancies between simulation and reality, and can guide human operators or higher-level planning systems in understanding and improving manipulation strategies or physics models.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work serves as a pioneering example for system identification in robotic volumetric elastoplastic object manipulation using differentiable, particle-based physics simulation. It opens avenues for future research into developing more accurate physics models, refining numerical approximations, integrating active learning for optimal interaction design, improving real-world perception robustness, and extending the framework to more complex multi-object contact dynamics.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The project's open-source nature facilitates reproducibility and encourages further development by the research community.

Despite some observed limitations, such as potential under-representativeness of current physics models for certain materials and the influence of initial parameter guesses on convergence, the DPSI framework represents a significant step toward robust and precise autonomous robotic manipulation of highly deformable materials.
