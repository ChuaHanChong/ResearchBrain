# Self-supervised Learning Of Visual Pose Estimation Without Pose Labels By Classifying LED States

> [!summary] Summary
> A deep learning model is presented that performs monocular RGB relative pose estimation for ground robots by classifying the on/off states of onboard LEDs. This self-supervised approach eliminates the need for explicit pose labels or detailed CAD models, achieving 70% pose accuracy (Γ_45°1m) and 17 pixels localization error while enabling autonomous data collection.

## Problem

- Traditional visual pose estimation often requires extensive, expensive pose labels or detailed Computer-Aided Design (CAD) models.
- Models trained in simulation struggle with the "sim-to-real gap" when deployed in real-world environments.
- Acquiring ground truth pose data necessitates specialized external infrastructure, such as motion capture systems.

## Method

- Trains a ==Fully Convolutional Network (FCN)== using a ==self-supervised pretext task==: classifying the on/off states of multiple LEDs on a target robot.
- Implicitly learns robot pose (position and orientation) and distance by designing a ==loss function== that leverages spatial, orientation, and multi-scale information from LED state predictions.
- Resolves monocular depth scale ambiguity with a highly constrained calibration procedure, using only a single image captured at a known target distance.

## Results

- The self-supervised model achieved **70%** pose accuracy (Γ_45°1m), **17 pixels** median localization error, and **17 degrees** median orientation error, outperforming CAD-model-based baselines.
- Demonstrated robustness to LEDs being off during inference and generalization to unseen environments, with pre-training on diverse data significantly improving performance upon fine-tuning.
- The model is computationally efficient (**6.5ms** inference time), capable of multi-robot pose estimation, and can detect robot presence with **97.2%** AUC based on LED state prediction confidence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Observable and controllable LED states can serve as the sole source of supervision for learning complex spatial understanding, such as full 2D robot pose.
> - A carefully designed loss function, incorporating spatial weighting, orientation-based occlusion reasoning, and multi-scale processing, allows an FCN to infer pose details from basic LED state classification.
> - Autonomous data collection by pairs of robots toggling LEDs provides a scalable and infrastructure-free method for generating training data in diverse environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics relies significantly on accurate relative localization of mobile robots for coordination and task execution. Traditional approaches to visual object and robot pose estimation typically involve supervised learning methods. These methods often require extensive datasets where images of the robot are meticulously labeled with their true relative pose. The acquisition of such labeled datasets in real-world environments is generally expensive, labor-intensive, and necessitates specialized external infrastructure for generating ground truth data, such as motion capture systems.

Alternatively, pose estimation models can be trained using simulated data, which offers a more economical means of generating labeled examples. However, this approach often mandates the availability of a realistic and textured Computer-AAided Design (CAD) model of the robot. Furthermore, models trained in simulation frequently encounter the "sim-to-real gap," where performance degrades when deployed in real-world environments due to discrepancies between simulated and actual visual data. Recent advancements in novel object pose estimation have also leveraged CAD models, utilizing them to generate templates for matching with input image patches via learning-based descriptors, or refining poses using techniques like PnP or iterative optical flow minimization. Other modern techniques, such as those based on Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) or Gaussian Splatting, assume access to images of static scenes labeled with camera poses, which are then used to estimate object poses by minimizing photometric errors. A common theme across these supervised and CAD-model-dependent approaches is their reliance on strong supervision sources, whether explicit pose labels, detailed 3D models, or calibrated views.

A distinct area of research, weakly supervised learning (WSL), has explored training models for tasks like object detection and image segmentation using less demanding image-level labels, often by formulating a classification pretext task. Similarly, self-supervised robot learning aims to reduce the reliance on labeled data by pre-training models on pretext tasks to acquire useful pattern recognition skills, which are then fine-tuned with limited labels for the final task. Examples include masking image patches for reconstruction or predicting missing areas. Some recent works have shown that LED state classification can be a useful pretext task for fine-tuning models for visual robot detection and pose estimation.

This current work differentiates itself by proposing an approach that learns full robot pose estimation (position and orientation) directly from a self-supervised pretext task without requiring pose labels, CAD models, or fine-tuning with strongly annotated data for the end task. It addresses the limitations of existing methods by operating under assumptions of minimal prior knowledge about the robot's geometry or appearance, relying instead on observable and controllable features: the on/off states of onboard LEDs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a model for monocular RGB relative pose estimation of a ground robot that can train effectively from scratch without requiring explicit pose labels or prior knowledge regarding the robot’s specific shape or appearance. The motivation stems from the significant challenges and costs associated with acquiring ground truth pose data for supervised learning or creating accurate CAD models for simulation-based and template-matching approaches. These traditional requirements limit the scalability and adaptability of robot pose estimation systems, particularly for custom-built or frequently modified robotic platforms.

The specific objectives driving this work are:

- **Eliminate Pose Labels and CAD Models:** To develop a training methodology that does not necessitate datasets labeled with robot poses, nor access to a detailed CAD model of the robot, thereby bypassing the expenses and complexities associated with these common requirements.
- **Leverage Observable Robot Features:** To utilize readily available and controllable features on a robot, specifically multiple independent LEDs whose states (on/off) are known at each frame, as the sole source of supervision during training. This approach posits that the visual changes induced by LED state transitions can implicitly encode information about the robot's pose relative to an observing camera.
- **Minimize Calibration Requirements:** To address the inherent ambiguity of monocular depth estimation with a highly constrained calibration procedure, requiring only a single calibration image captured at a known target distance. This avoids complex multi-view or multi-sensor calibration setups.
- **Enable Autonomous Data Collection:** To design a system where training data can be collected autonomously by a pair of robots moving randomly, without the need for external infrastructure (e.g., motion capture systems) or human supervision. This allows for flexible and efficient data acquisition in diverse, potentially unknown, environments.
- **Achieve Competitive Performance:** To demonstrate that a model trained using this self-supervised pretext task can achieve pose estimation performance competitive with state-of-the-art approaches that rely on stronger forms of supervision (pose labels or CAD models).
- **Ensure Inference Independence:** To ensure that once trained, the model can estimate robot pose at inference time regardless of the LEDs' states, meaning the LEDs are not required to be on or in any specific configuration during deployment.
- **Demonstrate Generalization and Robustness:** To evaluate the model’s ability to generalize to different domains (unseen environments), handle training data with infrequent robot visibility, maintain performance when LEDs are off, and support multi-robot pose estimation.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to provide a more practical, scalable, and adaptable solution for robot pose estimation, lowering the barrier for deployment in various real-world scenarios, especially for rapidly prototyped or evolving robot systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a self-supervised learning approach for monocular RGB relative pose estimation by training a deep neural network on a pretext task: classifying the states of multiple LEDs mounted on a target robot.

**A. Training Assumptions:**
At training time, the system relies on three primary assumptions:
1. **LED-equipped Robots:** Each robot is fitted with K LEDs whose states (on or off) are independent and known for every frame.
2. **Approximate LED Viewing Direction:** The approximate direction from which each LED is visible relative to the robot’s heading is known.
3. **Monocular Depth Calibration:** A single calibration image with a known target distance is available to resolve the scale ambiguity inherent in monocular depth estimation.

**B. Data Collection:**
Training data is collected autonomously by a pair of robots. These robots move randomly in an environment, and each robot independently toggles the states of its LEDs multiple times. During data collection, each robot broadcasts its LED states (e.g., via a radio link), which serve as ground truth labels for images acquired by the other robot. No external infrastructure or pose labels are required during this process, and the robots may only be visible in a small subset of the collected frames.

**C. Model Architecture and Output:**
The model `m_θ(I)` is a Fully Convolutional Network (FCN) designed to process an RGB image `I` from an "observer" robot and predict the pose `P = <x, y, ψ>` of a "target" robot relative to the observer's camera, along with the states of its K LEDs. The model outputs:
- `û, û`: Image-space coordinates of the robot.
- `d̂`: Estimated distance of the robot from the camera.
- `ψ̂`: Estimated robot orientation (bearing) relative to the camera.
- `l̂ = <l̂_1, ..., l̂_K>`: Predicted probabilities that each of the K LEDs is turned on.

The FCN architecture comprises convolution and pooling layers, outputting a set of 2D feature maps (H' × W'). Specifically, it generates:
- `L̂_k`: An LED state map for each of the K LEDs, where each cell indicates the confidence (0 to 1) that the k-th LED is on. A value of 0.5 indicates uncertainty if the LED is not within the receptive field (RF).
- `P̂`: A localization map, where each cell (0 to 1) indicates the belief about the robot's presence within its RF.
- `Ψ̂`: A relative bearing map, where each cell represents the robot's bearing relative to the camera (angle in [-π, π]).

**D. Loss Function Design:**
The model parameters `θ` are optimized via gradient descent using a Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss on the LED states. The overall loss function `L` is carefully constructed to implicitly drive the learning of pose information:

1. **LED State Classification (`L^k_led`):** The base loss is `BCE(l̂_k, l_k)` for each LED `k` at each cell. This initial loss is computed across spatial dimensions.

2. **Robot Localization (`L^k_loc`):** To guide the model to focus on areas containing the robot, the `L^k_led` map is spatially weighted. The `P̂` map, indicating robot presence, is normalized using a softmax function (`P̂_norm`) to ensure a proper spatial distribution of attention. The localization loss is then `L^k_loc = L^k_led ⊙ P̂_norm` (element-wise product). This formulation incentivizes the model to produce high values in `P̂_norm` where the robot is present, as these areas correspond to lower LED classification loss.

3. **Robot Relative Bearing Estimation (`L^k_ori`):** To account for self-occlusions of LEDs based on the robot's orientation, a weighting mechanism is introduced. The `Ψ̂` map (predicted bearing) is used to generate K visibility scores (`Λ̂_k`) for each LED using a differentiable cosine function: `λ̂_k = cos(ψ̂ + (2π/K)(k-1))`. These `Λ̂_k` maps are normalized to be non-negative and sum to one for a given orientation. The orientation loss is `L^k_ori = L^k_loc ⊙ Λ̂_k`, which allows the model to downplay errors for LEDs that are predicted to be occluded.

4. **Robot Distance Estimation (`L^k,s_ms`):** To infer distance from apparent object size, the same input image is passed through the FCN at multiple scales `{s_1, ..., s_S}`. The output maps from different scales are upscaled to match dimensions. A multi-scale localization map `P̂_ms-norm` is then computed by applying softmax across all scales. The final multi-scale loss is `L^k,s_ms = BCE(L̂_k,s, l_k) ⊙ P̂^s_ms-norm ⊙ Λ̂_k,s`. By normalizing across scales, the model is forced to combine scales, effectively selecting the scale where the robot's size best fits the receptive fields, which is directly related to its distance.

The complete loss function `L` averages these multi-scale, spatially, and orientation-weighted losses across all LEDs, scales, and spatial dimensions.

**E. Model Inference:**
During inference, the LEDs are not required to be active.
- **Localization (û, û):** Computed as the barycenter of the `P̂_ms-norm` maps, then scaled back to original image resolution.
- **Orientation (ψ̂):** Calculated as the weighted average of the `Ψ̂_s` maps, with weights from `P̂_ms-norm`.
- **Distance (d̂):** Estimated by measuring the robot's apparent size. This involves summing `P̂^s_ms-norm` maps over spatial dimensions to get scale compatibility weights, then computing a weighted average of inverse scale factors. This `d̂'` is then multiplied by a calibration factor `d_c` obtained from the single calibration image.
- **LED States (l̂_k):** Computed as the weighted average of `L̂_ks` by `P̂_ms-norm`, if needed (not for pose estimation itself).

The model operates at an average running time of 6.5ms per image (153 Hz) on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080.

**F. Experimental Setup:**
The DJI Robomaster S1 robot, equipped with a monocular RGB camera and six multi-color LEDs, served as the platform. Four LEDs (two on the turret, front, and back on the base) were used.
- **Datasets:** Data was collected in a laboratory (131K samples), classroom (34K), gym (48K), and break room (45K). The laboratory dataset was split into training, validation, and testing sets, with only 23% of training samples featuring a visible robot. Pose information for quantitative evaluation was collected using a motion capture system for a subset of the laboratory testing set (Q_ν_lab).
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against a Mean Predictor, CNOS (a state-of-the-art CAD-based novel object pose estimation approach), and an Upperbound model (a supervised version of the proposed architecture trained with ground truth pose labels).
- **Metrics:** Evaluation used E_uv (median localization error in pixels), E_ψ (median circular error for orientation in degrees), E_d (mean absolute percentage error for distance), and Γ_45°1m (pose accuracy: percentage of predictions with position error < 1m AND orientation error < 45°). LED classification was assessed by AUC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the proposed self-supervised pose estimation approach yielded several findings regarding its performance, generalization, and robustness.

**A. Competitive Performance:**
The self-supervised model, trained on the laboratory dataset (T_lab), demonstrated performance significantly superior to baseline methods that do not use ground truth pose data. Specifically, when evaluated on the Q_ν_lab testing set (samples with a visible robot), the approach achieved a Γ_45°1m pose accuracy of 70%, which is considerably higher than the Mean Predictor (10%) and the CAD-model-based CNOS (25%). The model's performance was competitive with the Upperbound model (a supervised version of the same architecture trained with pose labels), which scored 93% on Γ_45°1m.
- **Localization (E_uv):** The self-supervised model achieved 17 px, comparable to the Upperbound (18 px) and CNOS (13 px, lowest due to SAM segmentation).
- **Orientation (E_ψ):** It achieved 17 deg, close to Upperbound (14 deg), and significantly better than CNOS (72 deg).
- **Distance (E_d):** It recorded 24%, showing a larger gap with the Upperbound (11%), which is attributed to the proposed method's reliance on a discrete number of scales for distance estimation compared to the Upperbound's continuous regression.

**B. Robustness to LED State:**
Experiments conducted on a subset of Q_ν_lab where all visible LEDs were turned off indicated that the approach is robust and does not necessitate specific LED states for accurate pose estimation during inference. The performance metrics (E_uv = 19.6 px, E_ψ = 19.23 deg, E_d = 25.7%) showed only minor degradation compared to the full Q_ν_lab evaluation, with higher errors attributed to reduced robot visibility when LEDs are inactive.

**C. Generalization and Fine-Tuning:**
- **Generalization to Unseen Environments:** A model pre-trained on the Out-of-Domain (OOD) dataset (classroom, gym, break room environments) and directly applied to Q_ν_lab without fine-tuning showed an Γ_45°1m of 29%. While lower than the model trained on T_lab, it still outperformed the Mean Predictor, demonstrating some generalization capability.
- **Fine-tuning Effectiveness:** When the OOD-pretrained model was fine-tuned on increasing amounts of laboratory data (T_lab), its performance significantly improved. The fine-tuned model consistently outperformed a model trained from scratch on the same amount of target domain data, especially with limited data. This highlights the benefit of pre-training on diverse, unlabeled data.
- **Task-Specific Learning Rates:** The amount of data required for the "from scratch" model to match the fine-tuned model's performance varied by task: localization was easier to learn, followed by heading estimation, and then distance estimation.

**D. Multi-Robot Pose Estimation:**
The model demonstrated the capability to estimate the poses of multiple robots simultaneously in an image, despite being trained on datasets primarily featuring at most one visible robot. This was achieved by identifying multiple local maxima in the `P̂` localization map, indicating the presence of multiple robots.

**E. Robot Presence Detection:**
Beyond pose estimation, the model's learned features could be leveraged for robot detection. Using the maximum value of the `P̂_ms-norm` map as a belief in robot presence yielded an AUC of 83.4%. An alternative method based on the entropy of LED state predictions (model's confidence in LED classification) achieved a higher AUC of 97.2%, suggesting that the softmax function applied to `P̂_ms-norm` can accentuate noise when no robot is present, making the LED confidence a more reliable indicator for detection.

**F. Computational Efficiency:**
The inference process was efficient, with an average running time of 6.5ms per image (153 Hz) on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080, suitable for real-time robotic applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces a methodological contribution for learning visual pose estimation that mitigates several common challenges in robotics and computer vision.

**A. Reduced Supervision and Cost:**
The primary significance lies in eliminating the need for explicit pose labels or detailed CAD models, which are often expensive and time-consuming to acquire. By leveraging the simple, controllable states of onboard LEDs as the sole source of supervision, the approach significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing robot pose estimation systems. This is particularly impactful for custom-built or frequently evolving robotic platforms where CAD models are unavailable or become quickly outdated.

**B. Autonomous Data Collection and Adaptability:**
The ability for pairs of robots to autonomously collect training data without external infrastructure or human supervision is a key innovation. This facilitates continuous, self-scaling data acquisition in real-world environments, enabling online learning and rapid domain adaptation. This autonomy makes the approach highly adaptable to various operational environments and conditions, reducing dependency on laboratory settings for training.

**C. Real-World Robustness and Generalization:**
Training directly on real-world image data, rather than simulated environments, inherently addresses the "sim-to-real gap" that often plagues models trained in virtual settings. The demonstrated robustness to unseen environments, the ability to fine-tune models effectively, and the capacity for multi-robot pose estimation without explicit multi-robot training highlight the practical applicability and generalization potential of the method. The model's independence from LED states during inference further enhances its utility in diverse operational scenarios.

**D. Broad Applicability of the Pretext Task:**
The core methodological contribution – using a classification pretext task (LED state prediction) to implicitly learn complex spatial understanding (pose estimation) – has broader implications. The authors suggest that this principle could extend to other sensors and actuators, provided that changes in the target variable (e.g., position of a robot arm) are observable through sensor data (e.g., a camera). This opens avenues for self-supervised learning of various robotic perception tasks by linking internal robot states to visual appearance changes.

**E. Potential for Scalable Robotic Systems:**
By making pose estimation more accessible and adaptable, this research can contribute to the development of more autonomous, self-sufficient, and scalable multi-robot systems. The ability to localize peers without heavy supervision is fundamental for swarm robotics and collaborative robot tasks. Future extensions, such as handling larger groups of robots for data collection, could dramatically increase efficiency and the amount of useful training data.

While the approach currently focuses on 2D pose and has limitations regarding 3D rotation and handling severe occlusions, its foundational methodology represents a step towards more autonomous and less data-intensive robot learning paradigms, fostering innovation in areas where ground truth pose information or detailed models are difficult to obtain.
