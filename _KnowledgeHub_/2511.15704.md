# In-N-On: Scaling Egocentric Manipulation with in-the-wild and on-task Data

> [!summary] Summary
> UC San Diego researchers developed a scalable method called "In-N-On" that leverages large-scale egocentric human data, categorized into "in-the-wild" and "on-task" types, to train foundational robot manipulation policies. This approach enables emergent capabilities such as zero-shot language following for instructions unseen by the robot and 1-shot learning for complex bimanual tasks, showing improved performance and robustness on humanoid robots.

## Problem

- Existing robot manipulation policies lack real-world generalizability due to limited, often task-specific, robot demonstration data.
- Harnessing human egocentric data is challenging because its vast heterogeneity (diverse "in-the-wild" vs. task-aligned "on-task") can lead to poor transfer or catastrophic forgetting.
- Bridging the embodiment gap between human demonstrations and robot execution requires a unified representation and effective domain adaptation for skill transfer.

## Method

- A two-stage "**In-N-On**" training recipe: initial pre-training on large-scale "in-the-wild" human data, followed by post-training on task-aligned "on-task" data.
- Curation of PHSD, a comprehensive human-humanoid dataset, coupled with a unified human-centric state-action space and retargeting tools for cross-embodiment compatibility.
- Training a language-conditioned Human0 model using flow matching and a ==Gradient Reversal Layer==-based domain adaptation to create embodiment-invariant features.

## Results

- Human0 demonstrates zero-shot language following for instructions learned exclusively from human data, achieving 30/30 success in O.O.D. multi-object grasping compared to 16/30 for baselines.
- The model enables 1-shot learning, achieving a 5/20 success rate on the complex bimanual pouring task with only a single robot demonstration, outperforming all comparative methods.
- Significant performance gains were observed on challenging, long-horizon tasks like burger assembly, with over **100%** relative improvement in O.O.D. settings against baselines.
- Domain adaptation with a Gradient Reversal Layer was crucial, improving few-shot pouring task success from **15%** to **25%** by fostering embodiment-invariant representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A systematic, two-stage approach leveraging both diverse "in-the-wild" and task-specific "on-task" human data is essential for emergent robot capabilities like zero-shot language following.
> - Explicit domain adaptation through techniques like Gradient Reversal Layer prevents models from "cheating" by merely distinguishing embodiments, ensuring the learning of truly transferable skills.
> - Pre-training with large human datasets provides strong priors that significantly enhance few-shot learning, allowing robots to quickly adapt to new tasks with minimal robot-specific demonstrations.
