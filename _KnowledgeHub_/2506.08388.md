# Reinforcement Learning Teachers of Test Time Scaling

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Sakana AI developed Reinforcement-Learned Teachers (RLTs), a framework that trains specialized language models to generate high-quality, step-by-step explanations for known solutions instead of solving problems from scratch. This approach produces superior distillation datasets, enabling smaller student models to outperform those trained with existing pipelines based on much larger LMs, and achieves significant computational efficiency with an RLT training costing 280.4 GPU-hours.

## Problem

- Traditional reinforcement learning for reasoning Language Models (LMs) struggles with sparse, one-hot correctness rewards, leading to exploration challenges, instability, and high computational costs, often only benefiting very large models.
- Current supervised distillation methods for LMs face an objective mismatch where teachers optimized for problem-solving do not optimally generate instructive explanations for students, necessitating extensive heuristic postprocessing.
- Existing distillation pipelines are inefficient and rely on prohibitively large LMs or costly manual/LLM-based postprocessing for data refinement.

## Method

- Redefine the teacher's role by training ==Reinforcement-Learned Teachers (RLTs)== to generate step-by-step explanations for given question-solution pairs, rather than solving problems independently.
- Introduce a ==dense, two-part reward function== (r_SS for student understanding and r_KL for explanation interpretability) to provide continuous feedback on the quality and utility of the generated explanations for student learning.
- Employ a computationally efficient training paradigm using ==GRPO algorithm== with a small ==7B base model==, requiring minimal pre-training and a short reinforcement learning phase.

## Results

- Raw explanations from a **7B** RLT consistently led to higher student performance on challenging reasoning tasks (AIME, MATH, GPQA) compared to distillation from larger LMs (e.g., DeepSeek R1) with extensive postprocessing.
- RLT-generated traces proved more effective for cold-starting traditional reinforcement learning and exhibited strong zero-shot transferability, enabling successful distillation for out-of-domain tasks.
- The framework achieved substantial computational efficiency, with training a **7B** RLT and generating its dataset requiring only a fraction of the GPU hours compared to existing large-scale reasoning LM pipelines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing the reinforcement learning objective to focus on explanation generation for known solutions effectively circumvents the severe exploration challenges faced by traditional RL with sparse correctness rewards.
> - Aligning the teacher's training objective directly with its downstream distillation role, by optimizing for explanation quality, yields superior and more efficient knowledge transfer to student models.
> - Even relatively small language models possess latent 'teaching skills' that can be effectively elicited and refined through purpose-aligned dense reward functions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models has seen remarkable advancements, particularly with the integration of reinforcement learning to enhance reasoning capabilities. This paper directly addresses several critical challenges within this rapidly evolving domain, specifically concerning the efficiency and effectiveness of RL for reasoning LMs and their subsequent distillation.

**Current State of RL for Reasoning LMs:**
Reinforcement learning, particularly with human feedback (RLHF) or self-play, has become a potent method for fine-tuning LMs to exhibit advanced reasoning behaviors, moving beyond simple prompt engineering or search-based approaches. Pioneering works like DeepSeek R1 have demonstrated that RL can significantly improve performance on complex tasks such as mathematics and coding. However, the authors highlight a fundamental limitation: RL for reasoning, when relying on "one-hot correctness" rewards, suffers from a severe **exploration challenge**. Such sparse rewards provide no learning signal unless the model already possesses a non-trivial chance of solving the task. This means that, in practice, only large, already-capable models tend to benefit consistently from RL, and the process is often unstable and computationally expensive. This leads to the observation that RL might not be inducing entirely *new* reasoning skills but rather reinforcing existing latent abilities or steering the model towards generating longer, more reflective thought processes.

**The Role of Supervised Distillation:**
To mitigate RL's shortcomings and to deploy models more efficiently, **supervised distillation** has become a ubiquitous technique. In this "teacher-student" paradigm, powerful LMs (often those trained with RL) act as teachers, generating "reasoning traces" or step-by-step explanations. These traces are then used to train smaller, less capable student models via supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Distillation is also crucial for "cold-starting" future RL iterations, providing a stronger initialization point. However, the paper points out a critical **objective mismatch**: LMs trained with sparse correctness rewards are optimized to *solve problems*, not necessarily to *provide effective explanations* for students. This misalignment often necessitates extensive, heuristic-driven postprocessing of teacher outputs, adding further cost and complexity to distillation pipelines (e.g., using other LMs like GPT-4 for refinement or filtering correct responses).

**The Paper's Novel Contribution:**
Cetin et al.'s work directly intervenes in this landscape by proposing a radical redefinition of the "teacher" role within the RL framework. Instead of trying to make LMs *solve problems from scratch* with sparse rewards, they train specialized **Reinforcement-Learned Teachers (RLTs)** to *explain given solutions*. This shifts the objective from problem-solving to explanation-generation, a task that can be guided by dense, informative rewards. By avoiding the exploration challenge and aligning the teacher's training objective with its downstream distillation role, this work offers a potentially more efficient, stable, and generalizable alternative to current RL reasoning paradigms. It directly challenges the reliance on prohibitively large LMs and complex post-processing steps that characterize existing state-of-the-art distillation pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objectives and motivations driving this research stem from the observed limitations and inefficiencies in current reinforcement learning paradigms for language model reasoning and their subsequent distillation.

#### 2.1. Key Objectives

1. **Avoid RL's Exploration Challenge:** The primary objective is to circumvent the inherent exploration problem faced by traditional RL for LMs, which relies on sparse, one-hot correctness rewards. This sparsity makes it difficult for LMs to learn new tasks from scratch or to extrapolate significantly beyond their initial capabilities.
2. **Train Specialized Reinforcement-Learned Teachers (RLTs):** To achieve the first objective, the authors aim to introduce and train a new class of LMs, termed RLTs, which are specifically optimized not for solving problems, but for generating highly effective and instructive explanations for students.
3. **Optimize for Downstream Distillation:** The ultimate goal of RLTs is to yield the most effective downstream distillation datasets. This means aligning the teacher's training objective directly with its real-world use case: providing reasoning traces from which student models can genuinely learn and improve.
4. **Develop a Dense and Informative Reward Function:** A crucial objective is to design a reward function that provides continuous, meaningful feedback to the RLT, moving beyond sparse correctness signals. This reward should assess the quality of an explanation based on its utility for student learning.

#### 2.2. Motivation

1. **Inefficiency and Instability of Traditional RL:** Traditional RL for reasoning LMs is often computationally expensive, requiring orders of magnitude larger models and extended training sessions. It is also prone to instability and output degradation, especially for smaller models, hindering its wider applicability.
2. **Mismatch in Teacher's Objective:** Current RL-trained teachers are optimized for problem-solving, not for effective teaching. This means their reinforced problem-solving skills might not be perfectly aligned with the goal of generating high-quality distillation data that fosters student understanding.
3. **Reliance on Heuristic-Driven Postprocessing:** To compensate for the objective mismatch, existing distillation pipelines heavily rely on costly and often manual or closed-source LLM-based postprocessing steps (e.g., filtering, refining, reformatting) to improve the quality of reasoning traces. This adds significant overhead and reduces reproducibility.
4. **Analogy to Real-World Teachers:** The authors draw an insightful analogy: real-world teachers are not always measured by their ability to derive complex solutions from scratch, but rather by their capacity to *explain* readily available solutions in an instructive manner. This intuition motivates the new task formulation for RLTs.
5. **Democratizing RL Reasoning:** By developing a framework that enables smaller, more efficient, and reusable teachers, the research aims to lower the prohibitive costs associated with RL-based reasoning, making this powerful technique more accessible to a broader range of researchers and applications.
6. **Unlocking New Levels of Efficiency and Reusability:** The vision is to create specialized teachers that can efficiently produce high-quality distillation data and cold-start future RL iterations, potentially unlocking greater reusability across tasks and significantly reducing the overall resources needed for developing reasoning LMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the RLT framework lies in a novel task formulation, a dense reward function, and a tailored training paradigm, designed to directly address the exploration challenges of traditional RL and the objective mismatch in distillation.

#### 3.1. Task Formulation: Explaining Solutions, Not Solving from Scratch

The most significant departure from traditional RL reasoning is the RLT's task. Instead of prompting the LM to *solve* a problem `q` from scratch (as in Figure 2, Left), RLTs are given both the `question (q)` and the `solution (s)` as input (Figure 2, Right). Their explicit task is to generate an instructive, step-by-step *explanation* that "connects the dots" between the question and the provided solution. This formulation simplifies the problem for the LM, as it no longer needs to explore for a correct answer but rather focus on *how* to present a clear path to a known answer. The prompt design ensures that the RLT's output explanations can be directly repurposed for student distillation by simply replacing specific tags to format them as `think` tokens.

#### 3.2. Dense Reward Function for Explanation Quality

To provide continuous and informative feedback, the RLT framework introduces a novel dense reward function `r_RLT` composed of two primary terms, `r_SS` and `r_KL`, with carefully chosen reduction methods and weighting:

- **`r_SS` (Student Solution Understanding):** This term quantifies how well a *separate student model* `π_s` understands the ground-truth solution `s_i` given the question `q_i` and the RLT's generated explanation (think tokens `t_o_i`). It is computed from the student's log probabilities over the solution tokens. To prevent the RLT from just focusing on high-probability parts of the solution, `r_SS` combines both the average and minimum of these per-token log probabilities:
 `r_SS = avg{log π_s(s_i | t_o_i, q_i)} + α * min{log π_s(s_i | t_o_i, q_i)}`.
 A higher `r_SS` indicates that the student finds the explanation highly supportive in predicting the correct solution.

- **`r_KL` (Think Tokens Interpretability):** This term regularizes the RLT's explanations, ensuring they are logical continuations from the student's perspective. It measures the KL divergence between the RLT's output distribution over the think tokens `t_o_i` (conditioned on both `q_i` and `s_i`) and the *student's* distribution over the same `t_o_i` (conditioned only on `q_i`). This prevents the teacher from generating "shortcuts" or steps that only make sense *with* the solution in mind but are not logically derivable from the question alone for the student. Similar to `r_SS`, it combines average and maximum KL divergence values:
 `r_KL = avg{D_KL(π_θ(t_o_i | s_i, q_i) || π_s(t_o_i | q_i))} + α * max{D_KL(π_θ(t_o_i | s_i, q_i) || π_s(t_o_i | q_i))}`.
 This term encourages explanations that are interpretable and follow a logical path learnable by the student, rather than just repeating parts of the solution.

- **Combined RLT Reward:** The final reward for the RLT is `r_RLT_i = r_SS(o_i, s_i, q_i) - λ * r_KL(o_i, s_i, q_i)`. The negative sign for `r_KL` means the RLT is penalized for explanations that are too divergent from the student's natural reasoning path.

#### 3.3. RLT Training Paradigm

The RLTs are trained using a modified reinforcement learning objective. The authors employ the GRPO (Generalized Policy Optimization) algorithm, a lightweight online RL method that avoids a critic model. The objective is adapted to use the dense `r_RLT` reward function:
`J_RLT(θ) = E[A_RLT_i - β D_KL(π_θ || π_ref)]`.
`A_RLT_i` are advantages computed from the `r_RLT` rewards.

- **Base Model:** A small, off-the-shelf Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct LM is used as the base model for the RLT.
- **Pre-training Phase:** A short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) phase is performed to familiarize the Qwen-7B with the new RLT system prompt and input format, using a small dataset of pre-collected reasoning samples.
- **RL Phase:** The main RL training is conducted for a relatively short duration (125 steps, less than one epoch) with a batch size of 1024, using the GRPO algorithm with the designed dense reward. A separate Qwen-7B model is used as the student for reward computation.
- **Efficiency:** The training is conducted on a single compute node with 8 H100 GPUs, highlighting its computational efficiency compared to prior work.

#### 3.4. Student Distillation and Evaluation

- **Data Generation:** RLTs generate distillation datasets by producing explanations for a set of question-solution pairs. Crucially, these raw RLT outputs are used *directly* for student fine-tuning, without any additional filtering for correctness (guaranteed by input solutions) or heuristic postprocessing. This contrasts sharply with existing pipelines.
- **Student Training:** Student LMs (both 7B and 32B Qwen models) are fine-tuned on these RLT-generated distillation datasets, following similar recipes and hyperparameters as existing baselines to ensure fair comparison.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:** Performance is assessed on challenging reasoning tasks: AIME 2024, MATH 500, and GPQA Diamond. Additional experiments include LiveCodeBench (coding) and OlympiadBench (multilingual).
- **Cold-Starting RL:** RLT-generated data is also evaluated for its effectiveness in cold-starting traditional RL (with correctness-based rewards) for a student model.
- **Zero-Shot Transfer:** The ability of RLTs to transfer zero-shot to entirely new, out-of-distribution tasks (e.g., the Countdown game) to generate distillation data is tested.

This systematic approach of redefining the teacher's role and crafting a dense, purpose-aligned reward function allows the RLT framework to address the core limitations of RL in reasoning LMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results robustly support the efficacy and efficiency of the Reinforcement-Learned Teachers (RLT) framework, demonstrating significant advantages over existing distillation and cold-starting pipelines.

#### 4.1. Superior Distillation Performance for Students of Various Sizes

- **Outperforming Larger LMs:** A key finding is that the raw output explanations from a lightweight 7B RLT consistently provide higher final performance on challenging competition and graduate-level tasks (AIME, MATH, GPQA) compared to existing pipelines that rely on reasoning LMs with *orders of magnitude more parameters* (e.g., DeepSeek R1) and extensive heuristic postprocessing.
- **Consistency Across Data Sizes:** The effectiveness of RLT traces remains consistent whether distilling from a full 17K dataset or a randomly sampled 1K subset, suggesting robust data quality. In contrast, existing pipelines (like Bespoke) show significant performance drops when their data is subsampled.
- **Distilling Larger Students:** RLTs prove effective even when distilling much larger students. A 7B RLT successfully enables a 32B student to achieve superior performance over all prior methods, indicating the framework's ability to "punch above its weight" and transfer knowledge to more capable models.

#### 4.2. Enhanced Cold-Starting for Traditional RL

- RLT-generated reasoning traces demonstrate superior effectiveness in providing cold-start data for traditional RL. When a Qwen-7B model is cold-started with RLT traces for further RL optimization, it achieves better final performance than models cold-started with raw or even GPT-postprocessed traces from RL-trained 7B teachers. This suggests that RLTs produce higher quality and more informative initialization data for subsequent RL phases.

#### 4.3. Remarkable Zero-Shot Transferability to Out-of-Domain Tasks

- A significant and surprising finding is that RLTs can be transferred **zero-shot** to entirely new, out-of-distribution reasoning domains (e.g., the Countdown task) and still produce effective distillation datasets.
- Remarkably, students distilled from these zero-shot RLT explanations achieve even *higher performance* than direct RL optimization on the Countdown task itself, or students trained on the original reasoning questions. This suggests that RLTs learn a generalizable "teaching" skill rather than domain-specific reasoning, making them highly reusable.

#### 4.4. Validation of the Dense Reward Function

- An analysis of the RLT reward function demonstrates a strong correlation between the reward score of generated explanations and the downstream performance of students distilled from those explanations (Pearson coefficient > 0.89). This empirically validates that the dense reward effectively guides the RLT towards producing better teaching data.
- Qualitative analysis reveals that even small models, when guided by this reward, already possess latent "teaching skills."

#### 4.5. Qualitative Improvements in Explanations and Ablation Study Insights

- Qualitative examples show that RLT explanations are more grounded, often include alternative verification steps, and avoid idiosyncratic language or reliance on external tools sometimes seen in explanations from other larger LMs.
- Ablation studies confirm the critical role of each component of the reward function:
 - Removing the `r_KL` term (think tokens KL reward) leads to explanations that merely repeat the solution tokens, resulting in student performance *worse* than the base model. This highlights `r_KL`'s importance in ensuring logical flow and preventing superficial explanations.
 - Removing the min/max reduction terms in the reward leads to overly long and repetitive explanations, though still outperforming baselines. These terms are crucial for efficient and concise explanations.

#### 4.6. Significant Efficiency Gains

- The RLT framework dramatically reduces computational costs. Training a 7B RLT and generating its distillation dataset takes significantly less GPU hours (e.g., 280.4 GPU-hours for training, 6.7 for data generation on H100) compared to the estimated hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours for training and thousands for data generation with models like DeepSeek R1. This efficiency is a core practical benefit.

In summary, the main findings highlight that RLTs provide a more effective, efficient, and versatile paradigm for generating high-quality reasoning data for LM distillation and RL cold-starts, largely by reframing the teacher's task and utilizing a dense, purpose-aligned reward function.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Reinforcement Learning Teachers of Test Time Scaling" paper introduces a paradigm shift in how reinforcement learning can be leveraged for language model reasoning and knowledge distillation, with several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Democratization of Advanced LM Reasoning:** Perhaps the most profound impact is the potential to democratize access to advanced LM reasoning capabilities. By significantly reducing the computational costs associated with RL-based reasoning (requiring a 7B RLT teacher compared to orders of magnitude larger LMs), the framework lowers the barrier for researchers and institutions without access to massive computing resources. This could foster broader participation and innovation in the field, moving beyond a reliance on a few well-resourced labs.

2. **Increased Efficiency and Sustainability:** The substantial reduction in GPU hours for both training RLTs and generating distillation datasets translates directly into greater efficiency and potentially more sustainable AI development. Less energy consumption and fewer specialized hardware requirements make advanced LM training more environmentally friendly and economically viable.

3. **Reframing RL for LMs: From Problem-Solving to Explanation-Generation:** The work fundamentally redefines the role of RL in language models. By shifting the objective from "solving problems from scratch" to "generating instructive explanations for known solutions," the authors cleverly sidestep the intractable exploration challenge of sparse rewards. This new task formulation, guided by dense rewards, is more tractable and aligns the teacher's training perfectly with its downstream utility, potentially inspiring new RL objectives and applications in other AI domains.

4. **Automated and High-Quality Data Generation:** The RLT framework offers a robust, automated, and highly effective method for generating high-quality reasoning traces. This reduces the reliance on expensive human annotations, costly proprietary APIs, or complex, heuristic-driven postprocessing steps that are prevalent in current distillation pipelines. The raw outputs of RLTs are directly usable, simplifying the entire data creation process.

5. **Enhanced Model Generalization and Reusability:** The demonstrated zero-shot transferability of RLTs to out-of-distribution tasks is a powerful testament to their generalizability. This suggests that RLTs learn a more universal "teaching" skill rather than task-specific reasoning, making them highly reusable across diverse domains without requiring extensive retraining. This reusability can accelerate model development and deployment.

6. **Improved Student Learning and Cold-Starting:** The superior performance of students distilled from RLT explanations, and the enhanced effectiveness of RLT traces for cold-starting traditional RL, indicate that these teachers produce genuinely better learning material. This can lead to more capable and robust student models, as well as more stable and efficient subsequent RL training iterations.

7. **Future Research Directions:** The paper opens up exciting avenues for future research, such as the co-training of RLTs and students, allowing teachers to adapt explanations to student learning dynamics in real-time. The idea of a single model simultaneously acting as both teacher and student, through iterative self-distillation guided by this new RL framework, is particularly intriguing and could lead to highly autonomous and self-improving AI systems. Exploring larger RLTs and broader, more diverse distillation datasets will also further push the capabilities of this framework.

In conclusion, "Reinforcement Learning Teachers of Test Time Scaling" presents a significant advancement in the field of large language models. By ingeniously re-conceptualizing the teacher's role within an RL framework, the authors have introduced a highly efficient, effective, and generalizable method for enhancing reasoning capabilities and knowledge distillation, promising to make advanced AI more accessible and sustainable.
