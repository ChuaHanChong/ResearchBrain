# Consistency Policy: Accelerated Visuomotor Policies via Consistency Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Consistency Policy accelerates visuomotor control by distilling state-of-the-art diffusion models into faster student networks, achieving an order of magnitude faster inference speeds. This approach maintains competitive success rates across various simulation and real-world robot manipulation tasks, enabling deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art diffusion-based visuomotor policies (e.g., Diffusion Policy) suffer from slow inference speeds (often ~1 second per action) due to requiring many sequential denoising steps.
- This high inference latency and computational demand make these policies impractical for real-time control in dynamic robotic tasks or for deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms (e.g., mobile manipulators, quadrotors).
- Existing acceleration methods for diffusion models either degrade sample quality, increase memory requirements, or are not directly tailored for high-dimensional visuomotor control.

## Method

- A two-stage training process is employed: first, a multi-step EDM (Elucidating the ==Design Space== of Diffusion-Based Generative Models) diffusion model is trained as a teacher policy.
- Second, a faster student model (**Consistency Policy**) is distilled from the pre-trained teacher using a combined objective of Denoising Score Matching (DSM) loss and Consistency Trajectory Model (CTM) loss.
- The Consistency Policy enables single-step or few-step inference (e.g., 3-step chaining) by learning to perform large denoising steps along the Probability Flow ODE trajectory, directly predicting actions from noisy inputs.

## Results

- Consistency Policy achieves inference speeds of 1-3 Network Function Evaluations (NFE), compared to 9-27+ NFE for Diffusion Policy variants, translating to wall-clock times of 1ms (simulation) and 20-22ms (real-world on laptop GPU).
- It demonstrates an order-of-magnitude speedup (up to **9x**-**100x** faster than Diffusion Policy) while maintaining competitive success rates, often matching or surpassing diffusion baselines on tasks like Robomimic and real-world manipulation.
- The distilled policy exhibits robustness to the quality of the pre-trained teacher model, simplifying the practical application of the distillation process.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "CTM-local" consistency objective provides an effective balance between performance and training efficiency for visuomotor policy distillation.
> - Reducing the variance of the initial noise sample used for inference significantly improves the distilled policy's performance by keeping the trajectory within a more stable distribution.
> - Dropout within the Consistency Trajectory Model (CTM) objective is crucial for providing sufficient training signal and preventing the network from trivializing the consistency task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

**The Rise of Diffusion Models in Robotics:**
The field of robot learning has seen significant advancements with the adoption of deep learning techniques. Recently, diffusion models, originally popularized in image and audio generation, have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in imitation learning for robot control. Specifically, "Diffusion Policy" emerged as a leading approach, showcasing impressive capabilities across various robotics tasks by modeling action sequences as distributions that can be denoised. These models excel at handling the inherent multimodality and complexity of robot action spaces, learning from expert demonstrations to predict intricate control trajectories.

**The Inference Speed Bottleneck:**
Despite their superior performance, a significant drawback of traditional diffusion models, including Diffusion Policy, is their computational cost during inference. Diffusion models generate outputs by iteratively denoising from an initial noisy state, requiring multiple sequential forward evaluations of a neural network. For instance, the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) variant used by Diffusion Policy often requires 100+ denoising steps, translating to roughly one second per action generation on high-end GPUs. This latency is prohibitive for many real-world robotic applications, especially those involving dynamic environments or resource-constrained platforms (e.g., mobile robots with limited on-board compute, weight, and power budgets). Tasks like object balancing or high-speed navigation demand much faster control frequencies than current diffusion policies can provide, limiting their practical deployment to quasi-static tasks.

**Existing Acceleration Techniques and Other Policy Paradigms:**
To address the speed issue, several lines of research have emerged:
- **Reduced Denoising Steps:** Methods like Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDiM) and EDM allow for variable (and fewer) denoising steps, interpreting diffusion as solving a deterministic Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE). However, significantly reducing steps often degrades sample quality.
- **Parallel Sampling:** Techniques like ParaDiGMS attempt to parallelize the denoising process. While promising, these often drastically increase memory requirements, making them unsuitable for memory-constrained on-board robot compute.
- **Distillation:** Widely used in the image generation domain, distillation involves training a smaller, faster "student" model to mimic the behavior of a larger, slower "teacher" model. Consistency models are a specific type of distillation that train models to achieve single-step or few-step generation by enforcing self-consistency along diffusion ODE trajectories. This paradigm allows for very fast inference while maintaining high quality.
- **Non-Diffusion Baselines:** Before diffusion models, transformer-based policies like Behavioral Transformer and RT-1 were prominent. While powerful, these often require massive pre-training datasets or external cloud computation, and diffusion policies like Octo have shown improvements over them. This paper specifically avoids direct comparison with very large language/vision models (e.g., RT-2) that leverage cloud-based resources, as its focus is on on-board, resource-constrained inference.

**Contribution to the Landscape:**
"Consistency Policy" directly contributes to the critical problem of real-time deployment for high-performance robot learning. By adapting consistency distillation, a technique proven effective in image generation, to the robotics domain, this work offers a novel solution to the inference speed bottleneck of state-of-the-art diffusion policies. It positions itself as a faster and competitive alternative to Diffusion Policy, overcoming its primary limitation without compromising accuracy. This research is concurrent with other efforts applying consistency models to state-based continuous control, but distinguishes itself by focusing on visuomotor policies and high-dimensional action spaces, and demonstrating effectiveness through direct distillation of a pre-trained teacher model rather than teacher-free consistency training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a visuomotor policy that can perform low-latency decision-making in resource-constrained robotic setups, effectively addressing the primary limitation of existing state-of-the-art diffusion-based policies: their slow inference speed.

The motivation stems from several critical observations regarding the practical deployment of robot learning models:
1. **Computational Constraints on Robots:** Many robotic systems, such as mobile manipulators, quadrotors, or smaller research platforms, cannot be equipped with high-end GPUs due to limitations in space, weight, and power. This makes it impossible for them to directly leverage recent advancements in visuomotor policy architectures that demand significant computational resources for fast inference.
2. **Inference Latency of Diffusion Policies:** While Diffusion Policy has demonstrated impressive imitation learning performance, its reliance on multiple sequential denoising steps (e.g., 100+ for DDPM) leads to inference times of around one second per action. Such slow reaction times render these policies impractical for dynamic tasks (e.g., balancing, navigating dynamic environments) or tasks requiring high control frequencies.
3. **Need for Real-Time Control:** For robots to interact effectively and safely with their environments, especially in unstructured or dynamic settings, fast and low-latency control is essential. Enabling sub-100ms policy inference is crucial for reactive behaviors.
4. **Leveraging Advances in Generative Models:** The success of consistency models in image generation, which can produce high-quality outputs in a single or few steps from complex diffusion models, presents a promising avenue for acceleration in robotics. The authors are motivated to adapt these insights to visuomotor control.

Therefore, the **key objectives** are:
- To drastically reduce the inference time of visuomotor policies learned via imitation learning, specifically building upon the success of Diffusion Policy.
- To achieve this acceleration while maintaining competitive or superior success rates compared to existing diffusion-based methods.
- To develop a policy that is suitable for deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms.
- To explore and validate the key design decisions (e.g., consistency objective, initial sample variance, chaining steps) that enable high performance for the distilled policy.
- To demonstrate the robustness of the distillation process to the quality of the pretrained teacher model, simplifying practical application.

In essence, the paper seeks to bridge the gap between high-performance, computationally intensive policy learning and the practical demands of real-world robotic deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of Consistency Policy's methodology lies in adapting the concept of consistency distillation from the image generation domain to visuomotor robot control. This involves a two-stage training process: first, training a teacher diffusion model, and then distilling this teacher into a faster student model (the Consistency Policy).

**Preliminaries: Diffusion Models as Probability Flow ODEs:**
The paper begins by framing diffusion models in terms of Probability Flow ODEs (PFODEs). A diffusion model learns to map a noisy input (e.g., an action sequence corrupted with Gaussian noise) at time `T` to a clean, desired output (the expert action sequence) at time `0`. This mapping is achieved by estimating the "score function" (gradient of the noised data distribution) which guides the denoising process backwards along the ODE trajectory. Standard diffusion models like DDPM iterate many times (e.g., 100 steps) to traverse this trajectory, leading to slow inference.

**Two-Stage Training: Teacher and Student Models:**

- **Teacher Model (EDM):**
 The first stage involves training a teacher model, denoted `s_ϕ`, using the EDM (Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models) framework. EDM is chosen because it's a multi-step framework more commonly used for consistency distillation. The EDM teacher learns to estimate the derivative of the PFODE's trajectory, effectively predicting the initial clean action `x_0` given a noisy action `x_t` and time `t`. It is trained using the Denoising Score Matching (DSM) loss (`L_DSM`), which minimizes the pseudo-huber loss between the model's prediction of `x_0` and the actual `x_0`. The teacher model, like Diffusion Policy, uses a 1D Convolutional UNet architecture, conditioned on observations.

- **Student Model (Consistency Policy):**
 The second and crucial stage is distilling the trained EDM teacher into a student model, `g_θ`, which is the Consistency Policy. This student model is designed to perform large denoising steps, ideally single-step or few-step inference. The student network takes in a noisy action `x_t`, its current time `t`, a target earlier time `s` (where `s < t`), and the observation `o`, and learns to predict `x_s`.
 The student is trained with a combined objective:
 - **DSM Loss (`L_DSM`):** This is the same objective as used for the teacher, ensuring the student can directly predict the clean action `x_0` from a noisy input `x_t`.
 - **Consistency Trajectory Model (CTM) Loss (`L_CTM`):** Adapted from Kim et al., this is the core of the distillation. Intuitively, it enforces self-consistency: if two different noisy points, `x_t` and `x_u` (`u < t`), lie on the *same* PFODE trajectory, then when both are denoised to an even earlier common time `s`, their predictions (`g_θ(x_t, t, s; o)` and `g_θ(x_u, u, s; o)`) should ideally be the same. The loss is calculated after bringing both `x_s` predictions back to time `0` using the student model itself (with stopgrad on this final step to stabilize training). This "consistency" signal encourages the student to learn larger, robust denoising steps.
 - **Total Loss:** `L_CP = αL_CTM + βL_DSM`, where `α` and `β` are tunable hyperparameters.
 - **Architecture:** The student uses the same 1D Convolutional UNet as the teacher, but with expanded FiLM blocks to accommodate conditioning on the stop timestep `s`. It is warm-started with the trained teacher model's parameters, and the expanded layers are zero-initialized for faster convergence.

**Inference Procedures: Speed vs. Accuracy:**
Consistency Policy offers a trade-off between inference speed and accuracy, allowing users to choose based on task requirements:
- **Single-step Inference:** For maximum speed, the policy samples initial noise `z` from `N(0, I)` (unit Gaussian), then directly predicts the action `x = g_θ(z, T, 0; o)` in a single forward pass.
- **3-step Inference:** For improved accuracy with still significant speedup, the policy chains three denoising steps. It first performs a single-step denoise from `T` to `0`, then noises the resulting prediction back to an intermediate timestep `t_1`, denoising again to `0`. This process is repeated for another intermediate timestep `t_2`. This back-and-forth refinement improves prediction quality.

**Key Design Decisions and Implementation Details:**
The paper highlights several design choices crucial for Consistency Policy's performance:
1. **Choice of Consistency Objective:** The authors found that a "CTM-local" objective (adjacent `t` and `u` timesteps with arbitrary `s < u < t`) performed best, balancing competitive success rates with efficient training compared to the generalized CTM or original Consistency Distillation.
2. **Reduced Initial Sample Variance:** Instead of sampling the initial noisy input `z` from `N(0, T^2I)` (the standard unnormalizing initial sample for EDM), they used `N(0, I)` for single-step inference. This pushes the initial point closer to the mean of the normal distribution, empirically leading to better performance by keeping the trajectory more "in-distribution" and preventing outliers.
3. **Preset Chaining Steps:** For multi-step inference, they prioritize chaining from "early-middle" timesteps by subdividing the *discretized* time mesh (e.g., `t_{2N/3}, t_{N/3}`) rather than the continuous time interval. This strategy provides more important features and details during refinement, particularly for harder tasks.
4. **Role of Dropout:** Dropout, typically used for regularization, was found to be critical for providing sufficient training signal to the CTM objective, especially during the `s -> 0` denoising step. Disabling dropout in this specific region led to decreased success rates.
5. **Robustness to Teacher Quality:** The training procedure was found to be robust; the student's performance did not degrade significantly even when distilled from slightly suboptimal teacher models. This is attributed to the `L_DSM` component of the loss.
6. **Comparison to Consistency Training:** Direct consistency training (teacher-free, using Monte Carlo estimates) performed poorly for high-dimensional visuomotor policies compared to the teacher-distilled approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of Consistency Policy against Diffusion Policy baselines across multiple simulation and real-world robotic tasks, focusing on two key metrics: inference speed and success rate.

**Simulation Experiments:**
- **Tasks:** Evaluated on 6 diverse tasks:
 - **Robomimic Suite (Image-based):** Lift, Can, Square, Tool Hang (increasing difficulty).
 - **Push-T (State-based):** Pushing a T-shaped block to a target.
 - **Franka Kitchen (State-based, long-horizon):** Multi-stage task involving manipulating objects in a kitchen.
- **Baselines:** DDPM and DDiM variants of Diffusion Policy, with optimistic speedups incorporated from ParaDiGMS (e.g., DDPM NFE reduced from 100 to 27, DDiM NFE from 15 to 9).
- **Key Results (Table I & II):**
 - **Inference Speed (NFE):** Consistency Policy (CP) achieves NFE of 1 (single-step) or 3 (3-step), an order of magnitude faster than DDiM (9 NFE) and DDPM (27 NFE).
 - **Success Rates (Robomimic & Push-T):**
 - Single-step CP consistently performs competitively, often falling between DDPM and DDiM, or even outperforming them on simpler tasks (e.g., Lift, Can).
 - 3-step CP consistently matches or surpasses DDPM in accuracy, while being 3x faster than DDiM and 9x faster than DDPM. On harder tasks like Square and Tool Hang, 3-step CP notably improves over single-step CP.
 - **Franka Kitchen:** CP shows strong performance for the initial stages but slightly struggles in later, longer-horizon stages compared to DDPM, though it remains competitive.
 - **Wall Clock Time (Table III):** On an NVIDIA P5000 GPU, single-step CP achieved 1ms inference time, compared to 11ms for DDiM (15 steps) and 110ms for DDPM (100 steps), confirming the order-of-magnitude speedup.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- **Tasks:** Evaluated on three real-world tasks using a Franka Panda robot (Trash Clean Up, Plug Insertion) and a mobile manipulator (Microwave). Inference was performed on a laptop GPU (NVIDIA 3070 Ti, 8GB VRAM), highlighting the resource-constrained setting.
- **Baselines:** DDiM Diffusion Policy (15 steps) was chosen due to its faster inference and practicality on the limited hardware (ParaDiGMS was infeasible due to VRAM constraints).
- **Key Results (Table IV):**
 - **Trash Clean Up & Plug Insertion:** CP achieved identical success rates (0.8 and 0.7 respectively) as DDiM, but with dramatically lower inference times (21-22ms for CP vs. 192-198ms for DDiM), roughly a 9x speedup.
 - **Microwave (Mobile Manipulation):** CP performed slightly worse (0.4 success rate) than DDiM (0.5), though still demonstrating fast inference. The authors note that the increased training time for distillation on this complex, long-horizon task might have limited CP's final performance.

**Ablation Studies:**
The paper rigorously ablates key design choices, primarily using Robomimic Square and Tool Hang tasks:
- **Consistency Objective (Table V):** "CTM-local" (adjacent `t, u`, arbitrary `s`) provided the best balance of performance (comparable to full CTM) and training efficiency (as fast as Consistency Distillation, 40% faster than CTM).
- **Initial Sample Variance (Table VI):** Sampling from a low-variance Gaussian (`N(0, 1/T^2)`) significantly improved success rates for both single-step (92% vs 90%) and 3-step (96% vs 91%) CP compared to standard high-variance sampling (`N(0, 1)`), particularly for multi-step. This suggests that keeping initial samples "in-distribution" is crucial.
- **Preset Chaining Steps (Table VII):** Subdividing *discretized* time for chaining steps yielded better performance (96% on Square, 77% on Tool Hang) than subdividing continuous time (94% on Square, 72% on Tool Hang), especially for harder tasks.
- **Teacher Model Quality (Table VIII):** CP demonstrated robustness to teacher quality. Even with a teacher that performed worse (e.g., 84% success rate), the student CP still achieved a respectable 88% success rate, indicating that the DSM loss component helps maintain performance.
- **Role of Dropout in CTM Objective (Table IX):** Disabling dropout during the `s -> 0` generation step within the CTM loss significantly decreased success rates (86% vs 92%), suggesting dropout is crucial for providing effective training signal by preventing the network from trivializing the consistency objective.
- **Consistency Training vs. Distillation (Table X):** "Consistency Training" (teacher-free, Monte Carlo estimate) performed poorly on visuomotor tasks (e.g., 55% on Square) compared to the teacher-distilled Consistency Policy (92%), validating the necessity of a pre-trained teacher for high-dimensional policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Consistency Policy" framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly for the practical deployment of visuomotor control policies.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Enabling On-Board Deployment:** The most profound impact is enabling the deployment of high-performance visuomotor policies on resource-constrained robotic systems. By achieving an order-of-magnitude reduction in inference latency (e.g., 20ms range on a laptop GPU), Consistency Policy makes state-of-the-art imitation learning viable for mobile manipulators, quadrotors, and other platforms where space, weight, and power budgets preclude high-end GPUs or cloud-based computation. This directly addresses a critical barrier to real-world robot autonomy.
- **Real-time Reactive Control:** The dramatically faster inference speeds allow robots to operate with lower latency, facilitating more real-time reactive behaviors. This opens up new possibilities for dynamic tasks that require rapid decision-making, such as balancing objects, active perception, or navigating dynamic environments, which were previously impractical with slow diffusion policies.
- **Bridging Research and Application:** Consistency Policy bridges the gap between theoretical advancements in generative models and the practical requirements of robotics. It demonstrates how techniques from other domains (image generation) can be successfully adapted to solve long-standing problems in robotics.
- **Robust and Efficient Distillation:** The robustness of Consistency Policy to teacher quality, as shown in ablations, is a significant practical advantage. Practitioners do not need to extensively fine-tune or validate the teacher model, streamlining the overall training and deployment pipeline. The insights into the role of dropout and optimal chaining steps also provide valuable guidance for future implementations.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a strong foundation for future research in accelerating robot learning. The authors explicitly mention exploring ways to re-introduce multimodality into Consistency Policy and applying it to new robot platforms (legged, winged), indicating a clear path for further development.

#### 5.2. Limitations

Despite its significant advantages, the paper also acknowledges several limitations:
- **Loss of Multimodality:** Unlike stochastic diffusion models (like DDPM), Consistency Policy's reliance on deterministic ODE trajectories for distillation may lead to a loss of multimodality in the action distribution. While this did not negatively impact performance on the tested tasks, it could be a factor in highly multimodal scenarios.
- **Training Stability and Time:** Consistency Policy training is reported to be slightly less stable than Diffusion Policy, likely due to the self-referential nature of the consistency objective. The distillation process also requires more overall training time (both more epochs and longer per-epoch time due to teacher model evaluations) compared to training a standalone Diffusion Policy, especially for harder tasks like Microwave. This means a trade-off between deployment efficiency and training overhead.
- **Suboptimal Mobile Manipulation Performance:** The slight underperformance on the Microwave mobile manipulation task compared to DDiM could indicate challenges in distilling policies for very long-horizon, complex, and potentially multimodal tasks, or simply due to insufficient training time.

#### 5.3. Future Work

The authors propose several exciting avenues for future research:
- Exploring methods to re-introduce multimodality into Consistency Policy through more complex sampling schemes.
- Deeper investigation into the dynamics of chaining steps and the specific role of dropout in the `s -> 0` generations.
- Porting Consistency Policy to new robot platforms that can greatly benefit from fast inference speeds, such as legged robots, quadrotors, and other high-dynamic mobile systems.

#### 5.4. Conclusion

"Accelerated Visuomotor Policies via Consistency Distillation" introduces Consistency Policy, a novel framework that significantly boosts the inference speed of visuomotor robot control policies derived from state-of-the-art diffusion models. By successfully adapting consistency distillation techniques from image generation to the robotics domain, the authors demonstrate an order-of-magnitude increase in inference speeds (up to 9x-100x faster than Diffusion Policy baselines) while maintaining competitive or even superior success rates across a diverse suite of simulation and real-world manipulation tasks.

The paper meticulously details the two-stage training process involving an EDM teacher and a Consistency Policy student, highlighting crucial design choices such as the "CTM-local" consistency objective, reduced initial sample variance, and specific chaining step strategies for multi-step inference. Ablation studies provide deep insights into the efficacy of these design decisions and demonstrate the robustness of Consistency Policy to teacher model quality.

While acknowledging trade-offs in training stability and a potential loss of multimodality, this work delivers a highly practical solution to a critical bottleneck in robot learning. Consistency Policy paves the way for deploying high-performance, learning-based robot control on resource-constrained platforms, thereby enabling real-time reactive behaviors and expanding the scope of tasks that modern robots can tackle autonomously. This research represents a vital step towards making advanced robot intelligence a practical reality in diverse real-world applications.
