# How to Learn from What a Human Would Avoid? Intervention-Aware World Models with Real-World RL for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> WHIRL is a safety-aware real-world RL framework for a 16-DoF dexterous hand that turns binary human pedal takeovers into forward-looking risk predictions. A latent world model adds a per-state intervention-probability head alongside dynamics, reward, and termination; that head shapes a residual actor away from takeover-prone states, reaching 96.7% success on irregular-object grasping while cutting step-weighted operator intervention by up to 84%.

## Problem

- Dexterous hands have high-dimensional coupled arm-hand action spaces where each exploratory mistake costs real robot time, resets, and hardware wear, making online RL prohibitively expensive.
- Existing human-in-the-loop pipelines consume each takeover once as a behavior-cloning mask, replay weight, or metric, discarding the operator's forward-looking judgment about impending failure.
- Prior world-model human-in-the-loop work has operators intervene inside the learned model as a sandbox, and long imagined rollouts compound dynamics error under small real-robot data budgets.

## Method

- **Intervention-aware world model**: four heads on a detached critic latent predict ==dynamics ensemble==, reward, termination, and next-step takeover probability, trained jointly with a label-availability mask on pedal supervision.
- **Actor-side risk shaping**: a model utility subtracts weighted termination, predicted takeover probability, and ensemble disagreement, gated by uncertainty, while the critic's imagined ==Bellman target== deliberately excludes the takeover head.
- **Tri-channel teleoperation** with ==residual RL==: SpaceMouse arm, Manus glove hand, pedal takeover, a morphology-routed retargeter with delta-joint rebase, and hand residuals confined to a 3-dimensional ==postural synergy basis==.

## Results

- WHIRL beats the strongest model-free residual HIL baseline by **15-30 points** on all five tasks, hitting **100%** on Pick Cube, **96.7%** on Pick LEGO, **90%** Pick and Place, **95%** Pull Drawer, **85%** Long Horizon.
- Step-weighted rolling intervention fraction on Pull Drawer drops from the baseline's **0.113** to **0.018**, an **84%** reduction, with autonomous success improving simultaneously, ruling out early termination as the cause.
- Ablations show full WHIRL reaches near-zero intervention first; zeroing the actor risk weight while keeping world-model training still lags, and removing the world model entirely is slowest. Fisher one-sided **p below 0.05** on the three pick tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An operator's decision to take over is a forward-looking risk label, not just a correction, and it fires earlier than termination signals because humans act before failure.
> - Route learned behavior predictors into the actor objective, not the reward or Bellman target, so predictor bias shapes exploration without rewriting value estimates or the task optimum.
> - Predictors of human habit inherit that operator's caution threshold, so a differently calibrated operator at deployment may require refitting the risk head.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation, involving multi-fingered hands to grasp, reposition, and place objects, presents a significant challenge in real-world robotics due to its high-dimensional action space and the potential for costly hardware failures. Current approaches to robot learning in this domain include behavior cloning from teleoperated demonstrations and online reinforcement learning (RL). While behavior cloning can establish nominal motions, it often struggles with small variations and deployment shifts not covered in the demonstration set. Online RL offers a method for continuous policy improvement by learning from failures, but its application to high-dimensional dexterous hands is expensive, consuming significant robot time, requiring frequent resets, and demanding constant hardware attention.

Sim-to-real transfer methods alleviate some of this burden by pre-training policies in simulation, but real-robot adaptation remains necessary. Human-in-the-loop (HIL) RL emerged as a compromise, enabling policies to explore while allowing a human operator to intervene before critical failures occur. Existing HIL RL pipelines, such as HIL-SERL and SiLRI, predominantly treat human interventions as reactive corrections. These binary intervention signals are often used once—as a behavior-cloning mask, a replay priority, or a safety metric—and their potential as a predictive safety signal is largely unexploited. The core limitation in the broader landscape is the underutilization of the "rich safety signal" inherent in an operator's decision to take control.

This work positions itself as an advancement in HIL RL by addressing this limitation. It proposes a method to extract reusable, forward-predictive risk signals from human interventions, thereby improving the efficiency and safety of real-world dexterous manipulation learning. By transforming reactive corrections into proactive risk avoidance signals, the research aims to bridge the gap between human intuition about safety and the robot's predictive world modeling capabilities, leading to more robust and less demanding robot training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a safety-aware reinforcement learning framework that can proactively learn from human interventions to avoid risky situations in real-world dexterous manipulation tasks. The central motivating question guiding this work is: "How can we learn from what a human would avoid?"

This question arises from the observation that current Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) RL methodologies treat human interventions primarily as immediate corrections. When an operator takes control, this action provides a binary label indicating a situation the human deemed unsafe or prone to failure. However, existing pipelines often discard the predictive value embedded in this decision. The human operator's choice to intervene is based on an implicit assessment of future risk or impending failure, a signal that is rich with information about undesirable states or trajectories. Current systems typically use this signal only retrospectively for a single step or to re-prioritize past experiences, neglecting its potential to anticipate future unsafe states.

The motivation is multifaceted:
1. **Enhance Safety:** The high-dimensional action space of multi-fingered dexterous hands makes them prone to costly hardware failures during exploratory RL. Learning to predict and avoid intervention-prone states can mitigate these risks.
2. **Reduce Operator Burden:** Human supervision in HIL RL is resource-intensive. Each intervention requires operator attention, resets, and recovery time. By enabling the robot to proactively avoid situations that would trigger human intervention, the framework aims to significantly reduce the cognitive load and fatigue on the human operator.
3. **Improve Learning Efficiency:** Transforming interventions into a predictive risk signal allows the agent to learn from these experiences more effectively and generalize safety knowledge across different states and tasks. This approach aims to move beyond reactive error correction to a more proactive safety-aware exploration.
4. **Integrate Human Intuition:** The research seeks to systematically incorporate the operator's implicit safety thresholds and risk assessments into the robot's decision-making process. This transforms the human's "avoidance behavior" into an explicit, learnable component of the world model.

To achieve these objectives, the researchers propose to develop an intervention-aware latent world model. This model is designed to not only predict standard environmental dynamics, reward, and termination but also to specifically forecast the likelihood of a human takeover at future states. This novel prediction head serves as an actor-side risk-shaping term, guiding the policy away from "intervention-prone" regions and effectively teaching the robot what a human would avoid, thereby closing the loop between human safety intuition and predictive machine learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The WHIRL framework integrates three core components: a tri-channel Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) teleoperation system, a residual reinforcement learning (RL) backbone, and a novel intervention-aware world model.

#### 3.1. Human-in-the-Loop Teleoperation System

The system is designed to facilitate continuous mid-rollout human interventions and precisely log these events.
- **Tri-channel Control:** This interface uses a 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse for arm control, a Manus Quantum glove for dexterous hand control, and a foot pedal to switch between autonomous and human teleoperation modes. The state of the foot pedal (pressed or released) provides a binary indicator, $i_t$, for each transition, serving as both an evaluation metric and a target for the world model's intervention head.
- **Morphology-Routed Hand Retargeter:** To address the specific challenges of a 16-DoF LEAP Hand with a Manus glove, a hybrid retargeting approach is employed. For the non-thumb fingers (index, middle, ring), a per-joint affine map is used, calibrated once from three postures to ensure low latency. For the 4-DoF thumb, a workspace fingertip inverse kinematics (IK) solver is utilized, augmented with an opposition-direction term to maintain robust thumb-index opposition critical for power grasping. This balances robustness with latency requirements.
- **Intervention Rebase for Continuity:** Upon human takeover at time $t_0$, command discontinuities are prevented using a $\Delta$-joint rebase. The commanded LEAP joint state, $q_{L,cmd_t}$, is calculated as $q_{L,robot_{t_0}} + (R(g_t) - R(g_{t_0}))$, where $R(\cdot)$ is the retargeter function and $g_t$ is the Manus glove state. This ensures that the first command matches the robot's current hand pose, and subsequent commands apply only the retargeted glove displacement since the takeover, maintaining a continuous control transition.

#### 3.2. Residual RL Based on a Behavior Prior

WHIRL adopts a residual reinforcement learning paradigm to learn efficient policies.
- **Residual Learning Architecture:** The system builds upon a frozen action-chunk behavior prior, $\pi_{BC}$, derived from teleoperated demonstrations. The executed action, $a_t$, is composed of the behavior prior's action, $a_{BC}(o_t)$, and an online-updated residual policy, $\Delta_\theta(o_t)$. This approach allows the online RL to make localized corrections around a pre-learned competent behavior.
- **Replay Buffer:** The replay buffer, $D$, stores transitions from demonstrations ($D_{demo}$), autonomous exploration ($D_{auto}$), and human interventions ($D_{int}$). Intervention transitions specifically record the operator's command rather than the policy's action, alongside the pedal label $i_t \in \{0, 1\}$.
- **Synergy-Based Hand Exploration:** To mitigate the issues of uncoordinated movements in the high-dimensional hand joint space, the online hand residual is constrained to a low-dimensional postural synergy basis, $W \in R^{K \times 16}$. This ensures that exploratory actions are coordinated and physically plausible.
- **Actor Optimization:** The actor is trained to maximize return, incorporating regularization terms for both arm and hand residuals. The hand regularizer operates in synergy space, promoting coordinated postural changes. The actor's loss function includes a Q-value term and L2 penalties on the arm and synergy-space hand residuals.

#### 3.3. Intervention-Aware World Model

This is the central novel component of WHIRL, designed to learn predictive risk signals from human interventions.
- **Latent World Model Structure:** The world model operates on a detached latent representation $z_t = E(s_t)$, derived from the critic's encoder. Given a latent state-action pair $(z_t, a_t)$, it employs four distinct prediction heads:
 1. **Dynamics Ensemble:** An ensemble of models predicts the next latent state, $\hat{z}^{(m)}_{t+1}$. The disagreement among ensemble members provides an epistemic uncertainty estimate, $u(z_t, a_t)$.
 2. **Reward Probability:** Predicts the likelihood of receiving a positive reward, $\hat{r}_{t+1}$.
 3. **Termination:** Predicts the likelihood of task termination, $\hat{d}_{t+1}$.
 4. **Intervention Probability:** This novel head predicts the probability of a human takeover at the *next* step, $\hat{p}_{intv_{t+1}}$. It is trained using the binary pedal label $i_{t+1}$ as a forward predictor.
- **World Model Training:** The world model is jointly trained using a combined loss function: Mean Squared Error (MSE) for dynamics prediction and Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) for reward, termination, and intervention predictions. A mask $m_t$ is applied to the intervention loss to account for replay segments without logged takeover states.
- **Critic Path (Uncertainty-Gated Critic Targets):** The dynamics, reward, and termination heads provide imagined targets for critic learning. The critic, $Q(z, [a, a_{BC}])$, is base-aware, considering both the executed action and the behavior-prior action. The world model generates an imagined target, $y_{imag}$, and an auxiliary critic loss, $L_{WM_{critic}}$, gated by an uncertainty estimate $g_u$. This term is added to the standard Bellman loss for real transitions, but the intervention probability head is deliberately excluded from the critic's Bellman target to prevent operator habits from directly altering return estimates.
- **Actor Path (Actor-Side Risk Shaping):** The actor leverages the intervention head through a model-derived utility function, $J_{WM}(z, a) = \hat{r} - \alpha_d \hat{d} - \alpha_i \hat{p}_{intv} - \alpha_u u(z, a)$. This utility includes penalties for termination, intervention probability, and epistemic uncertainty. The term $\hat{p}_{intv}$ is crucial, providing an earlier risk signal than termination alone. The world model gradients are detached during actor and critic updates to ensure shaping influences exploration without distorting world model training.
- **Design Choice: Actor Shaping over Reward Bonus:** The intervention probability $\hat{p}_{intv}$ is used as an actor-side shaping term rather than a direct reward bonus. This choice is based on four properties: optimality invariance (operator behavior is not a true cost), decoupling from credit assignment (avoids propagating WM bias into value learning), no conflict with reward calibration, and a clean ablation surface for mechanism analysis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The WHIRL framework was evaluated on a 16-DoF LEAP Hand across five real-world dexterous manipulation tasks, ranging from simple object grasping (Pick Cube, Pick LEGO) to multi-stage long-horizon tasks (Pull Drawer + Place + Close). The evaluation used a progressive baseline ladder: Behavior prior ($\pi_{BC}$), ResFiT, Residual RL (model-free), and WHIRL. Metrics included real-robot success rate (autonomous execution) and step-weighted rolling intervention fraction (operator burden).

**Q1: Final Task Success**
WHIRL demonstrated consistent improvements in autonomous success rates compared to the strongest model-free HIL baseline (Residual RL model-free).
- **Quantitative Improvement:** WHIRL achieved success rates of 100% on Pick Cube, 96.7% on Pick LEGO, 90.0% on Pick & Place, 95.0% on Pull Drawer, and 85.0% on Long Horizon. These figures represent a 15 to 30 percentage point increase over the Residual RL model-free baseline across all tasks. For instance, on Pick & Place, WHIRL achieved 90% success compared to 60% for the baseline.
- **Statistical Evidence:** Fisher’s exact test yielded p < 0.05 for the three pick tasks (Pick Cube, Pick LEGO, Pick & Place), indicating statistically significant improvements. For Pull Drawer and Long Horizon, the p-values were approximately 0.08, which the authors suggest are indicative given the smaller sample sizes for these tasks. The results show that the improvement consistently appears with the addition of the world model and its intervention-aware components.

**Q2: Operator Intervention Burden**
The framework effectively reduced the reliance on human intervention during online training.
- **Reduced Intervention Fraction:** WHIRL consistently maintained a lower step-weighted rolling 5-episode intervention fraction (ρk) across all tasks compared to the model-free baseline. This indicates that WHIRL required less human control time.
- **Faster Convergence to Autonomy:** On grasping tasks (Pick Cube, Pick LEGO, Pick & Place), both WHIRL and the model-free baseline eventually reached near-zero intervention rates, but WHIRL achieved this state earlier in the training process.
- **Significant Burden Reduction:** The most pronounced reduction was observed on the Pull Drawer task, where WHIRL decreased the final rolling intervention fraction from 0.113 (baseline) to 0.018, representing an 84% reduction. The report emphasizes that this reduction reflects fewer prolonged rescue segments, not just fewer pedal events, due to the step-weighted nature of the metric. The operator's role shifted from frequent, sustained recovery to infrequent, brief boundary corrections.

**Q3: Source of the Gain (Mechanism Ablation)**
An ablation study was conducted on Pick LEGO and Pull Drawer tasks to isolate the contributions of the intervention-aware world model and its actor-side risk shaping.
- **Actor-Side Shaping Contribution:** The condition `WHIRL (α_i = 0)`, where the world model was trained but the actor did not utilize the intervention probability head, showed delayed convergence compared to the full WHIRL system. This demonstrates that the actor's consumption of the intervention head's prediction is a direct source of performance gain, beyond just the auxiliary BCE training of the world model.
- **World Model Pathway Importance:** The `WHIRL w/o WM` condition, which removed the entire world model pathway, was the slowest to converge to a low intervention rate. This confirms that the predictive capabilities of the world model, even without the specific intervention shaping, contribute to improving learning efficiency.
- **Timing of Risk Shaping:** On tasks like Pull Drawer, there was an initial brief cost for WHIRL before its intervention fraction curve crossed over the `WHIRL (α_i = 0)` curve. This suggests that the risk shaping mechanism becomes effective after the intervention head has processed sufficient replay data and learned reliable predictions.

In summary, the findings indicate that WHIRL significantly enhances both the success rate of autonomous dexterous manipulation and the efficiency of human-in-the-loop training by proactively learning from human intervention signals. The mechanism ablation confirmed that the actor-side risk shaping, driven by the intervention-aware world model, is a key factor in these improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The WHIRL framework introduces a significant methodological advancement in real-world reinforcement learning for dexterous manipulation, particularly in human-in-the-loop (HIL) settings. Its primary significance lies in transforming human interventions from mere reactive corrections into valuable, forward-predictive safety signals.

The potential impacts of this work are substantial:
- **Enhanced Real-World Robot Training Safety and Efficiency:** By proactively guiding the robot away from states that would likely trigger human intervention, WHIRL inherently reduces the risk of hardware failures and the need for costly physical repairs. This also translates to more efficient use of real-robot time, as fewer episodes are wasted on recovering from or preventing failures.
- **Reduced Operator Burden and Fatigue:** The framework’s ability to achieve high success rates with significantly fewer operator interventions (up to 84% reduction in step-weighted burden) directly addresses a critical bottleneck in HIL RL. This reduces the cognitive load and physical fatigue on human operators, making it more feasible to train complex robotic systems for extended periods or across multiple tasks.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** Learning from what a human would avoid allows the policy to develop a more robust understanding of unsafe regions of the state-action space, rather than merely correcting specific past errors. This predictive risk avoidance can lead to policies that are more generally safe and adaptable to minor variations in task execution.
- **Practical Safety-Aware RL Recipe:** WHIRL offers a concrete, implementable "recipe" for safety-aware RL in complex, high-DoF robotic systems. The clear distinction between using intervention predictions for actor-side shaping versus direct reward modification prevents entanglement of operator habits with task optimality, providing a robust design principle.
- **Broader Applicability to Complex Robotic Tasks:** While demonstrated on dexterous manipulation, the core concept of learning reusable risk from human avoidance signals could extend to other domains where human supervision is critical for safety and performance, such as complex assembly, human-robot collaboration, or field robotics in unstructured environments.
- **Advancement in World Modeling:** The introduction of a per-state intervention-probability head within a latent world model is a novel contribution to the field of model-based RL, expanding the predictive capabilities of world models beyond standard dynamics, reward, and termination.

The work acknowledges its current limitations, such as evaluation primarily within in-region randomization and reliance on a single operator per task for training traces. However, by establishing a clear link between human intuition and predictive world modeling, WHIRL provides a foundational step towards more autonomous, safer, and human-efficient real-world robot learning, opening avenues for future research into finer-grained manipulation and diverse hand morphologies.
