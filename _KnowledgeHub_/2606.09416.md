# Harness Engineering for Physical AI: Robot Middleware Is the Harness Layer

> [!summary] Summary
> A position paper naming robot middleware as the harness layer for Physical AI. It argues ROS 2 over DDS or Zenoh is the lowest robot-stack layer mediating control, computing, and communication at once, and proposes three enforcement mechanisms, Projection, Isolation, and Transfer, bound to a declarative ROS 2 Harness Profile that middleware consumes and enforces.

## Problem

- Language-agent harness work quarantines nondeterminism at tool-call boundaries, an assumption that collapses once a learned policy sits inside a robot's real-time control loop.
- A learned model perturbs control, computing, and communication simultaneously, yet every single-axis enforcer, controller, scheduler, or network stack, is blind to the other two.
- ROS 2 offers surfaces for declaring constraints, QoS deadlines, ParameterDescriptor ranges, callback groups, but no mechanism binds a declaration to runtime behavior, leaving enforcement as hand-built application code.

## Method

- **Projection**: a publish-path admission gate evaluating each sample against trajectory stability, executor schedulability, and link feasibility, turning ==QoS deadlines== and value ranges into per-sample admit, drop, or hold-last decisions.
- **Isolation**: a single reservation primitive deriving a callback-group compute slot and a ==DDS partition window== jointly from declared output rate, payload size, and worst-case inference time.
- **Transfer**: a middleware lifecycle controller consuming the model's out-of-distribution score to swap authority to a pre-staged verified baseline, all three declared in a YAML ==ROS 2 Harness Profile==.

## Results

- No experiments; evidence is citational. Language-agent benchmarks held the model fixed and swapped harnesses, reporting task reliability swinging by an order of magnitude.
- A cited ROS 2 accelerator manager cuts safety-critical callback-chain latency up to **91%**, but is hand-rebuilt per accelerator, illustrating the missing platform-level Isolation primitive.
- Under the paper's own test, control-barrier filters and Nav2 recovery fail to compose across axes; only an autonomous-racing stack ties control safety to scheduler reaction time, without a reusable contract.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Composition, not per-axis depth, is the harness contribution: the right governing layer is the one that sees all axes, not the strongest enforcer of any.
> - A stable command that misses its inference slot is unsafe; failures invisible to any single monitor appear only when axes are checked jointly.
> - Caution: enforcement is not certification, declared bounds remain upstream work, and per-sample publish-path gating at 1 kHz carries unmeasured latency cost.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly learned policies, planners, and vision-language-action (VLA) models, into deployed robotic systems represents a significant shift in the field of robotics, giving rise to what the authors term "Physical AI." This integration introduces new challenges related to system reliability, safety, and predictability, as AI models often exhibit stochastic behavior that is difficult to bound by traditional deterministic control methods.

Within the broader AI research landscape, particularly in the domain of language agents, the concept of a "harness" has emerged. This harness is an external system or layer that mediates tool usage, manages state, bounds resources, and records execution for AI models. Research in this area has demonstrated that the design of the harness can significantly impact task reliability, sometimes more so than the AI model itself. Existing language-agent harnesses primarily operate at the software level, assuming that nondeterminism can be isolated at the boundary of each tool call, such as file system operations or web service interactions.

This paper addresses a gap in the robotics community, which has not yet fully adopted or adapted the "harness" framing for physical systems. When learned models become causal participants on the control path of a robot, the assumptions made by software-centric harnesses no longer hold. The nondeterminism introduced by AI models affects control, computing, and communication simultaneously and internally within the control loop, rather than at external tool boundaries. Existing robot middleware, such as ROS 2, provides surfaces for quality-of-service (QoS), scheduling, and discovery, but these were not designed to bound stochastic producers like AI models. Current robotic implementations of AI typically involve hand-built, application-level code to manage these complexities, leading to duplicated effort and a lack of standardized governance.

This work positions itself as an effort to bridge the conceptual framework of the "harness" from language-agent research to the unique requirements of Physical AI. It aims to reframe the discussion within the robotics and middleware communities, proposing that robot middleware, by virtue of its position in the robot stack, is the appropriate layer to serve as this Physical AI harness, moving beyond its traditional focus on narrow performance metrics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this paper is to propose and argue that robot middleware is the appropriate "harness layer" for Physical AI systems. The authors aim to define the essential functions this middleware harness must provide to govern AI models operating in physical environments.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations:

- **Shift in Binding Constraint:** In AI agent development, the binding constraint on an agent's behavior has shifted from the AI model itself to the layer that governs it, i.e., the harness. This is evidenced by benchmarks where changing only the harness drastically altered task reliability. The robotics community needs to acknowledge this shift.
- **Unique Challenges of Physical AI:** Unlike software agents, Physical AI systems involve learned policies, planners, and perception stages acting as causal participants in the control path of deployed robots. This introduces pervasive nondeterminism:
 - **Control:** A learned policy's output is a sample from a distribution, which can affect the controller's trajectory and stability margin.
 - **Computing:** Inference times for AI models are often input-dependent and lack tight, closed-form bounds, complicating real-time scheduling.
 - **Communication:** Message payloads and cadences from AI models can be variable, impacting network bandwidth and bounded-delay envelopes.
- **Inadequacy of Current Approaches:**
 - **Language-Agent Harnesses:** Existing harness concepts from the language-agent domain are insufficient because they assume nondeterminism can be quarantined at software tool-call interfaces, which is not applicable when an AI model intrinsically produces nondeterminism inside a physical control loop.
 - **Robot-Specific Implementations:** Currently, when AI models are deployed on robots, critical governance mechanisms are reimplemented manually at the application level. This leads to bespoke solutions that are not reusable, certifiable, or consistent across different robotic platforms.
 - **Middleware Limitations:** Current robot middleware (e.g., ROS 2) provides mechanisms for basic communication and task management but lacks inherent capabilities to bind and enforce the stochastic behavior of AI models across control, computing, and communication simultaneously. While it offers surfaces for specification (e.g., QoS), it does not provide enforcement mechanisms for AI-induced nondeterminism.
- **Need for a Unified Governing Layer:** The complexities introduced by AI models require a single, coherent layer that can observe and control all three perturbed axes (control, computing, communication) to compose enforcement effectively. No single existing substrate (compute, communication) or the application layer alone can achieve this.
- **Redefining Middleware's Role:** The authors argue for a re-evaluation of robot middleware's role, shifting its focus from a narrow competition on performance metrics (e.g., latency, jitter) to addressing the more significant challenge of governing AI models for safety and reliability in physical systems. The motivation is to prevent every new AI model from requiring a complete rebuild of its governing infrastructure.

Therefore, the key motivation is to formalize the role of a governing layer for Physical AI, identify robot middleware as that layer, and define the specific enforcement mechanisms required to transform middleware from a descriptive configuration surface into an active runtime enforcer of AI model contracts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper employs a conceptual analysis and argumentation approach rather than presenting experimental results or a finished system. The methodology can be broken down into several key steps:

1. **Definition and Scope Delimitation:**
 - The authors first define "Physical AI" as systems where a learned model (policy, planner, perception stage) becomes a causal participant in the closed loop driving an actuator, thereby perturbing control, execution, and transmission simultaneously.
 - They clarify that the proposed harness is necessary specifically when an AI model is a real-time causal participant unbounded on all three axes at once, distinguishing it from advisory or offline AI components.
 - The concept of a "harness" is synthesized from existing language-agent literature, defining it as the extra-model layer that turns capability into governed action, encompassing aspects like what the model sees, does, when it runs, and how its behavior is constrained, bounded, and recovered.

2. **Critique of Existing Harness Boundaries:**
 - The paper argues that the "tool-call interface" boundary for isolating nondeterminism, effective in software-only language-agent harnesses, is insufficient for Physical AI. In physical systems, AI models introduce nondeterminism *inside* the control loop, affecting stability margins, execution schedulability, and network delays simultaneously.

3. **Analysis of Governance Layers in Robotics:**
 - The authors conduct a comparative analysis of four main governance layers in the robot stack that interact with the control loop: robot application, robot middleware, communication substrate, and compute substrate.
 - Table 1 ("Observe-and-control capability by governance layer") is introduced to systematically evaluate each layer's ability to natively own, mediate through its own abstractions, indirectly influence, or delegate control over the control, computing, and communication axes.
 - This analysis concludes that while each substrate is the strongest enforcer on its own axis, only robot middleware provides mediated abstractions (e.g., QoS for control, callback groups for computing, QoS/partitions for communication) across all three axes simultaneously, making it uniquely positioned for *compositional enforcement*.

4. **Mapping Harness Components to ROS 2:**
 - Six generic harness components are synthesized from existing literature: Role, Stage, State (organizational aspects) and Contract, Adapter, Recovery (governance aspects).
 - Table 2 maps these components to existing ROS 2 surfaces. The analysis reveals that ROS 2 already provides structural elements for Role, Stage, and State. However, for Contract, Adapter, and Recovery, ROS 2 primarily offers *declarative surfaces* (e.g., IDL types, QoS policies, ParameterDescriptor ranges, callback groups, lifecycle transitions) but *lacks enforcement mechanisms* to bind these declarations to runtime behavior, especially for stochastic AI models.

5. **Proposal of Enforcement Mechanisms (PIT):**
 - To address the identified lack of enforcement, the paper proposes three specific, interdependent enforcement mechanisms that robot middleware must host:
 - **Projection:** A mechanism to gate each candidate output at publication time against declared control, schedule, and link contracts. This transforms declarative constraints into active admission decisions.
 - **Isolation:** A mechanism to wrap model computation and communication to ensure its timing and resource footprint stay within a unified reservation, translating declared output rate, payload size, and worst-case inference time into a coherent and verifiable specification.
 - **Transfer:** A mechanism to reroute authority to a pre-staged verified baseline when the AI model’s declared operating regime (e.g., distributional conditions) can no longer be sustained, and to reinstate the model when conditions return within bounds. This leverages ROS 2 lifecycle states and QoS events.
 - The methodology emphasizes the critical *co-location and interdependence* of these three mechanisms within the middleware, arguing that their composition is essential for effective governance across axes.

6. **Conceptualization of a "ROS 2 Harness Profile":**
 - The paper sketches a practical deployment artifact, a "ROS 2 Harness Profile" (e.g., a YAML file), as a means for AI models or operators to declare their output region, inference budget, and operating regime. This profile is intended to be consumed and enforced by the middleware, not just interpreted by application code.

The approach is primarily theoretical and architectural, identifying a critical missing layer in Physical AI deployment and proposing a conceptual framework for its implementation within existing robot middleware, specifically ROS 2. It sets the stage for future implementation and empirical validation work.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings and conceptual results regarding the governance of AI models in physical robotic systems:

- **Identification of Robot Middleware as the Physical AI Harness:** The central finding is that robot middleware, exemplified by ROS 2 over DDS or Zenoh, is the most appropriate and uniquely positioned layer to function as the "harness" for Physical AI. This conclusion is based on an analysis (Table 1) showing that middleware is the lowest robot-stack layer possessing mediating abstractions (though not native ownership) across all three axes perturbed by AI models: control, computing, and communication. Other layers (application, compute/communication substrates) either lack a comprehensive view or cannot compose enforcement across these axes.

- **Existing Middleware Provides Structure, Lacks Enforcement for AI:** While existing robot middleware like ROS 2 provides foundational structural components of a harness (e.g., nodes, executors, QoS history for Role, Stage, and State), it primarily offers *declarative surfaces* for critical governance aspects such as Contract, Adapter, and Recovery. These surfaces allow for specifying constraints (e.g., IDL types, QoS deadlines, ParameterDescriptor ranges, callback groups, lifecycle transitions) but do not inherently provide *runtime enforcement mechanisms* to bind these declarations to the stochastic and dynamic behavior of AI models. This means that an AI model’s declared output region, inference budget, or operating regime is not actively enforced by the middleware.

- **Proposal of Three Essential Enforcement Mechanisms (PIT):** The paper identifies and defines three interdependent enforcement mechanisms that robot middleware must host to effectively govern AI models:
 - **Projection:** This mechanism is responsible for admitting or rejecting an AI model's output at publication time. It evaluates each candidate output against three concurrent predicates: the stability of the resulting trajectory, schedulability under the executor’s current load, and feasibility of delivery within the network link’s reserved window. This transforms declarative constraints (e.g., data-type ranges, freshness tests, lifecycle state gates) into real-time, per-sample admission decisions, preventing unsafe or unfeasible commands from entering the control loop.
 - **Isolation:** This mechanism aims to ensure that an AI model's computation and communication footprints adhere to a pre-defined reservation. It involves specifying and verifying a coherent reservation that spans both a compute slot (governed by callback scheduling) and a communication window (governed by partitions and flow control). The objective is to prevent the model's execution from overrunning its allocated slot and ensure the slot does not starve other critical nodes. This reservation should be validated at configuration time to reject inconsistent combinations.
 - **Transfer:** This mechanism enables automated rerouting of authority from an AI model to a pre-staged, verified baseline when the AI model’s declared operating regime (e.g., distributional conditions like out-of-distribution scores) can no longer be sustained. It leverages ROS 2 lifecycle state transitions as the switching mechanism, moving beyond manual or simple failure-mode-specific fallbacks. A critical aspect is also defining the conditions for reinstating the AI model, which requires confirmation that its resource use and behavior have returned within declared bounds.

- **The Necessity of Compositional Enforcement:** A key finding is that these three mechanisms (Projection, Isolation, Transfer) must be co-located within the middleware because they are interdependent. Projection relies on Isolation’s established schedulability boundaries, and Transfer is triggered by Projection’s admission rate failures, while its reinstatement depends on Isolation’s resource monitoring. No single layer below the middleware can observe all three axes, and no layer above can act within the real-time control loop. This compositionality, rather than per-axis depth of enforcement, is identified as the core contribution of the harness.

- **Introduction of a "ROS 2 Harness Profile":** To facilitate the declaration and enforcement of AI model properties, the paper proposes a "ROS 2 Harness Profile." This manifest, suggested as a YAML file, would declare the AI model's output region, inference budget, and operating regime. This profile would serve as a deployment artifact consumed by the middleware, allowing it to apply the PIT mechanisms. This moves the middleware from a system where contracts are merely declared to one where they are actively enforced at runtime.

In essence, the paper concludes that current robot middleware is structurally capable of hosting a harness but lacks the specific runtime enforcement mechanisms necessary to safely and reliably integrate stochastic AI models into physical control loops. The proposed Projection, Isolation, and Transfer mechanisms, implemented within the middleware and guided by a "Harness Profile," constitute a framework for achieving this.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the fields of robotics, AI, and middleware development:

- **Enhanced Reliability and Safety for Physical AI:** By explicitly defining and proposing runtime enforcement mechanisms for AI models' declared output regions, inference budgets, and operating regimes, the paper offers a conceptual framework to significantly improve the reliability and safety of robots. This addresses a critical challenge posed by the inherent nondeterminism of learned models when they directly influence physical systems. The proposed mechanisms could help prevent unstable trajectories, missed deadlines, and out-of-distribution behaviors from escalating into system failures.

- **Paradigm Shift in Robot Middleware Development:** For a decade, the robot middleware community has largely focused on optimizing performance metrics like latency, jitter, and scalability. This paper advocates for a paradigm shift, arguing that governing stochastic AI models is a more consequential challenge for Physical AI. By relocating the question from "which implementation is fastest" to "what every middleware should host," it could reorient research and development efforts in middleware towards robustness, safety, and AI integration.

- **Standardization and Reusability of Governance Mechanisms:** Currently, many organizations implementing AI in robotics build ad-hoc, application-level governance code. The proposal to integrate Projection, Isolation, and Transfer into the middleware layer promotes the standardization of these crucial functions. This would reduce duplicated effort, foster code reuse, and potentially accelerate the deployment of AI-powered robots by providing a common, platform-level infrastructure for AI governance.

- **Systematic Integration of AI in Robotics:** The concept of a "ROS 2 Harness Profile" offers a structured way to declare AI model properties to the middleware. This manifest could streamline the process of integrating new AI models into robotic systems, enabling a more systematic and verifiable approach to AI deployment rather than manual configuration and troubleshooting. It clarifies the contract between the AI model and the underlying robotic system.

- **New Benchmarking and Evaluation Methodologies:** The paper highlights a methodological move from the language-agent community: benchmarking the "harness" rather than just the model. Applying this to robotics, the authors suggest a "harness card" and benchmarks that hold the learned policy fixed while varying the harness. This could lead to new evaluation metrics and methodologies for assessing a robot's safety and reliability, attributing performance not solely to the AI model but also to the effectiveness of its governing layer. This could drive innovation in robust AI deployment.

- **Addressing the Nondeterminism Challenge:** One of the fundamental difficulties in deploying AI in real-world, safety-critical systems is managing its probabilistic and often unpredictable nature. The proposed harness mechanisms directly confront this by converting declarative constraints into runtime obligations and providing explicit mechanisms for bounding, monitoring, and recovering from out-of-spec AI behavior.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays out several open research directions for implementing and evaluating the proposed PIT mechanisms, such as the cost-benefit analysis of Projection hooks, the development of joint admission tests for Isolation, and robust return conditions for Transfer. This provides a clear roadmap for subsequent theoretical and experimental work in making Physical AI deployable and certifiable.

In summary, this research proposes a fundamental architectural shift for robot middleware, transforming it into an active enforcer for AI contracts. This shift has the potential to significantly advance the reliability, safety, and broad applicability of AI in physical robotics by providing a standardized, systematic, and robust framework for governing learning-based components.
