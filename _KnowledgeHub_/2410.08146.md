# Rewarding Progress: Scaling Automated Process Verifiers for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> A new approach, Process Advantage Verifiers (PAVs), is introduced to measure step-level progress in multi-step reasoning traces of large language models. This method leads to improved accuracy and efficiency in tasks like mathematical problem-solving during both test-time search and online reinforcement learning.

## Problem

- Large language models (LLMs) often struggle with complex multi-step reasoning tasks, making errors that are difficult to pinpoint within a long reasoning trace.
- Traditional outcome-only feedback (Outcome Reward Models) provides a sparse signal, hindering effective identification of helpful or harmful intermediate steps.
- Existing process reward models face challenges such as the high cost of human annotation, limited effectiveness of automatically generated labels, and an unclear definition of what constitutes a "good" intermediate step.

## Method

- Introduces ==Process Advantage Verifiers (PAVs)==, models trained to predict =="progress"== at each step of a reasoning trace.
- Progress is defined as the ==step-level advantage==: the difference in the probability of a prover policy completing a trace correctly before and after a given step.
- PAVs are trained via supervised learning, using empirically estimated progress derived from completing partial traces with a designated =="prover policy"== and calculating success rate differences.

## Results

- Using PAVs for test-time beam search yielded **1.5-5x** compute efficiency and **8-10%** accuracy improvements across different LLM sizes (Gemma-2B, 9B, 27B).
- When integrated into online reinforcement learning as dense rewards, PAVs achieved **6x** sample efficiency and **6-7%** accuracy improvements on mathematical reasoning tasks.
- PAVs consistently outperformed traditional outcome reward models and other automated process reward baselines across various experimental settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Process rewards are defined as "progress," representing the increase in the likelihood of a correct solution after a step, mathematically formulated as a step-level advantage based on a prover policy's Q-values.
> - Optimal "prover policies" for estimating progress are not necessarily the most capable, but rather those that are "complementary" to the base policy, meaning they can effectively distinguish between good and bad steps it generates.
> - The approach enables scalable process supervision by automating the generation of progress labels, circumventing the need for expensive human annotation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive capabilities across many domains, but they still struggle with complex multi-step reasoning tasks. When these models make errors in mathematical problem-solving or logical reasoning, it's often unclear which specific step went wrong and how to guide them toward correct solutions. This paper introduces a new approach to address this challenge through Process Advantage Verifiers (PAVs), which measure "progress" at each step of a reasoning trace.

The figure above illustrates the core concept: starting from a mathematical problem, different reasoning paths can be taken. Traditional approaches only evaluate the final answer, but PAVs evaluate the progress made at each intermediate step, allowing for more effective guidance during both training and inference.

### 2. Understanding Process vs Outcome Verification

The fundamental challenge in improving LLM reasoning lies in providing effective feedback. Traditional **Outcome Reward Models (ORMs)** only evaluate whether the final answer is correct, providing a sparse signal that makes it difficult to identify which steps in a multi-step reasoning process were helpful or harmful.

**Process Reward Models (PRMs)** attempt to address this by providing feedback at each step. However, existing approaches face significant limitations:

1. **Human annotation bottleneck**: Collecting dense, per-step human labels is expensive and impractical at scale
2. **Limited effectiveness**: PRMs trained on automatically-generated labels have shown modest improvements
3. **Unclear reward design**: It's not obvious what makes a "good" intermediate step

This work proposes a fundamentally different approach to designing process rewards by focusing on measuring "progress" rather than step-level correctness.

### 3. The Progress-Based Approach

The key insight is to define process rewards in terms of **progress**: how much does each step increase the likelihood of eventually reaching a correct solution? This progress is measured relative to a **prover policy** μ that can complete partial reasoning traces.

Mathematically, progress at step t is defined as:

```
Progress(s_t, a_t) = Q^μ(s_{t+1}) - Q^μ(s_t)
```

Where:
- `s_t` is the current state (partial reasoning trace)
- `a_t` is the action taken (next reasoning step)
- `Q^μ(s)` represents the probability that prover policy μ can complete state s correctly

This formulation connects process rewards to concepts from reinforcement learning, specifically step-level advantages, while being practically implementable through supervised learning.

### 4. Process Advantage Verifiers

Process Advantage Verifiers (PAVs) are models trained to predict the progress defined above. The training process involves:

1. **Data collection**: Generate partial reasoning traces from a base policy
2. **Completion**: Use a prover policy to complete these traces multiple times
3. **Progress estimation**: Calculate empirical progress by comparing success rates before and after each step
4. **Supervised training**: Train a neural network to predict these progress values

The figure shows how PAVs work in practice. States are evaluated by their Q-values under the prover policy, and advantages (progress) are computed as the difference in Q-values between consecutive states.

### 5. Theoretical Framework

The paper provides theoretical analysis showing that good prover policies should be **complementary** to the base policy. This means they should be able to distinguish between good and bad steps produced by the base policy, rather than simply being more capable overall.

The analysis reveals that:
- Very weak prover policies cannot provide meaningful progress signals
- Very strong prover policies may not distinguish well between base policy steps
- Intermediate-strength "complementary" provers (like Best-of-K policies) work best

### 6. Experimental Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrates significant improvements across multiple settings:

#### 6.1. Test-Time Search

Using PAVs for beam search shows:
- **1.5-5x compute efficiency** compared to outcome-based methods
- **8-10% accuracy improvements** across different model sizes
- Consistent benefits across Gemma-2B, 9B, and 27B models

#### 6.2. Online Reinforcement Learning

When used as dense rewards in RL training:
- **6x sample efficiency** compared to outcome-only rewards
- **6-7% accuracy improvements** on mathematical reasoning tasks
- Better exploration and discovery of new solution strategies

#### 6.3. Comparison with Alternative Approaches

PAVs consistently outperform:
- Traditional ORMs
- Q-value based PRMs
- Other automated process reward approaches

The choice of prover policy is crucial, with Best-of-4 and Best-of-8 policies showing optimal performance across different base model sizes.

#### 6.4. Scaling Properties

The approach shows favorable scaling properties:
- Performance improves with more training data
- Benefits persist across different model sizes
- Scaling laws suggest continued improvements with larger datasets

### 7. Implications and Future Directions

This work has several important implications for the field:

1. **Scalable process supervision**: PAVs provide a way to scale process-level feedback without human annotation
2. **Improved sample efficiency**: Dense rewards enable more efficient RL training
3. **Better test-time performance**: Step-level guidance improves search effectiveness
4. **Theoretical grounding**: The connection to RL theory provides principled design choices

The approach opens several directions for future research:
- Exploring different prover policy designs
- Applying the framework to other reasoning domains beyond mathematics
- Investigating the interaction between PAVs and other improvement techniques
- Scaling to even larger models and datasets

The work demonstrates that by rethinking how we define and measure progress in multi-step reasoning, we can achieve significant improvements in both efficiency and effectiveness of LLM reasoning systems. The progress-based formulation provides a principled foundation for automated process supervision that scales beyond the limitations of human annotation.
