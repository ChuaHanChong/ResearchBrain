# MuRF: Unlocking the Multi-Scale Potential of Vision Foundation Models

> [!summary] Summary
> MuRF, developed by the University of Wisconsin-Madison, enhances visual representations from frozen Vision Foundation Models by fusing features extracted at multiple input resolutions during inference. This approach consistently improves performance across diverse tasks including semantic segmentation, depth estimation, VQA, and unsupervised anomaly detection, demonstrating better integration of global context and fine-grained details.

## Problem

- Vision Foundation Models (VFMs), despite being trained with multi-resolution capabilities, are typically used with a single, fixed input resolution during inference.
- Single-scale inference creates an inherent trade-off, either compromising global semantic context (from low-resolution views) or fine-grained local precision (from high-resolution views).
- This practice overlooks valuable, complementary information available at different visual scales, leaving a significant portion of the VFM's potential unexploited at inference time.

## Method

- **MuRF** generates an ==input image pyramid== by resizing to multiple scales, then extracts ==patch-level feature maps== for each scale using a frozen ==VFM encoder==.
- It spatially aligns these ==multi-scale feature maps== to a common resolution and concatenates them along the channel dimension into a single, high-dimensional MuRF representation.
- This universal MuRF representation is then adapted to various downstream tasks (e.g., dense prediction, MLLMs, anomaly detection) using lightweight, ==task-specific heads==, with only these heads requiring training.

## Results

- Achieved mIoU boosts on ADE20k (e.g., **47.4%** vs **46.1%** for DINOv2) and PASCAL VOC for semantic segmentation, and lower RMSE on NYU Depth V2 (**0.368** vs **0.394**) for depth estimation.
- Improved multimodal understanding in LLaVA 1.5 across multiple VQA benchmarks (e.g., MME score from **1291.6** to **1357.1**), while maintaining comparable VRAM usage and latency by fixing the number of visual tokens.
- Demonstrated state-of-the-art AU-PRO_0.05 scores (**62.3%**) on the MVTec AD 2 TEST_priv,mix benchmark for unsupervised anomaly detection, surpassing methods like PatchCore (**52.6%**) and SuperAD (**59.3%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low-resolution inputs are effective at capturing global semantic context for recognition, while high-resolution inputs are critical for resolving fine-grained details and precise boundaries.
> - Channel-wise concatenation of multi-scale features projects them into a higher-dimensional space, strictly preserving the independence of scale-specific signals, rather than blending them destructively.
> - Significant performance enhancements can be achieved for VFM-based systems at inference time by actively fusing multi-scale information, without modifying or retraining the VFM backbone itself.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of computer vision has transitioned towards Vision Foundation Models (VFMs), which offer robust and transferable representations for a wide range of tasks. Initially, Vision Transformers (ViTs) required fixed-size inputs, leading to limitations such as the discarding of visual information through aggressive resizing or cropping. Subsequent advancements, exemplified by DINOv2, NaViT, and FlexViT, introduced "multi-resolution" or "native resolution" training, enabling models to process images of varying sizes during the training phase.

Despite these developments in training flexibility, standard VFM inference protocols commonly revert to a single, fixed input resolution. This single-scale approach is recognized by the authors as potentially suboptimal, as visual perception inherently benefits from complementary information available at different resolutions: low resolutions facilitate global semantic recognition, while high resolutions are crucial for fine-grained detail and precise boundary delineation. Current single-scale inference methodologies are argued to compromise either global context or local precision.

Historically, multi-scale representation learning has been addressed through techniques such as image pyramids in earlier computer vision and feature pyramid networks (FPNs) in deep learning. More recently, in the context of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) processing high-resolution images, strategies like dividing images into smaller tiles have been employed. However, these methods can introduce issues such as boundary artifacts or necessitate task-specific training. This work positions Multi-Resolution Fusion (MuRF) as an approach that revisits and modernizes the classical image pyramid concept, adapting it for the VFM era to aggregate multi-resolution features in a training-free manner, thereby addressing the limitations of existing single-scale inference and tile-based strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from an observed disparity between the multi-resolution training capabilities of modern Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) and their predominantly single-scale inference practices. The authors highlight a fundamental property of visual perception, termed the "Recognition vs. Refinement" dynamic, where different input resolutions provide complementary inductive biases. Specifically, lower-resolution views are proficient at capturing global semantic context, which is beneficial for robust object recognition. Conversely, higher-resolution views are essential for resolving fine-grained details, precise boundaries, and local features, facilitating detailed refinement.

The prevalent single-scale inference paradigm is identified as a limitation, as it inherently forces a trade-off: selecting a low resolution compromises fine-grained detail, while selecting a high resolution may introduce noise or incomplete segmentation due to a diminished global context. The authors argue that this single-scale restriction prevents VFMs from fully realizing their potential by discarding a wealth of information inherent in multi-scale visual data. Figure 1 visually illustrates this dynamic, showing how a 266-pixel input provides a globally coherent representation, while a 784-pixel input offers sharper boundaries but risks noisy interiors or incomplete segmentation.

Based on this motivation, the key objective of this work is to introduce Multi-Resolution Fusion (MuRF), a strategy designed to explicitly harness and synergize these complementary multi-resolution views at inference time. The overarching goal is to construct a unified, scale-robust visual representation from a frozen VFM that can benefit a wide array of downstream tasks. The authors emphasize that MuRF aims to achieve this without modifying or retraining the VFM backbone, making it a training-free enhancement to visual representation. A core objective is to demonstrate the universality of MuRF, showing its effectiveness across various VFM families and diverse computer vision tasks, thereby establishing it as a fundamental principle rather than a task-specific adaptation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MuRF operates as an inference-time strategy to construct a multi-scale, scale-robust visual representation from a frozen Vision Foundation Model (VFM) encoder. The methodology involves two main stages: multi-resolution feature fusion and task-specific adaptation.

#### 3.1. Multi-Resolution Feature Fusion

The core of MuRF is the aggregation of features extracted from an input image at multiple resolutions.
1. **Input Pyramid Construction:** Given an input image `x`, it is resized to a predefined set of different scaling factors, `S_res = {s_1, s_2..., s_k}`, creating a collection of resized images `{x_s}`.
2. **Feature Extraction:** Each resized image `x_s` is passed through a frozen VFM encoder (denoted as `Φ`). The feature map from a specified layer (typically the last layer or a target block) is extracted for each scale, yielding `ℱ_s = Φ(x_s) ∈ R^(H_s × W_s × d)`, where `(H_s, W_s)` are the spatial dimensions and `d` is the feature channel dimension.
3. **Feature Fusion:** To create a unified representation, each feature map `ℱ_s` is first upsampled to a common target spatial resolution `(H', W')`, typically equivalent to the original input size, using bilinear interpolation. These spatially aligned feature maps are then concatenated along their channel dimension. This process results in the final MuRF representation, `ℱ_MuRF = Concat_s∈S_res (Upsample(ℱ_s)) ∈ R^(H' × W' × D)`, where `D` is the sum of channel dimensions across all scales (`D = |S_res| × d`).

**Rationale for Channel-wise Concatenation:** The authors justify the use of channel-wise concatenation over alternatives like element-wise addition or mean pooling. They posit that ViT features are highly localized and scale-dependent. Concatenation projects features into a higher-dimensional space, preserving the distinct "recognition" and "refinement" signals from different scales. This allows a lightweight downstream head to adaptively utilize the appropriate scale information without requiring a heavy, parameterized fusion network.

#### 3.2. Task-Specific Adaptation

The `ℱ_MuRF` representation is designed to be task-agnostic and is adapted for various downstream tasks by attaching lightweight, trainable heads:

1. **Dense Prediction Tasks (Semantic Segmentation, Depth Estimation):** A simple dense prediction head (`Head_dense(⋅)`), typically comprising one or more convolutional layers (e.g., 1x1 convolution), projects the `D`-dimensional `ℱ_MuRF` features to the required output channels (e.g., semantic classes or depth values). The final prediction map `Ŷ` is `Upsample(Head_dense(ℱ_MuRF))`. Only the parameters of `Head_dense` are trained.

2. **Unsupervised Anomaly Detection:** This task utilizes a training-free paradigm extended from nearest-neighbor approaches. For each resolution `s` (and often each intermediate layer `l` as per Appendix A.4), a dedicated memory bank `ℳ_s` is constructed from anomaly-free training images. At inference, a separate anomaly score map `Ŝ_s` is computed for each feature map `ℱ_s` by calculating the L2 distance of each feature vector to its nearest neighbor in `ℳ_s`. The final robust anomaly score map `Ŝ` is generated by averaging all individual score maps after upsampling them to the original image dimensions.

3. **Multimodal Language Models (VQA, Visual Grounding):** The `ℱ_MuRF` representation serves as the visual input to an MLLM. It is processed by a perception module or projection layer (`Head_MLLM(⋅)`), mapping the visual features into the LLM's word embedding space, `E_visual = Head_MLLM(ℱ_MuRF)`. These `E_visual` embeddings are treated as a sequence of "visual tokens" and prepended to text token embeddings. This approach ensures the number of visual tokens remains constant, avoiding increased computational cost for the LLM. Only `Head_MLLM` and other designated MLLM parameters are trained.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **VFM Backbone:** DINOv2-ViT-B/14 is the primary frozen VFM encoder. SigLIP2-Base (Patch16 NaFlex version) is also used to demonstrate generalization.
- **Resolutions (`S_res`):** The number of resolutions varies by task: 5 for anomaly detection, 3 for segmentation/depth estimation/PCA, and 2 for MLLMs. Specific resolutions are chosen based on original task implementations or adapted to VFM patch sizes (e.g., for SigLIP2).
- **Training Details:** For all downstream tasks, only the lightweight task-specific heads are trained, keeping the VFM backbone and MuRF fusion module frozen. Optimization configurations align with original papers (DINOv2, LLaVA 1.5).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across diverse computer vision tasks demonstrate that Multi-Resolution Fusion (MuRF) consistently enhances Vision Foundation Model (VFM) representations, leading to performance improvements over single-scale baselines.

#### 4.1. Semantic Segmentation

- **Results:** MuRF achieved higher mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) scores on both the ADE20k and PASCAL VOC benchmarks compared to single-scale DINOv2 baselines (Table 1). On ADE20K, MuRF improved mIoU by 2.8% and on PASCAL VOC by 1.2% relative to the high-resolution DINOv2 baseline.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Visualizations in Figure 3 illustrate that MuRF produces segmentation masks that combine the global coherence from low resolutions with the sharper boundary details from high resolutions, leading to more accurate dense predictions.
- **Efficiency:** For PASCAL VOC segmentation, MuRF required approximately 1.3 times the training time of its single-resolution counterpart.

#### 4.2. Depth Estimation

- **Results:** MuRF yielded substantially lower Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) on both NYU Depth V2 (in-domain) and SUN RGB-D (transfer learning) datasets (Table 1). For NYU Depth V2, MuRF (Lin. 1) achieved an RMSE of 0.361, a 7.2% relative improvement over the medium-resolution DINOv2 baseline (0.389). Similar improvements were observed for the Lin. 4 configuration.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Figure 4 demonstrates that MuRF generated smoother and more accurate depth maps, better preserving global scene structure and sharpening local geometry compared to single-scale predictions.
- **Computational Cost:** Table 2 indicates that MuRF achieved the best performance (RMSE of 0.361) with a VRAM usage of 0.56 GB and latency of 58.35 ms/iter, which is a reasonable trade-off compared to single-scale baselines (e.g., 1.0x at 0.43 GB VRAM and 22.61 ms/iter).

#### 4.3. Visual Question Answering (VQA)

- **Results:** When integrated as the visual encoder for LLaVA 1.5 variants using DINOv2 or SigLIP2, MuRF consistently improved multimodal understanding capacities across various VQA benchmarks, including MME, VLMisAreBiased, V*, RealWorld QA, GQA, and mmbench (Table 3). For instance, with DINOv2 as the vision encoder, MuRF improved the MME score from 1291.6 to 1357.1.
- **Efficiency:** MuRF maintained comparable VRAM usage and training/inference latency to single-resolution baselines, incurring zero extra sequence-length computational cost for the LLM due to its channel-wise concatenation strategy that keeps the number of visual tokens constant.

#### 4.4. Unsupervised Anomaly Detection

- **Results:** MuRF achieved competitive and, in several cases, state-of-the-art AU-PRO scores on the MVTec AD 2 TEST dataset (Table 4). MuRF (62.3%) outperformed methods like PatchCore (52.6%) and SuperAD (59.3%) on TEST_priv,mix.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Figure 5 shows that MuRF successfully combined robust anomaly detection from low-resolution views (identifying presence) with sharp boundary details from high-resolution views (precise localization), while mitigating noise or "holes" seen in large anomaly areas at high resolutions.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Comparison (PCA)

- Figure 6 illustrates the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of feature embeddings. It shows that lower resolution images yield smoother internal representations but coarser boundaries, while higher resolution images provide sharper boundaries but can exhibit noise or internal holes. MuRF effectively balances these characteristics, generating high-quality representations that are good in both aspects.

#### 4.6. Analysis of Resolution and Feature Concatenation

- **Multi-scale vs. Multi-layer:** Table 5 compares MuRF (multi-scale, single layer) with multi-layer feature concatenation (Lin. 3) for depth estimation. MuRF demonstrated superior performance on the in-domain NYU Depth V2 (0.361 vs. 0.376), indicating its effectiveness in capturing fine-grained structural details. Multi-layer features performed comparably on SUN RGB-D (0.418 vs. 0.419).
- **Combined Approach:** The highest performance (RMSE 0.357 on NYUd, 0.409 on SUN RGB-D) was achieved by combining both methodologies (Lin. 3 + MuRF), suggesting that multi-scale and multi-layer feature concatenations offer complementary benefits.

#### 4.7. Ablation Study

- **Depth Estimation (Table 7):** Performance improved monotonically as the number of fused resolutions increased. All two-scale variants outperformed single-scale baselines, and including the coarsest view (0.5x) in the three-scale MuRF configuration (`{0.5, 1.0, 1.5}x`) still provided a measurable gain over the two-scale `{1.0, 1.5}x` (0.361 vs. 0.366 RMSE).
- **Semantic Segmentation (Table 6):** MuRF also generalized successfully to SigLIP2 as a backbone, showing an improvement from the best single-scale (512, 35.27%) to MuRF (37.10%) on ADE20K.
- **Anomaly Detection (Table 8):** Similar to depth estimation, single-scale models were consistently weaker. Fusing multiple resolutions progressively improved AU-PRO scores, confirming that covering a spectrum of coarse-to-fine views enhances anomaly detection robustness and precision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Multi-Resolution Fusion (MuRF) as a practical and effective strategy for enhancing Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) at inference time. Its significance lies in demonstrating a general principle for unlocking the multi-scale potential of pre-trained visual encoders without requiring architectural modifications or additional training of the backbone.

The consistent and notable performance gains observed across a diverse set of fundamental computer vision tasks—including semantic segmentation, depth estimation, multimodal reasoning (VQA), and unsupervised anomaly detection—underscore the broad applicability of MuRF. Its universality is a compelling attribute, as it is not tied to a specific VFM architecture, having been validated with DINOv2 and SigLIP2. The training-free nature of MuRF for the VFM backbone simplifies its integration into existing VFM-based systems, reducing computational costs and development time typically associated with extensive multi-scale training.

MuRF's ability to create a unified representation that effectively synergizes global contextual information from low-resolution views with fine-grained details from high-resolution views addresses a critical limitation of traditional single-scale inference. This allows downstream tasks to leverage a richer, more robust visual context. The findings suggest that explicitly aggregating multi-resolution features at inference is not merely a task-specific optimization but a fundamental enhancement for visual representation quality.

The potential impact of MuRF is multifaceted. For researchers, it offers a straightforward yet powerful method to improve the performance of VFM-based systems. For practical applications, especially those requiring both broad semantic understanding and precise localization (e.g., autonomous driving, medical imaging, industrial inspection), MuRF could lead to more accurate and reliable computer vision deployments. Its efficiency, particularly in multimodal language models where it maintains token sequence length, allows for enhanced visual reasoning capabilities without prohibitive computational overhead. Ultimately, MuRF contributes to making VFMs more adaptable and performant across varied real-world scenarios by systematically harnessing the inherent multi-scale nature of visual data.
