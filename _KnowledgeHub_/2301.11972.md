# Using Social Cues to Recognize Task Failures for HRI: Overview, State-of-the-Art, and Future Directions

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper reviews existing literature and proposes a framework for enabling robots to recognize their own task failures by interpreting human social cues. It synthesizes insights from behavioral science on human error and reactions, HRI approaches to robot failure, and machine learning methods for detecting these cues, aiming to foster socially self-aware robots.

## Problem

- Robots operating in collaborative human environments are susceptible to making errors.
- Current robots often lack the ability to autonomously detect their own task failures.
- Humans naturally leverage social cues to identify others' mistakes, a capability absent in most robotic systems.

## Method

- The paper proposes an interdisciplinary framework enabling robots to recognize ==task failures== using ==human social cues==.
- This framework integrates perspectives from ==behavioral science== on human feedback, ==HRI== research on robot failures, and ==machine learning== methods.
- It surveys existing approaches and methodologies for conceptualizing, detecting, and implementing ==social cue-based failure recognition==.

## Results

- The paper defines how human social cues serve as feedback mechanisms and introduces a taxonomy of error detection relevant to HRI.
- It outlines promising machine learning approaches, including unsupervised, supervised, and reinforcement learning, suitable for interpreting multimodal human reactions to robot failures.
- The work establishes a comprehensive research agenda and provides practical recommendations for developing robots capable of detecting task errors through human social cues.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A new taxonomy for error detection emphasizes "Type 3 errors," where robots are unaware of a mistake but humans detect it via social cues.
> - Human social cues, including gaze, facial expressions, and body language, provide a rich, albeit subtle, source of feedback for robots to infer task failures.
> - Effective implementation requires interdisciplinary integration of behavioral science understanding, HRI design principles, and advanced machine learning for multimodal data interpretation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is a review and framework proposal situated within the interdisciplinary field of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), specifically addressing the critical challenge of robot error detection. As robots become increasingly integrated into complex environments and human-centric tasks, the inevitability of their committing errors necessitates advanced capabilities for identifying and addressing these failures.

The work builds upon established HRI literature that explores robot failures, such as prior taxonomies and understandings of failure by Honig and Oron-Gilad, Giuliani et al., and Tian and Oviatt. However, it differentiates itself by focusing specifically on "tangible methods to test and implement social responses to failures as a way of improving human-robot interaction," rather than solely deepening the understanding of failure itself. This perspective aligns with the Theory of Graceful Extensibility, which suggests that robots should adapt to contingencies by leveraging the socio-technical human-robot ecosystem for failure repair.

Furthermore, the paper integrates insights from behavioral science concerning human social cues and communication processes, as well as advancements in machine learning (ML) for activity recognition and affective computing. It positions itself as an effort to bridge these disparate fields to enable robots to autonomously detect their own task failures by interpreting human social feedback. This interdisciplinary approach aims to foster the development of "socially self-aware robots" that can leverage human reactions, similar to how humans detect errors in each other's actions, thereby enhancing robot performance and interaction naturalness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observation that robots operating in collaborative or human-centric environments are prone to making errors. Effective error detection is identified as a prerequisite for error repair and, consequently, for ensuring efficient and productive robot operation. Humans naturally utilize social feedback—ranging from subtle cues like eye gaze to overt gestures and language—to discern the success or failure of another person's actions. The core motivation of this paper is to explore how robots can acquire a similar capability, leveraging advancements in computing and artificial intelligence.

The paper's key objective is to propose a comprehensive framework for how human social cues can serve as feedback signals for robots to recognize task failures in HRI. This framework is intended to establish a research agenda grounded in insights from behavioral science, HRI, and machine learning.

The specific objectives, outlining the scope of the paper's analysis, are threefold:
1. **Defining Feedback from Human Social Cues:** To articulate how human social cues are conceptualized as feedback mechanisms within behavioral science literature. This involves understanding what constitutes human error and how people typically react when observing another person's mistake.
2. **Recognizing Task Failures in HRI Research:** To review existing approaches and taxonomies for identifying robot task failures within HRI research, and to examine how robots can potentially utilize nonverbal human feedback for error detection.
3. **Identifying State-of-the-Art Machine Learning Tools and Datasets:** To survey current machine learning methodologies and relevant datasets that can be employed for autonomous detection of HRI task failures based on human social cues.

In pursuing these objectives, the authors aim to achieve several contributions:
- To contextualize existing HRI efforts within a broader body of work on social cues, thereby mapping out a comprehensive research agenda.
- To highlight the benefits of social cue recognition for HRI, including increased feedback availability, more natural interactions, and the fundamental inclusion of human interactants as stakeholders in determining task success.
- To introduce a novel taxonomy for error detection that considers both the robot's self-awareness of an error and the detection of that error by others, particularly emphasizing Type 3 errors (errors unrecognized by the robot but detected by humans).
- To offer practical recommendations for HRI researchers and practitioners interested in developing robots capable of detecting task errors using human social cues, with a particular emphasis on technical implementation details for ML systems.

The work explicitly distinguishes itself from prior reviews by focusing on actionable methods for implementing social responses to failures, rather than solely exploring the theoretical understanding of failure itself.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper adopts the methodology of a comprehensive literature review combined with the proposal of an interdisciplinary framework and research agenda. The authors' approach involved a multi-faceted strategy for gathering and synthesizing relevant academic literature:

1. **Literature Collection:** Key papers from the domains of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) and Machine Learning (ML) that investigated task failures between humans and robots were initially gathered.
2. **Citation Network Expansion:** The Citation Gecko software tool was utilized to expand the literature pool. This tool helped identify papers that cited the initial seed papers or were cited by them, thereby creating a network of interconnected research.
3. **Database Searches:** Supplementary searches were conducted through established academic literature databases, including the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) Digital Library and Google Scholar, to ensure broad coverage.
4. **Subjective Selection:** The authors explicitly state that the papers included in the review were selected based on their subjective belief regarding relevance and potential impact within the field. This implies that the review does not aim to be an exhaustive, replicable survey of all existing literature but rather a curated overview intended to initiate discussion and establish foundational groundwork.

The core of the approach involves organizing the synthesized insights into a structured framework comprising three main themes, each corresponding to one of the paper's overarching research questions:

- **Theme 1: Human Social Cues as Feedback: Perspectives from Behavioral Science.** This section delves into definitions of social cues and human error from a behavioral science standpoint, examines how humans detect their own and others' errors, and analyzes human behavior when observing mistakes. It also introduces a custom taxonomy of error detection based on self-awareness and social feedback.
- **Theme 2: Recognizing Task Failures in Human-Robot Interaction Research.** This theme explores existing taxonomies of robot errors and reviews how HRI research has approached harnessing nonverbal human feedback, including challenges and existing studies.
- **Theme 3: Machine Learning Methods for Failure Detection in HRI with Social Cues.** This section focuses on the technical aspects, providing an overview of state-of-the-art algorithmic approaches (unsupervised, supervised, and reinforcement learning) and relevant datasets for detecting task failures using social cues. It also discusses the practical requirements for implementing these technical solutions.

The paper maintains a specific scope by concentrating on *error detection* and consciously refraining from an in-depth exploration of error *repair* strategies. This focused approach allows for a detailed examination of the mechanisms and challenges associated with the initial recognition of robot failures through human social signals.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a synthesis of findings across three core themes, leading to the development of a framework and research agenda for social cue-based task failure recognition in HRI.

**Theme 1: Human Social Cues as Feedback (Behavioral Science)**

- **Definition of Social Cues:** Social cues are defined as "biologically and physically determined features salient to observers because of their potential as channels of useful information". The "Computers Are Social Actors" (CASA) paradigm suggests that people interpret computer (and robot) behavior socially and express social cues towards them.
- **Taxonomy of Error Detection:** A novel, simplified taxonomy of error detection is introduced, based on self-awareness and social feedback from others. This taxonomy highlights four types of errors, with **Type 3 (N/Y)** being particularly relevant for robots: an error not recognized by the self (robot) but identified by others (humans).
- **Understanding Human Error:** The paper adopts Hollnagel's definition of errors as "actions not as intended," emphasizing that the ultimate judgment of an action's success lies with the human stakeholder. It notes a shift in error research from individual components to system-wide failures within socio-technical contexts.
- **Detecting Errors:** Sellen's taxonomy of self-error detection is discussed, noting that the category of "undetected" errors (errors detected by others) provides a clear opportunity for robots to leverage human social feedback. Human behavioral cues like body position or gaze can be used for intention recognition, and datasets like "Oops!" exist for studying unintentional actions.
- **Human Reactions to Mistakes:** People use "grounding sequences" to build mutual understanding, which includes providing feedback on correctness. Reactions to observed mistakes can be verbal or non-verbal, encompassing gaze, facial expressions, nonverbal utterances, nodding, body position, and gestures. Non-verbal cues are important for conveying empathy and can serve as social reinforcement, influencing future behavior. The authors caution against attributing intrinsic meaning or direct causality to social cues, advocating for a correlation-based approach due to multiple confounding factors.

**Theme 2: Recognizing Task Failures in HRI Research**

- **Types of Robot Errors:** Robot errors are complex and varied, ranging from anthropomorphic to non-anthropomorphic contexts. The paper recommends taxonomies such as that proposed by Honig et al., which categorizes social failures (interactional, technical) and performance failures. Other taxonomies exist based on severity, recoverability, or communication issues.
- **Harnessing Nonverbal Human Feedback:** While robots have been designed to use nonverbal cues to impact human performance (e.g.,), the inverse—robots leveraging human nonverbal feedback for error detection—is gaining traction. Studies show humans exhibit complex, multimodal reactions to robot failures, including verbalizations, body motion, gaze, and facial expressions.
- **Challenges in HRI:** Challenges include the subtlety of human signals, the potential absence of reactions in certain contexts, and incongruence in responses. Factors such as the nature of the failure (social norm violation vs. technical failure), the presence of other people, and robot appearance (anthropomorphism) significantly influence human reactions. These variations necessitate the development of generalizable methods.

**Theme 3: Machine Learning Methods for Failure Detection in HRI with Social Cues**

- **Technical Approaches to Failure Detection:** Historically, failure detection used rule-based or heuristic models. Modern ML offers automated classification but faces challenges in data collection, generalizability, handling variations, and scarcity of annotated datasets. Affective computing, utilizing ML/DL for emotional state prediction, provides relevant methods for interpreting human reactions.
- **Promising ML Approaches:**
 - **Unsupervised Learning:** Suitable for unlabeled data, methods include Self-Supervised Learning (SSL), probabilistic distributions, and neural networks. Examples include 3D-CNNs and attention-based transformers on the "Oops!" dataset for unintentional action detection, and models leveraging 3D kinematics for intentionality. Unsupervised methods are beneficial for "in-the-wild" data with automated labeling.
 - **Supervised Learning:** Relies on labeled data and common algorithms like SVM, kNN, and Random Forest. Various multimodal data (wearable sensors, LIDAR, respiration, facial expressions) have been used. Multimodal fusion often outperforms unimodal approaches in activity, affect, and engagement recognition, despite integration challenges. Specific HRI applications include multimodal data for conversational failure detection and facial expressions for robot error detection.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** A less explored but promising area where robots learn iteratively from the environment. Interactive RL involves humans providing feedback during the learning process, leading to personalized and natural adaptation. Examples include robots learning humor and engagement from multimodal social cues and using facial expressions for human reinforcement in the TAMER framework. RL is suitable for scenarios where initial errors are permissible for learning.
- **Requirements for Application:** Successful application requires addressing multimodal data fusion, adapting lab-trained models to real-world scenarios, and considering data privacy and demographic variables. Key questions for practitioners involve defining failure types, available data inputs, labeling resources, precision/recall trade-offs, and real-time processing needs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance of this work lies in its comprehensive synthesis of interdisciplinary knowledge to address a fundamental challenge in HRI: enabling robots to recognize their own task failures through human social cues. This capacity is crucial for the development of more robust, adaptive, and human-friendly robots.

The potential impacts are substantial:

- **Advancement of Robot Autonomy and Adaptability:** By providing robots with the ability to interpret human social feedback, the research facilitates the creation of robots that are more "socially self-aware." This enhanced awareness allows robots to detect errors that might otherwise go unnoticed by their internal sensors, enabling them to adapt to complex and dynamic environments more effectively and gracefully recover from unexpected contingencies.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction Quality:** The framework promotes more natural, intuitive, and efficient interactions. When robots can infer their performance failures from human reactions, they can initiate corrective actions, repair communication breakdowns, or seek clarification, thereby fostering greater trust and collaboration between humans and robots. This inherently includes the human interactant as a vital stakeholder in defining task success.
- **Foundational Research Agenda:** The paper lays out a concrete research agenda, highlighting critical gaps and recommending future directions. This includes:
 - **Standardizing Error Definitions:** Moving towards a flexible understanding of errors as "actions not as intended" that acknowledges complexity.
 - **Driving Dataset Development:** Emphasizing the urgent need for publicly available, ethically managed, multimodal, and "in-the-wild" datasets of human reactions to robot failures. Such datasets, akin to the "Oops!" dataset for human actions, are crucial for training and validating robust ML models.
 - **Exploring Diverse Social Cues:** Encouraging the investigation of less-common data streams (e.g., body pose, biosignals, speech prosody) and varied interaction contexts (e.g., operator vs. bystander reactions, anticipatory cues) to enrich the robot's understanding.
 - **Facilitating Real-World Deployment:** Stressing the importance of transitioning research from controlled laboratory settings to real-world scenarios, while carefully considering ethical implications, data privacy, and the influence of demographic factors (age, gender, culture, disability) on human responses.
 - **Promoting ML Best Practices:** Advocating for rigorous benchmarking, transparent reporting of performance metrics (including balanced accuracy and Cohen's Kappa), and exploring cost-aware pre-training for deep learning models.
- **Guidance for Practitioners:** The paper offers practical questions and considerations for researchers and developers in selecting appropriate technical approaches, streamlining the implementation of social cue-based failure detection systems in various applications.
- **Interdisciplinary Collaboration:** By explicitly integrating perspectives from behavioral science, HRI, and machine learning, the work fosters a collaborative environment that can accelerate progress in this complex domain.

In conclusion, this paper provides a critical overview and strategic roadmap for developing robots capable of discerning their task failures from human social cues. Its significance lies in charting a path toward more intelligent, adaptable, and socially fluent robotic systems, ultimately enhancing their utility and acceptance in human-populated environments.
