# 3D Flow Diffusion Policy: Visuomotor Policy Learning via Generating Flow in 3D Space

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at GIST developed the 3D Flow Diffusion Policy (3D FDP), a visuomotor learning framework that models scene-level 3D flow as an intermediate representation for action generation. This approach significantly improved robot manipulation success rates, outperforming baselines by up to 2x in real-world tasks and achieving higher success rates in complex simulated environments.

## Problem

- Existing visuomotor policies often overlook fine-grained, localized motion cues crucial for precise and contact-rich manipulation tasks.
- Many current approaches, including object-centric representations, struggle to capture comprehensive scene-level dynamics and how actions propagate through an entire environment.
- Prior flow-based methods for robotics primarily operate in 2D image space, lacking the 3D spatial precision necessary for effective physical manipulation.

## Method

- Introduces **3D ==Flow Diffusion Policy==** (3D FDP), a framework that learns visuomotor policies by explicitly modeling scene-level 3D flow, captured by sampled query points, as a structured intermediate representation.
- Decomposes the policy into two jointly trained conditional diffusion models: a flow predictor for future 3D flow and an action generator conditioned on observations and the predicted flow sequence.
- Employs a scalable method for generating ground-truth 3D flow in real-world settings by leveraging 2D point trackers (CoTracker) and depth information to lift 2D trajectories into 3D space.

## Results

- In simulation (MetaWorld benchmark), 3D FDP achieved the highest success rates across all difficulty levels, outperforming DP3 by **4.2** percentage points on hard tasks (**55.6%** vs. **52.4%**) and by **4.0** percentage points on medium tasks (**55.3%** vs. **51.1%**).
- On a challenging real-world benchmark of 8 tasks, 3D FDP demonstrated an average success rate of **56.9%**, more than double the **27.5%** achieved by the DP3 baseline.
- 3D FDP uniquely succeeded in complex real-world tasks involving long-horizon or non-prehensile interactions (e.g., **30%** for Hang, **20%** for Shelve, **35%** for Non-prehensile Manipulation), where DP3 consistently failed with **0%** success rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling scene-level 3D flow provides superior interaction-aware spatial grounding compared to direct observation-to-action mappings or object-centric pose predictions, especially for precise and contact-rich tasks.
> - Integrating 3D flow prediction directly into a unified diffusion architecture ensures action generation is consistent with and explicitly conditioned on predicted scene dynamics, enhancing policy robustness and generalizability.
> - Dense, scene-level 3D flow, captured by query points across the entire environment, is more informative for complex manipulation than sparse or object-only flow, improving understanding of broad action consequences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust visuomotor policies capable of generalizing across varied objects and interaction dynamics represents a significant challenge in robotic manipulation. Historically, approaches to visuomotor control have often relied on directly mapping observations to actions or compressing perceptual inputs into either global or object-centric features. While such methods, including those leveraging transformers and recent diffusion models, have advanced the field by enabling stable training and multimodal action generation, they frequently encounter limitations. Specifically, they can overlook fine-grained, localized motion cues that are critical for precise and contact-rich manipulation tasks.

Recent advancements in visual imitation learning have introduced more structured priors. Diffusion models, exemplified by Diffusion Policy and 3D Diffusion Policy, have shown promise in generating actions by iteratively denoising. Concurrently, some research has explored intermediate representations, such as object poses (e.g., Motion Before Action) or future frame predictions. However, object-pose-based methods can struggle to capture comprehensive scene-level dynamics, while future frame prediction approaches often incur substantial computational costs. Furthermore, many existing flow-based methods for robotics, such as Track2Act, ATM, and Im2Flow2Act, operate primarily in 2D image space, thereby lacking the 3D spatial precision necessary for effective physical manipulation. While methods like PPI have begun to incorporate object-level 3D point flow, these are typically object-centric and may not explicitly structure action generation to be consistent with predicted motion across the entire scene.

This work contributes to this landscape by introducing 3D Flow Diffusion Policy (3D FDP), which addresses these limitations. It positions scene-level 3D flow as a learnable, intermediate representation that explicitly captures fine-grained local interaction dynamics. By moving beyond object-centric representations and integrating flow prediction directly into a unified diffusion architecture for action generation, 3D FDP aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of scene motion. This approach is designed to improve generalization capabilities, particularly in complex, contact-rich scenarios where existing methods may fall short due to their reliance on less granular or less integrated motion cues. The proposed methodology seeks to enhance spatial grounding and enable the policy to reason about the broader consequences of actions within a dynamic environment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a visuomotor policy capable of generating low-level robot actions by explicitly modeling and reasoning about local interaction dynamics within a 3D environment. This is achieved through the use of scene-level 3D flow, captured by sampled query points, as a structured intermediate representation.

The motivation for this approach stems from several identified limitations in existing robotic manipulation policies:
- **Overlooking Localized Motion Cues:** Many current visuomotor policies, including end-to-end architectures and those relying on global or object-centric features, often abstract away the localized motion patterns that are essential for precise, contact-rich manipulation tasks. This can lead to brittle policies that lack the necessary fine-grained control.
- **Limited Scene-Level Dynamics:** Approaches that utilize object-level intermediate representations, such as object poses, may struggle to capture the complex, dynamic interactions that occur across an entire scene, especially in cluttered or multi-object environments. Reasoning about how an action propagates through a scene is challenging with only object-specific information.
- **Computational Burden of Predictive Models:** While predicting future frames or full scene states can offer rich temporal information, these methods are often computationally intensive, making them less suitable for real-time robotic deployment.
- **Desire for Interaction-Aware Spatial Grounding:** There is a need for policies that are spatially grounded in the dynamics of interaction between the robot and its environment. Capturing how the robot gripper's motion correlates with changes in manipulated objects and their surroundings can lead to more robust and adaptive behavior.
- **Leveraging Advances in Visual Tracking:** Inspired by the success of modern visual trackers (e.g., CoTracker, SpatialTracker) in densely estimating motion, the authors aim to apply the concept of flow-based representations to directly inform robotic actions, but in a 3D, scene-level context. This moves beyond prior flow-based methods that were often 2D or object-centric.
- **Improving Generalization:** By providing a model with an explicit understanding of scene-level motion, the authors hypothesize that the policy will achieve better generalization across variations in object geometry, appearance, and dynamics, as it can adapt to the underlying physical interactions rather than just visual patterns.
- **Unified Learning Framework:** The motivation includes integrating flow prediction and action generation into a single, unified diffusion-based framework. This aims to ensure that action generation is explicitly conditioned on and consistent with the predicted motion, thereby making the intermediate representation a core component of the policy rather than an auxiliary input.

These motivations collectively drive the development of 3D FDP, aiming to enhance the robustness, precision, and generalizability of visuomotor policies for complex robotic manipulation in unstructured environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The 3D Flow Diffusion Policy (3D FDP) framework is designed to learn visuomotor policies by explicitly modeling scene-level 3D flow as an intermediate representation. The overall approach decomposes the policy into two learnable components: a flow predictor ($g_\theta$) and an action generator ($h_\theta$), both implemented as conditional diffusion models.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

At each timestep $t$, the robot receives an observation $o_t = (p_t, s_t)$, consisting of a 3D point cloud ($p_t$) from an RGB-D camera and its proprioceptive state ($s_t$). The robot generates a continuous low-level action $a_t$. The policy learns from a dataset of expert demonstration trajectories.
Interaction dynamics are represented by a set of $M$ query points $\{q_j \in \mathbb{R}^3\}_{j=1}^M$, sampled from the initial point cloud. The 3D flow for a query point $q_j$ at time $t$ is its displacement $\Delta q_{j,t} = q_{j,t} - q_{j,t-1}$. The full 3D flow representation is a sequence of these displacements for all query points over a future horizon $T_f$, denoted as $F_{t:t+T_f}$.
Instead of a direct observation-to-action mapping, the policy $f_\theta$ is defined as a composition $h_\theta \circ g_\theta$. The flow predictor $g_\theta$ maps the observation history $O_{t-T_h:t}$ to a predicted future flow sequence $\hat{F}_{t:t+T_f}$. The action generator $h_\theta$ then produces the final action sequence $\hat{A}_{t:t+T_a}$, conditioned on both the observation history $O_{t-T_h:t}$ and the predicted flow $\hat{F}_{t:t+T_f}$.

#### 3.2. Perception

- **3D Scene Representation:** The scene is represented as a 3D point cloud obtained from a single RGB-D camera. Static background regions are cropped, and a fixed number of points ($N_p = 1024$) are sampled using Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) to ensure spatial coverage. The point cloud is processed by a modified PointNet architecture, which outputs both a global scene feature and point-wise local features. The method relies solely on geometric information, without additional semantic features from large pretrained models, to reduce computational cost.
- **Query Point Sampling:** $M=100$ query points are sampled from the first frame of the observation sequence using FPS. These points act as anchors for flow prediction and are tracked throughout the episode to capture interaction-aware motion.

#### 3.3. 3D Scene Flow Ground-Truth Generation

Ground-truth 3D flow trajectories are required for training and are obtained differently in simulation and real-world settings:
- **Simulation (MetaWorld):** In the MetaWorld simulator, the ground-truth mesh poses of objects are accessible. Query points sampled from the initial depth-based point cloud are associated with the nearest vertices on the object meshes. Their 3D trajectories are then reconstructed by transforming these associated vertices using the known mesh poses over time.
- **Real-world Environments:** For real-world scenarios, a scalable method is employed. 3D query points are sampled from an initial depth image point cloud using FPS and then projected onto the corresponding 2D RGB frame to determine their initial pixel locations. CoTracker, a 2D point tracking model, is used to predict the 2D trajectories of these points across the episode. To improve robustness against tracking failures due to occlusions, inference is performed in overlapping temporal chunks. Finally, these 2D trajectories are lifted back into 3D space using the depth information at each timestep, yielding continuous 3D flow trajectories.

#### 3.4. Flow-Conditioned Policy Learning

The policy consists of two conditional diffusion models:
- **3D Flow Generation ($g_\theta$):** This module predicts the future flow sequence $\hat{F}_{t:t+T_f}$. It is implemented as a denoising diffusion model. A condition vector $\hat{O}^{flow}_t$ is computed from the observation history by concatenating the global scene feature and MLP-processed per-point local features from the PointNet encoder. A denoising network $\epsilon^{flow}_\theta$ takes a noisy flow sequence, the condition vector $\hat{O}^{flow}_t$, and the diffusion timestep $k$ as input, predicting the noise to be removed according to a defined noise schedule.
- **Action Generation ($h_\theta$):** This module produces the final action sequence $\hat{A}_{t:t+T_a}$. It is also a conditional diffusion model, conditioned on both the observation history and, critically, the *predicted* flow sequence $\hat{F}_{t:t+T_f}$ from the flow generator. A condition vector $\hat{O}^{act}_t$ is formed by encoding the predicted flow sequence (using a 1D temporal convolution followed by global average pooling to create a plan-level embedding) and concatenating it with the global observation feature. This $\hat{O}^{act}_t$ remains fixed during denoising. A denoising network $\epsilon^{act}_\theta$ refines a noisy action sequence to generate the output, using the same noise schedule as the flow generator.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Training:** Both the flow predictor and action generator are jointly trained using two L1 objectives: $L_F$ for flow prediction and $L_A$ for action prediction. Separate AdamW optimizers are used for each module. The PointNet encoder parameters are shared, but the diffusion models are separate. A DDIM scheduler is used with 100 training timesteps. Training runs for 1000 epochs with a batch size of 128. An observation history of $T_h=2$ and prediction horizons of $T_f=T_a=16$ are used. Exponential Moving Average (EMA) is applied to model parameters for evaluation.
- **Inference:** During inference, 100 query points are sampled once at the start of an episode. At each timestep, the policy sequentially predicts the future flow (16 steps), which then conditions the generation of a 16-step action sequence using a DDIM scheduler with 10 inference steps. The first action from this sequence is executed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of 3D FDP was conducted through extensive experiments in both simulation (MetaWorld benchmark) and real-world robotic tasks.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments (MetaWorld)

- **Environment and Dataset:** Experiments were performed on the MetaWorld benchmark, comprising 50 diverse manipulation tasks. All methods were trained on a consistent dataset of 10 expert demonstrations per task. 3D FDP used 3D scene flow supervision, while the MBA baseline used 9-DoF object poses, both taking 3D point clouds as input.
- **Baselines:** The method was compared against DP3, a diffusion policy without an explicit intermediate representation, and MBA, which uses predicted 9-DoF object poses as its intermediate representation.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Policies were trained once and evaluated across three different seeds. Performance was measured by mean success rate, averaged over 200 evaluation episodes per seed (600 total episodes per task) for main results, and 20 episodes per seed for supplementary experiments.
- **Simulation Results (Table I):** 3D FDP achieved the highest success rates across all MetaWorld difficulty levels, demonstrating particularly pronounced improvements on medium and hard tasks. For instance, on easy tasks, 3D FDP scored 86.5% compared to DP3's 85.8% and MBA's 84.2%. On medium tasks, 3D FDP achieved 55.3%, surpassing DP3 (51.1%) and MBA (49.7%). Similarly, on hard tasks, 3D FDP recorded 55.6% versus DP3's 52.4% and MBA's 53.0%. These results suggest that the scene-level 3D flow representation is more informative for action generation, especially in more complex scenarios.
- **Additional Experiments:**
 - **Effect of Query Points (Table II):** An analysis on the MetaWorld hard set indicated that increasing the number of query points from 50 to 200 consistently improved success rates (from 52.2% to 60.8%). This suggests that denser flow tracking provides richer interaction cues beneficial for action generation.
 - **Query Point Sampling Strategy (Table III):** Sampling query points from the entire scene yielded higher success rates than sampling only from the manipulated object. This was particularly evident in tasks like "Hammer," where observing environmental changes (e.g., nail displacement) through scene-level flow was crucial for task progress. For "Basketball," scene sampling achieved 98.3% compared to object-only sampling's 93.3%.
 - **Learning Efficiency (Fig. 4):** Learning curves on representative tasks showed that 3D FDP converged faster and achieved higher peak performance compared to DP3, indicating improved sample efficiency.

#### 4.2. Real-world Experiments

- **Real Robot Benchmark:** The approach was validated on a challenging real-world benchmark of 8 tasks, utilizing an 8-DoF action space (3D position, 4D quaternion, binary gripper state) with a Franka Panda manipulator (Fig. 5). Tasks included Assemble, Disassemble, Press, Pull & Grasp, Open, Hang, Shelve, and Non-prehensile Manipulation.
- **Demonstrations and Training:** 50 expert demonstrations were collected per task using a GELLO teleoperation device, with object poses randomized within a ±1 cm range. Policies were trained for 1000 epochs without data augmentation.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** 3D FDP was compared against DP3. Each policy was tested 20 times per task, and success was determined by human evaluation of the final scene state.
- **Real-world Results (Fig. 5):** 3D FDP demonstrated a substantial performance advantage, achieving an average success rate of 56.9%, more than double DP3's 27.5%.
 - **Robustness in Structured Tasks:** 3D FDP showed greater robustness in tasks like Assemble (60% vs. DP3's 15%) and Press (100% vs. DP3's 25%).
 - **Complex Interactions:** The performance gap widened in scenarios requiring long-horizon or complex interactions. In tasks like Hang and Shelve, which involve varied in-hand poses, occlusion, and interaction with surrounding objects, DP3 consistently failed (0% success), while 3D FDP achieved 30% and 20% success rates, respectively.
 - **Non-prehensile Manipulation:** In the non-prehensile task, where a translational push needs to induce rotational motion, 3D FDP achieved a 35% success rate. DP3 failed (0%), indicating its inability to predict such complex motion relationships (Fig. 6b).
- **Visualization of Predicted Flow (Fig. 6):** Visualizations illustrate that 3D FDP correctly predicts flow capturing both the robot's motion and the displacement/rotation of manipulated objects, even when the resulting object motion is indirect.

These findings indicate that conditioning policies on an explicit model of scene dynamics via 3D flow provides a more effective and generalizable approach for robotic manipulation, particularly in challenging, contact-rich, and non-prehensile scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of the 3D Flow Diffusion Policy (3D FDP) framework introduces a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation. By explicitly modeling scene-level 3D flow as a structured intermediate representation, this work addresses a key limitation in current visuomotor policies: their tendency to overlook localized motion cues critical for precise and contact-rich interactions.

The primary significance of this work lies in its demonstration that a scene-level, interaction-aware 3D flow representation, learned jointly with action generation within a unified diffusion architecture, can substantially enhance the robustness and generalizability of robotic policies. This moves beyond object-centric representations or direct observation-to-action mappings, which have shown limitations in capturing the full spectrum of scene dynamics and the downstream consequences of actions.

Potential impacts include:
- **Improved Generalization in Robotic Manipulation:** The consistent outperformance of 3D FDP across diverse tasks in MetaWorld and particularly in challenging real-world scenarios suggests a step towards more generalizable robotic skills. This could reduce the need for extensive task-specific engineering or vast amounts of demonstration data for every new manipulation task.
- **Enhanced Performance in Contact-Rich and Non-Prehensile Tasks:** The method's notable success in real-world tasks involving precise contact (e.g., Assemble, Press), long-horizon planning, and complex non-prehensile interactions (e.g., Shelve, Non-prehensile Manipulation) indicates its potential to unlock capabilities for robots operating in unstructured or cluttered environments where such interactions are common. The ability to reason about indirect motion propagation (e.g., translational input leading to rotational output) is a notable advance.
- **Foundational for Adaptive Behavior:** By grounding manipulation in localized dynamics and enabling the policy to reason about broader scene-level consequences, 3D FDP provides a mechanism for robots to respond more adaptively to unexpected perturbations or variations in the environment, moving beyond rigid, pre-programmed behaviors.
- **Scalable Real-world Deployment:** The developed method for generating ground-truth 3D flow in real-world environments, which leverages 2D trackers and depth information rather than requiring expensive motion capture systems or complex reconstruction pipelines, suggests a more scalable and practical approach for data collection and model training in real robotic systems.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The findings emphasize the effectiveness of flow-based representations as an inductive bias for visuomotor learning. This may inspire further research into other forms of dynamic intermediate representations and their integration into sophisticated control architectures, potentially combining with vision-language models for more semantic understanding of motion intent.

In summary, 3D FDP offers a pathway toward more capable and autonomous robotic systems by endowing them with a more nuanced understanding of physical interactions and dynamics, thereby enabling broader applications in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and service robotics.
