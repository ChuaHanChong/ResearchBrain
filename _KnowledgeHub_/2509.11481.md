# RAPTOR: A Foundation Policy for Quadrotor Control

> [!summary] Summary
> RAPTOR, a compact recurrent neural network, establishes a foundation policy for quadrotor control, leveraging Meta-Imitation Learning to enable zero-shot adaptation and real-time emergent system identification across various unseen physical platforms. This policy, with only 2084 parameters, achieves trajectory tracking performance comparable to specialized controllers while deploying on resource-constrained microcontrollers.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies for quadrotors commonly overfit to specific dynamics, necessitating full retraining for minor system changes or Sim2Real gaps.
- Existing adaptive quadrotor control policies often require high-level inputs, are not fully end-to-end, or are too computationally intensive for embedded systems.
- Current approaches struggle with real-time adaptation to unseen or changing quadrotor dynamics, contrasting with human-like rapid adaptability.

## Method

- **RAPTOR**, an end-to-end recurrent neural network (GRU) foundation policy, is designed for in-context learning to adapt to unknown quadrotor dynamics.
- A broad distribution of quadrotor parameters is defined, and 1000 specialized teacher policies are pre-trained using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC).
- Meta-Imitation Learning distills the collective behavior of these teacher policies into a single, compact student policy by minimizing action distribution divergence.

## Results

- The RAPTOR policy demonstrated robust zero-shot adaptation and control across 10 diverse real quadrotors and 2 simulators, including out-of-distribution platforms.
- Achieved trajectory tracking Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of **0.07**-0.19m for 10s trajectories and **0.17**-0.29m for 5.5s trajectories, comparable to specialized controllers.
- Exhibited robustness to strong wind gusts (7-10 m/s), varying payloads (up to **1.2** kg), aggressive physical disturbances, and successfully extrapolated its context window 10-fold.
- A linear probe on the policy's latent representation accurately predicted thrust-to-weight ratio (R2 of **0.949**), confirming emergent implicit system identification.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A recurrent neural network policy can perform emergent implicit system identification by inferring unobserved quadrotor dynamics parameters from its interaction history.
> - The 'foundation model' paradigm, when applied to robotic control through broad distribution training and in-context learning, enables robust zero-shot adaptation.
> - A highly compact recurrent policy (2084 parameters) can achieve both high performance and broad adaptability, making it feasible for resource-constrained embedded systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous robotic control systems, particularly for quadrotors, has seen significant advancements. However, a prevalent challenge in the field, especially with policies trained using Reinforcement Learning (RL), is their tendency to overfit to specific environments or dynamics models. This specialization results in a lack of adaptability, where even minor differences, such as those encountered in the Simulation-to-Reality (Sim2Real) gap or changes in system components (e.g., propellers or batteries), necessitate extensive system identification and retraining. This contrasts with human capabilities, which allow for rapid adaptation to new conditions (e.g., driving an unfamiliar car) with minimal prior experience.

Existing research in quadrotor control has explored various approaches, ranging from classical cascaded controllers that require manual retuning to modern nonlinear methods like Model Predictive Control (MPC) and RL. While RL policies have demonstrated proficiency in tasks such as quadrotor racing, they often either overfit a single dynamics model or rely on domain randomization without incorporating adaptation. Domain randomization can lead to conservative policies because they must generalize over a broad range of fixed parameters rather than identifying and adapting to the specific system in real-time. Furthermore, many neural network-based quadrotor control methods are not fully end-to-end, often outputting higher-level setpoints (e.g., collective thrust and body rates) rather than direct motor commands. Recent works have investigated end-to-end control, but these policies typically remain highly optimized for a single quadrotor, demanding full retraining for platform changes.

Attempts to address adaptability and generalization in neural network policies for multiple quadrotors have been made. For instance, some approaches train single, Markovian policies designed to operate across a limited range of platforms or develop adaptive policies that require high-level control inputs rather than being fully end-to-end. These adaptive policies often utilize significantly larger neural networks, limiting their deployment on resource-constrained hardware.

This work positions itself within this landscape by drawing inspiration from the concept of "foundation models" from vision and language domains, which are characterized by training on broad data distributions and enabling in-context learning through causal sequences. The paper aims to bridge the gap between specialized robotic control policies and the adaptability observed in biological systems by developing a single, end-to-end, foundation policy for quadrotor control that can perform emergent implicit system identification and adapt zero-shot to a wide variety of unseen quadrotor platforms. This approach seeks to overcome the limitations of overfitting, reduce retraining requirements, and facilitate broad adoption of learned control methods in diverse real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of current robotic control systems, particularly those based on Reinforcement Learning (RL). These systems are typically highly specialized, or "overfit," to a single environment or a narrow range of pre-defined system dynamics. This specialization renders them brittle: even minor changes, such as those arising from the Simulation-to-Reality (Sim2Real) gap, altering propeller types, or simply deploying on a slightly different quadrotor, necessitate extensive system identification and subsequent retraining. This process is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and limits the broad applicability of advanced control policies in varied real-world scenarios. The paper highlights a fundamental disparity between these robotic systems and human adaptive capabilities, where extensive initial training is followed by rapid, few-shot adaptation to novel, unseen systems (e.g., a new car with different responses).

To address this challenge, the key objective is to devise a control policy for quadrotors that can adapt to unseen system dynamics with minimal real-time data, emulating human-like adaptability. This objective is directly inspired by the progress in "foundation models" in fields such as vision and language, which rely on two core premises:
1. **Broad Distribution**: Training on an expansive data distribution ensures that most deployment-time queries fall within the model's learned distribution.
2. **In-Context Learning**: Utilizing causal sequences as input facilitates adaptation and learning from ongoing interactions.

Therefore, the central goal is to develop an end-to-end neural network policy capable of rapidly adapting to novel quadrotor systems by processing a high-frequency sequence of interactions. This process is conceptualized as "emergent implicit system identification," where the recurrent policy learns to infer unobserved or latent dynamics variables on the fly, inferring relevant parameters (e.g., thrust-to-weight ratio, motor delays) without explicitly reconstructing them, solely driven by the objective of performing well according to a reward function.

The research is structured around addressing six main research questions:
1. **Feasibility**: Can a recurrent, end-to-end neural network policy exhibit the desired adaptive behavior for quadrotor control?
2. **Size and Real-time Capability**: What are the parameter requirements for such a recurrent neural network policy, and can it operate in hard real-time on small microcontrollers?
3. **Context Window**: How long of a sequence can the policy effectively process for in-context learning without forgetting system dynamics, given the known challenges of training recurrent neural networks for long sequences?
4. **Generalization**: Does the policy generalize to unseen quadrotors, both those within the training distribution and those considered out-of-distribution?
5. **Adaptation Time**: How much time is required for the policy to gather sufficient information to stably control a quadrotor, particularly when activated mid-flight?
6. **Agility-Adaptability Trade-off**: Is there an inherent compromise between the agility of the quadrotor's control and the policy's adaptability?

In pursuit of these objectives and questions, the paper identifies five main contributions:
1. **RAPTOR (Real-time Adaptive Policy Through Online Reasoning)**: A comprehensive method for training an end-to-end foundation policy for quadrotor control, encompassing the design of a broad distribution over dynamics parameters, a novel Meta-Imitation Learning algorithm, and a formal architectural derivation.
2. **Efficient Open-Source Implementation**: Provision of a highly efficient, open-source implementation to enable reproducibility with resource-constrained hardware.
3. **Scaling Laws Study**: Investigation into the scaling behavior of the Meta-Imitation Learning process.
4. **Extensive Experimental Validation**: Conducting comprehensive indoor and outdoor experiments across 10 diverse real quadrotor platforms to validate the policy's capabilities against the stated research questions.
5. **Robust Deployment Means**: Providing practical and robust methods for deploying the foundation policy across various flight controller firmwares and simulation environments, fostering future research and adoption.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on developing "RAPTOR," a foundation policy for quadrotor control, through a multi-stage training process inspired by principles from foundation models and probabilistic graphical models.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The quadrotor control problem is formally defined as a Bayes Adaptive Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (BAPOMDP). This framework includes:
- **State (S)**: Comprising position, orientation, linear/angular velocity, previous action, motor states, and a random external force.
- **Initial State Distribution (S₀)**: Randomly sampled position, orientation, linear/angular velocity, with a 10% chance of starting at the target state. External force resembles wind disturbance.
- **Termination Relation (D)**: Defines states that lead to episode termination (e.g., exceeding position, velocity, or angular velocity limits).
- **Actions (A)**: Individual motor commands (RPM setpoints).
- **Transition Probabilities (T)**: Governed by quadrotor dynamics, implemented using the L2F simulator.
- **Reward Function (r)**: Deterministic, penalizing position error, orientation deviation, action changes, and termination, while providing a survival bonus.
- **Observation Space (O)**: A subset of the state space, including position, orientation, linear/angular velocity, and previous action, but occluding motor states and external disturbances, as these are typically unobservable on real platforms.
- **Discount Factor (γ)**: Set at 0.99.
- **Domain Parameters (Ξ)**: A set of system parameters (mass, arm length, thrust-curve coefficients, moment coefficient, inertia matrix, motor delays) that define the specific quadrotor dynamics.

The BAPOMDP framework is crucial as it allows factoring out the system parameters (Ξ), which are sampled once per episode and remain constant. A probabilistic graphical model (Bayesian Network) is used to formally derive the RAPTOR architecture, illustrating how the optimal action (a*\_t) depends on the current state (s\_t) and dynamics parameters (Ξ). Critically, since s\_t and Ξ are unobserved by the student policy, the graphical model demonstrates that the optimal action distribution becomes dependent on the entire history of observations (o\_0:t) and actions (a\_0:t-1). This motivates the design of a recurrent policy capable of in-context learning.

#### 3.2. Domain Randomization

A key component of RAPTOR is training on a broad distribution of quadrotor dynamics to facilitate zero-shot adaptation. This involves designing a physically plausible generative distribution over Ξ parameters. Instead of direct joint distribution formulation, a factorized approach with ancestral sampling is used to capture realistic correlations between parameters like mass, geometry, inertia, thrust curves, and motor delays. The sampling process ensures a wide, yet physically realistic, range of quadrotor configurations, preventing disproportionate biasing towards certain sizes or characteristics. For example, mass is sampled cubically with size, and relationships between thrust-to-weight, torque-to-inertia, and inertia matrix are established based on physical principles and empirical observations.

#### 3.3. Training Methodology

The training process is divided into two main phases:

#### 3.3.1. Pre-Training (Teacher Policies)

1. **Sampling Quadrotors**: 1000 unique quadrotors are sampled from the designed domain randomization distribution.
2. **Individual Expert Policies**: For each of the 1000 sampled quadrotors, a dedicated expert *teacher* policy is trained using Reinforcement Learning. Each teacher is specialized for its specific quadrotor dynamics (Ξ).
3. **RL Algorithm**: Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), an off-policy algorithm, is chosen for its observed robustness, enabling reliable convergence across all 1000 training runs without specific hyperparameter tuning or cherry-picking seeds.
4. **Observability**: Teacher policies are granted full observability, including ground-truth states (position, orientation, velocities, previous action, motor states, external force), as they are not deployed on real hardware.
5. **Overparameterization**: Teachers utilize larger neural networks (three-layer fully connected, hidden dimension 64, >3x larger than the student policy) to improve training stability.
6. **Reward Function and Task**: The reward function is tuned with a termination penalty and action derivative penalty. Training includes position control (returning to origin from varied initial states) and tracking random trajectories sampled from a second-order Langevin process.

#### 3.3.2. Meta-Imitation Learning (Student Foundation Policy)

After the 1000 teacher policies are trained, their combined behaviors are distilled into a single *student* foundation policy.
1. **Student Architecture**: A small Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)-based neural network (three layers: dense input, GRU, dense output) is chosen for the student. It has a hidden dimensionality of 16, resulting in only 2084 parameters. This choice prioritizes computational efficiency for deployment and allows for context window extrapolation.
2. **In-Context Reasoning**: The recurrent nature of the GRU allows the student policy to process the history of observations and its own actions (o\_0:t, a\_0:t-1) to infer the unobservable dynamics parameters (Ξ) of the quadrotor it is currently controlling. This is the core of emergent implicit system identification.
3. **Training Objective**: The Meta-Imitation Learning objective is to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the optimal action distribution (provided by the relevant teacher for the current Ξ) and the student's predicted action distribution. Under the mild assumption of unit standard deviations for action distributions, this objective simplifies to minimizing the Mean-Squared Error (MSE) between the teacher's and student's actions.
4. **Data Collection**: On-policy imitation learning is performed. After an initial warm-up phase (10 epochs) using teacher rollouts, subsequent data collection for training the student uses trajectories generated by the student itself.
5. **Computational Efficiency**: The pre-training phase is "embarrassingly parallel," allowing for significant speed-up by distributing the 1000 independent RL runs across multiple processors. The Meta-Imitation Learning phase, while sequential, is less computationally intensive. The small size of the final foundation policy ensures minimal computational requirements (<10% of available capacity on tiny microcontrollers) during real-time deployment.

#### 3.4. Linear Velocity Feedback Delay Mitigation

For real-world deployment on platforms lacking EKF-based state estimators (e.g., Mahony/Madgwick filters often paired with motion capture), the paper identifies that communication delays in linear velocity feedback can induce low-frequency z-axis oscillations. To mitigate this, an accelerometer integral grounded through an exponential decay (an IIR filter) is overlaid on the linear velocity observation. This essentially generates an estimate of acceleration from the policy's actions, which helps the policy infer dynamics parameters more robustly in the presence of delayed velocity feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive findings regarding the RAPTOR framework's ability to train a robust and adaptive foundation policy for quadrotor control.

#### 4.1. Training Outcomes

- **Pre-Training Stability**: The pre-training phase, involving 1000 independent RL runs for different quadrotor dynamics, demonstrated remarkable stability. All 1000 Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) training runs reliably converged to high-quality policies using a single initial seed, without requiring individual hyperparameter adjustments. This stability is attributed to overparameterization of teacher networks, a carefully tuned reward function, and the use of an off-policy RL algorithm. Most policies achieved good behavior after 100,000 steps, with full convergence to maximum episode lengths after 1 million steps. The entire pre-training could be completed in approximately 34 hours on a single consumer laptop, due to its parallelizable nature.
- **Meta-Imitation Learning Efficiency**: The Meta-Imitation Learning process, distilling the knowledge of 1000 teachers into a single student policy, was computationally less intensive, taking about 1.9 hours. The student policy rapidly learned to imitate the teachers, demonstrating good flight behavior on a validation set of 7 unseen quadrotors within approximately 1000 epochs.
- **Scaling and Model Size**: Analysis of scaling laws revealed a Pareto frontier between performance and the number of teachers, as well as between performance and student model size. The final foundation policy was chosen with 1000 teachers and a hidden dimensionality of 16, resulting in a compact network of only 2084 parameters. This small size is critical for deployment on resource-constrained microcontrollers, consuming less than 10% of computational power.

#### 4.2. Emergent System Identification

The policy successfully exhibits in-context learning. By examining the hidden state trajectory during flight, the research showed that a linear probe could accurately predict the quadrotor's thrust-to-weight ratio (MSE of 0.047, R² of 0.949) from the policy's latent representation. This demonstrates that the recurrent policy implicitly identifies relevant system dynamics parameters on the fly, and this estimate improves over time with more interactions.

#### 4.3. Deployment and Generalization

- **Broad Platform Adaptability**: The foundation policy was deployed and tested on 10 different real quadrotors and 2 simulators, showcasing both Simulation-to-Reality (Sim2Real) and Simulation-to-Simulation (Sim2Sim) transfer. The platforms varied significantly in weight (31.9g to 2.4kg), size (65mm to 500mm), thrust-to-weight ratios (≈1.75 to 12), motor types (brushed/brushless), frame types (soft/rigid), propeller types (2/3/4-blade), and flight controllers (PX4/Betaflight/Crazyflie/M5StampFly) and state estimators (EKF/Mahony/Madgwick).
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization**: The policy demonstrated generalization not only to in-distribution quadrotors but also to several OOD scenarios, including platforms with significantly higher thrust-to-weight ratios (e.g., Flightmare at ≈12 vs. ≤5 in training), flexible frames (only rigid in training), and mixed propeller types (only identical in training).
- **Sim2Real Observations**: Contrary to common assumptions, the study found that simulation-to-reality transfer was not primarily hampered by dynamics model mismatch but more significantly by qualitative differences, particularly firmware implementation details leading to delays and artifacts in state estimation.
- **Mitigation of Z-axis Oscillations**: Low-frequency z-axis oscillations observed on non-EKF-based quadrotors (e.g., those using Mahony or Madgwick filters) due to linear velocity feedback delays were effectively mitigated by integrating a simple accelerometer integral with exponential decay.

#### 4.4. Trajectory Tracking Performance

- **Versatile Tracking**: The policy demonstrated robust trajectory tracking on Lissajous-based figure-eight trajectories of varying agility (10s, 5.5s intervals, and "fastest possible") across all 12 test platforms.
- **Quantitative Performance**: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for 10s trajectories ranged from 0.07m to 0.19m (mean 0.11m), and for 5.5s trajectories, it ranged from 0.17m to 0.29m (mean 0.20m).
- **Comparison to Specialized Policies**: The foundation policy's tracking performance was comparable to state-of-the-art policies specifically trained for a single quadrotor (e.g., 0.19m RMSE on Crazyflie for 5.5s figure-eight, slightly elevated compared to 0.17m for a dedicated policy). This indicates that the RAPTOR policy achieves similar performance while offering broad, zero-shot adaptability.
- **Error Distribution**: The tracking error was primarily observed in the x-y plane, suggesting effective adaptation to varying thrust-to-weight ratios, battery levels, and other conditions to cancel z-axis errors. Agility in tracking was found to be potentially bottlenecked by the lack of trajectory lookahead in observations.

#### 4.5. Robustness to Disturbances and Extrapolation

- **Outdoor Performance**: Outdoor tests with the x500 platform demonstrated stability in strong winds (7-10 m/s gusts) and high linear velocities (up to 10 m/s over ground, >15 m/s relative to wind). The policy also successfully controlled the quadrotor with payloads up to 1.2 kg (total 2.4 kg take-off weight) and tracked trajectories with 600g payload.
- **Disturbance Rejection**: The policy recovered from aggressive disturbances, including physical pokes that tilted the quadrotor beyond 90 degrees, adapted to added weight (tool resting on top), and maintained control under propeller swaps (mixing 2- and 3-blade propellers).
- **Context Window Extrapolation**: Despite being trained on 5-second trajectories, the policy successfully extrapolated its context window 10-fold, consistently tracking 50-second trajectories (5 consecutive 10s loops) without performance degradation. It showed no practical limits, flying until battery depletion over multiple minutes, demonstrating remarkable repeatability.
- **Agile Recovery**: When activated mid-air at 4.5 m/s after manual control, the policy demonstrated rapid, zero-shot adaptation to stabilize the quadrotor, confirming its ability to infer dynamics quickly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RAPTOR framework represents a notable advancement in the field of robotic control, particularly for quadrotors, by demonstrating the feasibility of a single, end-to-end foundation policy capable of robust, zero-shot adaptation across a diverse range of physical platforms. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Adaptability and Generalization**: The core contribution is overcoming the pervasive issue of overfitting in reinforcement learning policies. RAPTOR eliminates the need for platform-specific retraining or extensive system identification for each new quadrotor, a costly and time-consuming bottleneck in robotics deployment. This broad adaptability, validated across 10 real quadrotors with diverse characteristics and even out-of-distribution scenarios, facilitates rapid deployment in varied real-world applications.
- **Real-time Emergent System Identification**: The policy's ability to implicitly identify system dynamics in real-time through in-context learning is a critical capability. This allows the quadrotor to self-adjust to unknown parameters, environmental conditions, or even unexpected changes (e.g., propeller damage, payload variations) without explicit modeling or sensor-based parameter estimation.
- **Computational Efficiency for Embedded Systems**: The resulting foundation policy is remarkably lightweight, with only 2084 parameters, enabling deployment on tiny, resource-constrained microcontrollers commonly found in small quadrotors. This low computational footprint opens possibilities for implementing advanced, adaptive control on a wider array of hardware platforms, including those where traditional, larger neural networks would be infeasible.
- **Performance Parity with Specialized Policies**: Despite its general-purpose nature and adaptability, RAPTOR's policy achieves trajectory tracking performance comparable to or exceeding state-of-the-art policies explicitly specialized for single quadrotors. This suggests that generality does not necessitate a compromise in performance for typical flight tasks.
- **Foundation Model Paradigm for Robotics**: This work demonstrates the potential of applying the "foundation model" paradigm, previously successful in AI domains like language and vision, to robotic control. By training on a broad distribution of dynamics and leveraging in-context learning, RAPTOR provides a blueprint for creating highly versatile robotic behaviors that can adapt to novel situations without catastrophic failure.
- **Reduced Development and Deployment Costs**: By minimizing the need for system-specific development, tuning, and retraining, RAPTOR has the potential to significantly reduce the engineering effort and costs associated with developing and deploying quadrotor systems for diverse applications, from package delivery to inspection and search and rescue.
- **Future Research Avenues**: The identified limitations and future research directions—such as addressing the Sim2Real gap related to flight controller firmware delays and state estimation artifacts, optimizing for linear velocity observation reliance, extending domain randomization, and integrating trajectory lookahead—provide a clear roadmap for further improving the robustness and capabilities of adaptive robotic control policies. These avenues suggest a progressive move towards increasingly robust and autonomous robotic systems.

In conclusion, RAPTOR represents a significant step towards creating more intelligent and versatile autonomous aerial vehicles, paving the way for adaptive, general-purpose robotic controllers that can operate reliably and effectively in complex and changing environments.
