# One-Shot Hierarchical Imitation Learning of Compound Visuomotor Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> One-shot hierarchical imitation learning composes multi-stage manipulation from a single unsegmented human video. Domain-adaptive meta-learning (DAML) turns each video segment into a pixels-to-velocities policy, while separate human and robot phase predictors regress temporal progress to segment the demo and terminate each primitive online. Simulated Sawyer order fulfillment reaches 73.3% on one-object tasks; a real PR2 succeeds 10/20 on kitchen serving.

## Problem

- Prior compound-task learning needs demonstrations labeled by activity, command, or text, or a library of pre-specified primitives, placing heavy annotation burden on humans.
- Robots typically learn from kinesthetic guidance or teleoperation and need many demonstrations, whereas raw human video is easy to provide but carries no direct action supervision.
- Temporally extended skills treated as one monolithic policy demand impractical data, and low-dimensional segmentation tools like changepoint detection fail on raw pixel observations.

## Method

- ==Primitive phase predictors==: recurrent conv-LSTM networks regress partial video to fractional progress t/T, self-supervised from frame indices, giving both human-demo segmentation and robot termination signals.
- **DAML** (domain-adaptive meta-imitation learning) trains initial parameters plus a learned adaptation loss so one gradient step on a human video yields a ==behavioral-cloning== policy for that primitive.
- One-shot composition algorithm: cut the test video wherever human phase exceeds 1-epsilon, adapt a policy per segment, then roll each out until robot phase exceeds 1-epsilon before advancing.

## Results

- Simulated Sawyer order fulfillment: **73.3%** success on one-object and **46.7%** on two-object tasks, versus **50.0%**/**16.7%** for a sliding-window ablation lacking phase prediction.
- An LSTM one-shot meta-learner replacing gradient-based DAML scored **0.0%** on both simulated task lengths, showing gradient-based adaptation is essential for pixel-level compound tasks.
- Real PR2 kitchen serving from a human video: **10/20** trials succeeded with same-target pushing and **7/20** with a different target, while sliding window achieved **0/20** on both.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Temporal progress is a cheap self-supervised label: frame indices alone yield both a segmenter and a termination condition, removing the need for primitive labels.
> - Meta-training on primitives alone can generalize to unseen compositions, so long-horizon demonstrations are not required to acquire long-horizon behavior.
> - Hierarchical accuracy is bottlenecked by the primitive learner; success degrades with sequence length from compounding behavioral-cloning errors, motivating online correction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning from demonstration (LfD) aims to enable robots to acquire skills by observing human examples. Historically, LfD methods have often relied on precise forms of supervision, such as physical guidance, teleoperation, or demonstrations explicitly labeled with primitive actions or task segments. These approaches, while effective for certain tasks, present challenges when applied to complex, multi-stage tasks or when seeking to reduce the burden on human demonstrators. Specifically, teleoperation can be cumbersome, and requiring large amounts of data for each new task or object can be impractical, particularly for tasks involving raw visual inputs.

Prior research has addressed aspects of this problem domain. Some works have focused on imitating manipulation primitives directly from raw video demonstrations provided by humans, demonstrating success in learning individual skills from visual cues. However, scaling these methods to temporally extended, compound tasks—sequences of multiple primitives—remains a challenge. Treating a compound task as a single, monolithic skill often necessitates significantly more demonstration data than is feasible to collect.

Other approaches to learning temporally extended skills have leveraged demonstration data that is either pre-segmented into individual activities, annotated with commands or textual descriptions, or relies on a pre-existing library of pre-programmed primitives. While these methods allow for composition, they introduce a dependency on explicit labeling or predefined skill sets, which limits their generality and increases the overhead for human demonstrators. Furthermore, many existing methods for skill decomposition, such as change point detection or latent variable models, have primarily operated on labeled or low-dimensional state representations, making them less directly applicable to end-to-end visuomotor policies derived from raw, unsegmented human video.

This research addresses a gap in the literature by focusing on learning multi-stage, vision-based tasks from a *single, unsegmented video of a human demonstration*, without requiring teleoperation for the compound task, and while only leveraging prior demonstration data of *primitive subtasks performed with other objects*. The work specifically aims to develop an end-to-end visuomotor policy that maps raw image observations to robot actions, bypassing the need for intermediate language grounding, command structures, or manual segmentation of the compound demonstration. By employing meta-learning to "learn how to learn" primitives and integrating a novel "phase prediction" mechanism for online decomposition and composition, this paper contributes to advancing the state of the art in data-efficient, visually-driven robot imitation learning for complex tasks. It moves towards enabling robots to emulate a human-like ability to infer and execute multi-stage behaviors from minimal, naturalistic observation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a robot to learn to perform complex, multi-stage, vision-based tasks from a single, raw video demonstration provided by a human. This objective is pursued under the constraint that the robot has only prior experience with primitive subtasks, often involving different objects, and does not have access to any teleoperated or labeled demonstrations of the specific compound task it needs to learn at test time.

This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and addresses significant challenges in current robot learning paradigms:

- **Bridging the Human-Robot Imitation Gap:** Humans possess an inherent ability to observe a complex behavior once and then imitate it. In contrast, robots typically require extensive, precisely supervised data (e.g., physical guidance, teleoperation, or numerous demonstrations) to learn even simple tasks. The authors aim to move robot imitation learning closer to human capabilities by enabling learning from weak, high-level supervision (raw human video) and minimal data (one-shot).
- **Addressing Data Scarcity for Compound Tasks:** When a task involves a sequence of multiple primitive skills, treating it as a single, monolithic behavior leads to a significantly longer temporal horizon and a combinatorial increase in the amount of demonstration data required for successful learning. This makes learning complex tasks intractable with conventional methods. The research seeks to overcome this by leveraging the compositional structure of such tasks, thereby reducing the data burden.
- **Reducing Human Burden in Providing Demonstrations:** Current robot learning methods often place a high demand on human demonstrators, requiring them to operate robots via teleoperation, physically guide them, or meticulously label demonstration segments. Raw video demonstrations are considerably easier and more natural for humans to provide, which could democratize robot teaching. The work is motivated by the desire to minimize the need for direct robot interaction or expert knowledge during the demonstration phase.
- **Generalization to Novel Objects and Settings:** Robots frequently encounter novel objects or variations in task setups in real-world environments. Existing methods may struggle to adapt to these novelties without extensive re-training or additional demonstrations. The paper aims for a solution that can generalize learned primitive behaviors to tasks involving previously unseen objects and configurations, solely based on how they are presented in a new human demonstration.
- **End-to-End Visuomotor Learning:** The motivation includes developing an approach that can directly map raw pixel observations to robot actions. This "end-to-end" paradigm avoids reliance on handcrafted features, intermediate state representations, or explicit scene understanding modules, which can be brittle or limit generality. It also streamlines the process from raw observation to action execution.
- **Automated Task Decomposition and Composition:** The research is motivated by the need for robots to autonomously identify and sequence primitive skills within a larger compound task. This includes segmenting an unsegmented human demonstration video into its constituent primitive parts and then dynamically composing the learned policies for these primitives in the correct order, with learned termination conditions, without any manual intervention or prior knowledge of the task's structure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, termed "One-Shot Hierarchical Imitation Learning of Compound Visuomotor Tasks," integrates meta-learning with a mechanism for "primitive phase prediction" to enable robots to learn and execute multi-stage tasks from single human video demonstrations. The approach operates in two main phases: meta-training and meta-test time (one-shot composition).

**A. Meta-Training Phase**

During the initial meta-training phase, the robot is provided with a dataset of demonstrations for various primitive subtasks. This dataset consists of two types of demonstrations for each primitive `P_k` with a specific set of objects:
- **Human Demonstrations (`d_h_i`):** Sequences of raw images (`o_h_1, ..., o_h_T_i`) showing a human performing the primitive. These serve as weak supervision.
- **Robot Demonstrations (`d_r_j`):** Sequences of robot images and corresponding actions (`o_r_1, a_r_1, ..., o_r_T_j, a_r_T_j`) where a robot (e.g., via teleoperation) performs the same primitive. These provide direct action supervision.
Crucially, these human and robot demonstrations for primitives do not need to be temporally aligned, nor do they need to be performed with the same speed or object positions.

The meta-training phase involves training two core components:

1. **Domain-Adaptive Meta-Learning (DAML) for One-Shot Imitation:**
 - The method builds upon DAML, an extension of Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML), which is designed to learn how to adapt a deep network's parameters quickly from a small amount of data.
 - DAML in this context learns an initial set of model parameters (`θ`) for a robot policy and a learned adaptation objective (`L_ψ`).
 - The adaptation objective `L_ψ` acts as a "critic," guiding the learning process. When a single human demonstration `d_h` for a new primitive is provided, one or a few gradient steps are taken with respect to `L_ψ` to quickly adapt `θ` to `φ_T` (the task-specific policy parameters).
 - The meta-objective, `L_BC`, is a mean-squared error behavioral cloning loss. It evaluates the performance of the adapted policy `φ_T` on corresponding robot demonstrations `d_r`. This allows the system to learn how to translate human visual demonstrations (`d_h`) into executable robot policies (`φ_T`).
 - Each distinct primitive, potentially with different objects, constitutes a separate meta-learning task. The DAML policy network utilizes convolutional layers for visual input processing, followed by fully connected layers or temporal convolution layers, depending on the robot platform.

2. **Primitive Phase Predictors (`φ_h` and `φ_r`):**
 - Two separate phase predictor models are trained: `φ_h` for human demonstrations and `φ_r` for robot observations.
 - These models are designed to predict the temporal progress, or "phase," of any given primitive. Given a partial video segment (`o_1:t`), the predictor outputs a scalar value representing `t/T`, where `t` is the current timestep and `T` is the total length of the full primitive demonstration.
 - This phase information provides crucial supervision without requiring manual segmentation or labeling of activities.
 - Both `φ_h` and `φ_r` are implemented as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to handle variable-length input sequences. Their architecture includes 5 convolutional layers (with the first layer initialized using VGG-16 pre-trained weights), followed by an LSTM layer, and a linear output layer. They are trained using a mean-squared error objective.

**B. Meta-Test Time: One-Shot Composition of Primitives**

At meta-test time, the robot is presented with a single, unsegmented video of a human performing a novel compound task (`o_h_1, ..., o_h_T`). This compound task may involve novel objects or configurations not seen during primitive meta-training, although the general families of subtasks (e.g., pushing, grasping) are assumed to have been part of the primitive training data. The process unfolds as follows:

1. **Decomposition of Human Demonstration:**
 - The human phase predictor (`φ_h`) is used to segment the continuous human video of the compound task into a sequence of individual primitive demonstrations (`d_h_1, d_h_2, ...`).
 - This is achieved by feeding frames sequentially into `φ_h`. When `φ_h(o_h_t':t)` exceeds a threshold (e.g., `1 - ε`), it indicates the completion of the current primitive segment, `d_h_i = o_h_t':t`. The process then restarts from the next timestep `t+1` for the subsequent primitive.

2. **Policy Learning for Each Primitive:**
 - For each segmented human primitive `d_h_i`, the meta-learned DAML model is utilized. The meta-learned initial parameters `θ` are adapted using `d_h_i` and the learned adaptation objective `L_ψ` to infer a specific robot policy `π_φ_i` for that primitive. This policy is tailored to the specific primitive and objects observed in `d_h_i`.

3. **Sequential Composition and Execution:**
 - The robot executes the inferred policies (`π_φ_1, π_φ_2, ...`) sequentially.
 - During the execution of each `π_φ_i`, the robot continuously monitors its own visual observations (`o_r_1:t`).
 - The robot phase predictor (`φ_r`) is used to determine when the current primitive's execution is complete. When `φ_r(o_r_t':t)` exceeds the threshold `1 - ε`, the robot terminates the current policy `π_φ_i` and transitions to execute the next policy in the sequence, `π_φ_i+1`.
 - The robot's actions are typically in Cartesian end-effector control space. For the PR2 robot experiments, a discretized action space with a cross-entropy loss was employed.

This methodology allows the robot to dynamically interpret, learn, and execute multi-stage tasks from a single, high-level, and visually complex human demonstration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate the method's ability to learn and perform compound vision-based tasks from a single human video, compose primitives dynamically, and generalize to novel objects. Two primary experimental setups were used: simulated order fulfillment tasks with a Sawyer robot and kitchen serving tasks with a real PR2 robot. The method's performance was compared against ablations to understand the importance of its key components.

**A. Simulated Order Fulfillment (Sawyer Robot)**

- **Task:** The simulated Sawyer robot had to pick and place specified novel objects into a bin, then push the bin to a target location. Tasks varied in the number and type of objects.
- **Meta-training Data:** Consisted of expert demonstrations (PPO for pick-and-place, scripted for pushing) for approximately 1800 distinct pick-and-place primitives and 56 bin pushing primitives, each with diverse textures and object positions.
- **Meta-test Time:** Single visual demonstrations of compound tasks were generated by concatenating expert primitive demonstrations.
- **Comparisons:**
 - **"Sliding window (no phase prediction)":** This baseline used fixed-length segmentation for both human video decomposition and robot policy execution, assuming equal human and robot execution times.
 - **"LSTM one-shot learner (no DAML)":** An LSTM-based meta-learner for visual inputs, similar to prior work but adapted to this visual setting.
- **Results (Table I):**
 - **One-shot skill composition (ours):** Achieved a success rate of 73.3% for 1-object tasks and 46.7% for 2-object tasks.
 - **Sliding window:** Achieved 50.0% for 1-object tasks and 16.7% for 2-object tasks.
 - **LSTM one-shot learner:** Achieved 0.0% for both task types.
- **Key Finding:** The results indicated that both the gradient-based meta-learning (DAML) and the learned phase prediction mechanism are essential for robust performance. The "sliding window" approach performed significantly worse, particularly for longer tasks, highlighting the importance of dynamic segmentation and termination. The poor performance of the "LSTM one-shot learner" suggested the efficacy of the DAML framework for this specific problem. Performance degradation for longer tasks (2-objects vs. 1-object) was observed, attributed to compounding errors inherent in behavioral cloning approaches. Failures were primarily related to grasping, which demands precise visual object localization and control.

**B. PR2 Kitchen Serving (Real Robot)**

- **Task:** The real PR2 robot had to grasp an object, place it into a specific container, and then push either that container or a different one towards a goal position.
- **Meta-training Data:** Collected from an existing dataset (for placing, pushing, pick-and-place primitives) combined with newly collected human demonstrations for these primitives and their transitions.
- **Meta-test Time:** Used 10 novel bowls/containers and 5 novel items, with a single human video demonstration for each compound task.
- **Comparisons:** Only the "sliding window" baseline was evaluated against the proposed method, as the LSTM one-shot learner performed poorly in simulation.
- **Results (Table II):**
 - **One-shot skill composition (ours):** Succeeded in 10 out of 20 trials for placing an object into the target container and pushing the same container, and 7 out of 20 trials for placing and pushing a different container.
 - **Sliding window:** Achieved 0 out of 20 successful trials for both task types.
- **Key Finding:** In the real-world setting, the phase prediction mechanism proved critical. The "sliding window" baseline completely failed, indicating that assuming fixed-length primitive executions from human videos is not viable for real robots where execution times can vary. Similar to the simulation, most failures of the proposed method were attributed to the underlying one-shot imitation learner's inability to precisely perform a primitive, especially in complex pick-and-place operations (e.g., inaccurate grasping).

In summary, the experiments demonstrated that the proposed method, integrating DAML with primitive phase prediction, can enable robots to learn and compose policies for multi-stage visuomotor tasks from single, unsegmented human video demonstrations. This capability was shown to generalize to novel objects in both simulated and real-world environments, outperforming alternative approaches that lacked dynamic segmentation or effective meta-learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robot learning from demonstration, particularly in addressing the challenges associated with complex, multi-stage visuomotor tasks. Its significance and potential impact stem from several aspects:

- **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Accessibility:** The method allows a robot to learn complex, multi-stage tasks from a *single, unsegmented video of a human demonstration*. This significantly reduces the data requirements compared to traditional imitation learning methods that often necessitate numerous demonstrations, physical guidance, or teleoperation. By making demonstration acquisition easier and more natural for humans, it could broaden the accessibility of robot teaching to non-experts.
- **Generalization to Novel Objects and Task Configurations:** A key strength of the approach is its ability to learn and apply skills involving *novel objects and configurations* at test time, using only meta-training data from primitive skills performed with *other objects*. This capability is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic, unstructured environments where they frequently encounter unseen items.
- **End-to-End Visuomotor Learning from Weak Supervision:** The system operates directly on *raw pixel inputs* for both human demonstrations and robot observations, mapping them to low-level robot actions. This end-to-end approach bypasses the need for explicit state estimation, feature engineering, or semantic labels, which can be brittle and domain-specific. Learning from raw, unsegmented human video constitutes "weak supervision," offering a more natural and less laborious way for humans to communicate tasks.
- **Automated Task Decomposition and Composition:** The introduction of learned "primitive phase predictors" for both human demonstrations and robot executions allows for the *automatic decomposition of compound tasks* and the *dynamic composition of learned primitive policies*. This eliminates the need for manual segmentation or pre-defined task graphs, increasing the autonomy and adaptability of the robot.
- **Foundation for Future Robot Learning Systems:** The work demonstrates a viable approach for hierarchical imitation learning that combines meta-learning for skill acquisition with a mechanism for task structuring. This provides a strong foundation for future research in several directions:
 - **Improving Core Imitation Capabilities:** The paper identifies that the performance of the overall system is often limited by the precision of the one-shot imitation learner. Future advances in single-demonstration visuomotor learning could directly enhance the capabilities of this hierarchical framework.
 - **Robustness to Compounding Errors:** The method highlights the challenge of compounding errors in long-horizon tasks. Integrating techniques like reinforcement learning or online feedback (e.g., DAgger) could significantly improve the robustness and reliability of robot execution over extended task sequences.
 - **Unstructured Data and Open-Ended Skill Learning:** The approach could be extended to more unstructured demonstration data, such as general long videos of human or robot activities that are not explicitly segmented into primitives. This would push towards truly unsupervised skill discovery and alignment, further reducing the reliance on engineered training data.

In summary, this research contributes to making robot learning from demonstration more practical, adaptable, and scalable. By mimicking how humans infer and combine behaviors from observation, it moves towards a future where robots can acquire complex skills with minimal, intuitive instruction.
