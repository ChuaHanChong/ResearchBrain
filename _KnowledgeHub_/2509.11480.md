# Cross-Platform Scaling of Vision-Language-Action Models from Edge to Cloud GPUs

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Northeastern University systematically characterizes Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model performance and scaling behavior across a continuum of computing environments, from power-constrained edge devices to high-performance datacenter GPUs. It identifies specific architectural choices that yield improved accuracy and efficiency, demonstrating that an optimized VLA model on a modern edge device can achieve higher throughput than on an older datacenter GPU.

## Problem

- A significant gap exists in understanding VLA model performance and scaling across heterogeneous hardware platforms and varying power budgets.
- Prior studies focused predominantly on algorithmic improvements or single hardware platform evaluations, neglecting practical trade-offs in accuracy, latency, throughput, and memory utilization for diverse robotic deployments.
- Lack of comprehensive performance data impedes informed decision-making regarding VLA model selection, architectural optimization, and hardware provisioning for real-world robotic systems.

## Method

- Evaluated ==five representative VLA models==, including established baselines (**OpenVLA**, **SpatialVLA**, **OpenVLA-OFT**) and two newly developed architectures (**VOTE** variants, **QwenVLA**), with varying ==LLM backbones== and ==action heads==.
- Characterized performance across diverse hardware platforms: ==NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin== (edge, with multiple power modes) and four datacenter GPUs (NVIDIA H100, A100, A6000, V100).
- Measured key metrics including ==success rate on the LIBERO benchmark==, peak memory usage, ==inference latency==, and ==throughput== for consistent performance comparison.

## Results

- VOTE-1T achieved the highest average success rate of **96.9%** on the LIBERO benchmark, outperforming OpenVLA-OFT (**95.3%**), while the smaller QwenVLA (1.5B backbone) surpassed the larger OpenVLA (7B backbone) with **78.8% vs. 76.5%** SR.
- QwenVLA and SpatialVLA exhibited the lowest peak memory footprints (approximately **7.4 GB** and **7.8 GB** respectively), showcasing the impact of smaller backbones on memory efficiency.
- The Jetson AGX Orin in MAX power mode achieved **55.57 Hz** throughput with VOTE-MLP4, which exceeded the NVIDIA V100 datacenter GPU's **32.28 Hz** for the same model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA architectural choices, such as action tokenization strategies and language model backbone size, critically influence inference throughput and memory footprint.
> - Modern high-end edge devices can, under certain configurations and with optimized VLA models, achieve higher throughput than older datacenter GPUs, challenging conventional hardware superiority assumptions.
> - Optimized chunked decoding strategies offer a pathway to high throughput with minimal accuracy trade-offs, providing guidance for designing efficient VLA models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent a paradigm shift in robotic control, moving towards generalist policies that can interpret complex instructions and generate actions directly from visual and linguistic inputs. These models, exemplified by architectures like OpenVLA and RT-2, integrate large language and vision backbones with specialized action heads, demonstrating promising generalization across diverse robotic tasks, from manipulation to long-horizon planning. This development holds the potential to reduce the need for task-specific, custom-engineered robotic models, enabling a single policy to operate across varied robots and environments.

Despite rapid algorithmic advancements aimed at improving VLA model accuracy and adapting vision-language architectures for action generation, a significant gap exists in understanding their performance characteristics and scaling behavior across heterogeneous hardware platforms and varying power budgets. Prior studies have predominantly focused on algorithmic improvements or evaluations on single hardware platforms under fixed resource configurations. Consequently, there is limited insight into the practical trade-offs among accuracy, inference latency, throughput, and memory utilization when deploying VLAs across the entire spectrum of computing environments—from power-constrained edge devices to high-performance datacenter GPUs. This lack of comprehensive performance scaling data impedes informed decision-making regarding VLA model selection, architectural optimization, and hardware provisioning for real-world robotic deployments, where computational resources, latency requirements, and energy budgets are diverse and often stringent. This research directly addresses this knowledge gap by providing a systematic characterization of VLA model performance across this edge-to-cloud computing continuum.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to characterize the scaling trends of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models across different model architectures, hardware classes, and power budgets. This overarching objective is motivated by several critical considerations pertinent to the practical deployment of advanced robotic control systems.

Firstly, VLA models are increasingly moving from controlled laboratory settings to real-world applications such as service robots, mobile manipulators, and autonomous drones. In these diverse operational environments, hardware resources, latency requirements, and energy budgets vary significantly. For instance, embedded platforms demand a careful balance between model size, inference throughput, and accuracy to ensure responsive and reliable operation within tight computational and power constraints. Conversely, cloud-hosted or datacenter settings prioritize maximizing throughput and managing memory usage to optimize operational costs and scalability. The absence of a systematic understanding of how VLA architectures behave across this wide edge-to-cloud spectrum leads to suboptimal deployment decisions, including the potential for over-provisioning hardware or underutilizing available resources.

Secondly, existing research has largely concentrated on advancing VLA algorithms for improved accuracy or adapting vision-language models for action generation. However, these studies typically evaluate models on a single hardware platform with static resource settings, neglecting the dynamic interplay between model architecture and diverse hardware capabilities. This creates a knowledge deficit regarding the practical implications of architectural choices—such as the size of the Large Language Model (LLM) backbone, the design of the action head, or output tokenization strategies—on system-level metrics like latency, throughput, and memory footprint, especially when operating under different power envelopes.

Thirdly, the researchers aim to distill actionable guidelines for engineers and developers. By systematically evaluating representative VLA models, including both established baselines and newly developed architectures, across a range of edge (NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin with multiple power modes) and datacenter (NVIDIA H100, A100, A6000, V100 GPUs) platforms, the study seeks to provide empirical data. This data is intended to inform the selection and optimization of VLA models tailored to specific deployment scenarios, thereby improving efficiency and performance in practice.

Finally, a specific motivation is to challenge prevailing assumptions about the inherent superiority of datacenter hardware for all robotic inference tasks. The study seeks to determine if modern, high-end edge devices, under certain configurations, can achieve performance comparable to or even surpassing older-generation datacenter GPUs, which would have significant implications for distributed and autonomous robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a systematic evaluation methodology to characterize the scaling trends of VLA models across varied hardware platforms and power constraints.

**A. Evaluated VLA Models:**
Five representative VLA models were selected for evaluation, comprising three established baselines and two newly developed architectures:
- **Baselines:**
 - **OpenVLA:** A 7B-parameter model utilizing a Llama 2 language backbone with DINOv2 and SigLIP visual encoders, employing Discrete Action Tokens (DAT) and a chunk size of 1.
 - **SpatialVLA:** A 4B-parameter model based on PaliGemma 2, SigLIP+Ego3D visual encoders, and Adaptive Action Grids (AAG) with a chunk size of 4.
 - **OpenVLA-OFT:** A 7B-parameter model similar to OpenVLA but enhanced with Optimized Fine-Tuning (OFT) for efficiency and success rate, using continuous actions with L1 regression (Cont-L1) and a chunk size of 8.
- **Newly Proposed Architectures:**
 - **VOTE:** This model utilizes a Llama 2-7B backbone with DINOv2 and SigLIP encoders, focusing on minimizing action tokens through a Special Token (ST)-based action head to reduce latency. Three configurations were evaluated:
 - **VOTE-1T:** Outputs one `<ACT>` token with a chunk size of 8, using a 2-layer MLP action head.
 - **VOTE-2T:** Outputs two `<ACT>` tokens with a chunk size of 8, yielding 16 actions, using a 2-layer MLP action head.
 - **VOTE-MLP4:** A single `<ACT>` token variant with a 4-layer MLP head, designed for a chunk size of 16 to enhance performance.
 - **QwenVLA:** This model was developed to investigate the impact of a smaller language backbone, using Qwen 2.5-1.5B with the DINOv2 and SigLIP vision stack and a Cont-L1 action head, similar to OpenVLA-OFT, with a chunk size of 8. It adapts the Prismatic VLM architecture and was fine-tuned using OFT on the LIBERO benchmark.

All VOTE variants and QwenVLA were fine-tuned on the LIBERO benchmark. VOTE used AdamW with a learning rate of 1e-4 and a global batch size of 40. QwenVLA used AdamW with a learning rate of 1e-3 and a global batch size of 64. Both utilized Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) with rank `r = 32` and `α = 16`.

**B. Hardware Platforms:**
The evaluation covered a range of hardware platforms representing both edge and datacenter computing environments.

- **Edge Computing Platform:**
 - **NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin:** A System-on-Chip (SoC) designed for power-efficient AI workloads. It integrates a CPU (12-core Arm Cortex-A78AE), a GPU (NVIDIA Ampere Architecture with 2048 CUDA cores), and 32 GB LPDDR5 memory. The Orin was tested across multiple configurable power modes (15 W, 30 W, 50 W, and MAX, up to 60 W), which dynamically adjust CPU core count, CPU/GPU clock frequencies, and memory frequency to explore performance-energy trade-offs.

- **Datacenter GPU Platforms:**
 - Four discrete NVIDIA GPUs representing different architecture generations and performance tiers were used:
 - **H100 (Hopper):** High-performance, 14,592 CUDA cores, 94 GB HBM3, 3.35 TB/s memory bandwidth, 700 W TDP.
 - **A100 (Ampere):** High-performance, 6,912 CUDA cores, 40 GB HBM2e, 1.6 TB/s memory bandwidth, 400 W TDP.
 - **A6000 (Ampere):** Workstation-class, 10,752 CUDA cores, 48 GB GDDR6, 768 GB/s memory bandwidth, 300 W TDP.
 - **V100 (Volta):** Older generation, 5,120 CUDA cores, 32 GB HBM2, 900 GB/s memory bandwidth, 300 W TDP.

**C. Experimental Methodology:**
- **VLA Configurations:** Standard implementations were used for OpenVLA, SpatialVLA, and OpenVLA-OFT. Specific configurations for VOTE variants (VOTE-1T, VOTE-2T, VOTE-MLP4) were detailed regarding their output token count, chunk size, and MLP layer depth.
- **Benchmark:** Model accuracy was evaluated using the LIBERO benchmark, which encompasses four task suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. Each suite comprised 10 tasks, with 20 repetitions per task, totaling 200 trials per suite to calculate success rates (SR).
- **Performance Evaluation:** Inference efficiency was measured using the following metrics:
 - **Latency:** Average time to generate a single action chunk.
 - **Throughput:** Number of actions generated per second.
 - **Peak Memory Usage:** Maximum VRAM consumed during inference.
 Each inference test involved processing a single 224x224 RGB image and a fixed language prompt ("What action should the robot take to pick the cup?"). To ensure measurement stability, several untimed warm-up runs were executed before recording wall-clock times for 100 consecutive inferences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation provided insights into the scaling trends of VLA models across architectural choices, power budgets, and hardware platforms.

**A. VLA Model Accuracy Comparison:**
- The VOTE variants demonstrated competitive average success rates (SR) on the LIBERO benchmark. VOTE-1T achieved the highest overall performance with an average SR of 96.9%, surpassing the strongest baseline, OpenVLA-OFT (95.3%). VOTE-1T also achieved the highest SR in three out of the four LIBERO task suites.
- VOTE-2T and VOTE-MLP4 exhibited minor reductions in accuracy (up to 2.9% relative to VOTE-1T) in exchange for increased throughput, which is attributed to their larger output chunk sizes.
- QwenVLA, despite its significantly smaller 1.5B backbone, achieved an average SR of 78.8%, surpassing the larger OpenVLA baseline (76.5%). It performed particularly well in the LIBERO-Object and LIBERO-Goal suites, indicating that competitive task performance can be achieved with reduced model size.

**B. Peak Memory Usage:**
- Peak VRAM usage varied substantially across models. OpenVLA-OFT (19.20 GB) and OpenVLA (14.35 GB) were the most memory-intensive baselines.
- SpatialVLA (7.82 GB) and QwenVLA (7.39 GB) exhibited the lowest memory footprints, with QwenVLA having the lowest usage overall, directly correlating with its smaller 1.5B language backbone.
- All VOTE configurations showed similar memory footprints (ranging from 14.40 GB to 14.45 GB), comparable to OpenVLA, suggesting that action head design variations (e.g., token count or MLP depth) had a negligible impact on memory usage compared to the LLM backbone and vision encoder choices.

**C. Latency Comparison (Orin MAX vs. H100):**
- The NVIDIA H100 datacenter GPU consistently achieved substantially lower per-chunk latencies than the Jetson AGX Orin in its MAX power mode. Latencies on the H100 ranged from 0.03 ms (VOTE-1T, VOTE-MLP4) to 0.34 ms (SpatialVLA).
- On the AGX Orin, latencies were approximately an order of magnitude higher, ranging from 0.29 ms (VOTE-MLP4) to 1.95 ms (SpatialVLA).
- VOTE configurations maintained competitive latencies on both platforms, with VOTE-MLP4 achieving the lowest latency on the Orin, indicating its efficiency.

**D. Throughput Scaling Across Datacenter GPUs:**
- **H100:** Demonstrated the highest throughput, with VOTE-MLP4 reaching 474.78 Hz. This was over 64 times faster than OpenVLA (7.36 Hz) on the same hardware.
- **A100:** Followed a similar scaling pattern to the H100 but with lower absolute values, VOTE-MLP4 sustained 276.82 Hz.
- **A6000:** Showed strong performance for smaller models like SpatialVLA (10.00 Hz) and QwenVLA (84.39 Hz), but its advantages diminished for more compute-intensive models.
- **V100:** Exhibited the lowest throughput due to its older architecture and lower memory bandwidth, with VOTE-MLP4 peaking at 32.28 Hz.
- Across all datacenter GPUs, throughput improvements in VOTE configurations scaled positively with the output chunk size and MLP depth. Smaller-backbone models like QwenVLA offered competitive performance per parameter but did not match the absolute throughput of optimized VOTE variants.

**E. Throughput Scaling Across Edge Power Budgets (Jetson AGX Orin):**
- Throughput on the AGX Orin scaled directly with the allocated power budget. In MAX mode, VOTE-MLP4 achieved 55.57 Hz, which was more than 46 times faster than OpenVLA (1.20 Hz) under the same conditions.
- The relative ordering of model performance remained consistent across all power modes (MAX, 50W, 30W, 15W), with VOTE configurations leading, followed by QwenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, and then OpenVLA and SpatialVLA.
- Throughput reductions were non-linear as power budgets decreased. Dropping from 50 W to 30 W resulted in more pronounced performance losses, particularly for computationally demanding models, while smaller models like QwenVLA retained a higher fraction of their MAX-mode throughput.

**F. Throughput Scaling Across Edge and Datacenter (Comparative Analysis):**
- Comparing the Orin in MAX mode to the H100 revealed a significant performance gap: VOTE-MLP4 ran 8.5 times faster on the H100, and QwenVLA was approximately 4.8 times faster in the cloud. This disparity increased for models with larger backbones or more complex decoders, which benefited disproportionately from the higher memory bandwidth and parallelism of datacenter GPUs.
- However, a key finding was that the Jetson AGX Orin in MAX mode, when running VOTE-MLP4, achieved a throughput of 55.57 Hz, which surpassed the throughput of the older V100 datacenter GPU (32.28 Hz) for the same model. This result challenges the general assumption that any datacenter GPU will inherently outperform edge devices in robotic inference tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a comprehensive empirical analysis of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model performance across a spectrum of hardware platforms and power constraints, yielding several significant contributions and potential impacts.

Firstly, the study generates **actionable insights and guidelines** for the selection and optimization of VLA models in practical robotic deployments. By systematically quantifying the trade-offs between model accuracy, memory usage, latency, and throughput under varying hardware and power conditions, the findings enable engineers and developers to make data-driven decisions. This informs how to best configure VLA models and provision hardware based on specific deployment constraints, such as the need for minimal latency in real-time control, maximum throughput for batch processing, or stringent power budgets for embedded systems. This can lead to more efficient resource utilization and reduced development costs in robotics.

Secondly, the research **challenges conventional assumptions about hardware superiority**. The finding that a modern, high-end edge device like the Jetson AGX Orin in its maximum power mode can outperform an older datacenter GPU (NVIDIA V100) for specific VLA workloads is a notable outcome. This suggests that the architectural generation and optimization of the VLA model play a crucial role, and datacenter hardware is not universally superior for all robotic inference tasks. This insight can influence future hardware purchasing decisions and lead to more nuanced considerations for distributing computational loads in hybrid edge-cloud robotic architectures.

Thirdly, the work highlights the **impact of architectural choices on performance scaling**. The identification that architectural decisions, such as action tokenization (e.g., VOTE's special token approach) and backbone size (e.g., QwenVLA's smaller LLM), strongly influence throughput and memory footprint is significant. The demonstration that optimized chunked decoding strategies can achieve high throughput with minor accuracy trade-offs provides a clear direction for designing VLA models tailored for efficiency without significant performance degradation. This encourages a holistic approach to VLA development, where algorithmic improvements are considered in conjunction with their hardware implications.

Finally, the study contributes to the broader advancement of **real-world robotic system deployment**. As VLA models transition from research environments to practical applications, the ability to operate them efficiently across diverse hardware, from power-constrained mobile robots to high-performance cloud backends, is paramount. This research facilitates a more informed transition, potentially accelerating the adoption of generalist policies in autonomous systems, intelligent manufacturing, and service robotics. Future work extending this analysis to include quantization strategies and real-world robotic deployments promises to further refine VLA inference optimization under practical constraints.
