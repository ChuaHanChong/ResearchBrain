# LM-X: Explainable Action Modeling with Progress, Event, and Uncertainty Prediction for Generalist Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> LM-X is a ~6B generalist vision-language-action foundation model built on a Cosmos-Reason2-2B backbone that jointly pretrains three explicit predictive signals with action generation: return-to-go for task progress, event-to-go action chunks for the next semantic transition, and a heteroscedastic variance head inside the flow-matching action expert. Trained on 20,000+ hours of real-robot data, it reaches 74.1% on 50 RoboTwin2.0 tasks versus 55.4% for GR00T N1.7.

## Problem

- Generalist VLA policies remain action-only black boxes: they emit commands without exposing whether execution is progressing, which intermediate transition is pursued, or how reliable the motion is.
- Existing VLA uncertainty is inferred after training from token entropy, action-head disagreement, inference-time procedures, or small ensembles, never learned as a native pretrained policy output.
- Prior hierarchical subgoal methods need auxiliary image generators, skill libraries, or language interfaces, and success-only demonstrations rarely expose regression, hesitation, or unreliable states.

## Method

- **LM-X**'s ==return-to-go== head: a two-layer Transformer over ==Cosmos-Reason2-2B== backbone layer-16 tokens predicts 128 ordered return bins under ==smooth-L1== loss; proprioception is withheld so progress is judged from instruction-conditioned scene state.
- ==Event-to-go== expert: a 32-layer ==Diffusion Transformer== trained with ==conditional flow matching== predicts a 60-step action chunk padded to the next human-verified semantic boundary, conditioned on return-to-go and proprioception.
- ==Uncertainty-aware action flow==: the action expert adds a ==log-variance projection== trained with ==Gaussian negative log-likelihood==, then propagates variance through ==Euler integration== using a ==Hutchinson-estimated Jacobian covariance== term into action space.

## Results

- Five-task pretraining gate: full LM-X reached **79.6%** mean success versus **63.6%** for the action-only backbone (**+16.0** points), beating the strongest single-head variant by **10.8** points.
- On **50** randomized-hard RoboTwin2.0 tasks LM-X scored **74.1%** versus **55.4%** for GR00T N1.7, with stamp_seal rising from **22%** to **74%** but beat_block_hammer regressing **15** points.
- Across **seven** real tasks on four embodiments, LM-X hit **68.6%** versus **50.7%** (**+17.9 pp**); variance spiked during hesitation and failed rollouts, while RTG fell at missed grasps.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Auxiliary objectives can be individually brittle yet jointly strong: event-only supervision cut one task from 54% to 16%, while all three combined improved every gate task.
> - A cheap held-out ablation gate before a 20-day, 64-GPU pretraining run is a reusable pattern for de-risking expensive foundation-model design decisions.
> - Temporal correspondence is not calibration: variance and progress traces look diagnostic, but thresholded failure detection still needs labeled windows, held-out calibration, and closed-loop recovery tests.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is experiencing advancements with the development of generalist vision–language–action (VLA) policies. These policies, exemplified by systems such as RT-1, RT-2, Open X-Embodiment, Octo, OpenVLA, π0, and GR00T N1, leverage large-scale visual-language knowledge for closed-loop robot control, demonstrating capabilities in long-horizon manipulation across various embodiments.

However, a prevalent limitation of many existing VLA systems is their operation as "black boxes." They receive observations and instructions to emit actions without overtly communicating their internal assessment of task progress, intermediate intentions, or the reliability of the generated motions. This lack of transparency means that while an operator can observe a robot's actions, the policy's underlying reasoning—whether it believes it is progressing, what subgoal it is pursuing, or how trustworthy its command is—remains implicit.

The presented work, LM-X, addresses this gap by aiming to integrate intrinsic explainability directly into the VLA foundation model. It draws inspiration from biological sensorimotor control, which exhibits a hierarchical organization of temporal states (tasks, subtasks, primitive actions), outcome-sensitive evaluation of state quality, and internal models for predicting consequences and representing uncertainty. Previous VLA research has started incorporating aspects of this biological organization, such as progress/value-aware objectives and hierarchical or event-conditioned models for long-horizon behavior. However, the explicit learning of heteroscedastic action uncertainty as a native output, jointly pretrained within the action expert, has largely been approached indirectly through post-training inference methods like token entropy, output disagreement, or policy ensembles.

LM-X positions itself as an advancement by proposing a large-scale generalist VLA that *jointly pretrains* and explicitly outputs three types of predictive state: task progress (Return-to-Go, RTG), event intention (Event-to-Go, ETG), and action reliability (heteroscedastic uncertainty). This integration aims to move beyond detached or post-hoc explanations, making these explanatory signals fundamental to the policy's control computation and available online during inference.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop LM-X, which the authors state is the first large-scale generalist VLA to jointly pretrain heteroscedastic action uncertainty inside its action expert, along with explicit progress, event intention, and action generation. The core motivation stems from the limitations of current generalist VLA policies, specifically their "black-box" nature, which hinders transparency and diagnostic capabilities.

The detailed objectives and motivations include:

- **Enhancing Transparency and Explainability:** Current VLAs provide actions but do not reveal their internal state regarding task progress, intermediate intentions, or action reliability. This makes it challenging for human operators to understand why a robot acts in a certain way or to diagnose failures effectively. LM-X aims to make these internal assessments explicit and accessible.
- **Integrating Brain-Inspired Hierarchical Control:** Inspired by biological sensorimotor systems that manage behavior across multiple timescales (task, event, motor), LM-X seeks to functionally replicate this by maintaining distinct predictive states for progress, event structure, and motor uncertainty. This computational principle serves as an engineering prior to structure the control policy.
- **Joint Pretraining of Explanatory Signals:** Instead of inferring confidence or progress post-training, LM-X is designed to *learn* these signals intrinsically during the pretraining phase alongside action generation. This ensures that the explanatory outputs are integral to the policy's decision-making process and are available online at every control step. The specific signals are:
 - **Return-to-Go (RTG):** To answer "Is the visible state improving?". This signal quantifies task-scale progress, providing a high-level assessment of how close the robot is to successfully completing the task. Its supervision incorporates both successful and failed trajectories to distinguish actual progress from regression.
 - **Event-to-Go (ETG):** To answer "What transition is the policy pursuing?". This signal represents the next semantic intermediate transition, organizing long-horizon behavior into meaningful segments. It is designed as an executable action chunk, aligning it directly with motor control.
 - **Heteroscedastic Action Uncertainty:** To answer "How reliable is the local command?". This signal quantifies the predicted reliability or dispersion of the generated action. Unlike previous approaches that infer uncertainty externally, LM-X learns this directly within its action expert through a dedicated log-variance head.
- **Leveraging Diverse and Failed Robot Data:** To effectively learn progress and uncertainty, the model requires exposure to states beyond nominal successful demonstrations. The motivation is to use over 20,000 hours of real-robot trajectories, including over 1,000 hours of *failed rollouts*, which provide crucial examples of regression, hesitation, and unreliable states. This data helps the model learn to distinguish between progress and regression, and commitment from ambiguity.
- **Validating Component Complementarity:** Before undertaking a resource-intensive full pretraining, the authors motivated a "pretraining gate" (a controlled ablation study on a smaller set of tasks). The objective here was to empirically confirm that the proposed RTG, ETG, and uncertainty modules individually and, more importantly, *jointly* contribute to improved control performance, thereby justifying the investment in the full-scale architecture.
- **Demonstrating Performance and Diagnostic Utility:** A key objective is to show that LM-X not only provides these explanatory signals but also achieves competitive or superior performance compared to existing generalist VLAs in both simulation and real-world tasks. Furthermore, the research aims to demonstrate through qualitative analysis that the emitted RTG and uncertainty signals exhibit meaningful temporal correspondences with observable robot behaviors like progress, regression, hesitation, and failure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LM-X is formulated as a multi-output prediction problem where, at each control step \( t \), given visual observations \( o_t \), a language instruction \( l \), and proprioceptive state \( s_t \), the policy \( \pi_\Theta \) outputs not only a fine-grained action chunk \( \hat{A}_t \) but also estimated task progress \( \hat{R}_t \), an event-level transition \( \hat{E}_t \), and propagated terminal action variance \( \hat{q}_t \). These explanatory outputs are directly supervised, emitted online, and integrated into the action pathway.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Inductive Bias

The model encodes a central inductive bias: task-scale state evaluation constrains event prediction, which in turn constrains motor generation. This is represented by a directed factorization of the probability distribution:
\( p_\Theta(R_t, E_t, A_t | o_t, l, s_t) = p_\phi(R_t | z_t) \cdot p_\psi(E_t | z_t, s_t, R_t) \cdot p_\theta(A_t | z_t, s_t, R_t, E_t) \).
Here, \( z_t = f_{VL}(o_t, l) \) is the visual-language embedding. The final factor for action \( A_t \) is a heteroscedastic conditional flow. The approach distinguishes overall action variance into components: one due to unresolved task/event context and another for local motor residuals, which LM-X's heteroscedastic variance aims to model.

#### 3.2. LM-X Architecture and Information Flow

- **Backbone:** LM-X is built upon the Cosmos-Reason2-2B vision-language backbone, which uses a Qwen-VL front end for processing language instructions and various camera views (head, wrist, embodiment-specific).
- **Proprioception:** Proprioceptive state \( s_t \) bypasses the vision-language backbone and is fed directly into the event and action experts, ensuring that task assessment from visual-language cues is separated from embodiment-specific control.
- **Shared Representation:** Hidden tokens from the 16th layer of the backbone form the shared visual-language representation \( z_t \).
- **Experts:**
 - **RTG Expert:** A two-layer Transformer that operates solely on \( z_t \).
 - **Event Expert:** A 32-layer Diffusion Transformer that receives \( z_t \), \( s_t \), and the predicted RTG. Its hidden state, \( z_{\text{event},t} \), is then exposed.
 - **Action Expert:** Another 32-layer Diffusion Transformer that conditions on \( z_t \), \( s_t \), RTG, and \( z_{\text{event},t} \). This expert produces both the mean-flow action and log-variance projections, sharing all preceding features.
- **Parameters:** The complete model contains approximately 6 billion parameters.
- **Conditioning:** During training, ground-truth RTG and event targets supervise the hierarchy. During inference, predicted signals replace the ground truths, maintaining a staged conditioning where semantic assessment (RTG) precedes embodiment-grounded transition prediction (ETG), which then precedes fine-grained motor control (Action).

#### 3.3. Return-to-Go (RTG) as Progress and State Quality

- **Definition:** The empirical RTG at time \( t \) is defined as \( R_t = \sum_{t'=t}^T r_{t'} \), where \( T \) is the terminal timestep.
- **Reward Structure:**
 - \( r_t = 0 \) if \( t = T \) and the episode succeeds.
 - \( r_t = -C \) if \( t = T \) and the episode fails (where \( C \) exceeds half the maximum successful episode length).
 - \( r_t = -1 \) otherwise.
 This structure ensures that RTG approaches zero for successful trajectories and receives a distinct negative margin for failed endpoints, effectively combining remaining duration and degraded state quality.
- **Prediction:** The RTG head models \( p_\phi(R_t | z_t) \) over 128 ordered bins. A scalar estimate is recovered from the expected bin center, and a smooth \( \ell_1 \) objective is optimized.
- **Design Choice:** The RTG head intentionally excludes joint state to encourage assessment of progress from instruction-conditioned scene state.
- **Supervision:** RTG is computed backward from the terminal state only after validating the episode outcome and temporal boundaries, converting sparse outcomes into dense learning signals across successful and failed trajectories.

#### 3.4. Event-to-Go (ETG) Prediction

- **Definition:** An event is a semantically meaningful intermediate state. ETG \( E_t \) is an action chunk of length \( H_E = 60 \) steps, representing the action sequence from the current timestep \( t \) to the next verified event index \( k(t) \). If \( k(t) - t + 1 > H_E \), it is truncated; otherwise, the terminal event action \( a_{k(t)} \) is repeated to pad it to \( H_E \).
- **Rationale:** Using an action chunk preserves short-range temporal structure, improving behavioral cloning, and eliminates the need for a separate image generator or symbolic skill vocabulary. The longer horizon of 60 steps (compared to 30 for fine-grained actions) allows ETG to represent semantic transitions.
- **Training:** The ETG head is trained using conditional flow matching, minimizing \( L_{\text{event}} \) (Eq. 9).
- **Event Annotation:** A two-stage process: task-dependent signals (e.g., gripper transitions, alignment markers, velocity minima, contact signals) propose candidate timesteps, which are then verified and corrected by human annotators to match intended semantic transitions. The learned representation remains uniform (action chunk) despite varied annotation heuristics.

#### 3.5. Uncertainty-Aware Action Flow

- **Heteroscedastic Prediction:** The action expert predicts an element-wise mean velocity \( \bar{v}_{\tau,\theta} \) and a log variance \( \rho_{\tau,\theta} = \log((\sigma_{\tau,\theta})^2 + \epsilon) \) conditioned on the flow state, current proprioception, visual-language embedding, RTG, and event embedding.
- **Objective:** The Gaussian negative log-likelihood of the target velocity (Eq. 12) is minimized, where the expectation includes averaging over action dimensions. This objective estimates conditional residual scale, not epistemic uncertainty or out-of-distribution safety. Mean and variance projections share features from the action expert, coupling uncertainty to control generation.

#### 3.6. Variance Propagation to Action Space

- **Propagation:** The flow head's local velocity variance is propagated to the final action sample through numerical integration, using a diagonal approximation. This involves a forward Euler update (Eq. 13) and approximating the variance evolution (Eq. 14, 15), where the trace \( q_0 = 0 \) measures accumulated predictive variance.
- **Scalar Uncertainty:** The scalar uncertainty \( U_t \) is the mean terminal variance across selected action dimensions (Eq. 16), with higher values indicating greater modeled action dispersion. Arm-specific traces can localize uncertainty in bimanual tasks. For reported rollouts, only the mean velocity is used; uncertainty traces are diagnostic.

#### 3.7. Joint Training and Online Interpretation

- **Objective:** The complete pretraining objective \( L_{LM-X} \) is a weighted sum of \( L_{RTG} \), \( L_{\text{event}} \), and \( L_{\text{action-U}} \) (Eq. 17).
- **Pretraining Data:** Over 20,000 hours of real-robot trajectories, including over 1,000 hours of failed policy rollouts, augmented with outcome, progress, and event labels. This mixture includes public datasets and newly collected data from over 20 embodiments (single-arm, dual-arm, humanoid).
- **Pretraining Gate:** Before the full 20-day pretraining on 64 NVIDIA B200 GPUs, a controlled five-task RoboTwin2.0 study validated the complementarity of RTG, ETG, and uncertainty modules. For this, 20 failed episodes were collected for RTG supervision, updated intermittently during pretraining steps.
- **Inference:** All outputs (RTG, ETG, variance) are available at every control step, allowing downstream monitors to use them for detecting decreasing RTG, event changes, or variance spikes before terminal failure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate the contribution of the explicit signals to control performance, their transferability, and their diagnostic utility.

#### 4.1. Component Ablations as a Pretraining Gate

A controlled study on five held-out RoboTwin2.0 tasks was conducted prior to large-scale pretraining.
- **Performance:** The complete LM-X design improved mean success from 63.6% (action-only backbone) to 79.6% (+16.0 percentage points, pp). It also exceeded the strongest single-head variant by 10.8 pp.
- **Complementarity:** Individual components showed task-dependent benefits (e.g., RTG best for microwave opening, ETG for microphone handover, uncertainty for pot lifting). However, isolated auxiliary objectives could be brittle (e.g., event-only training reduced open-microwave success from 54% to 16%). This suggested that the objectives are complementary, with ETG providing coarse targets, RTG evaluating state quality, and uncertainty capturing local ambiguity.
- **Conclusion:** The results provided sufficient evidence to retain all three modules for full pretraining, particularly due to LM-X's consistent improvement across all five tasks.

#### 4.2. RoboTwin2.0 Benchmark

After full pretraining on real-robot data, LM-X was evaluated on 50 randomized-hard RoboTwin2.0 tasks against GR00T N1.7.
- **Overall Performance:** LM-X achieved a mean success rate of 74.1% across all 50 tasks, compared to 55.4% for GR00T N1.7, an absolute gain of 18.7 pp.
- **Task-Specific Performance:** LM-X outperformed GR00T N1.7 on 8 out of 10 representative tasks, tied on one (`click_alarmclock`), and underperformed on one (`beat_block_hammer`).
- **Significant Gains:** The largest gains were observed in multi-stage tasks, such as `stamp_seal` (+52 pp), color- and size-based block ranking (+33 pp and +30 pp, respectively), and two-block stacking (+25 pp). This indicates that intermediate supervision benefits multi-stage tasks.

#### 4.3. Real-World Evaluation

LM-X was evaluated on seven real-world tasks across four robot embodiments.
- **Overall Performance:** LM-X achieved a mean success rate of 68.6% versus 50.7% for GR00T N1.7 (+17.9 pp).
- **Task-Specific Performance:** LM-X improved five tasks, tied one (`place water bottle into tray, single-arm`), and regressed on one (`pick and place tape roll`).
- **Largest Improvement:** The most significant gain was on `tableware organization` (+60 pp), indicating a strong benefit for long-horizon organizational tasks. Precision part insertion, a geometrically demanding task, reached 60% success.

#### 4.4. RTG Tracks Progress and Local Regressions

Qualitative analysis of RTG traces on held-out real-robot episodes demonstrated its diagnostic utility:
- **Semantic Progress:** RTG generally increased as a successful trajectory neared completion and exhibited stepwise changes near semantic events (e.g., completion of a subtask).
- **Local Regressions:** RTG decreased rapidly during observable execution regressions, such as the robot returning to an initial pose, missing a grasp, or switching targets. This non-monotonic behavior indicated that RTG captures more than just elapsed time or nominal progress.
- **Contact-Rich Tasks:** In precision part insertion, RTG rose sharply when the object's tip first entered the slot and again upon final alignment, correlating with critical task-relevant transitions.

#### 4.5. Uncertainty Identifies Hesitation and Failure

Qualitative analysis of the propagated terminal variance (uncertainty) provided insights into policy behavior:
- **Failure Correlation:** Failed episodes exhibited more frequent and larger variance spikes in one or both arms compared to successful episodes, which generally showed lower and smoother variance after initial transients. This suggested that failure is associated with recurring intervals of ambiguous local control.
- **Behavioral Correspondence:** Variance peaks correlated with specific observable behaviors, such as oscillation between poses, hesitation before committing to a grasp, or delayed commitment before releasing an object.
- **Diagnostic Structure:** Arm-specific uncertainty traces provided additional diagnostic information, allowing localization of issues (e.g., unilateral peaks for grasp problems, synchronized peaks for coordination issues).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LM-X research introduces a framework for intrinsically explainable generalist VLA policies, marking a progression in the transparency and diagnostic capabilities of robot control systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancement in Generalist VLA Transparency:** LM-X directly addresses the "black-box" problem of modern VLAs by making crucial internal policy assessments—task progress, intermediate intention, and action reliability—explicit, online, and integral to the control process. This moves beyond post-hoc explanations, providing a more direct insight into the policy's operational state.
- **Improved Control Performance:** The empirical results demonstrate that jointly pretraining these explanatory signals with action generation leads to substantial improvements in task success rates. LM-X outperformed a strong baseline (GR00T N1.7) by significant margins in both randomized simulation and real-world tasks, particularly for multi-stage and complex manipulation behaviors. This suggests that the structured objectives contribute to shaping more effective and robust control representations.
- **Enhanced Diagnostic Capabilities:** The qualitative analyses of RTG and action uncertainty indicate their utility as diagnostic tools. RTG can track semantic progress, identify regressions, and highlight critical task transitions. Action uncertainty can pinpoint periods of hesitation, oscillation, or ambiguity in local control, correlating with potential failure points. This provides valuable information for human operators monitoring robot performance and for developing future autonomous fault detection and recovery systems.
- **Effective Utilization of Diverse Data, Including Failures:** The methodology demonstrates a scalable approach to leveraging a large dataset of robot trajectories, crucially including failed rollouts, to learn robust progress and uncertainty signals. This expands the learning landscape beyond nominal successful demonstrations, enabling the model to learn from degraded states that are vital for real-world robustness.
- **Foundation for Future Explainable AI in Robotics:** By establishing a foundation model that inherently generates explanatory states, LM-X sets the stage for further research into explainable AI for embodied systems. This includes quantitative calibration of these signals for robust failure detection, evaluating their causal faithfulness through interventions, and using them in closed-loop systems for adaptive control, error recovery, or human-robot collaboration.
- **Biological Inspiration as an Engineering Prior:** The work demonstrates how functional principles from biological sensorimotor control, such as hierarchical predictive state, can be effectively translated into an engineering design to achieve practical benefits in robot learning.

In conclusion, LM-X represents a significant step towards developing more transparent, capable, and diagnosable generalist robot policies, offering a practical pathway to bridge the gap between strong control performance and intrinsic explainability.
