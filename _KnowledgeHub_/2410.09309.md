# Adaptive Compliance Policy: Learning Approximate Compliance for Diffusion Guided Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP) is a diffusion visuomotor policy that predicts, alongside a reference end-effector pose, a virtual target pose and a stiffness magnitude, letting a low-level admittance controller vary compliance spatially and temporally. Compliance labels come from a simple rule: low stiffness along the measured force direction, high stiffness elsewhere. ACP raises contact-rich task success over 50% versus visuomotor baselines.

## Problem

- Visuomotor policies control position only, ignoring compliance, so they cannot trade position accuracy against contact force under uncertainty in contact-rich manipulation.
- Estimating human stiffness needs repeated identical demonstrations, known hand mass and damping, or covariance statistics, none available from single varied visuomotor demonstrations.
- Existing compliant policies use pre-selected task-specific parameters or one uniform constant stiffness, missing direction-dependent and stage-dependent compliance needs and failing under perturbation.

## Method

- **ACP**'s ==approximate compliance labeling rule== with proof: low stiffness only along the feedback force direction, high stiffness in the orthogonal basis, avoiding internal forces while preserving tracking.
- Action representation adds a ==virtual target pose== plus scalar low-direction stiffness to the reference pose, converting a force target into a controller-agnostic position target for ==admittance control==.
- Force encoding via ==FFT spectrograms== through a ==CoordConv ResNet-18==, fused with ==CLIP ViT-B/16== image features by a transformer encoder conditioning the ==diffusion head==; kinesthetic low-stiffness teaching rig collects demos.

## Results

- Item flipping across five scenarios: **96%** success for ACP versus **23%** for the compliant-stiffness baseline and **14%** for the stiff diffusion policy, over **100** trials each.
- Vase wiping: **93.75%** overall for ACP versus **43.75%** for the compliant baseline; the stiff policy broke its tool and was excluded from the comparison.
- Ablating FFT encoding for causal convolution costs little on flipping (**95%** versus **96%**) but drops wiping to **81.25%**, mainly on large marks (**60%** versus **80%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An approximate, provably safe compliance label can beat exact stiffness estimation: policies need a useful profile, not the true human one.
> - Encoding force as a virtual target pose makes force targets portable across impedance or admittance robots without force sensors, decoupling policy from controller hardware.
> - Frequency-domain force encoding matters most when high-frequency contact signals carry task information, such as wiping; smooth quasi-static motions gain little over temporal convolution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP)" positions itself within the critical and challenging domain of contact-rich robot manipulation. Historically, robot control has largely focused on either precise position control or force control, with mechanical compliance being a specialized area often requiring extensive modeling or manual tuning. The broader landscape of robotics research has seen a significant shift towards **visuomotor policies**, where robots learn to map visual observations directly to actions. However, a major limitation of many state-of-the-art visuomotor policies is their predominant focus on *position control*, often assuming a rigid interaction or relying on pre-selected, uniform compliance parameters. This oversight makes them less robust for tasks requiring delicate physical interaction, where forces and torques are significant.

**Existing approaches to compliance in robotics can be broadly categorized:**

- **Model-based Compliance Control:** Early work established the theoretical foundations for impedance and admittance control, allowing robots to exhibit compliant behavior. While effective, these methods typically require careful modeling of contact geometry, friction, and environmental dynamics, making them difficult to generalize and scale to diverse, unstructured environments.
- **Learning Compliance with Reinforcement Learning (RL):** RL methods have shown promise in learning compliant behaviors by exploring force-motion variations. However, RL policies often require extensive training, struggle with transferability to new scene variations, and frequently rely on fixed-parameter low-level compliance controllers, which lack the adaptability needed for highly dynamic tasks.
- **Learning Compliance from Human Demonstrations:** Efforts to extract compliance from human demonstrations have been made, but they often face fundamental challenges. Estimating a full stiffness matrix from a single demonstration is an ill-posed problem due to insufficient data variations. Prior methods have often required multiple identical demonstrations, assumed perfect knowledge of human dynamics, or restricted the learned compliance to uniform stiffness. A concurrent work computes compliance profiles based on heuristics from manually divided task stages, which still requires manual input.
- **Visuomotor Policies with Force Feedback:** Many modern visuomotor policies incorporate force/torque data as input. However, despite processing force signals, most still output only position targets, with the underlying compliance typically being uniform and constant, failing to capture the rich spatial and temporal variations of desired compliance. Diffusion Policy is a recent example of a highly successful visuomotor policy framework, excelling at learning complex action distributions for position control.

ACP addresses a critical gap by providing a scalable, data-driven approach to learn *adaptive, spatially and temporally varying compliance* directly from human demonstrations, without needing explicit physical models or an exhaustive exploration phase like RL. It innovatively sidesteps the "ill-defined" problem of estimating exact human stiffness by instead deriving a simpler, approximate compliance profile that guarantees practical and robust interaction. By building upon the robust learning capabilities of diffusion policies, ACP effectively integrates the crucial dimension of compliant interaction into sophisticated visuomotor control, moving beyond purely position-focused robot learning. This represents a significant step towards enabling robots to perform highly dexterous, contact-rich tasks in complex, uncertain environments with human-like adaptability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop **Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP)**, a novel framework that empowers robots to dynamically adjust their mechanical compliance both spatially and temporally for complex manipulation tasks, learning these behaviors directly from human demonstrations.

The motivation behind ACP stems from several critical challenges and limitations in current robot manipulation:

1. **The Insufficiency of Position-Only Control:** Many real-world manipulation tasks, such as assembly, object reorientation, wiping, or precise insertion, inherently require the concurrent control of both position and force. Current state-of-the-art visuomotor policies, while highly effective for motion planning and execution, often focus solely on position control. This oversight leads to policies that are brittle, prone to failure in the presence of even small uncertainties (e.g., object variations, sensor noise, minor perturbations), and can even cause damage to the robot or environment if unexpected contacts generate excessive forces.
2. **The Dynamic Nature of Desired Compliance:** The optimal compliance for a robot is not static; it varies drastically throughout a task. For example, a robot might need to be stiff for precise movement in free space, then become compliant in specific directions upon contact to absorb forces, and change its compliance profile again as contact modes evolve. Examples like flipping an object (requiring low stiffness in pushing directions and high stiffness elsewhere) or wiping a vase (demanding 3D compliance adjustments for holding, tracing, and force application) vividly illustrate these complex, spatial, and temporal compliance requirements.
3. **Limitations of Existing Compliance Methods:**
 - **Manual Tuning/Model-based Methods:** Manually tuning compliance parameters is time-consuming, requires expert knowledge, and doesn't generalize well across different tasks or variations. Model-based approaches, while effective, demand precise physical models of the environment and objects, which are often unavailable or difficult to acquire in real-world scenarios.
 - **Learning from Reinforcement Learning (RL):** While RL can learn compliant behaviors, it typically requires extensive exploration, struggles with sample efficiency, and often produces policies tied to specific task instances, lacking generalization to novel scene configurations or disturbances.
 - **Learning from Human Demonstrations (Ill-Posed Problem):** Extracting the *full* stiffness matrix (representing complete compliance information) from a single human demonstration is an inherently ill-defined problem due to a lack of sufficient variations in force-motion signals. Prior work has either made restrictive assumptions (e.g., uniform stiffness, perfect dynamics knowledge) or required many identical demonstrations, which are impractical for diverse tasks.
4. **Harnessing Human Expertise:** Humans exhibit remarkable adaptability and dexterity in contact-rich tasks, naturally adjusting their compliance in response to perceived forces and task requirements. The motivation is to leverage this rich source of human expertise through demonstration, allowing robots to implicitly learn these complex, adaptive compliance profiles without explicit programming or extensive modeling.

By addressing these motivations, ACP aims to significantly enhance the robustness, adaptability, and safety of robot manipulation in uncertain, contact-rich environments, paving the way for more capable and versatile robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP) lies in its innovative approach to learning and applying adaptive compliance from human demonstrations, integrating it seamlessly with a diffusion-based visuomotor policy. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

**A. Core Idea: Approximate Compliance Learning**
Instead of attempting to solve the ill-posed problem of estimating the *exact* human stiffness matrix from a single demonstration, ACP focuses on deriving an *approximate* but highly functional compliance profile. This profile is designed with two crucial properties:
1. **Avoiding large internal forces:** Prevents damage to the robot or environment during contact.
2. **Encouraging accurate tracking:** Ensures the robot follows the desired motion as precisely as possible.

This approximation is achieved by representing the compliance profile not just with a stiffness value, but also with a "virtual target pose" in addition to the standard "reference pose." The directional difference between these two poses implicitly encodes the spatial distribution of stiffness.

**B. Demonstration Collection: Kinesthetic Teaching**
Human demonstrations are collected using a kinesthetic teaching system (Fig. 3). This method is chosen over teleoperation because it allows the human operator to directly feel and demonstrate variable compliance behaviors through haptic feedback.
- **Hardware Setup:** UR5e robot, GoPro RGB camera for visual data, and an ATI mini-45 force/torque (F/T) sensor mounted near the robot's end-effector.
- **Compliance for Demonstration:** During teaching, the robot's low-level compliance controller is set with very low stiffness, damping, and mass. This makes the robot "soft" and easy for the human to manipulate, allowing them to naturally demonstrate different levels of resistance and compliant motion. The tool center point (TCP) is set near the handle to make rotations intuitive.

**C. Estimating Compliance Labels from Demonstrations (Key Innovation)**
This is the most critical part of ACP, addressing the "ill-conditioned problem" of compliance estimation.
- **Assumptions:** The method relies on assumptions about the manipulation environment:
 - **Assumption I: Contact force dominates:** Inertia, friction, and gravity are negligible compared to contact forces. This is managed by avoiding very fast robot motions and using lightweight objects.
 - **Assumption II: Nonzero contact force:** All contacts made should exert a non-zero force.
 - **Assumption III: No pinching contacts:** The contact geometry is not overly restrictive (e.g., robot not jammed in a tight corner).
- **Stiffness Direction Rule:** The core strategy is: apply a *low stiffness (k_low)* in the direction of the measured force feedback, and a *high stiffness (k_high)* in all other orthogonal directions.
 - **Theoretical Justification (Theorem 1):** The paper provides a theorem demonstrating that by allowing velocity freedom in the direction of the feedback force, constraint violations can be avoided. This allows the system to remain stiff in other directions for precise tracking without generating excessive internal forces.
 - **Stiffness Matrix Formulation (Eq. 6):** The full stiffness matrix `K` is constructed using a diagonal matrix `K0` (with `k_low` in the force direction and `k_high` elsewhere) rotated by a matrix `S` whose first column aligns with the force direction.
- **Stiffness Magnitude Rule (Eq. 7):**
 - `k_high` is an empirically set value for accurate position tracking.
 - `k_low` is not set to zero but decreases continuously with increasing force magnitude, transitioning from `k_max` (for very low forces, where high stiffness is desired for tracking) to `k_min` (for high forces, where compliance is critical), within defined force thresholds (`f_min`, `f_max`). When force is negligible, `k_high` is used in all directions.
- **Data Preprocessing:** Wrench data from demonstrations undergoes a moving average filter (one-second window size). This heavy filtering serves two purposes: smoothing the virtual target trajectories and providing "hindsight information" about upcoming contacts, which is crucial for smooth contact engagement. The virtual target pose is then computed based on this filtered force and the derived stiffness, effectively converting a force target into a position target for the low-level controller.

**D. Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP) Design**
ACP integrates the learned compliance profile into a visuomotor policy framework, building upon the "Diffusion Policy".
- **Policy Formulation:** The policy models the distribution of future actions (reference pose, virtual target pose, and stiffness magnitude) as a diffusion process, conditioned on current observations.
- **Inputs and Encoding:**
 - **Visual Data:** Fisheye RGB images (past 2 timesteps), encoded using a CLIP-pretrained ViT-B/16 model.
 - **Proprioception:** Robot end-effector poses (past 3 timesteps).
 - **Force/Torque Data:** Two encoding strategies are explored:
 - **Temporal Encoding:** Causal Convolutional Network on 32 past timesteps of wrench data.
 - **FFT Encoding (Novel):** Converts 6D force/torque readings into 2D spectrograms, processed by a ResNet-18 model with a modified input channel, and augmented with a CoordConv layer for translational invariance. This aims to capture frequency-domain characteristics of interaction forces.
 - **Feature Fusion:** All encoded features (visual, force) are passed through a transformer encoder layer with self-attention to create an adaptive multimodal representation, which is then concatenated with robot poses to condition the diffusion policy head.
- **Outputs and Decoding:** The policy predicts a 19-dimensional vector per robot arm:
 - 9D reference pose.
 - 9D virtual target pose.
 - 1D scalar stiffness value (for the low-stiffness direction).
- **Execution:** At inference, the full stiffness matrix is reconstructed using Eq. 6 (where the force direction is now derived from the vector connecting the reference pose and the virtual target pose). Both the reconstructed stiffness matrix and the virtual target pose are sent to a standard low-level admittance controller for execution. This unifies the action representation, allowing even robots without F/T sensors to execute the virtual target.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research effectively demonstrates the significant advantages of Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP) in contact-rich manipulation tasks, achieving substantial performance improvements over baseline methods. The experiments were conducted on real-world UR5e robots across two distinct tasks: object flipping and vase wiping.

**A. Overall Performance Improvement:**
- ACP consistently achieved **over 50% performance improvement** compared to state-of-the-art visuomotor policies that primarily focus on position control or use uniform constant compliance.

**B. Task I: Item Flipping**
The task involved flipping an item by pivoting it against a fixture, requiring consistent contact force and adaptation to various item properties and external disturbances. The policy was trained on 230 episodes across 15 items and tested in five challenging scenarios (Training Items, Unseen Items, Push&Flip, Varied Fixture Pose, Unstable Fixture).

- **Quantitative Results (Table I):**
 - **ACP:** Achieved an impressive 96% overall success rate.
 - **ACP w.o. FFT:** Performed similarly well with a 95% overall success rate.
 - **Compliant Policy (uniform k=500N/m):** Showed a significantly lower 23% success rate.
 - **Stiff Policy (position-only with force input):** Had the lowest success rate at 14%.
- **Key Findings:**
 - Both ACP variations (with and without FFT encoding) demonstrated remarkable robustness, consistently maintaining necessary contacts and tolerating position uncertainties. They adapted well to novel item geometries, varied fixture placements, and unexpected fixture movements.
 - Baseline policies (Compliant and Stiff) often failed due to breaking contact, motion getting stuck, or triggering robot force violations. While they might occasionally succeed by exploiting passive system compliance, this was inconsistent, especially with rigid items or inconvenient uncertainties.
 - For the flipping task, the two force encoding strategies (temporal convolution and FFT) yielded very similar results. This suggests that the high-frequency components of the force signal were less critical for this specific task, where contact forces might be relatively smoother.
- **Qualitative Observations (Fig. 6):** Visualization of predicted stiffness showed dynamic adjustments. The robot initially decreased X-axis stiffness while approaching, then shifted compliance to the Z-axis, aligning with the contact normal during pivoting. The virtual target poses and compliance directions clearly illustrated the adaptive behavior.

**C. Task II: Vase Wiping**
This bimanual task involved one arm holding a vase while the other wiped markings on it, requiring delicate contact engagement, force application, and accurate trajectory tracking. The policy was trained on 200 demonstrations with diverse vase poses and markings. Tested in four scenarios (Small Mark, Large Mark, Perturbation before Contact (PbC), Perturbation after Contact (PaC)).

- **Quantitative Results (Table II):**
 - **ACP:** Achieved the highest success rate at 93.75%.
 - **ACP w.o. FFT:** Achieved an 81.25% success rate, notably lower than full ACP.
 - **Compliant Policy:** Achieved a 43.75% success rate.
 - **Stiff Policy:** Was excluded from the full comparison because it frequently broke the robot tool, indicating a severe lack of robustness.
- **Key Findings:**
 - **Superiority of FFT Encoding:** For the vase wiping task, the FFT encoding (used in full ACP) demonstrated a clear advantage over temporal convolution (ACP w.o. FFT). This suggests that for tasks involving continuous, dynamic contact with potentially more complex or higher-frequency force variations, spectral analysis of the force signal is beneficial for making better decisions, such as identifying optimal wiping locations.
 - **Robust Contact and Accuracy:** ACP safely engaged and maintained contact throughout the wiping motion while accurately tracking the desired trajectory (Fig. 8, top row).
 - **Baseline Failures:**
 - **Stiff Policy:** Failed catastrophically due to excessive contact forces causing tool breakage (Fig. 8, middle row), highlighting the critical need for compliance.
 - **Compliant Policy:** While safer, its fixed uniform compliance made it overly sensitive to friction. This led to deviations from the target trajectory, insufficient wiping quality, and eventual loss of contact (Fig. 8, bottom row). It could not maintain both safety and accuracy simultaneously.
- **Qualitative Observations (Fig. 7):** The predicted stiffness of the wiping arm showed dynamic changes, with the compliant direction generally aligning with the contact normal. The virtual target poses clearly demonstrated how the policy guided the robot to maintain contact despite variations.

In summary, the results conclusively show that ACP provides a significantly more robust and effective solution for contact-rich manipulation tasks compared to existing visuomotor policies, especially when dealing with uncertainties and dynamic interactions. The advantage of FFT encoding for more complex contact dynamics in the wiping task further highlights the nuanced capabilities of the proposed approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Adaptive Compliance Policy (ACP) represents a significant advancement in robot manipulation, with profound implications and potential impact across various domains of robotics:

1. **Enhanced Robustness for Contact-Rich Tasks:** The most immediate and critical impact is ACP's ability to make robots far more robust and adaptable in contact-rich manipulation. Many real-world applications (e.g., manufacturing, domestic robotics, surgical assistance) involve delicate physical interactions under uncertainty. By dynamically adjusting compliance, robots can safely and reliably handle object variations, sensor noise, and unexpected disturbances, minimizing failures, damage, and downtime. This directly addresses a major bottleneck in the widespread deployment of autonomous robots.

2. **Bridging the Gap between High-Level Learning and Low-Level Control:** ACP effectively integrates learned, adaptive compliance into high-level visuomotor policies. This bridges a critical gap where advanced learning algorithms often focus solely on position control, leaving compliant interaction to simplistic, fixed low-level controllers or manual tuning. By allowing the policy to learn and output compliance parameters, ACP creates a more holistic and capable control system.

3. **Practical and Scalable Learning from Demonstration:** The paper's innovative approach to extracting *approximate but useful* compliance profiles from single human demonstrations is a breakthrough. It sidesteps the "ill-defined" problem of estimating exact human stiffness, making the process of learning complex interaction behaviors practical and scalable. This means human expertise, naturally expressed through compliant interaction, can be more readily transferred to robots without requiring extensive analytical modeling or laborious parameter tuning. This democratizes the development of compliant robot skills.

4. **Advancement in Multimodal Sensor Fusion for Control:** The demonstrated advantage of FFT encoding for force/torque data, particularly in the vase wiping task, highlights the importance of sophisticated feature representations for dynamic force signals. This suggests new avenues for research in multimodal sensor fusion, moving beyond simple temporal convolutions to leverage frequency-domain characteristics, which can be crucial for understanding and reacting to nuanced contact dynamics. This can inspire further innovation in how robots perceive and interpret physical interactions.

5. **Broader Applicability and Generalization:** The framework's ability to handle diverse items, varied scene configurations, and external perturbations in the tested tasks suggests strong generalization capabilities. This opens doors for applying ACP to a wide range of tasks previously considered too delicate or complex for autonomous robots, including:
 - **Assembly and Kitting:** Robustly inserting parts, handling deformable components, and mating intricate objects.
 - **Material Handling:** Manipulating fragile, soft, or irregularly shaped objects.
 - **Service Robotics:** Tasks like cleaning, cooking, or personal assistance that involve unpredictable physical interaction.
 - **Human-Robot Interaction:** Enabling safer and more natural physical collaboration by allowing the robot to adapt its stiffness to human movements and forces.

6. **Future Research Directions:** ACP lays a strong foundation for future work. It encourages exploration into:
 - More sophisticated learned compliance models (e.g., 6D wrench compliance).
 - Integrating learned compliance with other robot learning paradigms (e.g., offline reinforcement learning).
 - Developing even more advanced force encoding techniques or alternative representations of interaction dynamics.
 - Applying this framework to increasingly complex, multi-contact, and dexterous manipulation challenges.

In conclusion, ACP is a significant contribution that makes robots more intelligent, robust, and capable manipulators in the real world. By intelligently extracting and applying adaptive compliance from human demonstrations, it moves robotics closer to achieving human-level dexterity and robustness in uncertain, contact-rich environments.
