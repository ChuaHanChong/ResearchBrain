# Yes, Q-learning Helps Offline In-Context RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Artificial Intelligence Research Institute (AIRI) demonstrate that explicitly incorporating reinforcement learning objectives into offline in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) models enhances performance. Their methods achieved approximately 30% average performance improvement over Algorithm Distillation (AD) across over 150 diverse datasets, and doubled performance in complex environments like XLand-MiniGrid.

## Problem

- Existing offline In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL) methods, such as Algorithm Distillation (AD), primarily rely on supervised training objectives, which do not directly optimize for cumulative reward.
- The supervised learning paradigm in offline ICRL often leads to suboptimal policies, especially when dealing with low-quality datasets or a lack of optimal problem-solving trajectories.
- Current AD-like methods often assume structured 'learning histories' in the data, which may not always be available or easy to curate in real-world offline datasets.

## Method

- The authors modified the standard ==Algorithm Distillation (AD)== architecture by replacing its supervised next-action prediction head with explicit ==value-function and policy heads==.
- They implemented in-context versions of several reinforcement learning algorithms, including ==IC-DQN, IC-CQL, and IC-IQL== for discrete environments, and IC-TD3, IC-TD3+BC, and IC-IQL for continuous environments.
- The proposed methods were rigorously evaluated across over **150** synthetically generated datasets, varying in coverage, expertise levels, and the structural ordering of learning histories, spanning GridWorld and MuJoCo environments.

## Results

- RL-based approaches achieved an average performance improvement of approximately **30%** (measured by Normalized Area Under the Curve) over Algorithm Distillation (AD) across more than **150** GridWorld and MuJoCo datasets.
- In the challenging XLand-MiniGrid environment, RL-based methods significantly outperformed AD, doubling its performance even with a highly subsampled (**1%** of original size) dataset.
- RL-based methods demonstrated greater robustness to variations in dataset coverage, expertise levels, and the absence of explicit learning history structure, consistently outperforming AD in these challenging conditions across discrete and continuous environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly optimizing reinforcement learning objectives, rather than relying solely on supervised learning, is crucial for effective offline In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL).
> - RL-based ICRL methods are more robust to data imperfections, exhibiting higher performance with limited dataset coverage, varied data expertise levels, and absence of structured learning histories.
> - Offline-specific regularization (e.g., as in Conservative Q-Learning or Implicit Q-Learning) is particularly important for robust ICRL performance in continuous state and action spaces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the intersection of Reinforcement Learning (RL), Offline RL, and In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL), particularly focusing on Transformer-based models. The broader landscape of RL has seen the emergence of powerful sequence generation models, such as Transformers, enabling "in-context learning" where models can perform new tasks given a description and examples without parameter updates. This capability is desirable in meta-RL, aiming for agents that adapt efficiently to unseen tasks.

Existing offline ICRL methods, such as Algorithm Distillation (AD) and Decision-Pretrained Transformer (DPT), have demonstrated promise. However, the authors note a limitation: these methods primarily rely on supervised training objectives. Supervised approaches in offline RL are recognized for their limitations, especially in tasks requiring the agent to "stitch" suboptimal trajectories into optimal policies or when dealing with low-quality datasets. This contrasts with the core objective of RL, which is the maximization of cumulative reward. While some online ICRL methods have explored off-policy RL techniques, their reliance on heuristics and untested performance in offline settings leaves a gap. The current work addresses this gap by systematically investigating the integration of explicit RL objectives within an offline ICRL framework, using a Transformer backbone, a direction previously explored in online settings but not comprehensively in offline ICRL. This work distinguishes itself by providing a systematic empirical study, rather than introducing a new RL objective, focusing on controlled comparisons within a shared architecture.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically investigate whether methods that explicitly optimize RL objectives can achieve improved performance in offline ICRL and whether these improvements are consistent across various experimental axes. This investigation is driven by the observation that predominant offline ICRL methods, like Algorithm Distillation (AD) and Decision-Pretrained Transformer (DPT), utilize supervised training objectives. This approach may not effectively leverage the information in offline datasets to achieve optimal behavior, a critical requirement in offline RL. The authors highlight that this oversight can lead to significant challenges, especially given that online interaction, which could compensate for such limitations, is not feasible or safe in offline settings.

To systematically address this, the authors pose several specific research questions:
1. **Impact of Explicit RL Objective Optimization**: Does directly optimizing the RL objective improve performance in offline ICRL? This question seeks to determine if aligning the learning objective with reward maximization leads to better agent behavior compared to supervised learning.
2. **Dependence on Data Quality and Coverage**: How does the effectiveness of RL objective optimization vary with the coverage and quality of offline datasets? This addresses the robustness of RL-based ICRL under different data conditions, a critical factor in real-world offline RL scenarios where data characteristics can vary significantly.
3. **Necessity of Offline-Specific RL Techniques**: Do specialized offline RL techniques, beyond basic RL algorithms, offer additional benefits for effective offline ICRL? This explores whether incorporating conservative or implicitly regularized Q-learning methods (e.g., CQL, IQL) is advantageous over standard Q-learning in an offline ICRL context.
4. **Handling Unstructured Trajectories**: How do algorithms perform when access to structured "learning histories" (sequences of improving trajectories) is not available, and only a random set of trajectories is provided? This investigates the reliance of ICRL methods on the specific data organization typically assumed by approaches like AD.
5. **Generalization to Mixed and Out-of-Distribution Dynamics**: Does explicit RL objective optimization better handle environments with a mixture of dynamics or when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) dynamics? This evaluates the adaptability and generalization capabilities of RL-based ICRL agents in more complex and challenging environments.

By addressing these questions, the study aims to provide empirical evidence and methodological insights into developing more robust and efficient offline ICRL agents, particularly by emphasizing the importance of explicitly optimizing for reward maximization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic empirical study built upon the Algorithm Distillation (AD) framework, a Transformer-based architecture, as its baseline. The core methodological approach involves adapting this Transformer backbone to incorporate explicit Reinforcement Learning (RL) objectives, subsequently evaluating their performance across a diverse set of offline datasets.

#### 3.1. RL Incorporation

- **Base Architecture:** The study uses a Transformer backbone, similar to AD, which processes a sequence of trajectory transitions. The original AD objective is to predict the next action given a learning history and current observation.
- **Modifications:**
 - Input tuples are augmented to include previous "done" flags (indicating episode termination) and the current episode step, encoded into a single token through concatenation.
 - The next-action prediction head of AD is replaced with value-function heads, trained using specific RL loss functions. For continuous action spaces, a policy head is also added.
- **RL Algorithms Adapted:**
 - **Discrete Environments:**
 - **Twin Deep Q Network (DQN):** A basic RL method without offline-specific components.
 - **Conservative Q-Learning (CQL):** An offline RL approach that introduces value-function pessimism. The discrete version incorporates a cross-entropy loss and a CQL term into the IQL loss.
 - **Implicit Q-Learning (IQL):** A popular offline RL method known for implicit regularization and strong performance, adapted from its NLP variant (ILQL) by adding a CQL term to its loss in discrete settings.
 - **Continuous Environments:**
 - **TD3 (Twin Delayed DDPG):** An online RL baseline.
 - **TD3+BC:** A minimalist offline modification of TD3, incorporating a behavior cloning term.
 - **Continuous IQL:** The IQL algorithm adapted for continuous action spaces.
- **Training Details:**
 - Q- and V-values are learned using Temporal Difference (TD) loss, where the Transformer predicts next values conditioned on the full offline context window.
 - Target value networks are implemented as separate, lightweight heads to improve memory efficiency and training speed.
 - Gradients from Q- and V-head losses are backpropagated into the Transformer backbone. When a policy head is present (for continuous actions), its gradients are detached from the backbone to enhance stability.
- **Inference:** Discrete approaches select actions using the `argmax` operator on Q-values, while continuous methods use a deterministic policy output.

#### 3.2. Environments and Datasets

- **Primary Environments:**
 - **Dark Room (DR):** A discrete Markov Decision Process (MDP) for navigation to an unknown target. Versions include 9x9 (episode length 20) and 19x19 (episode length 100).
 - **Dark Key-to-Door (K2D):** A partially observable MDP (POMDP) for finding a key then a door. Versions include 9x9 (50 steps/episode) and 13x13 (100 steps/episode). These were transformed into fully observable MDPs for data collection.
 - **MuJoCo Environments:** Popular continuous control tasks for meta-RL: HalfCheetahVel (HCV), AntDir (ANT), HopperParams (HPP), and Walker2DParams (WLP), truncated after 200 steps.
- **Dataset Collection:**
 - **Discrete:** Q-learning was used to collect training histories, varying the number of histories per target (1 or 5) and the number of unique training targets (e.g., DR9: 20, 40, 70 targets; K2D: 250, 500, 1000 goals).
 - **Continuous:** Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm was used, collecting histories from 25, 50, or 100 environment instances.
- **Data Quality/Expertise:** Datasets were partitioned into "early," "mid," and "late" expertise levels by dividing complete learning histories into three equal trajectory-wise parts, representing low-quality, interpolated, and near-optimal data, respectively.
- **Specialized Environments/Conditions:**
 - **XLand-MiniGrid:** A more challenging discrete environment to test generalization in complex settings. A "tiny" subsampled dataset (1% of original size) was used.
 - **Janus (Modified DR19):** An environment with mixed dynamics to test learning across different dynamics and deployment into OOD dynamic zones.
 - **Absence of Learning History Structure:** Experiments were conducted with randomly shuffled datasets and datasets sorted by discounted return, rather than ordered learning histories.
 - **Visual Observations (MW-DR9):** A MiniWorld-based visual variant of Dark Room to test transferability of findings to visual state spaces, using a convolutional encoder.

#### 3.3. Evaluation

- **Rollouts:** Policies were rolled out for 100 episodes in discrete environments (tracking performance at 25, 50, 100 episodes) and 4 episodes in continuous environments (tracking at 1, 2, 4 episodes).
- **Metric:** Normalized Area Under the Curve (NAUC) of episode performance is the primary metric, providing a single robust value for comparison and hyperparameter selection. This metric is normalized by the expert policy AUC.
- **Robustness:** Different random seeds were used for hyperparameter search and final evaluation, following a robust evaluation protocol. The rliable library was used for statistical validation.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** Tuning was primarily performed on the largest complete dataset for AD. RL approaches reused AD's general hyperparameters but had a significantly smaller tuning budget for their specific RL parameters (e.g., discount factor, regularization weights).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of RL objectives in offline ICRL across various experimental setups:

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **RL Methods Outperform AD:** RL-based approaches consistently outperformed Algorithm Distillation (AD) on average across all discrete datasets.
- **Offline RL Superiority:** While all RL methods showed similar performance on training targets, performance on test targets varied. Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) achieved the best average performance with a 28.8% average improvement in Normalized Area Under the Curve (NAUC) compared to AD. Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) followed closely with a 23.7% improvement, while Twin Deep Q Network (DQN) showed a 16.6% improvement. This performance gap between offline RL objectives (CQL, IQL) and the online RL method (DQN) indicates the advantage of offline RL algorithms in this setting.

#### 4.2. Dataset Coverage

- **Improved Data Efficiency:** RL-based approaches demonstrated greater data efficiency and tolerance for limited target repetition in learning histories compared to AD.
- **Robustness to Limited Histories:** In complex K2D environments, RL methods maintained performance with single history per target setups, whereas AD experienced a notable performance drop.
- **Exceptions:** AD slightly outperformed offline RL on smaller DR9 datasets with limited targets and only one history per target. DQN, surprisingly, sometimes surpassed offline RL on DR19 tasks.

#### 4.3. Dataset Expertise

- **AD's Weakness on Early Data:** AD performed poorly on "early" (low-quality) datasets, failing to achieve NAUC scores above 0.4.
- **RL's Advantage on Early Data:** All RL approaches achieved significantly higher scores on early datasets, with DQN performing best, suggesting less regularization is beneficial for low-quality data.
- **Offline Regularization's Importance:** On "late" (near-optimal) datasets, offline RL methods (CQL, IQL) significantly outperformed DQN, underscoring the importance of offline regularization for high-quality data. AD was competitive on late datasets with high coverage but showed lower data diversity.

#### 4.4. Absence of Learning History Structure

- **RL Robustness to Unstructured Data:** When learning histories were randomly shuffled, RL approaches generally outperformed AD. DQN showed superiority on DR19 under random ordering, consistent with its performance on sub-optimal datasets.
- **Sorting by Return:** When randomly sampled trajectories were sorted by discounted return, offline RL methods consistently outperformed both AD and DQN in the K2D environment. However, on DR19, only CQL maintained its lead, while IQL and DQN surprisingly performed better with randomly ordered data than with sorted samples.

#### 4.5. Evaluation on XLand-MiniGrid Environment

- **Significant Gains:** On a challenging, data-sparse (1% of original size) XLand-MiniGrid environment, RL-based approaches approximately doubled the NAUC and mean return scores compared to AD (e.g., AD: 0.22 NAUC; IQL: 0.46 NAUC). This indicates the benefits extend to more complex, data-sparse settings.

#### 4.6. Continuous State and Action Spaces

- **Offline RL Outperforms AD:** In continuous MuJoCo environments, offline RL approaches (TD3+BC, IQL) consistently outperformed AD across most dataset expertise levels. AD only matched performance on "late" (near-expert) datasets.
- **Online RL Limitations:** The online RL method (TD3) demonstrated lower performance than AD in continuous domains, attributed to its lack of offline-specific regularization and proneness to drift with out-of-distribution actions/states. This highlights the critical importance of the offline component in continuous settings.

#### 4.7. Mixture of Dynamics (Janus Environment)

- **Superiority in Mixed Dynamics:** RL-based approaches consistently demonstrated superiority over AD when trained on environments with different dynamics and deployed in environments with both mixed and out-of-distribution (OOD) dynamics.
- **Performance Preservation:** In isolated tests (deployment in a single dynamic), IQL outperformed AD by approximately 50%, while DQN and CQL achieved roughly twice the performance. In the combined Janus environment, RL methods better preserved performance under one dynamic and showed slightly improved performance under the other. However, unconstrained offline RL methods might struggle with OOD state-action pairs, which are unavoidable in the Janus setup.

#### 4.8. Complementary Analyses (Appendices)

- **Return-To-Go (RTG) Augmentation:** Adding RTG conditioning to AD slightly degraded performance in discrete tasks (likely due to exploration bias) but had negligible effect in continuous tasks.
- **Model Size Sensitivity:** Moderate Transformer sizes (width 256–512, depth 2–4 layers) generally yielded the best performance, suggesting that simply increasing model size does not automatically lead to better performance in this ICRL setting. The findings remained qualitatively stable across tested sizes.
- **Visual States (MW-DR9):** Findings generalized to visual-based environments, with IC-CQL and IC-IQL outperforming IC-DQN and becoming competitive or superior to AD as the number of training targets increased.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study provides empirical evidence that explicitly optimizing RL objectives offers substantial benefits for offline In-Context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL). The findings suggest a re-evaluation of current offline ICRL approaches that predominantly rely on supervised learning.

The key implications and potential impacts are:
- **Improved Performance and Robustness:** Integrating RL objectives, particularly those from offline RL (e.g., CQL, IQL), consistently leads to improved performance compared to supervised baselines like Algorithm Distillation (AD). This improvement is observed across various data conditions, including different dataset coverages, expertise levels (from low-quality to near-optimal demonstrations), and data structures (ordered learning histories vs. randomly shuffled trajectories).
- **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** RL-based methods demonstrate greater data efficiency and tolerance for limited target repetition, making them potentially more suitable for real-world applications where extensive, perfectly structured learning histories are often unavailable.
- **Better Handling of Data Quality and Structure:** The superior performance of offline RL methods on early (low-quality) datasets and their robustness to unstructured data suggest they can extract more useful information from diverse and imperfect offline data. This is crucial for expanding the applicability of ICRL in practical scenarios.
- **Applicability to Complex Environments:** The significant performance gains observed in the challenging XLand-MiniGrid environment and improved handling of mixed and out-of-distribution dynamics in the Janus environment indicate that the benefits extend beyond simple settings to more complex and realistic scenarios.
- **Importance of Offline Regularization:** The comparison between online and offline RL methods (e.g., TD3 vs. TD3+BC/IQL) in continuous control highlights that simply incorporating an RL objective is insufficient; offline-specific regularization is critical for stability and performance, especially in continuous state and action spaces.
- **Advancing ICRL:** By aligning ICRL learning objectives more closely with the intrinsic reward-maximization goal of RL, this work paves the way for more robust and efficient offline ICRL methods, potentially enabling the development of more adaptive and generalizable meta-RL agents.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge limitations, including the reliance on large model capacities and diverse training data for in-context capabilities to emerge, even in relatively simple environments. While improved performance was observed in out-of-distribution (OOD) test environments, the method does not fully resolve this challenge and lacks specific mechanisms for adaptation to novel environments or OOD observations, a common limitation in offline RL. Future work could address these by evaluating more complex environments, integrating architectural enhancements (e.g., N-gram heads, Mixture-of-Experts), exploring visual observations, and investigating offline-to-online settings where model weights can be updated during deployment for further improvement.
