# A Durable Vision-Based Tactile Fingertip for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A durable rounded vision-based tactile fingertip pairs a soft platinum-cured silicone gel (Shore A 6.5) with a nonpigmented, textured 85 um thermoplastic-polyurethane protective film bonded by corona plus silane treatment, housed in a snap-on replaceable cartridge. Two accelerated tests, rotating-drum sanding and repetitive probe loading, show over two orders of magnitude longer life than GelSight Mini and DIGIT, with gradual rather than catastrophic failure.

## Problem

- Commercial vision-based tactile sensors give rich contact signals but their pigmented elastomer surfaces abrade and rupture quickly under sustained sliding and concentrated repeated loading.
- Hardening or thickening protective layers to resist damage trades away compliance and tactile image quality, so prior designs face an unresolved sensitivity-versus-service-life tension.
- Literature lacks data on extended repetitive concentrated loading, progressive failure behavior, and maintenance-oriented construction, and no shared durability protocol exists for layered tactile sensors.

## Method

- Sensing surface stacks an 85 um BASF Elastollan 1170A10 ==TPU film==, applied as six wet-on-wet coatings from a thermally conditioned THF/MEK solution, onto a 3 mm XP565 silicone gel mixed 15:1.
- ==Corona activation== plus Momentive SS4120 ==silane coupling== bonds TPU to gel to a Septon 2104 injection-molded cartridge that snaps onto the finger housing, swappable without electrical, optical, or calibration work.
- Two ==accelerated durability protocols== quantify lifetime: a 76 mm rotating drum at 80 rpm with 400 or 150 grit paper, and a repetitive probe test driving a 6 mm probe at 39.2 N, 45 cycles per minute.

## Results

- Sanding to protective-film rupture took a mean **180 minutes** at 400 grit and 1.96 N, versus roughly **30 seconds** for DIGIT and **24 seconds** for GelSight Mini under identical conditions.
- All **nine** fabricated sensors stayed functionally usable at the repetitive probe endpoints, seven at **5 days**, one at **6**, one at **8 days** (about **520,000 contacts**); Mini and DIGIT ruptured within **35** and **25 minutes**.
- Lifetime fell roughly **tenfold** from 200 g to 400 g load; thermal conditioning of the coating solution raised sanding lifetime **two to three fold**, and graded multilayer TPU proved unnecessary.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Failure mode matters as much as lifetime: localized film rupture and small delamination blisters preserve usable tactile imaging, letting maintenance be scheduled rather than emergency.
> - A single well-processed polymer chemistry can match an engineered graded multilayer stack, and process variables like solution thermal conditioning can dominate architectural complexity.
> - Cheap accelerated abrasion and repetitive-load rigs are sensitive development tools, but their loads are deliberately severe and do not predict field service life.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing in robotics is crucial for tasks where external vision alone is insufficient, providing information such as contact location, local surface geometry, contact-force distribution, shear, and incipient slip. This capability is particularly relevant for robotic hands operating in unstructured environments where object properties may be unknown.

Vision-based tactile sensors (VBTSs) represent a significant development in this field, utilizing internal cameras to observe the deformation of a compliant sensing surface. This approach offers advantages such as high spatial resolution and rich contact information using relatively simple optical and imaging components. Pioneering work, such as the GelSight sensor, established the use of camera-imaged reflective elastomers for high-resolution measurement of contact geometry and force. Subsequent designs have led to more compact sensors, including DIGIT, and soft, rounded fingertips suitable for dexterous manipulation.

The broader landscape of VBTSs encompasses various architectures. Many GelSight-type sensors use pigmented or reflective contact surfaces to suppress external scene images. However, other designs employ transparent or selectively transmissive surfaces to integrate tactile sensing with visual or proximity information. Examples include FingerVision, which uses a transparent marked skin for internal cameras to observe both contact deformation and the surrounding scene, and Finger-STS, which further develops combined visual, proximity, and tactile perception. TIRgel utilizes total internal reflection to differentiate contact from external visual data. These advancements demonstrate that nonpigmented sensing surfaces can provide both optical contact information and external visual data.

Despite the advancements in VBTS technology, a significant challenge limiting their widespread deployment in demanding robotic applications is mechanical durability. The compliant elastomeric surface, necessary for tactile sensitivity, must simultaneously resist abrasion, repeated concentrated loading, cutting, tearing, and interfacial delamination. Attempts to improve resistance, such as increasing hardness or protective-layer thickness, often introduce a trade-off by potentially reducing compliance or degrading tactile-image quality.

Recent studies have begun to address aspects of VBTS durability. Rayamane et al. explored adding a latex protective layer to enhance the robustness of silicone-based optical tactile sensors. The PolyTouch system introduced a protected, replaceable tactile structure, reporting an extended lifetime compared to commercial sensors under repeated rubbing. Davis and Stuart investigated the resilience of silicone and polyurethane VBTS materials against repeated compression, shear, and abrasion, indicating the potential of polyurethane for improved surface resilience.

This prior work highlights a growing interest in tactile-surface durability but indicates a continued need for research into extended repetitive concentrated loading, progressive failure behavior, and maintenance-oriented sensor construction. Limited directly comparable field-lifetime data from industrial robotic operations also remain a gap. This paper positions itself within this landscape by focusing not solely on introducing a new tactile-imaging principle, but specifically on enhancing the durability and maintainability of the replaceable sensing surface, addressing key practical limitations for industrial deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop a vision-based tactile fingertip that offers improved durability and maintainability for deployment in demanding robotic manipulation tasks. The authors identified current commercial vision-based tactile sensors as providing rich contact information but being vulnerable to abrasion and repeated concentrated loading, which restricts their utility in harsh environments. This vulnerability creates a significant barrier to the broader adoption of these sensors in industrial and other challenging robotic applications.

The motivation behind this work stems from several detailed considerations:

1. **Addressing Durability Limitations:** Existing VBTSs often employ compliant elastomeric surfaces that, while providing sensitivity, are susceptible to mechanical damage. The paper aims to overcome this by engineering a protective surface capable of resisting various forms of wear, including sustained abrasion, repetitive concentrated normal loading, and lateral displacement, without compromising tactile sensitivity. The design specifically seeks to improve resistance to surface rupture, interfacial delamination, and permanent deformation.

2. **Achieving Gradual Degradation:** For practical industrial use, a sensor's utility is not solely determined by the initial onset of damage but also by how damage progresses. Catastrophic failure can lead to abrupt operational halts. The authors aimed to develop a sensor where damage, when it occurs, progresses gradually. This characteristic would allow for continued, albeit potentially degraded, functionality, providing a window for scheduled maintenance rather than requiring immediate, unplanned repairs. The motivation is to preserve useful tactile information even after localized damage begins, allowing maintenance to be scheduled before a complete loss of sensing capability.

3. **Enabling Rapid Field Replaceability:** Regardless of durability improvements, any sensing surface will eventually wear out. The practical value of a sensor in an industrial setting is significantly enhanced if worn components can be replaced quickly and economically. This motivated the design of a replaceable sensing cartridge that can be exchanged without requiring sensor disassembly, electrical disconnection, optical realignment, or recalibration. Such modularity is intended to reduce downtime and simplify maintenance procedures.

4. **Establishing Comparative Testing Procedures:** The absence of broadly accepted durability standards for layered vision-based tactile sensors motivated the development of defined, accelerated laboratory procedures. These procedures – specifically, a rotating-drum sanding test and a repetitive probe test – were designed to evaluate resistance to sustained abrasive wear and repeated concentrated mixed-mode loading, respectively. The objective was to provide a standardized, comparative means of assessing durability across different sensor constructions under controlled laboratory conditions, aiding both development and future evaluations.

In summary, the core motivation was to move beyond simply demonstrating tactile sensing capabilities towards creating a robust, practical, and maintainable VBTS suitable for real-world industrial and robotic applications, addressing both the physical limitations of sensor materials and the operational challenges of sensor deployment and upkeep.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research involved the design, fabrication, and rigorous comparative testing of a novel vision-based tactile fingertip. The methodology focused on achieving improved durability and maintainability through specific material selection, fabrication processes, and the development of standardized accelerated testing protocols.

#### 3.1. Sensor Design and Hardware

The developed sensor features a compact, rounded fingertip geometry, designed as an interchangeable platform. It comprises four main components: a miniature color camera, RGB LED illumination boards, a rigid finger housing, and a removable sensing cartridge. This modular construction allows for rapid replacement of damaged sensors without requiring disassembly of the vision-based hardware.

The sensing cartridge itself consists of a soft silicone gel, a nonpigmented, textured, thin thermoplastic-polyurethane (TPU) protective film, and a rigid cartridge substrate. The sensor utilizes a nonpigmented surface and total internal reflection, with a finely textured outer surface to emphasize objects in contact while minimizing distant scene interference. The soft silicone gel was selected to ensure compliance for sensitive tactile imaging.

#### 3.2. Materials Selection

Material choices were guided by a set of requirements: high optical transparency, resistance to repetitive abrasion, rapid elastic recovery with minimal compression set, durable covalent bonding between layers, and compatibility with a reproducible manufacturing process.

- **Gel Material:** Platinum-cured silicone (Silicone Inc., XP565) was selected. It was formulated at a non-standard 15:1 (A:B) ratio to achieve a Shore A hardness of approximately 6.5, providing optical clarity, compliance, and excellent elastic recovery with negligible compression set. Its low tear strength was addressed by the protective film.
- **Skin Material:** Thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPU) were chosen after evaluating several options, including TPE-S films and polyvinyl alcohol. TPU (BASF Elastollan 1170A10, Shore A 70) offered a combination of superior abrasion resistance, optical transparency, high elasticity, and chemical durability. A simplified architecture of six sequential coatings of this single TPU grade was adopted, replacing initially considered multilayer structures. Thermal conditioning of the coating solution was optimized to significantly enhance abrasion resistance.
- **Interfacial Bonding:** To ensure durable service life, strong interfacial bonding between the cartridge, silicone gel, and TPU protective skin was critical. This was achieved through corona activation followed by silane coupling treatment.
- **Cartridge Material:** The cartridge was injection molded from Kuraray Septon 2104, selected for its balance of rigidity for structural support and compliance for snap-in replacement.

#### 3.3. Sensor Fabrication Process

Sensors were fabricated using a textured aluminum mold and the removable polymer cartridge. The process involved:
1. **Mold Preparation:** Cleaning and application of a mold release.
2. **Polyurethane Protective Film Deposition:** Six sequential wet-on-wet coatings of a 7 wt.% TPU solution (in THF/MEK solvent) were applied to the mold. Thermal conditioning (cooling to 60°F, then warming to 90°F) was applied to the solution. The coated mold was then oven and vacuum dried. The final film thickness was approximately 85 μm.
3. **Cartridge Surface Preparation:** The polymer cartridge underwent corona treatment to increase surface energy, followed by a brush coating of Momentive SS4120 silane coupling agent to promote adhesion with the silicone gel.
4. **Coated Mold Surface Preparation:** The exposed TPU surfaces on the coated molds were lightly corona-treated and then treated with a silane coupling agent to promote adhesion with the silicone gel.
5. **Silicone Gel Casting:** The mold cavity was filled with the platinum-cured silicone gel (XP565, 15:1 ratio). The prepared cartridge was inserted into the uncured gel, and the assembly was cured at 180°F (82°C) for 3.5 hours.
6. **Demolding:** The completed sensor was removed, with the mold's texture directly replicated onto the polyurethane film.

#### 3.4. Durability Testing Protocols

Two accelerated durability tests were developed to evaluate the sensor's performance comparatively:

- **Repetitive Probe Test (RPT):**
 - **Apparatus:** A rigidly mounted finger sensor positioned beneath a vertically actuated probe attached to a load cell on a linear carriage. A 6 mm diameter cylindrical probe with radiused edges and a 20° angled contact face was used.
 - **Loading Conditions:** The probe applied a peak force of 39.2 N (4.0 kgf) at 45 cycles per minute, holding peak force for 0.3 s and remaining unloaded for 0.5 s. The test induced approximately 3.5 mm of probe penetration and 1.1 mm of lateral displacement, creating severe confined shear deformation.
 - **Failure Monitoring:** Damage progression was monitored by periodic surface photographs and tactile images. The criterion was functional usability, observing interference with tactile imaging.
- **Rotating-Drum Sanding Test:**
 - **Apparatus:** A 3-inch (76 mm) diameter cylindrical drum wrapped with 400-grit abrasive paper, rotating at 80 rpm. The sensor was pressed against the drum by a pivoting arm with calibrated weights.
 - **Loading Conditions:** Tests primarily used a 200g scale reading, corresponding to approximately 1.96 N normal force.
 - **Failure Criterion:** Testing continued until the first visually detectable rupture of the TPU protective film exposed the underlying silicone gel, defined as the abrasion endpoint. Progression was monitored by direct observation and photographs.

#### 3.5. Comparative Testing

Commercially available GelSight Mini and DIGIT cartridges were tested using the identical sanding apparatus, abrasive paper, drum speed, and normal loads, utilizing custom 3D-printed fixtures to adapt them to the test setups. For RPT, commercial sensors were oriented at 20° to align with the probe's angled contact face.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research produced quantitative and qualitative results regarding the durability and degradation characteristics of the developed sensor compared to commercial counterparts.

#### 4.1. Abrasion Resistance (Sanding Test)

- **Developed Sensor Performance:** Under standard test conditions (400-grit paper, 200g scale reading, 1.96 N normal force), the developed sensor exhibited a mean time to first protective-film rupture of 180 minutes (approximately 2–3 hours, n=2). Increased loads or coarser grit significantly reduced lifetime; for instance, 18 minutes at 400g (3.92 N) with 400-grit paper, and 4 minutes at 400g with 150-grit paper.
- **Commercial Sensor Performance:** Under the same standard test conditions, commercially available DIGIT and GelSight Mini sensors reached their defined abrasion endpoint (initial surface-film rupture) significantly faster, after approximately 0.5 minutes (30 seconds) and 0.4 minutes (24 seconds), respectively.
- **Comparative Improvement:** The developed sensor demonstrated an improvement in abrasion resistance of more than two orders of magnitude compared to the commercial specimens under the defined accelerated sanding conditions.
- **Damage Progression:** Abrasion damage on the developed sensor progressed gradually, initially causing increased light scattering in the worn region, followed by localized rupture of the TPU protective film. Crucially, tactile images of contacted objects retained useful detail throughout and beyond the defined protective-film rupture endpoint, indicating a non-catastrophic failure mode.

#### 4.2. Repetitive Probe Testing (RPT)

- **Developed Sensor Performance:** Nine sensors incorporating the current formulation were subjected to continuous repetitive probe testing (39.2 N force, 45 cycles/min).
 - Seven sensors remained functionally usable when testing was discontinued after 5 days (approximately 324,000 contacts).
 - One remained usable after 6 days (approximately 388,800 contacts).
 - One remained usable after 8 days (approximately 518,400 contacts).
 - Functional failure was not observed within the respective test durations for any of the developed sensors.
- **Commercial Sensor Performance:** Commercial GelSight Mini and DIGIT sensors exhibited initial rupture of their pigmented surface films within approximately 35 minutes and 25 minutes, respectively.
- **Comparative Improvement:** This represents an improvement in resistance to repetitive concentrated loading of more than two orders of magnitude, with the full extent of the improvement not quantified as the developed sensors had not reached functional failure.
- **Damage Progression:** All tested developed sensors developed a faint circular imprint corresponding to the probe circumference. This minor defect produced little detectable interference with tactile imaging. In three of the nine sensors, localized blister formation due to delamination of the TPU protective layer from the silicone gel occurred near the probe contact region (e.g., 1 mm diameter blister on the 6-day specimen). These delamination defects grew slowly and were visible in tactile images but did not substantially impair overall tactile sensing at the test endpoints. The sensor tested for eight days showed only the circular probe imprint with no evidence of delamination. This indicates a gradual degradation rather than sudden failure.

#### 4.3. General Observations on Failure Behavior and Maintainability

- The developed sensor consistently exhibited gradual damage progression in both tests. In abrasion, rupture was localized; in RPT, imprints and small delamination blisters formed gradually. Useful tactile imaging was largely preserved beyond the onset of physical damage.
- The sensing surface was designed as a replaceable cartridge. Replacement was rapid and did not require sensor disassembly, electrical disconnection, optical realignment, or recalibration, only a simple prying tool. This feature, combined with gradual degradation, supports a practical, scheduled maintenance approach.
- The simplified sensor architecture, achieved through a single TPU chemistry and silicone formulation with a reproducible bonding process using commercially available materials, is noted as a practical outcome for manufacturing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a critical limitation in the widespread deployment of vision-based tactile sensors (VBTSs) in robotic manipulation: their limited mechanical durability and challenges related to maintenance. The work demonstrates a practical approach to developing a VBTS that combines enhanced durability with considerations for real-world operational requirements.

**Enabling Robust Robotic Manipulation:** By achieving durability improvements of more than two orders of magnitude under defined accelerated laboratory conditions compared to commercial sensors, this work indicates that durability need not be a primary limiting factor for VBTSs in industrial and demanding robotic environments. This allows robots to perform contact-rich manipulation tasks more reliably and for longer durations without sensor failure, thereby expanding the applicability of VBTS technology in areas such as manufacturing, logistics, and hazardous material handling.

**Improving Operational Efficiency and Cost-Effectiveness:** The sensor's gradual degradation behavior, where damage progresses incrementally rather than catastrophically, is significant. This characteristic can provide advanced warning of impending functional decline, allowing for proactive, scheduled maintenance rather than reactive emergency repairs. Combined with the rapidly replaceable sensing cartridge, which requires no electrical or optical reconnection, recalibration, or specialized tools, this design reduces operational downtime and maintenance costs. The use of commercially available materials and a simplified fabrication process also supports economical manufacture and replacement, further contributing to practical deployment.

**Advancing Sensor Design Principles:** The study contributes to the understanding of material science and engineering for VBTSs. It identifies specific material combinations (platinum-cured silicone gel, TPU protective film, and specific bonding agents) and fabrication techniques (e.g., thermal conditioning of coating solutions, sequential coating applications) that significantly enhance durability. The development and application of defined accelerated abrasion and repetitive-contact durability tests also provide valuable methodologies for future research and development in the field, enabling systematic comparison and optimization of new sensor constructions.

**Broader Applicability and Future Directions:** While the presented work focuses on a nonpigmented vision-based tactile sensor, the underlying principles of a highly wear-resistant protective surface, gradual degradation, and a replaceable sensing cartridge are broadly applicable. These concepts can be adapted to other VBTS architectures, including those employing pigmented elastomers or embedded surface markers, suggesting a wide potential impact across the VBTS landscape. The identified future work, such as increasing resistance to interfacial delamination and improving abrasion-test repeatability, further indicates avenues for refining these robust sensor designs.

In summary, this research offers a substantial step toward overcoming the practical barriers that have historically limited the wider adoption of VBTSs. By integrating high durability with maintainability considerations, it provides a foundational framework for developing reliable and cost-effective tactile sensing solutions for advanced robotic applications.
