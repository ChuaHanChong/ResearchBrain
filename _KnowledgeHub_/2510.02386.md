# On The Fragility of Benchmark Contamination Detection in Reasoning Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Research reveals that existing benchmark contamination detection methods are ineffective for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), as Reinforcement Learning (RL) training can conceal contamination introduced during model evolution. Furthermore, extensive Chain-of-Thought (CoT) contamination on advanced LRMs substantially boosts performance while remaining largely undetectable, challenging detection methods reliant on memorization.

## Problem

- Benchmark contamination in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) leads to inflated performance metrics and undermines evaluation fairness on public leaderboards.
- Existing contamination detection methods primarily assume direct memorization of benchmark data, making their effectiveness uncertain for LRMs that learn complex reasoning through multi-stage training (SFT, RL) and exhibit generalization.
- Model developers rarely release intermediate reasoning steps (CoT data), making it difficult to detect contamination when LRMs acquire reasoning abilities through complex, sequential training phases.

## Method

- A systematic study of benchmark ==contamination== in LRMs was conducted across two scenarios: contamination during model evolution (SFT and subsequent RL) and contamination via Chain-of-Thought (CoT) on advanced LRMs.
- Ten existing contamination detection methods were evaluated against these scenarios using base models and advanced LRMs on six reasoning benchmarks, alongside theoretical analysis and ablation studies.
- Controlled experiments with different RL algorithms and objective components (e.g., clipping term) were performed to isolate mechanisms responsible for concealing contamination.

## Results

- Contamination introduced during SFT, initially detectable by methods like LiRA (achieving ~**89%** AUROC), experienced a significant decrease in AUROC (e.g., LiRA dropped by **9%** to **23%**+) after subsequent GRPO training.
- Advanced LRMs contaminated extensively with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) data showed up to a **12%** average increase in Pass@1 performance, but nearly all existing detection methods performed at near-random levels (50-**65%** AUROC).
- Theoretical and empirical analysis confirmed that the importance sampling and clipping terms in PPO-style RL objectives are the primary drivers of contamination concealment, with their removal largely eliminating the effect.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement Learning (RL) training, particularly using PPO-style objectives like GRPO, can conceal benchmark contamination introduced during Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) without the model "forgetting" the data.
> - The concealment mechanism is attributed to the importance sampling and clipping terms within PPO-style RL objectives, which reduce the negative log-likelihood gap between member and non-member samples.
> - Advanced Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) generalize from Chain-of-Thought (CoT) contaminated data, increasing confidence on unseen but similar samples, which undermines the core memorization assumption of current detection methods.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has witnessed rapid advancements, leading to the development of sophisticated models capable of excelling in various real-world tasks. This progress has fostered a competitive environment, with leaderboards serving as key indicators of model performance. This competitive landscape inadvertently incentivizes practices such as optimizing directly on benchmark suites, which can include incorporating evaluation benchmarks into training data. This practice, known as benchmark contamination, can lead to inflated performance metrics and misrepresent a model's true capabilities.

Prior research has addressed benchmark contamination, primarily proposing detection methods predicated on the assumption that contamination in LLMs largely involves memorization of benchmark data. These methods typically identify discrepancies in distributions between "members" (seen during training) and "non-members" (unseen samples) using techniques based on instance similarity, probability analysis, instance generation, or answer memorization. However, with the evolution of LLMs into Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) that employ Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning and undergo multi-stage training (e.g., Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL)), the effectiveness of these existing detection methods has become uncertain. The challenges are amplified by the fact that model developers rarely release intermediate reasoning steps (CoT data), and LRMs acquire reasoning abilities through complex, sequential training phases.

This paper provides a systematic investigation into benchmark contamination within the context of LRMs, exploring two distinct scenarios where contamination can occur: during the pre-LRM evolutionary stages (SFT and RL) and as a final fine-tuning step on already advanced LRMs. It aims to bridge a gap in the understanding of how LRM training dynamics interact with contamination detection, particularly in light of their advanced reasoning and generalization capabilities, which may invalidate the core "memorization" assumption of many current detectors. The work builds upon existing contamination detection literature while critically examining its applicability to the unique characteristics of LRMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct the first systematic study of benchmark contamination specifically in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) and to evaluate the fragility of existing contamination detection methods under LRM training paradigms. The study is motivated by the increasing competition among LRM developers to achieve high rankings on public leaderboards, which can lead to deliberate or inadvertent benchmark contamination. This contamination results in inflated performance metrics, thereby undermining the fairness of evaluation and threatening the integrity of these leaderboards.

The research focuses on two main scenarios where contamination may occur in practice, each posing distinct challenges to existing detection approaches:

1. **Stage I (pre-LRM): Contamination during the evolution of a base model into an LRM via SFT and RL.**
 - **Motivation:** LRMs develop their reasoning abilities through sequential SFT and RL stages. This multi-stage process presents opportunities for developers to introduce benchmark data during earlier phases (e.g., SFT) and potentially obscure the contamination evidence through subsequent training (e.g., RL). The researchers aim to determine if SFT-introduced contamination remains detectable after further RL training, and if so, how.
 - **Specific Objectives:**
 - Evaluate whether SFT contamination of a base model, when acquiring reasoning abilities, is initially detectable by a range of existing methods.
 - Investigate if subsequent Reinforcement Learning (RL) training, specifically using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), can conceal these contamination signals.
 - Isolate the underlying mechanisms responsible for any observed concealment, distinguishing between "forgetting" due to additional training on clean data versus algorithmic properties of RL.
 - Provide theoretical analysis to explain the observed concealment capabilities of RL algorithms, particularly focusing on the role of PPO-style importance sampling and clipping objectives.
 - Empirically validate theoretical predictions through controlled ablation studies comparing different RL objectives (e.g., RAFT, RAFT++, GRPO).

2. **Stage II (post-LRM): Contamination with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) applied to advanced LRMs as the final training stage.**
 - **Motivation:** Advanced LRMs already possess significant reasoning capabilities. Introducing benchmark data with CoT reasoning steps at this late stage might be a strategy to boost performance without leaving clear "memorization" traces, as models might generalize rather than strictly memorize. Existing detection methods, often designed for memorization-centric LLMs, might not be effective when LRMs internalize reasoning processes rather than discrete answers.
 - **Specific Objectives:**
 - Assess the extent of performance inflation resulting from extensive SFT contamination with CoT on advanced LRMs.
 - Evaluate the effectiveness of existing contamination detection methods against this type of contamination, particularly given the absence of access to internal CoT reasoning during detection.
 - Analyze the log-probability distributions of member and non-member samples to understand why detection methods might fail, specifically investigating whether LRMs generalize to similar unseen samples, thereby reducing the separability relied upon by detectors.

Overall, the overarching motivation is to expose critical vulnerabilities in current LRM evaluation protocols, highlight the ease with which benchmark contamination can be concealed, and thereby advocate for the urgent development of more robust detection methods and trustworthy evaluation frameworks tailored to the unique attributes of LRMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a systematic empirical and theoretical approach to study benchmark contamination in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) across two distinct stages of model development.

#### 3.1. Contamination Setups

- **Base Models:** Experiments were conducted using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as base models. Advanced LRMs, including DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B/14B, and OpenThought3-7B, were used for Stage II experiments.
- **Datasets:** Six mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks were used: OlympiadBench, GPQA-Diamond, AIME2025, AIME2024, Minerva Math, and AMC2023. Additionally, LiveCodeBench v2 (coding) and MMLU-Pro (non-STEM general QA domains) were used to evaluate applicability beyond math/science. Training data included 10K/30K clean samples from OpenThoughts3 and 4096 clean samples from DeepMath-103K for RL. All training datasets were deduplicated against evaluation benchmarks using 13-gram overlap.
- **Member/Non-Member Split:** For each benchmark, half of the questions were randomly sampled as the "member set" (used for contamination), and the remaining half as the "non-member set" (for detection evaluation).

#### 3.1.1. Stage I (Pre-LRM): Contamination during Model Evolution

- **SFT Contamination:** The base model was exposed to benchmark questions and their responses, which were distilled from an advanced LRM (QwQ-32B). Responses were generated with a temperature of 0.6, top-p of 0.95, and a maximum of 32768 output tokens. Rejection sampling with 64 rollouts was used to select correct trajectories for distillation. Member set questions and responses were replicated 3 times (for Qwen2.5-7B) or 9 times (for Llama-3.1-8B) within the SFT training set alongside clean samples.
- **RL Training:** Following SFT, the models underwent RL training using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For RL contamination, member questions were replicated 2 times within a clean RL training set (4096 samples from DeepMath-103K). GRPO was chosen as the RL algorithm, and models were trained for one epoch.
- **Controlled Experiments:** To isolate the cause of concealment, additional RL variants (RAFT, RAFT++) were used, and specific components (e.g., clipping term) of the GRPO/RAFT++ objectives were ablated. Experiments also varied the number of RL training steps and included a baseline of further SFT on clean data.

#### 3.1.2. Stage II (Post-LRM): Contamination with CoT on Advanced LRMs

- **SFT Contamination with CoT:** Advanced LRMs were subjected to extensive SFT exclusively on the benchmark's member data, which included Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning steps.

#### 3.2. Contamination Detection Methods

Ten representative detection methods were selected and categorized:
- **Generation-based:** Verbatim (ROUGE-L on partial prompts), CDD (edit distance variation).
- **Perturbation-based:** Neighbor (calibrates score with semantically similar unseen questions).
- **Reference-based:** LiRA, Ref (calibrates target model's log-probability with a reference model, Bespoke-Stratos-7B).
- **Reference-free:** LOSS (raw negative log-likelihood), Zlib (NLL calibrated by Zlib compression), Min-K%, Min-K%++, Max-K% (focus on K% tokens with lowest/highest log-probabilities).

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics and Procedures

- **Detection Performance:** Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) was used, comparing detection scores between member and non-member sets. Higher AUROC indicates better detection. For each question, 8 responses were generated, and their detection values averaged. Log-probabilities were primarily computed on response tokens, with ablation studies confirming its superiority over question-only or combined token signals.
- **Model Performance:** Pass@1 was used to measure LRM performance on benchmarks, with 10 rollouts for AIME/AMC and 3 for Olympiad/GPQA/Minerva.
- **Log-Probability Analysis:** Distributions of average log-probabilities for member and non-member samples were plotted before and after various training stages to visualize separability.
- **Theoretical Analysis:** A first-order Taylor expansion of log-likelihood was used to analyze the Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) gap (Gk) between members and non-members, focusing on how PPO-style clipping and importance sampling affect this gap (Theorem 3.1).

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **SFT Training:** LLaMA-Factory implementation with specified hyperparameters (e.g., full training, batch size 128/32, context length 32768, learning rate schedule, bfloat16 precision). Efficiency was enhanced using FlashAttention-2, DeepSpeed ZeRO-1, Liger kernels, and asynchronous activation offloading.
- **RL Training:** Verl implementation, following RAFT and DAPO for specific algorithm configurations. GRPO was implemented without a KL term.
- **Inference:** vLLM was used for inference (temperature 0.6, top p 0.95, max new tokens 32768).
- **Hardware:** Experiments were run on a single node with 9x NVIDIA L40S GPUs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings that highlight the fragility of benchmark contamination detection in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) under various training scenarios.

#### 4.1. Stage I (Pre-LRM): RL Conceals Contamination Introduced during Model Evolution

- **SFT Contamination is Initially Detectable but RL Conceals It:** When benchmark data is introduced during Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) of a base model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct or Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), existing detection methods, particularly reference-based (LiRA) and some reference-free approaches (Min-K%, Max-K%, Loss), initially show reasonable detection capabilities (e.g., LiRA achieving ~89% AUROC on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). However, subsequent Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) training, even for a short duration with clean samples, consistently leads to a significant decrease in AUROC across all detection methods and benchmarks. For instance, LiRA's average AUROC dropped by almost 9% for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and over 23% for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.
- **More GRPO Steps, More Concealment:** The concealment effect strengthens with increased GRPO training steps. AUROC values monotonically decline, with some reference-free methods approaching random guessing (AUROC ≈ 50%) after only 156 steps. This indicates that extensive RL training can render contamination almost undetectable.
- **Concealment is Not Due to Forgetting:** Controlled experiments demonstrated that the concealment is not a result of the model "forgetting" the contaminated data. The models maintained performance inflation (e.g., an average 7.14% increase in Pass@1 across benchmarks for the GRPO-trained contaminated model compared to the clean SFT baseline). Furthermore, additional SFT training on clean data did not conceal contamination, ruling out simple overwriting as the mechanism.
- **PPO-Style Objectives are the Root Cause:** Theoretical analysis indicated that the importance sampling and clipping terms within PPO-style objectives (like GRPO) are the primary drivers of concealment. These terms reweight trajectories, selectively dampening off-policy successes (more pronounced in non-members) and allowing on-policy successes to steer updates. This leads to a greater reduction in Negative Log Likelihood (NLL) for non-members compared to members, causing the NLL gap (and thus separability) to contract.
- **Empirical Validation of Theoretical Analysis:** Ablation studies confirmed this. RAFT, a rejection sampling method without importance sampling or clipping, showed no significant change in detector performance. In contrast, RAFT++ (a RAFT variant incorporating importance sampling and clipping) and GRPO (with clipping enabled) caused substantial drops in detector performance. When clipping was removed from RAFT++ and GRPO, the concealment effect largely disappeared, reinforcing the theoretical prediction.
- **Generalizability Across Model Sizes and Domains:** The RL concealment effect was observed across different model sizes (3B, 7B, 14B) and extended beyond mathematical/scientific reasoning domains to general QA (MMLU-Pro non-STEM) and coding (LiveCodeBench).

#### 4.2. Stage II (Post-LRM): CoT Contamination on Advanced LRMs Barely Leaves Evidence

- **Significant Performance Inflation from CoT Contamination:** Extensive SFT contamination with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning applied to advanced LRMs (e.g., DeepSeek-R1-Distill, OpenThought3 models) as a final training step resulted in substantial performance inflation (e.g., up to 12% average increase in Pass@1 on benchmarks). This suggests that developers can artificially boost leaderboard rankings with this strategy.
- **Near-Random Detection Performance:** Despite significant performance inflation, almost all existing contamination detection methods performed near random guesses (AUROC typically between 50% and 65%) when applied to these extensively contaminated advanced LRMs. Even LiRA, a previous state-of-the-art method, only achieved an average of 58.74% AUROC across six benchmarks.
- **Generalization Undermines Memorization Assumption:** Analysis of log-probability distributions revealed that after extensive CoT contamination on member samples, the log probabilities of both member and *unseen non-member* samples increased at a similar margin. This indicates that contaminated LRMs exhibit increased confidence even on unseen samples that share distributional similarities with the training set, benefiting from their internalized reasoning process rather than strict memorization of specific reasoning trajectories. This generalization capability directly challenges the core assumption of many existing detection techniques, which rely on a distinct "memorization gap" (lower loss or less diversity) between seen and unseen samples.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research reveals a critical vulnerability in the current evaluation landscape for Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), carrying significant implications for the integrity of public leaderboards and the trustworthiness of LRM development.

#### 5.1. Undermining Evaluation Fairness and Leaderboard Integrity

The most immediate impact is the demonstration that model developers can easily contaminate LRMs to achieve inflated benchmark performance while leaving minimal detectable traces. This ability directly undermines the fairness of evaluation by creating an uneven playing field where contaminated models can artificially rank higher, potentially influencing investment, adoption, and public perception based on misleading performance figures. The integrity of leaderboards, which are widely used to benchmark and compare state-of-the-art models, is severely compromised.

#### 5.2. Inadequacy of Current Detection Methods

The study conclusively shows that existing benchmark contamination detection methods are largely fragile and ineffective in the context of modern LRM training paradigms. Their foundational assumption—that contamination primarily manifests as direct memorization of training samples—is challenged by the generalization capabilities of LRMs and the concealment effects of Reinforcement Learning. This highlights that many current detection tools, designed for a prior era of LLM development, are becoming obsolete for LRMs.

#### 5.3. Urgent Need for Advanced Detection Methods

The findings underscore an urgent need for the scientific community to develop new, more sophisticated contamination detection methods tailored to LRMs. These future methods must move beyond simple memorization-driven assumptions and explicitly account for:
- The effects of multi-stage training processes, particularly the concealment mechanisms inherent in RL algorithms like GRPO/PPO.
- The generation of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, as detectors currently lack access to these intermediate steps and must infer contamination from final outputs.
- The generalization capacity of LRMs, where models internalize underlying knowledge and reasoning processes rather than memorizing exact sequences, leading to increased confidence even on distributionally similar unseen data.

#### 5.4. Call for Trustworthy Evaluation Protocols

Beyond technical detection methods, the research implicitly calls for broader changes in LRM evaluation protocols. The authors explicitly suggest two potential directions:
- **Release of Intermediate Training Checkpoints:** Model developers releasing more intermediate training checkpoints could enable greater transparency and allow the community to monitor and regulate potential benchmark contamination at various stages of model development.
- **Contamination-Robust Evaluation Benchmarks:** The observation that LRMs can generalize from contaminated data to similar unseen samples implies that minor benchmark modifications might be insufficient to prevent contamination. This necessitates the development of entirely new, contamination-robust evaluation benchmarks or dynamic evaluation systems that adapt to model training data.

In conclusion, this work serves as a critical wake-up call, exposing a fundamental vulnerability in how advanced reasoning models are evaluated. It shifts the focus from simple memorization to more complex interactions between contamination, training dynamics, and model generalization, thereby opening significant avenues for future research in AI safety, trustworthy AI, and robust machine learning evaluation.
