# Hierarchical Latent Action Model

> [!summary] Summary
> HiLAM provides a hierarchical latent action model for robots that automatically extracts variable-length, high-level skills from unlabelled video data. By leveraging these discovered skills for pretraining, it achieves a 94% success rate on the LIBERO-Long benchmark and demonstrates substantial data efficiency, reducing reliance on costly action-labeled demonstrations.

## Problem

- High cost and effort of collecting action-labeled data for robot training, limiting scalability and diversity for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- Existing Latent Action Models (LAMs) only capture short-horizon, low-level motions, failing to extract long-term temporal structure or higher-level skills from observation-only videos.
- Traditional hierarchical skill discovery methods often assume fixed skill primitives or fixed-length action sequences, which are not suitable for dynamic, variable-duration real-world skills.

## Method

- **HiLAM** utilizes a two-phase framework: first, learning ==latent skills== from observation-only videos, then training ==hierarchical policies==.
- Employs a pre-trained ==Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM)== to infer low-level latent actions and an ==H-Net== architecture for ==dynamic chunking== to discover variable-length, high-level latent skills.
- Pretrains a hierarchical policy (high-level predicting skills, low-level predicting primitive actions) using the discovered latent skills and actions as ==pseudo-labels== from large-scale actionless videos, followed by fine-tuning on expert demonstrations.

## Results

- HiLAM consistently outperformed the BAKU baseline across all four challenging LIBERO benchmark suites, achieving a **94%** success rate on LIBERO-Long and **84%** success with only **50%** of expert demonstrations, indicating strong data efficiency.
- Ablation studies confirmed that hierarchical policy learning and pretraining on large-scale actionless videos are crucial for performance, with human videos slightly outperforming robot videos as pretraining data.
- Qualitative evaluation revealed that the dynamic chunking mechanism successfully segments complex tasks into semantically meaningful, variable-length skill segments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Long-term temporal information can be effectively modeled by dynamically chunking low-level latent actions into variable-length, high-level latent skills from unlabelled video data.
> - Hierarchical policies, pre-trained using these automatically discovered latent skills and actions, achieve superior performance and data efficiency compared to flat policies or those without large-scale pretraining.
> - The learned latent actions maintain their dynamic motion properties, enabling consistent future-frame prediction and serving as reliable pseudo-labels for robot control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

HiLAM is situated at the intersection of two critical and rapidly evolving fields in robot learning: Latent Action Models (LAMs) and Hierarchical Robot Learning. The broader research landscape in robot control is increasingly moving towards leveraging large-scale, diverse datasets to train robust and generalizable policies. However, a major bottleneck remains the prohibitively high cost and effort associated with collecting action-labeled robot data. This challenge has spurred the development of methods that can learn from observation-only data, such as videos.

Latent Action Models (LAMs) have emerged as a prominent solution to this problem. Prior work, including LAPO (Schmidt & Jiang, 2024), Genie (Bruce et al., 2024), and LAPA (Ye et al., 2025), demonstrates the capability of LAMs to infer abstract "latent actions" from video frames, effectively providing pseudo-labels for robot behaviors without explicit human annotation. These latent actions have proven useful for various applications, from pretraining Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to enabling interaction within world models. However, existing LAMs typically focus on short-horizon frame transitions, primarily capturing low-level, primitive motions. This limitation means they often "overlook longer-term temporal structure" and miss "higher-level structure, such as temporally extended skills," which are abundant in real-world actionless videos.

Concurrently, hierarchical robot learning has demonstrated its efficacy in handling long-horizon and complex tasks by abstracting low-level actions into higher-level "skills." This approach enables policies to reason at multiple temporal granularities. Yet, existing skill discovery methods often suffer from their own limitations: they might assume a fixed set of skill primitives (Zhu ets al., 2022; Liang et al., 2024), encode skills from fixed-length action sequences (Pertsch et al., 2021a), or rely on different modalities like language to define skill boundaries (Shi et al., 2025). Real-world skills, however, are dynamic, varying in duration and execution speed, making fixed-length or predefined skill assumptions restrictive.

HiLAM directly addresses these critical gaps by proposing a hierarchical latent action model that explicitly "discovers latent skills by modeling long-term temporal information" from actionless data. It uniquely combines the strengths of LAMs (learning from observation-only data) with the advantages of hierarchical learning (modeling long-term dependencies and complex tasks) through a novel dynamic chunking mechanism. By doing so, HiLAM advances the field by enabling unsupervised discovery of variable-length, semantically meaningful skills directly from diverse video sources, thereby moving beyond the short-term limitations of standard LAMs and the rigid constraints of many hierarchical skill learning methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of HiLAM is to enable robots to learn and execute complex, long-horizon tasks more effectively by leveraging vast amounts of readily available actionless video data. The authors are specifically motivated by the prohibitive cost and logistical challenges associated with collecting large-scale, action-labeled datasets for robot training, which hinders the scalability and diversity of robot learning applications.

The key objectives that HiLAM aims to achieve are:

1. **Discovery of Latent Skills:** To develop a model that can automatically identify and encode high-level, temporally extended "skills" directly from observation-only videos, without requiring explicit action labels or pre-defined skill sets. This moves beyond the low-level motion patterns typically captured by existing Latent Action Models (LAMs).
2. **Modeling Long-Term Temporal Structure:** To capture dependencies across long temporal horizons within videos, thereby extracting more abstract and meaningful behavioral units than short-horizon frame transitions.
3. **Adaptive Skill Duration:** To handle the inherent variability in skill durations in real-world data, allowing for the discovery of skills that are not confined to fixed-length windows, which is a common limitation in prior work.
4. **Hierarchical Policy Learning:** To leverage these discovered latent skills to train hierarchical policies, where a high-level policy predicts skills and a low-level policy translates these skills into primitive actions. This structured approach is crucial for managing the complexity of multi-stage and long-horizon tasks.
5. **Interpretability of Representations:** To ensure that the learned latent actions and skills are not merely abstract embeddings but retain their "action-like" properties and dynamic motion information, allowing for meaningful predictions and control.
6. **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** To improve the data efficiency of downstream robot control tasks by enabling effective pretraining on large-scale actionless videos, thereby reducing the amount of expensive action-labeled data needed for fine-tuning.

In essence, HiLAM seeks to unlock the full potential of unsupervised learning from video data for robotics by moving beyond primitive motions to the discovery of semantically rich, variable-length skills, thereby paving the way for more capable and autonomously learning robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HiLAM, the Hierarchical Latent Action Model, introduces a two-phase framework designed to discover latent skills from observation-only videos and utilize them for hierarchical policy learning. The core innovation lies in its ability to dynamically chunk sequences of low-level latent actions into meaningful, variable-length latent skills.

**Phase 1: Latent Skill Learning**
The first phase focuses on training HiLAM to identify and encode latent skills. This begins by obtaining low-level latent actions. Given an observation-only video $V = \{I_1, \ldots, I_T\}$, HiLAM leverages a pre-trained Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) (specifically, UniSkill's IDM) to infer a sequence of low-level latent actions, $z^l = \{z^l_1, \ldots, z^l_{T-k}\}$, between consecutive frames $I_t$ and $I_{t+k}$ with a fixed temporal interval $k$. These latent actions capture the primitive motion between frames.

The key to HiLAM's hierarchical capability is its adoption of the H-Net architecture (Hwang et al., 2025), which implements a dynamic chunking mechanism. The sequence of low-level latent actions $z^l$ serves as the initial input $z_0$ to HiLAM. The H-Net architecture consists of multiple stages, where at each stage $s$, an encoder $E_s$ processes the input sequence $z_s$. It then predicts binary boundary indicators $b^s_t \in \{0,1\}$ for each token, which determine whether a token marks the beginning of a new chunk. These boundary predictions are based on the dissimilarity of feature vectors of consecutive tokens, meaning that a boundary is likely to be placed where there's a significant change in motion patterns. The tokens corresponding to these boundaries are selected to form a shorter, chunked sequence $z_{s+1}$, which represents a higher-level abstraction. These higher-level tokens $z_s$ (for $s > 0$) are treated as latent skill representations, denoted $z^h$. After hierarchical processing through encoder-main-decoder stacks, HiLAM predicts the next latent actions at the lowest level, $\hat{z}^0 = (\hat{z}^0_2, \ldots, \hat{z}^0_{L_0+1})$, based on a next-token prediction approach.

The training objective for latent skill learning (Equation 4) is a weighted combination of three terms:
- $L_{latent}$: An element-wise $\ell_1$ loss that penalizes the difference between predicted next latent actions and target latent actions, enforcing the next-token prediction task.
- $L_{rec}$: A visual supervision term that ensures the predicted latent actions maintain their dynamic motion properties. This is achieved by using a pre-trained Forward Dynamics Model (FDM) (also UniSkill's) to predict future frames conditioned on the predicted latent actions. $L_{rec}$ is the reconstruction error between the predicted and ground-truth future frames.
- $L_{ratio}$: The H-Net ratio regularizer, which prevents degenerate boundary patterns and controls the average length of the discovered chunks.

After training, latent skills $z^h$ are extracted by expanding the stage-wise latent representations $z_s$ (specifically $z_2$ in the experiments) back to the original video's temporal resolution using the unfolded boundary pattern. This yields a per-timestep latent skill sequence $\bar{z}_s$, which then conditions the high-level policy.

**Phase 2: Hierarchical Policy Learning**
The second phase involves training a hierarchical policy comprising a high-level policy $\pi_h$ and a low-level policy $\pi_l$, using the learned latent skills and actions.

- **Pretraining:** Both policies are initially pretrained on large-scale, actionless videos (e.g., human or robot videos) using the HiLAM-extracted latent skills ($z^h$) and latent actions ($z^l$) as pseudo-labels. The high-level policy $\pi_h$ predicts a target latent skill $\hat{z}^h_t$ based on the current observation $o_t$ and a task instruction $l$. The low-level policy $\pi_l$, conditioned on $o_t$ and the predicted skill $\hat{z}^h_t$, predicts the corresponding low-level latent action $\hat{z}^l_t$. The goal is to match these predictions to the extracted targets $z^h_t$ and $z^l_t$.
- **Fine-tuning:** For deployment, the high-level policy $\pi_h$ is frozen, and the low-level policy $\pi_l$ is fine-tuned on target-domain data that includes ground-truth actions. During execution, $\pi_l$ takes the predicted skill $\hat{z}^h_t$ from $\pi_h$ and the current observation $o_t$ to output a real action $a_t$ for robot execution.

For implementation, HiLAM utilizes a two-stage H-Net and leverages UniSkill's IDM/FDM. The policies are based on the BAKU architecture, and a T5 encoder is used for language processing. Experiments are conducted on diverse datasets like Something-Something V2 (human videos), Droid, and BridgeV2 (robot videos), with evaluation on the LIBERO benchmark. The choice of $z_2$ (stage-2 representation) for latent skills is informed by its ability to capture longer-range temporal context and semantically clustered segments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of HiLAM yielded several significant findings, demonstrating its effectiveness in skill discovery and hierarchical robot control.

#### 4.1. LIBERO Benchmark Results

HiLAM was benchmarked against BAKU (Haldar et al., 2024), a strong state-of-the-art baseline, across the four challenging suites of the LIBERO benchmark: LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long.
- **Consistent Outperformance:** HiLAM consistently outperformed BAKU across all four benchmark suites (Figure 3a). This result strongly indicates that learning temporally extended latent skills from actionless videos, as HiLAM does, significantly enhances policy performance in diverse robotic tasks.
- **Superior Data Efficiency on LIBERO-Long:** The LIBERO-Long suite, which consists of long-horizon and multi-stage tasks, served as a crucial testbed for the learned hierarchical skills. HiLAM demonstrated remarkable data efficiency during fine-tuning with expert demonstrations (Figure 3b).
 - With only 10% of expert demonstrations, HiLAM achieved a 45% success rate, nearly doubling BAKU's 23%.
 - At 50% of the demonstrations, HiLAM reached an 84% success rate, which was comparable to BAKU trained with 100% of the available data.
 - Using the full 100% of demonstrations, HiLAM achieved an impressive 94% success rate, significantly surpassing BAKU. These results underscore HiLAM's ability to leverage large-scale actionless data for effective pretraining, substantially reducing the reliance on expensive labeled data during fine-tuning.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

To validate the design choices, extensive ablation studies were conducted on the LIBERO-Long suite, focusing on the pretraining data and the specific stages used for latent skill/action conditioning (Table 1).
- **Pretraining Data Effectiveness:** Both human videos (Something-Something V2) and robot videos (BridgeV2) proved effective for pretraining, with human videos yielding slightly better overall performance (0.94 success rate with human pretraining vs. 0.90 with robot pretraining). This suggests the generality of HiLAM's approach across different observation modalities.
- **Optimal Skill/Action Conditioning:** The study revealed that using the deepest hierarchical representation ($s=2$) for latent skills ($z_h$) and the raw lowest-level representation ($s=0$) for latent actions ($z_l$) resulted in the best performance (0.94 success rate with human pretraining). This supports the architectural design, where deeper stages capture more abstract, longer-range skills, while the shallowest stage maintains fine-grained control for primitive actions.
- **Importance of Hierarchical Policy and Pretraining:**
 - Pretraining a non-hierarchical (flat) policy like BAKU with latent actions, while beneficial, still lagged behind the full hierarchical HiLAM policy. This highlights the distinct advantages of hierarchical policy learning in addition to latent action benefits.
 - Training the hierarchical policy without any large-scale pretraining significantly degraded performance (0.67 success rate). This finding conclusively demonstrates that the substantial gains observed are attributed to HiLAM's pretraining process on actionless videos, rather than solely the hierarchical policy architecture.

#### 4.3. Dynamic Skill Chunking

Qualitative analysis of the predicted boundary indicators $b^s_t$ confirmed HiLAM's ability to dynamically identify semantically meaningful skill boundaries within untrimmed trajectories (Figure 4). The visualizations show HiLAM segmenting complex actions into coherent chunks, such as "gripper moves across workspace," "gripper picks up the bowl," and "gripper moves to target and places the bowl." This crucial result demonstrates that the unsupervised dynamic chunking mechanism effectively captures high-level temporal structure and groups low-level latent actions into distinct, variable-length skills.

#### 4.4. Latent Action Prediction

To verify the interpretability and action-like properties of the predicted latent actions, future-frame prediction experiments were conducted (Figure 5). Given a current image and a predicted latent action, HiLAM, using a pre-trained FDM, was able to generate a consistent future frame. This qualitative result indicates that the predicted latent actions retain meaningful motion information, effectively modeling temporal dynamics and confirming their utility as pseudo-labels for robot control.

In summary, HiLAM's experiments robustly demonstrate its capability to discover and utilize hierarchical latent skills from actionless data, leading to state-of-the-art performance and remarkable data efficiency in challenging robot manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HiLAM represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of data scarcity for complex, long-horizon tasks. Its core contribution lies in demonstrating a novel and effective method for unsupervised discovery of temporally extended, high-level latent skills directly from observation-only videos, without relying on costly action labels or restrictive predefined skill sets.

The potential impact of HiLAM is multi-faceted:

- **Enabling Scalable Robot Learning:** By dramatically reducing the need for action-labeled data, HiLAM makes it feasible to train robot policies on the vast and diverse pool of unlabeled video data available online (e.g., human demonstration videos, unannotated robot logs). This scalability is crucial for developing more general-purpose and adaptable robotic agents.
- **Improved Performance on Complex Tasks:** The hierarchical nature of HiLAM, which explicitly models long-term temporal dependencies through skills, directly translates to superior performance on multi-stage, long-horizon tasks, as evidenced by its strong results on the LIBERO-Long benchmark. This ability to reason at different temporal granularities is a key step towards more intelligent and robust robot autonomy.
- **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** The significant data efficiency shown during fine-tuning means that developers can achieve high task success rates with substantially fewer expert demonstrations. This will accelerate the deployment of robots in new environments and for novel tasks, as the burden of data collection and labeling is substantially alleviated.
- **Bridging Research Gaps:** HiLAM effectively bridges the gap between low-level latent action models and higher-level hierarchical learning approaches. It synthesizes the strengths of both fields, moving beyond primitive motion capture to extracting semantically rich behavioral units, thus opening new avenues for research in unsupervised robot skill discovery.
- **Foundation for Future Robot Intelligence:** The interpretable nature of the discovered latent actions and skills (demonstrated through dynamic chunking visualizations and future frame prediction) lays a strong foundation for future research. It suggests that these learned representations could be further leveraged for planning, reasoning, and even human-robot interaction in more intuitive ways.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards promising future research directions:
- **Simulation vs. Real-World Validation:** While the LIBERO benchmark provides a strong simulated environment, validating HiLAM's effectiveness through real-world robot experiments would further solidify its practical utility.
- **End-to-End Training:** HiLAM currently relies on a pre-trained Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) for computational efficiency. Exploring an end-to-end training approach for the entire architecture could potentially lead to a deeper and more cohesive joint understanding of both low-level motions and high-level skills, albeit with increased computational demands.
- **Integration with Language:** The paper highlights the potential for combining hierarchical latent action modeling with natural language. Motion cues and language instructions offer complementary information; integrating both signals could lead to a powerful synergy, enhancing both generalizability (via language instructions) and the execution of complex, nuanced tasks (via imitated motion cues). This aligns with the broader trend of multimodal foundation models in AI.

In conclusion, HiLAM makes a substantial contribution to robot learning by offering a practical and effective solution for learning complex behaviors from easily accessible, unlabelled video data. Its ability to automatically discover and utilize hierarchical skills has the potential to significantly accelerate the development of more capable and autonomously learning robotic systems.
