# Learning Generalizable Robotic Reward Functions from "In-The-Wild" Human Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> DVD (Domain-agnostic Video Discriminator) learns a multi-task robotic reward by training a classifier to judge whether two videos perform the same task, mixing a large in-the-wild human video dataset with a few robot demos. Conditioned on a single human demo, it scores imagined rollouts inside visual model predictive control, yielding 15-20% absolute gains in generalization to unseen environments and tasks, including on a real WidowX200.

## Problem

- Robot interaction datasets remain small and costly to collect at scale, so learned reward functions fail to generalize across new environments, objects, and tasks.
- Prior inverse reinforcement learning work targets single tasks in a single environment, giving no mechanism to produce rewards for a new task from one demonstration.
- Earlier human-video methods either hand-design human-to-robot mappings or use a fixed task classifier, so they cannot condition on a demo and transfer to unseen tasks.

## Method

- **DVD** (Domain-agnostic Video Discriminator): a binary same-task classifier trained with ==cross-entropy== on triplets of anchor, positive, and negative videos drawn jointly from human and robot data.
- Architecture pairs a frozen ==3D-convolutional video encoder== pretrained on Something-Something-V2 with a randomly initialized fully connected similarity head over concatenated embeddings.
- Task execution runs ==visual model predictive control==: an **SV2P** action-conditioned prediction model imagines rollouts, and DVD scores each against the human demo via ==cross-entropy-method search==.

## Results

- Adding human videos improved environment generalization by about **20%** average success over robot-only training, and beat Concept2Robot, demo-conditioned behavior cloning, and random by over **20%**.
- On task generalization, DVD with 9 human tasks reached **55.1%** average success versus **32.7%** robot-only with a human demo and **15.4%** for behavior cloning.
- On a real WidowX200 in an unseen environment, 6 human tasks gave **13/20** and **14/20** successes versus at most **8/20** robot-only; **20** robot demos nearly matched **120**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cheap, unlabeled-domain human video adds generalization even when its tasks are unrelated to the targets, acting as diversity regularization rather than task-specific supervision.
> - A small amount of in-domain robot data matters: pure human-video classifiers underperform, so bridging the embodiment gap needs both sources mixed during training.
> - Discriminator validation accuracy drops as human data grows (99% to 89%), yet downstream control improves, so held-out classification accuracy misleads as a reward-quality proxy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robots capable of executing a wide array of tasks across diverse environments is a significant objective in robotics research. A core component in achieving this objective is the ability of a robot to acquire general-purpose reward functions. These functions enable robots to gauge task success from onboard sensor observations, such as RGB images, which is fundamental for techniques like reinforcement learning, planning, and determining when to seek assistance. For a robot to be truly general-purpose in real-world scenarios, these reward functions must exhibit broad generalization capabilities across different environments, tasks, and objects.

Recent advancements in computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP) have demonstrated substantial generalization through deep learning models trained on large and diverse datasets. However, translating these successes to robotics has been challenging, primarily due to the scarcity of high-quality, large-scale robotic interaction data. While some initiatives are underway to collect such datasets for robotic learning, these efforts face practical limitations related to data quality, scalability, and safety requirements in real-world robot interactions.

In contrast, readily available "in-the-wild" human videos from platforms like YouTube offer an extensive, diverse, and pre-existing source of visual data depicting humans performing various tasks in a multitude of settings. Leveraging such data presents an alternative pathway to achieving broad generalization in robotic learning. However, this approach introduces several challenges, including significant domain shift between human and robot observation spaces (morphology, visual appearance), differences in action spaces, and the inherently low quality, noise, and varied viewpoints of "in-the-wild" videos.

Prior work in reward learning, often referred to as inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) or inverse optimal control, has a rich history, with methods ranging from learning from full demonstrations to inferring rewards from desired outcomes. Many of these techniques have been successfully applied to single-task or single-environment problems on real robots. This research distinguishes itself by focusing on learning generalizable multi-task reward functions from visual inputs, conditioned on a single video demonstration of a human performing the desired task.

Within the domain of robotic learning from human videos, existing approaches include explicit object or hand tracking to translate human actions into robot behaviors, end-to-end learning methods that translate human goals to robot perspectives, and techniques that infer actions, rewards, or state-values from human videos for predictive models or reinforcement learning. Other strategies involve learning keypoint or object-centric representations from videos or leveraging simulation for supervision. This work differentiates itself by explicitly targeting large and diverse "in-the-wild" human video datasets, such as Something-Something-V2, which feature extensive variations in individuals, viewpoints, backgrounds, objects, and tasks, rather than smaller, more domain-similar datasets. While some works have used such datasets for navigation or concept learning (e.g., Concept2Robot), this paper specifically focuses on learning reward functions conditioned on a human video demonstration to enable generalization to new tasks, a capability not directly offered by certain prior methods like Concept2Robot.

Finally, this research aligns with a broader trend in robotic learning that explores how learning from extensive datasets can enhance generalization. While many works in this area focus on collecting diverse robotic data or developing general-purpose policies from such data, the current study seeks to circumvent the challenges of collecting diverse robotic data by instead capitalizing on existing, plentiful human video sources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the ambition to develop generalist robots. Such robots would possess the capability to undertake a broad spectrum of tasks across numerous environments, rather than being limited to a specific task in a pre-defined setting. A fundamental requirement for these generalist robots is the ability to autonomously acquire a metric of task success, or a reward function, solely from their onboard sensor observations, such as RGB images. This reward signal is crucial for various robotic learning paradigms, including reinforcement learning and planning, and for the robot to determine when it needs assistance.

The central challenge addressed is the need for these reward functions to generalize broadly. This means they must remain effective across varying environments, different tasks, and diverse objects without requiring extensive, task-specific, or environment-specific re-engineering or data collection. Current methods in robotic learning often struggle with generalization due to the prohibitive cost and difficulty of collecting large, diverse, and high-quality robotic interaction datasets that would parallel the scale of data used in fields like computer vision and natural language processing.

To overcome this data scarcity, the authors propose leveraging "in-the-wild" videos of humans. These videos, readily available from sources like YouTube, represent a vast and diverse repository of human activities performed in a multitude of real-world contexts. This rich data source offers a potential pathway to learning broadly generalizable reward functions.

The key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Develop a simple and scalable method for learning multitask reward functions:** The goal is to create an approach that can effectively learn reward functions from a heterogeneous mix of limited robot interaction data and extensive, diverse human videos. This method should be capable of measuring the functional similarity between a robot's behavior and a human demonstration.
2. **Achieve zero-shot generalization to unseen environments:** The learned reward function should enable the robot to perform tasks effectively in new environments that were not encountered during its training phase, without any additional fine-tuning or data collection specific to those environments.
3. **Achieve zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks:** The reward function must also generalize to entirely new tasks for which the robot has no prior training data, relying only on a single human video demonstration to specify the desired task.
4. **Demonstrate real-world applicability:** The developed approach should be demonstrated on a physical robot, showing its capacity to solve robotic manipulation tasks in an unseen environment, guided by only a single human video demonstration.
5. **Evaluate the impact of diverse human data:** A core objective is to empirically demonstrate that leveraging diverse human videos, even those depicting tasks unrelated to the target robot tasks, significantly improves the generalization capabilities of the learned reward function compared to relying solely on robot data.

In essence, the research aims to bridge the data gap in robotic learning by exploiting the abundance of human video data, thereby enabling robots to learn generalizable reward functions crucial for developing adaptable, multi-purpose robotic agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on a novel approach called the Domain-agnostic Video Discriminator (DVD) for learning generalizable multi-task reward functions.

#### 3.1. Problem Statement Formalization

The problem considers a robot aiming to complete *K* tasks. Each task *T_i* is associated with an unobserved reward function *R_i*. The robot operates within a Markov Decision Process (MDP) *M_r_i*, where states are RGB images, and actions are robot-specific. Humans are assumed to operate in analogous MDPs, *M_h_i*, which share the state space, reward function *R_i*, and horizon with the robot's MDP, but may have different action spaces and transition dynamics. The reward at time *t* is assumed to depend on the last *H* timesteps (states *s_t-H:t*). The objective is to learn a parametric reward model *R_θ(s_1:H, d_i)* that approximates *R_i(s_1:H)*, conditioned on a variable-length video demonstration *d_i* of task *T_i*.

For training *R_θ*, the approach assumes access to:
- A dataset *D_h* of "in-the-wild" human videos for *N* tasks, without visual constraints on viewpoints, backgrounds, or quality.
- A limited dataset *D_r* of robot videos for *M* tasks, where *M ≤ N*. Crucially, *M << N*, indicating many more human videos and tasks in *D_h* than in *D_r*.
Both datasets are partitioned by task. The reward inference relies solely on visual observations, without access to actions or low-dimensional states, and explicitly handles large domain shifts between human and robot data. During evaluation, *R_θ* should be effective for solving task *T_i* specified by *d_i*, and importantly, generalize to unseen tasks *T_new* not present in *D_h* or *D_r*.

#### 3.2. Domain-Agnostic Video Discriminators (DVD) Training

The core idea of DVD is to train a binary classifier to predict whether two input videos, *d_i* from task *T_i* and *d_j* from task *T_j*, are completing the same task (i.e., *i = j*). This classifier, *R_θ*, learns to capture functional similarity between videos.
- **Data Source:** The classifier leverages activity labels associated with many human video datasets (e.g., Something-Something-V2) and a modest number of robot demonstrations.
- **Objective Function:** *R_θ* is trained to minimize the average cross-entropy loss over video pairs sampled from *D_h ∪ D_r*:
 - *J(θ) = E_D_h_∪_D_r_ [log(R_θ(d_i, d'_i)) + log(1 - R_θ(d_i, d_j))]*.
 - Here, *d_i* and *d'_i* are two videos performing the same task *T_i*, and *d_j* performs a different task *T_j*.
- **Domain Bridging:** By training on visually diverse examples—including human-human pairs with significant visual differences but same task, and human-robot pairs with large domain shift—DVD is compelled to learn abstract functional similarities rather than superficial visual cues.
- **Similarity Score:** The output of *R_θ* for two videos is interpreted as a "similarity score," indicating how functionally similar the tasks depicted in the videos are.

#### 3.3. DVD Implementation Details

- **Architecture:** *R_θ* is implemented as *R_θ(d_i, d_j) = f_sim(f_enc(d_i), f_enc(d_j); θ)*.
 - *f_enc*: A pretrained neural network video encoder (e.g., the same architecture as used in Concept2Robot, pretrained on the Something-Something-V2 dataset) encodes each video into a latent space. This encoder is fixed during DVD training.
 - *f_sim*: A randomly initialized fully connected neural network, parameterized by *θ*, takes the concatenated latent embeddings from *f_enc(d_i)* and *f_enc(d_j)* and outputs the binary classification (similarity score).
- **Batch Sampling:** During training, batches of video triplets (*d_i*, *d'_i*, *d_j*) are sampled such that each element (*d_i*, *d'_i*, *d_j*) has a 0.5 probability of being a robot demonstration, ensuring a rough balance between robot and human videos despite the dataset imbalance.
- **Video Preprocessing:** Input video clips are sequences of consecutive frames (random length 20-40 frames), randomly rotated, scaled, and cropped.

#### 3.4. Task Execution with DVD

Once trained, the *R_θ* reward function is integrated with a visual planning strategy:
- **Visual Model Predictive Control (VMPC):** The system uses VMPC which employs a learned visual dynamics model to plan actions.
- **Dynamics Model:** An action-conditioned video prediction model, SV2P, is trained separately on autonomously collected robot interaction data within each specific environment. This model predicts future states (image sequences) given current state and actions.
- **Planning Process:**
 1. A human video *d_i* specifying the desired task *T_i* is provided to DVD.
 2. At each planning step, the cross-entropy method (CEM) is used. *G* candidate action trajectories of length *H* are sampled.
 3. The SV2P dynamics model *p_φ* rolls out *G* corresponding predicted image trajectories.
 4. Each predicted image trajectory (*s_t+1:t+H*) is paired with the human demonstration *d_i* and fed into the trained *R_θ* to obtain a similarity score (reward).
 5. The action trajectory corresponding to the image sequence with the highest predicted similarity score is selected and executed on the robot.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup (Simulation and Real Robot)

- **Simulation Environment:** A MuJoCo simulated tabletop environment (adapted from Meta-World) with a Sawyer robot arm interacting with a drawer, faucet, and coffee cup/machine. Four variants of this environment are used to test environment generalization: Train Env, Train Env Rearranged, Test Env 1 (changed colors), Test Env 2 (changed colors and viewpoint), and Test Env 3 (changed colors, viewpoint, and object arrangement).
- **Simulation Tasks:** Three target tasks: closing a drawer, turning a faucet right, and pushing a cup away. Specified by unseen "in-the-wild" human videos.
- **Real Robot Environment:** A WidowX200 robot interacting with a file cabinet (training environment) or a toy kitchen set (testing environment), and a tissue box/stuffed animal.
- **Real Robot Tasks:** "Closing Something" (seen task) and "Pushing something right to left" (unseen task), specified by human videos.
- **Human Training Data:** Something-Something-V2 dataset, selecting videos from up to 15 different human tasks (853-3170 videos per task).
- **Robot Training Data:**
 - Simulation: 120 video demonstrations of 3 tasks in the training environment.
 - Real Robot: 80 video demonstrations of 2 tasks in the training environment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate DVD's ability to generalize across unseen environments and tasks by leveraging diverse human video data, and to compare its performance against baseline methods on both simulated and real robotic platforms.

#### 4.1. Experiment 1: Environment Generalization

- **Finding:** Training DVD with a combination of robot videos and human videos (Robot + K Human Tasks) resulted in significantly improved environment generalization compared to training with only robot videos (Robot Only). Specifically, an average absolute improvement of 20% in task success rate was observed across unseen environments. This improvement held even when the "Robot Only" baseline was provided with privileged robot demonstrations at test time.
- **Observation:** DVD's performance was robust to the number of human tasks included in training, even when these human tasks were unrelated to the target robot tasks. Including human videos from 9 unrelated tasks still yielded performance that greatly exceeded not using any human videos.
- **Qualitative Result:** DVD consistently assigned high similarity scores to predicted robot trajectories that functionally completed the task specified by the human video demonstration, despite substantial visual domain shift between the human videos and the simulated environments.

#### 4.2. Experiment 2: Task Generalization

- **Finding:** Leveraging human videos during training significantly enhanced task generalization performance, yielding an average absolute improvement of approximately 10-20% compared to training with robot videos alone. The "Robot Only" approach struggled to distinguish between different tasks, suggesting a lack of functional understanding.
- **Observation:** Including human videos from a greater number of unrelated tasks (e.g., 6 unrelated human tasks) was particularly beneficial for task generalization, demonstrating an over 20% performance gap compared to only training with robot videos.

#### 4.3. Experiment 3: Prior Work Comparison

- **Comparison Methods:** DVD was compared against Concept2Robot (a pretrained 174-way video classifier), a demo-conditioned behavioral cloning (BC) method, and a random policy.
- **Environment Generalization:** DVD (trained with 6 human videos) consistently outperformed all three comparison methods across various environments. It achieved an average of over 20% higher success rate than the next best-performing method.
- **Task Generalization:** DVD demonstrated an advantage of over 30% compared to both the demo-conditioned behavioral cloning policy and the random policy. The Concept2Robot method was not directly applicable for task generalization in this setup due to its non-demo-conditioned nature and prior training on all 174 tasks.
- **Implications:** These results suggest that incorporating even small quantities of robot data, alongside human videos, is important for effectively bridging the human-to-robot domain gap. Additionally, demo-conditioned behavioral cloning struggled, indicating that it likely requires a significantly larger number of robot demonstrations to learn robust policies and infer tasks from diverse human videos.

#### 4.4. Experiment 4: Real Robot Efficacy

- **Setup:** Evaluated on a real WidowX200 robot in an unseen environment with both a seen and an unseen task (closing a toy kitchen door and pushing a tissue box left).
- **Finding:** DVD trained with human videos achieved approximately double the success rate (65-70%) compared to methods relying only on robot demonstrations (maximum 40%) across both tasks.
- **Observation:** Consistent with simulation results, leveraging human videos from unrelated tasks generally improved performance on the real robot, highlighting the benefits of diverse human data for real-world generalization.
- **Qualitative Result:** On the real robot, DVD effectively captured the functional task being specified (e.g., closing the door), assigning high similarity scores to successful robot trajectories.

#### 4.5. Ablation on Amount of Robot Data for Training

- **Finding:** The performance of DVD showed only a marginal decrease when the amount of robot demonstrations was reduced significantly (e.g., from 120 to 20 demonstrations per task).
- **Implication:** This suggests that the diversity and scale of the human video data can compensate for a scarcity of robot-specific interaction data, allowing DVD to perform effectively even with minimal robot supervision.

In summary, the experiments consistently demonstrated that training the DVD reward function with a diverse dataset of "in-the-wild" human videos, even when those videos depict unrelated tasks, substantially improves its ability to generalize to unseen environments and new tasks. This enhanced generalization was observed in both simulated and real-world robotic settings and was found to be superior to existing methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robotic learning, particularly concerning the acquisition of generalizable skills. The proposed Domain-agnostic Video Discriminator (DVD) offers a practical and effective method for learning multi-task reward functions by judiciously combining limited robot interaction data with abundant, diverse "in-the-wild" human videos.

The primary significance lies in its direct approach to addressing one of the core challenges in scaling robotic learning: the scarcity of high-quality, large-scale robot-specific interaction data. By demonstrating that robust generalization can be achieved even with minimal robot data by leveraging readily available human video content, this work offers a path to mitigate the substantial data collection burden traditionally associated with robot learning. This could significantly lower the barrier to entry for developing robots capable of a broader range of tasks.

The potential impact of generalizable reward functions is substantial for the vision of "generalist robots." Such robots would be able to adapt to novel situations, perform diverse tasks, and operate in unfamiliar environments without requiring extensive re-training or explicit programming for each new scenario. The ability of DVD to generalize zero-shot to unseen environments and tasks, conditioned merely on a single human video demonstration, is a key step towards this goal. This implies that a robot could potentially learn a new task simply by being shown a human performing it, opening avenues for more intuitive and scalable robot instruction.

Furthermore, the qualitative findings, such as DVD's capacity to assign high similarity scores to task-completing robot trajectories despite large visual domain shifts between human and robot observations, underscore its ability to capture abstract functional understanding rather than superficial visual correlations. This functional understanding is crucial for true generalization.

While the research outlines limitations, such as the current focus solely on reward learning (not full policies or dynamics models) and the assumption of some robot demonstrations and task labels, it lays foundational groundwork. The findings suggest that future work could integrate these generalizable reward functions with more sophisticated generalizable policies and dynamics models to achieve fully autonomous, broadly adaptable agents. The methodology also encourages further exploration into reducing the need for explicit supervision, pushing towards even more scalable and autonomous robotic learning systems.

In summary, this work contributes to the development of more autonomous and versatile robots by providing a method to acquire broadly generalizable reward functions from an unprecedentedly rich and diverse data source. This has implications for reducing data collection costs, accelerating skill acquisition, and bringing the field closer to realizing truly generalist robotic agents capable of robust operation in complex, unconstrained real-world settings.
