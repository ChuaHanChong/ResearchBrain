# Self-Supervised Bio-Inspired Robotic Trajectory Planning with Obstacle Avoidance

> [!summary] Summary
> A neuro-inspired self-supervised learning framework was developed for robotic trajectory planning, integrating obstacle avoidance through trained forward and inverse models that provide internal supervision. The approach demonstrated generation of collision-free trajectories and revealed that smaller model capacities yielded better generalization and robustness in obstacle-rich environments.

## Problem

- Sampling-based trajectory planners incur high computational costs and unpredictable runtimes in complex, high-dimensional, obstacle-rich environments.
- Existing learning-based methods like Reinforcement Learning (RL) suffer from sample inefficiency and costly exploration, while Imitation Learning (IL) struggles with generalization beyond expert data.
- Previous self-supervised neuro-inspired trajectory planners lacked the capability to explicitly handle static obstacles, a crucial requirement for practical robotic deployment.

## Method

- Implemented and trained a ==Forward Model (FM)== and ==Inverse Model (IM)== to learn system dynamics and inverse dynamics, incorporating explicit information about obstacles.
- Utilized a ==recurrent neural network==-based ==Trajectory Model (TM)== that leverages the FM and IM in a ==self-supervised rectification== process to generate dynamically feasible and collision-free trajectories.
- Introduced mitigation strategies, including ==supervised pretraining== and ==end-effector-based geometric priors==, to counter the TM's tendency to 'exploit' imperfections in the self-supervised feedback.

## Results

- The framework achieved collision rates as low as **7.5%** and collision-free magnet success rates up to **92.5%** in obstacle environments (TM 5 in Configuration mode).
- All self-supervised models exhibited a notable deviation between predicted and executed trajectories in the simulator, indicating accumulation of errors from approximated internal dynamics.
- The smaller TM 1 architecture consistently showed better execution quality in obstacle environments, demonstrating superior collision avoidance, higher success rates, and fewer repeated actions compared to larger models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Higher-capacity Trajectory Models (TMs) can exploit ambiguities or imperfections in the self-supervised rectification feedback, leading to unnatural or oscillatory movements.
> - Providing structured guidance through supervised pretraining and geometric priors significantly improves trajectory quality and mitigates the exploitation phenomenon in self-supervised learning.
> - Smaller model architectures can demonstrate superior performance in obstacle environments, suggesting that constrained capacity implicitly encourages learning more generalized and robust collision-free behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Trajectory planning is a foundational challenge in robotics, requiring algorithms to generate efficient and collision-free sequences of states and actions for robots operating in potentially complex, obstacle-rich environments. The existing landscape is dominated by several approaches, each with specific advantages and limitations that this work seeks to address.

**Sampling-based methods**, such as Probabilistic Roadmaps (PRM) and Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT), are widely used and considered state-of-the-art. These methods construct a graph or tree by sampling the robot's configuration space and connecting nodes via a local planner. While probabilistically complete, they often incur high computational costs, particularly in high-dimensional spaces or environments with numerous obstacles. Their performance can be unpredictable, and the generated paths frequently require post-processing for smoothing to produce natural motion. Furthermore, they do not inherently guarantee optimal path quality.

**Learning-based approaches** offer an alternative paradigm. **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** enables robots to discover viable behaviors through interaction with an environment, aiming to maximize cumulative reward. However, RL typically suffers from sample inefficiency, demanding extensive and potentially hazardous or costly exploration, which complicates deployment in physical robotic systems. **Imitation Learning (IL)** bypasses the need for exploration by leveraging expert demonstrations. While avoiding direct exploration, its performance is contingent on the quality and diversity of the training dataset, which can limit generalization capabilities and prevent the discovery of optimal behaviors beyond those demonstrated. Supervised sequence modeling, a variation that reformulates task solving as generating temporally ordered states and actions, mitigates some RL disadvantages but retains common supervised learning limitations, such as a higher failure rate outside the training distribution.

The present work builds upon the authors' previous research, which introduced a neuro-inspired self-supervised learning framework for trajectory planning. This prior work proposed integrating separately trained forward (FM) and inverse (IM) models—analogous to cognitive internal sensorimotor models—to provide an internal supervisory mechanism for training a neural trajectory planner. This approach aimed to overcome the sample inefficiency of RL and the generalization limitations of IL by internalizing system dynamics and kinematic constraints, allowing for efficient, fixed-runtime trajectory generation.

This paper extends that framework by incorporating obstacle avoidance capabilities. It addresses the gap where efficient, constant-runtime neural planners struggle with dynamic or static environmental constraints, particularly the presence of obstacles. By adapting the self-supervised mechanism to account for obstacles, this research aims to push towards more robust and universally applicable neural trajectory planners that combine the efficiency of learning-based methods with the critical safety requirement of collision avoidance. The work thus positions itself at the intersection of bio-inspired AI, self-supervised learning, and practical robotic motion planning, contributing to the development of more autonomous and intelligent robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to advance robotic trajectory planning by developing a self-supervised, neuro-inspired framework that can generate collision-free and efficient trajectories in environments containing obstacles. This is motivated by several limitations observed in current trajectory planning methodologies:

- **Computational Expense of Sampling-Based Planners:** Traditional sampling-based methods, while robust, are computationally intensive. Their performance degrades significantly in high-dimensional planning spaces and complex, obstacle-rich environments, leading to unpredictable runtimes and challenges in finding near-optimal solutions. The motivation is to create a planner that can operate with a fixed runtime, providing greater efficiency for scenarios requiring numerous trajectory generations.
- **Limitations of Existing Learning-Based Approaches:**
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** While capable of discovering complex behaviors, RL suffers from low sample efficiency and demands extensive exploration, which can be costly or hazardous for physical robots. This makes it challenging to deploy in real-world systems.
 - **Imitation Learning (IL):** This approach relies on expert demonstrations, which limits its ability to generalize beyond the training data and discover potentially more optimal or novel behaviors. Its performance is strictly tied to the quality of the provided demonstrations.
- **Extending Prior Work to Obstacle Avoidance:** The authors previously introduced a self-supervised learning framework that utilized forward and inverse models to train a trajectory planner, addressing some of the sample efficiency and generalization issues of other learning methods. A key motivation for the current work is to extend this established framework to explicitly handle static obstacles in the environment, a crucial requirement for practical robotic deployment.

**The specific objectives of this research are:**

1. **Implement and Evaluate Obstacle-Aware Models:** To adapt and retrain the Forward Model (FM) and Inverse Model (IM) to incorporate information about obstacles present in the environment. This ensures that the internal models accurately reflect the system dynamics and constraints in the presence of physical barriers.
2. **Integrate Obstacle Avoidance into the Self-Supervised Trajectory Planner:** To utilize the retrained, obstacle-aware FM and IM as internal supervisory mechanisms during the training of a recurrent neural network-based Trajectory Model (TM). This self-supervised feedback mechanism is designed to guide the TM to generate trajectories that are consistent with the learned dynamics and actively avoid collisions.
3. **Assess the Feasibility and Robustness:** To empirically evaluate the performance of the proposed self-supervised trajectory planner in a simulated environment featuring an industrial 7-degree-of-freedom robotic arm and a single static obstacle. This evaluation includes assessing geometric consistency, collision rates, task success rates, and the adherence of executed trajectories to planned ones.
4. **Investigate and Mitigate Exploitation Tendencies:** To identify and analyze the tendency of the trajectory planner to "exploit" the self-supervised learning signal, leading to unnatural or inefficient movements, and to propose and evaluate strategies (such as supervised pretraining or geometric priors) to mitigate these issues.

Ultimately, the motivation is to contribute a robust, efficient, and safer approach to robotic motion planning that moves closer to the realization of constant-runtime neural trajectory planners for complex real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research employs a neuro-inspired self-supervised learning framework for robotic trajectory planning, building upon a previously proposed architecture and extending it to incorporate obstacle avoidance. The methodology involves the training of three distinct neural network components: a Forward Model (FM), an Inverse Model (IM), and a Trajectory Model (TM).

**State and Action Representation:**
The robot's state at time $t$, $s_t$, is represented as a vector encompassing various components:
- $\theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^7$: The joint-angle configuration of the 7-DOF robot.
- $mgt_t \in \{0, 1\}$: Binary state of the robot's magnet end-effector.
- $ef_{xyz,t} \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Position of the end-effector.
- $ef_{R,t} \in H$: Orientation of the end-effector (quaternion).
- $g_{xyz,t} \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Position of the goal object.
- $g_{R,t} \in H$: Orientation of the goal object.
- $o_{xyz,t} \in \mathbb{R}^3$: Position of the obstacle object.
- $o_{R,t} \in H$: Orientation of the obstacle object.

The action taken at time $t$, $a_t$, leading from $s_t$ to $s_{t+1}$, is defined as:
- $\Delta\theta_t = \theta_{t+1} - \theta_t$: Change in joint angles.
- $\Delta mgt_t = mgt_{t+1} - mgt_t$: Change in magnet state.

**Dataset Generation:**
All datasets were generated using a simulated environment built with the MyGym toolkit. The robot used was a KUKA LBR iiwa 7-DOF manipulator equipped with a magnet. The environment included a single static box obstacle. A specific "no obstacle" pose was defined outside the valid workspace.
- **Transition Datasets (D1, D2):** Two datasets of 500,000 transitions each, formatted as $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$. D1 contained multiple transitions per setup, while D2 had only a single transition per setup. Both datasets maintained a 9:1 ratio of obstacle-present to obstacle-free transitions and a 4:1 ratio of colliding to non-colliding transitions, aiming to increase sample density in complex dynamic regions.
- **Trajectory Dataset (D3):** A set of 12,000 trajectories, $\tau = (s_0, a_0, \ldots, s_T)$, with a maximum length of 52 states. This dataset was used for training the TM and included both obstacle-containing and obstacle-free scenarios with similar ratios to the transition datasets.

**Forward Model (FM) Training:**
The FM is a multi-layer perceptron trained to approximate the system dynamics, $FM(s_t, a_t) \approx s_{t+1}$. Its output is structured into multiple heads, each corresponding to a distinct subvector of the state (e.g., joint angles, end-effector position, obstacle pose). This allows for independent optimization of components that differ in scale and semantic meaning. The loss function, $L_{FM}$, is a weighted sum of prediction errors across these output heads. The FM was trained on dataset D1.

**Inverse Model (IM) Training:**
The IM, also a multi-layer perceptron, is trained to approximate the inverse dynamics, $IM(s_t, s'_{t+1}) \approx a_t$. Here, $s'_{t+1}$ is a reduced representation of the next state, excluding components directly resulting from the action. The loss function, $L_{IM}$, minimizes the error between the predicted and ground-truth action vectors. The IM was trained on dataset D2.

**Trajectory Model (TM) Training:**
The TM is a recurrent neural network implemented with Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) layers. It receives an initial state $s_0$ and a goal state $s_T$ as input and outputs a predicted sequence of intermediate states, $\hat{\tau}_s = (\hat{s}_1, \ldots, \hat{s}_{T-1})$. Similar to the FM, its output is time-distributed and divided into multiple heads for different state vector components.

**Self-Supervised Rectification:** The core of the self-supervised mechanism is a rectification process. Given the TM's predicted trajectory $\hat{\tau}_s$, the FM and IM are used to generate a *rectified* trajectory $\tilde{\tau}_s$.
- The first and final states of $\tilde{\tau}_s$ are fixed to the ground-truth $s_0$ and $s_T$.
- For intermediate steps $t$, the IM predicts an action $\hat{a}_{t-1}$ using the previous rectified state $\tilde{s}_{t-1}$ and the reduced predicted state $\hat{s}'_t$ from the TM.
- Subsequently, the FM uses $\tilde{s}_{t-1}$ and $\hat{a}_{t-1}$ to predict the next rectified state $\tilde{s}_t$.
This process provides an internal consistency check, guiding the TM to generate trajectories that are dynamically feasible according to the learned FM and IM.

**Loss Function for TM:** The TM is trained by minimizing $L_{TM}$, which is a combination of three terms:
- **Rectification Error:** $\sum_{t=1}^{T-1} L_s(\hat{s}_t, \tilde{s}_t)$, encouraging the predicted trajectory to align with the internally consistent rectified trajectory.
- **Initial State Connection Loss:** $L_{init}(\hat{s}_1, s_0)$, ensuring the predicted first step forms a valid connection from the initial state.
- **Goal State Connection Loss:** $L_{goal}(\hat{s}_{T-1}, s_T)$, ensuring the predicted last step forms a valid connection to the goal state.
Specific loss functions used for state subvectors include Mean Squared Error for joint configurations ($\theta$) and positional outputs ($ef_{xyz}, g_{xyz}, o_{xyz}$), Binary Cross-Entropy for magnet state ($mgt$), and Chordal distance for rotational components ($R$).

**Mitigation Strategies for Exploitation:** Initial experiments revealed that high-capacity TMs could exploit imperfections in the FM/IM, generating unnatural oscillatory movements to locally minimize the rectification loss. To counter this, the following strategies were explored:
- Supervised pretraining using ground-truth trajectories.
- Improved tuning of the FM and IM models.
- Introduction of additional geometric priors as a loss term, encouraging smoother spacing, preventing large steps, sharp angles, and unnecessary trajectory length/oscillations.

**Training and Evaluation Details:**
Five different TM architectures were trained using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-4}$ and gradient clipping (norm 2.0) to prevent gradient explosion in the recurrent network.
- **Phase 1: Geometric Evaluation (Offline):** Trajectories generated by the TMs were assessed for geometric properties (distance to start/goal, transition sizes, angles between segments, effective length) using a test set of 4,000 trajectories (half with obstacles).
- **Phase 2: Simulator Execution (Online):** The generated trajectories were executed directly in the MyGym simulator. Performance metrics included collision rate, magnet success rate (raw and collision-free), waypoint following rate, average repeated control steps, final distance to goal, mean executed step size, mean executed trajectory angle, and average deviation between predicted and executed trajectories. Execution was tested in two modes: directly following generated joint configurations (Cfg.) and following configurations derived from generated end-effector positions via inverse kinematics (EE+IK). Each model was tested over 200 episodes per mode and environment setup.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provided insights into the performance and challenges of the self-supervised trajectory planning framework with obstacle avoidance.

**Forward and Inverse Model Performance:**
The FM and IM demonstrated effective learning of system dynamics and inverse dynamics, respectively, as indicated by low mean absolute errors across various state and action subvectors (Table 1). However, prediction errors were generally higher for transitions classified as "colliding" compared to "non-colliding" or "no obstacle" transitions, particularly for joint angles ($\theta$), end-effector position ($ef_{xyz}$), and end-effector rotation ($ef_R$). This suggests that the dynamics within collision states or near obstacles are more complex and harder for the models to perfectly capture.

**Exploitation of Rectification Mechanism:**
A significant observation was the tendency of larger-capacity Trajectory Models (TMs) to "exploit" the self-supervised rectification feedback (Fig. 2). Instead of generating natural, goal-directed trajectories, these models learned to produce oscillatory or near-stationary movements in safe regions. These movements locally minimized the rectification loss, indicating that the TM was overfitting to imperfections or ambiguities in the FM and IM's learned dynamics. The FM and IM, being approximations, could have regions in the state-action space where unrealistic transitions still resulted in low rectification error, which the TM learned to exploit.

**Impact of Mitigation Strategies:**
The introduction of supervised pretraining and end-effector-based geometric priors significantly improved the quality of generated trajectories and reduced the observed oscillatory behavior (Fig. 3). These strategies appeared to guide the TM to first learn geometrically meaningful paths before the rectification process became the dominant learning objective. This suggests that providing additional structured guidance can make the self-supervised learning process more robust against exploitation.

**Simulator Execution Evaluation (Table 2):**
The simulator evaluation provided a practical assessment of the models' ability to generate executable and collision-free trajectories.
- **Collision Rate:** Model TM$_5$ (Cfg mode) achieved the lowest collision rate in obstacle environments (7.5%), followed by TM$_1$ (14.5%). Other models, particularly larger ones without sufficient mitigation, exhibited higher collision rates (e.g., TM$_2$ at 44.5%). In obstacle-free environments, most models achieved a 0% collision rate.
- **Success Rate (Collision-free magnet):** TM$_5$ (Cfg mode) also demonstrated the highest collision-free magnet success rate in obstacle environments (92.5%), with TM$_1$ (Cfg mode) close behind (85.5%).
- **Waypoint Following Rate:** TM$_1$ and TM$_5$ (Cfg mode) showed high waypoint following rates in obstacle environments (98.2% and 96.7% respectively), indicating better adherence to the planned path compared to other models. The EE+IK execution mode generally yielded very low (often 0%) waypoint following rates across all models, suggesting challenges with inverse kinematics solutions for the generated end-effector poses.
- **Efficiency Metrics:** TM$_1$ (Cfg mode) required the fewest average repeated control steps per waypoint (2.09 in obstacle environments), indicating higher execution efficiency. TM$_5$ (Cfg mode) also performed well (1.63). Larger models required significantly more repetitions.
- **Deviation between Predicted and Executed Trajectories:** All self-supervised models exhibited a notable average deviation between their predicted trajectories and the actual trajectories executed in the simulator. This indicated that the TMs learned to be consistent with the *approximated* dynamics of the internal FM and IM, rather than the true, underlying dynamics of the simulator. Errors in FM/IM predictions accumulated during execution, leading to divergence. TM$_1$ and TM$_5$ generally showed the lowest deviation among the Cfg mode executions.

**Role of Model Capacity:**
A key finding was the performance of the smaller TM$_1$ architecture. Despite its reduced capacity, TM$_1$ consistently achieved better execution quality in obstacle environments in the Cfg mode, showing superior collision rates, success rates, and waypoint reach rates, while requiring fewer repeated actions. The authors hypothesize that the smaller representation space compelled TM$_1$ to "economize," implicitly learning more generalized and robust trajectories that respected environmental constraints, and making it less susceptible to exploiting the weaknesses of the self-supervised rectification mechanism. This suggests that for obstacle-aware planning, smaller, more constrained models might be more favorable due to their lower complexity and better generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic motion planning by demonstrating the feasibility and identifying key challenges of a neuro-inspired self-supervised learning framework for trajectory planning with obstacle avoidance.

**Advancement in Trajectory Planning:** The work successfully extends the authors' prior self-supervised framework to handle static obstacles, a critical capability for real-world robotic deployment. It provides empirical evidence that recurrent neural networks, guided by internal forward and inverse models, can generate geometrically meaningful and executably feasible robot motions in constrained environments.

**Addressing Computational Efficiency:** By proposing a learning-based approach, this research moves towards constant-runtime trajectory planning, which is a significant advantage over the often computationally expensive and unpredictable sampling-based methods. This could lead to more responsive and agile robotic systems in dynamic or time-sensitive applications.

**Reduced Reliance on External Supervision:** The self-supervised nature of the framework mitigates the high data requirements and exploration costs associated with reinforcement learning, and the reliance on exhaustive expert demonstrations typical of imitation learning. This makes the approach potentially more scalable and practical for learning complex behaviors without extensive manual effort or risky real-world experimentation.

**Identification of Framework Limitations and Future Directions:** A crucial outcome is the identification and characterization of the "exploitation" phenomenon, where the trajectory model overfits to imperfections in the self-supervised feedback. This highlights a fundamental challenge in self-supervised learning for control and provides clear directions for future research:
- **Improving Internal Model Accuracy:** Emphasizing the need for more accurate and robust forward and inverse models that closely approximate true system dynamics to reduce the potential for exploitation and minimize divergence between planned and executed trajectories.
- **Enhancing Mitigation Strategies:** Further research into stronger geometric constraints, richer datasets, and more advanced neural architectures is indicated to prevent the TM from exploiting feedback imperfections.
- **Investigating Model Capacity:** The superior performance of the smaller TM$_1$ architecture suggests that constrained model capacity might implicitly encourage the learning of more robust and generalized behaviors, providing a valuable insight for designing efficient neural planners.

**Contribution to Bio-Inspired AI:** The framework's foundation in bio-inspired internal sensorimotor models contributes to the broader field of artificial intelligence, offering insights into how agents can learn and adapt through internal representations of their own dynamics and environment.

**Potential for Real-World Robotics:** If the identified limitations regarding exploitation and divergence from true dynamics can be effectively addressed, this self-supervised framework holds substantial potential for practical applications in robotics. It could pave the way for more autonomous, adaptable, and safer motion planners for industrial manipulators, service robots, and other robotic systems operating in complex, cluttered, and potentially human-shared environments.
