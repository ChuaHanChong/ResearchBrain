# Automating Reinforcement Learning with Example-based Resets

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Seoul National University developed a deep reinforcement learning framework that autonomously learns reset policies from examples of initial states, significantly reducing the need for manual intervention in real-world robot training. The system achieved competitive performance with an oracle method in simulations and enabled continuous, efficient learning on a physical UR3 manipulator.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) algorithms assume instantaneous, reliable episodic resets, which are trivial in simulation but challenging and costly for real-world robots.
- Existing real-world reset solutions rely on extensive human supervision, task-specific scripts, or specialized mechanical setups, hindering the goal of autonomous robot learning.
- These conventional workarounds introduce external dependencies and prior knowledge, contradicting DRL's aim for autonomous skill acquisition with minimal prior input.

## Method

- The framework concurrently trains a ==forward agent== (for the primary task) and a ==self-supervised reset agent==, integrating the reset mechanism directly into the learning process.
- The reset agent learns to return the system to an ==initial state distribution== solely from examples of desired initial states, without requiring explicit reward engineering.
- A ==preemptive reset trigger==, based on an ==ensemble of reset success classifiers==, activates the reset agent before the forward episode ends or leads to an irrecoverable state.

## Results

- In simulations, the method reduced manual resets and achieved performance comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, an oracle using dense, hand-engineered reset rewards (e.g., **19.51** average return vs. **10.38** for ball-in-cup (catch)).
- The learned reset agent imposed an implicit curriculum on the forward agent, confining its exploration during early training and expanding its range as reset capabilities improved.
- The method was successfully deployed on a physical **UR3** manipulator for peg-insertion and reaching tasks, demonstrating high train time efficiency (**85%** and **84%** respectively) and autonomous learning from scratch on hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-supervised learning of a reset policy from initial state examples, using Recursive Classification of Examples (RCE), effectively replaces complex, hand-engineered reset reward functions.
> - A preemptive reset mechanism, driven by an ensemble of reset success classifiers, acts as a safety layer, implicitly generating a curriculum for the forward agent by initially confining its exploration.
> - Alternating training of forward and reset agents enables continuous operation on real robots, significantly enhancing training efficiency by minimizing manual intervention.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has demonstrated considerable capability in enabling robots to acquire complex motor skills through environmental interactions, often with minimal prior knowledge. This progress is evident in both simulated environments and increasingly in real-world applications, ranging from strategy games to dexterous object manipulation. However, a fundamental challenge arises when translating DRL algorithms, which are often developed with simulation in mind, to physical robotic systems: the episodic reset mechanism.

Conventional DRL algorithms operate under the assumption that an agent can be reliably and instantaneously reset to a predefined initial state distribution at the conclusion of each episode. While trivial in simulation, implementing such resets in real-world robotics is typically resource-intensive. It frequently necessitates significant human intervention, involves the creation of task-specific scripts, or requires specialized mechanical setups designed to minimize manual effort. These workarounds, though effective for specific scenarios, contradict the broader objective of autonomous robot learning by introducing external dependencies and prior knowledge.

Previous research has explored various strategies to mitigate the reset problem or circumvent its traditional definition. Some approaches reformulate resets within multi-task or goal-conditioned reinforcement learning frameworks, where agents learn to return to diverse initial states or generate sub-goals to achieve a broader set of states. Other works focus on learning diverse control primitives or utilizing perturbation controllers to manage state distributions. Concepts like reversibility in RL have also been investigated, assuming environments where agents can escape undesired states without explicit resets. Value-based triggers have been used in multi-agent or safe RL contexts, where a recovery policy is activated based on a pre-trained safety critic. Related work has also explored multi-stage task methods that sequentially execute forward and reset policies.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by addressing the inherent incompatibility between the standard DRL episodic reset assumption and the goal of autonomous robotic learning. Instead of circumventing the need for a reset or relying on task-specific heuristics, the proposed method integrates the reset mechanism directly into the learning process. It distinguishes itself by learning a reset agent in a self-supervised manner, specifically from *examples* of initial states rather than requiring handcrafted reward functions for the reset task. This approach aims to provide a more generalizable and autonomous solution that does not assume environmental reversibility or rely on external design effort for reset rewards, moving towards a more complete autonomous DRL framework for real robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a recognized limitation in the application of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to real-world robotics: the reliance on an episodic reset mechanism. While DRL enables robots to learn complex skills with minimal prior knowledge, its algorithms typically assume that an agent can be reset to a fixed initial state distribution at the end of each training episode. This assumption holds for simulated environments, where resets are computationally inexpensive and instantaneous. However, in real-world robotic scenarios, providing such resets is challenging and often impractical.

The existing methods for managing resets in real robots usually involve one or a combination of the following:
- **Extensive Human Supervision:** Operators manually return the robot or its environment to a desired initial configuration, which is time-consuming, costly, and contradicts the goal of autonomous learning.
- **Scripted Actions:** Pre-programmed sequences of movements are used to return the robot to an initial state. While reducing human intervention for common cases, these scripts are task-specific and may fail in edge cases, still requiring human oversight.
- **Task-Specific Mechanical Setups:** Custom hardware rigs are designed with actuators and sensors to automate parts of the reset process. This is expensive, requires specialized engineering, and lacks generalizability across different tasks or robots.

These conventional solutions are described as "workarounds" that enable DRL application to real robots, but they do not address the fundamental incompatibility between the inherent assumptions of DRL algorithms and the requirements for truly autonomous robot learning. The need for such tailored reset mechanisms limits the scalability and general applicability of DRL in real-world robotics.

The key objectives of this work are to:
1. **Extend the conventional RL framework for greater autonomy:** Integrate resets as a part of the learning process rather than an external mechanism.
2. **Introduce a self-supervised reset agent:** Train an additional agent to learn how to return the system to the initial state distribution. This reset agent is trained without explicit rewards for the reset task.
3. **Learn to reset from examples of initial states:** Instead of requiring a handcrafted reward function (which is task-dependent and requires prior knowledge), the reset agent learns by observing examples of desired initial states. These examples can be collected in a task-agnostic manner at the beginning of each forward episode.
4. **Enable continuous training on real robots:** By learning from both forward and reset episodes, the system can operate continuously, minimizing interruptions for manual resets.
5. **Preemptively trigger resets:** Allow the reset agent to initiate a reset before the forward episode naturally terminates or leads to an irrecoverable state.
6. **Implicitly impose a curriculum on the forward agent:** The reset agent's ability to trigger resets can confine the forward agent's exploration during early training, gradually expanding its operational range as the reset agent's capabilities improve. This acts as a safety layer, preventing the forward policy from entering states from which a reset is impossible or from which the reset policy cannot yet recover.
7. **Reduce manual resets and prevent irrecoverable states:** The ultimate practical goal is to minimize human intervention and enhance the safety and robustness of real-world robot learning systems.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to make a step towards fully autonomous robot learning, where robots can learn complex skills in the real world with minimal human intervention and without the need for extensively engineered reset mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces an architecture that concurrently trains two distinct agents: a **forward agent** and a **reset agent**. This design extends conventional Reinforcement Learning (RL) to incorporate autonomous reset capabilities.

**Overall Architecture:**
- The **forward agent** is a standard RL agent responsible for learning the primary task defined by the environmental reward signal. It consists of a policy network (π_f) and a Q-function (Q_π_f).
- The **reset agent** acts as a safety layer and is responsible for returning the system to the initial state distribution. It comprises a policy network (π_r) and a **reset success classifier** (C_π_r), which serves as a proxy for the probability of successfully returning to the initial state.

Both agents adopt an off-policy actor-critic algorithm, specifically DDPG (Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient), though the authors state other off-policy variants are applicable.

**Learning the Reset Agent with Example-based Control (RCE):**
A core aspect of this methodology is the self-supervised training of the reset agent without explicit rewards. This is achieved by leveraging the **Recursive Classification of Examples (RCE)** framework, which casts control as a probabilistic inference problem.
- **Event Framework:** The method utilizes an event framework where a binary random variable `e_t ∈ {0, 1}` indicates whether a desired outcome (reaching the initial state) has been achieved at time `t`. The objective for the reset agent is to find a policy that maximizes the probability of achieving this outcome in the future, `p_π(e_t:∞ = 1)`.
- **Future Success Classifier:** RCE learns a classifier `C_π_θ(s_t, a_t)` that distinguishes between state-action pairs leading to future success (`p_π(s_t, a_t | e_t:∞ = 1)`) and marginal state-action pairs (`p(s_t, a_t)`). The probability of achieving the desired outcome given the current state and action, `p_π(e_t:∞ = 1 | s_t, a_t)`, can be recovered from this classifier via a simple ratio: `C_π_θ(s_t, a_t) / (1 - C_π_θ(s_t, a_t))`.
- **Classifier Loss:** The loss function for the future success classifier involves two cross-entropy terms:
 - One term assigns a label of 1 for desired outcomes (i.e., states from the initial state buffer S*).
 - The second term assigns a label based on a recursive definition involving `γω(t)/(γω(t)+1)` for transitions sampled from the replay buffer, where `ω(t)` is the classifier ratio at the next time step. This effectively provides a dense reward signal without explicit reward design.
- **Actor Update:** The reset agent's policy (actor) is updated using gradient ascent with respect to the future success classifier output.

**Training Scheme (Algorithm 1):**
Training proceeds in an alternating fashion between forward episodes and reset episodes.
1. **Initialization:** Networks for both agents are initialized, along with replay buffers (B_f for forward, B_r for reset) and an initial state buffer (S*). The environment is reset to an initial state.
2. **Forward Episode:**
 - An example of the current initial state is saved to S*.
 - The forward agent executes its policy (a_t ← π_f(s_t)).
 - **Reset Trigger Mechanism:** For each action taken by the forward agent, the reset agent evaluates the probability of successful reset, `p_π_r(e_t:∞ = 1 | s_t, a_t)`, using its ensemble of classifiers.
 - If this probability falls below a predefined threshold `p_thresh`, the reset agent **preemptively triggers a reset**.
 - If the forward episode reaches its maximum step limit without triggering a reset, a **reset is requested**.
 - Forward transitions (`s_t, a_t, r_t, s_t+1`) are saved to B_f, and the forward agent's policy and Q-function are updated.
3. **Reset Episode:**
 - When a reset is triggered or requested, the reset agent takes over and rolls out its reset policy (a_t ← π_r(s_t)).
 - The reset policy continues until the system returns to the initial state distribution.
 - If the reset agent fails to reach the initial state within its maximum allowed steps, a **manual reset** of the environment is performed.
 - Reset transitions are saved to B_r, and the reset agent's policy and success classifier are updated using the RCE method and the collected initial state examples from S*.

**Key Assumptions and Implementation Details:**
- **Unimodal Initial State Distribution:** The initial state distribution is assumed to be unimodal, simplifying the reset agent's objective to a single target.
- **Forward Task Achievability:** It is assumed that the forward task can be achieved without the agent necessarily falling into irrecoverable states, especially with the intervention of the reset agent.
- **Reset Success Notification:** The reset agent is notified upon successful reset, but receives no explicit reward function for *how* to reset.
- **Reset Trigger Robustness:** To prevent overestimation in untrained regions, an **ensemble of K networks** (`C_π_r^i(s_t, a_t)`) is used for the reset success classifier. Resets are triggered based on the ensemble average of probabilities.
- **Randomized Prior Functions (RPFs):** A technique where each network in the ensemble is a sum of a trainable part and an untrainable, randomized prior part, is used to encourage diversity within the ensemble.
- **Ensemble Minimum for Updates:** The ensemble minimum is utilized for both critic and actor updates for the reset agent. This aims to tackle overestimation, which is crucial for training a conservative reset policy and for reliable reset triggering.
- **N-step Returns:** N-step returns (n=10) are incorporated into the RCE critic update to improve stability and performance.
- **Probability-to-Time-Steps Connection:** The authors provide a justification connecting the probability of successful reset `p_π_r(e_t:∞ = 1|s_t, a_t)` to the expected time steps `T` to reach the initial state, showing that this probability approximately equals `γ^T` under certain ideal conditions. This connection provides an intuitive basis for using the probability as a trigger.
- **Hyperparameters:** Default hyperparameters are provided for network architectures, learning rates, buffer sizes, and the `p_thresh` value, which is adjusted for specific environments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents empirical evidence from both simulated and real-world continuous control tasks to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed autonomous reset learning framework.

**Simulation Results:**
The method was evaluated on six simulated tasks based on OpenAI Gym and DeepMind Control Suite, including manipulation (ball-in-cup catch/throw, peg-insertion insert/remove) and locomotion (cliff-cheetah, cliff-walker). Experiments were averaged over five random seeds.

1. **Overview of Method Performance (Figure 2):**
 - The reset agent successfully learned to return the system to initial states, leading to a reduction in manual resets over training time across most tasks.
 - The forward policy demonstrated gradual improvement in episodic return.
 - An inverse relationship between forward and reset task difficulty was observed: for instance, peg-insertion's "remove" task (easy for forward agent) was harder for the reset agent (requiring it to "insert" the peg), resulting in more manual resets compared to the "insert" task.
 - Environments without irrecoverable states (e.g., ball-in-cup, peg-insertion) generally showed the number of manual resets plateauing as the reset agent learned. In contrast, environments with physically irrecoverable states (cliff environments) showed persistent manual resets, indicating that the forward agent could sometimes still lead the system into states beyond the reset agent's recovery capability.

2. **Comparison Against Oracle (LNT) and Baseline (LNT-sparse) (Table I):**
 - The proposed method significantly **outperformed LNT-sparse**, a baseline using sparse reset rewards (0 or 1 upon success/failure), which represents a scenario where the reset agent receives minimal feedback. LNT-sparse often exhibited very low "forward share" (ratio of steps taken by the forward agent to total steps), suggesting overly pessimistic reset triggers that severely stunted forward agent learning.
 - The method performed **comparably to, and in some instances better than, LNT (oracle)**, which had full access to meticulously handcrafted, dense reset rewards. This is notable as the proposed method learns to reset in a self-supervised manner without such reward engineering. For instance, in `ball-in-cup (catch)`, our method achieved an average return of 19.51 compared to LNT's 10.38. For `peg-insertion (insert)`, the performance was similar (29.20 vs. 30.50). The competitive performance against an oracle highlights the efficacy of example-based learning for resets.

3. **Effect of Reset Trigger Threshold (`p_thresh`) (Figure 3):**
 - Varying `p_thresh` (the probability threshold for triggering a reset) demonstrated its role in balancing exploration and safety.
 - Lower `p_thresh` values allowed the forward agent to explore further from initial states, potentially leading to higher returns but also risked more manual resets, especially in environments with cliffs.
 - Higher `p_thresh` values confined the forward agent closer to initial states, limiting its exploration and sometimes stunting its episodic return, but potentially reducing manual resets by being more conservative.
 - An optimal `p_thresh` was observed for `cliff-cheetah`, indicating a sweet spot for balancing performance and safety.

4. **Implicit Curriculum for the Forward Agent (Figure 4):**
 - The reset trigger mechanism effectively imposed an implicit curriculum on the forward agent.
 - In early training stages, the forward agent was confined to states near the initial position (e.g., peg-insertion was prevented from straying far, cliff-cheetah prevented from approaching the cliff).
 - As the reset agent improved its capability to return from more diverse states, the forward agent was permitted to explore further. This demonstrated the reset agent's function as a safety layer, guiding the forward agent's learning while preventing irrecoverable states.

**Real Robot Results:**
The method was deployed on a 6DoF UR3 manipulator for two tasks: `ur3-peg` (placing a peg) and `ur3-reacher` (reaching a goal while holding a cup with a top-heavy stick without spilling, which is an irrecoverable state).

1. **Successful Real-World Deployment (Figure 5 & 6):**
 - The reset agent successfully learned to return the robot to the origin for `ur3-peg` and to its initial position without spilling the stick for `ur3-reacher`.
 - For both tasks, the number of manual resets (or irrecoverable states for `ur3-reacher`) plateaued after a period of training (160 minutes for `ur3-peg`, 80 minutes for `ur3-reacher`), indicating successful autonomous reset learning.
 - The system achieved high "train time efficiency" (85% for `ur3-peg`, 84% for `ur3-reacher`), calculated as actual training time divided by total wall time (including manual resets). This demonstrates that learning occurred continuously during both forward and reset episodes.
 - Forward episodic returns initially dipped (due to frequent reset agent interruptions) but then gradually increased as the forward agent learned to achieve its task without triggering resets.
 - The ability to learn from scratch on hardware, with the reset agent starting with zero examples of initial states, highlights the method's potential for truly autonomous robot learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a significant impediment to the widespread and autonomous application of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) in real-world robotics: the necessity for an effective and autonomous reset mechanism. The implications of this work are multi-faceted:

1. **Enhanced Autonomy in Robotic Learning:** By integrating the reset process into the learning framework and making it self-supervised, the method substantially reduces the reliance on human intervention, handcrafted scripts, or specialized mechanical fixtures for resets. This is a step towards robots that can learn and train continuously in real environments with minimal human oversight.

2. **Increased Training Efficiency and Robustness:** The ability to train a reset agent allows for continuous learning, reducing the downtime associated with manual resets. This can accelerate the learning process for complex tasks in real robots, where physical interaction and data collection are time-consuming. The preemptive reset mechanism also improves robustness by preventing the forward agent from entering physically irrecoverable states, thereby safeguarding hardware and improving overall system reliability.

3. **Generalizability Across Tasks:** The self-supervised nature of the reset agent, learning from examples of initial states rather than task-specific reward functions, makes the approach more generalizable. This reduces the engineering effort required to define reset objectives for each new task, a common bottleneck in robot learning.

4. **Implicit Curriculum Generation:** The learned reset trigger mechanism naturally generates an implicit curriculum for the forward agent. By initially confining the forward agent's exploration to states from which the reset agent can reliably recover, and gradually expanding this safe operational range as the reset agent improves, the system guides learning in a safe and progressive manner. This can facilitate learning in challenging environments where uncontrolled exploration might lead to catastrophic failures.

5. **Practical Real-World Applicability:** The successful demonstration of the method on a physical UR3 manipulator, learning complex tasks like peg-insertion and spill-free reaching from scratch on hardware, underscores its practical relevance. This moves beyond theoretical simulations to address a concrete challenge in deploying DRL to real-world robotic systems.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future advancements in autonomous robot learning. The identified challenges, such as the persistence of manual resets in environments with truly irrecoverable states or the potential to eliminate the explicit notification of a successful reset, provide clear directions for follow-up research. The concept could also be extended to multi-task settings or goal-conditioned forward agents, further broadening the scope of autonomous robot capabilities.

In summary, this research contributes to making DRL more compatible with the realities of robotic deployment by automating a critical, often overlooked, component of the learning pipeline. Its impact lies in enabling more autonomous, efficient, and robust robot learning, reducing development overhead, and bringing DRL closer to widespread adoption in real-world applications.
