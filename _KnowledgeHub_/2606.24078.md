# MinInter: Minimizing Trajectory Interpolation During Data Augmentation for Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> MinInter introduces a method for trajectory-level data augmentation in imitation learning, selecting expert demonstrations that minimize synthetic interpolations when adapted to new initial conditions. This approach reduced average interpolation in generated trajectories and improved data generation success rates by 12.8% and downstream policy success rates by 4.8% across 26 robotic manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Imitation learning requires large quantities of high-quality demonstration data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect.
- Trajectory-level data augmentation techniques that recompose expert segments often introduce synthetic, non-expert interpolated transitions to connect misaligned parts.
- These non-expert interpolations can degrade the quality of augmented data by introducing unnatural or suboptimal motions, negatively impacting learned policy performance.

## Method

- **MinInter** proposes a ==trajectory selection method== that quantifies the total interpolation needed when recomposing an expert trajectory's segments for a new initial condition.
- It uses a metric that combines positional and rotational differences between segment endpoints to calculate the interpolation amount.
- For each sampled initial state, MinInter selects the original expert trajectory that, when transformed, requires the minimal total interpolation, thereby maximizing fidelity to expert behavior.

## Results

- MinInter consistently reduced the average interpolation in generated trajectories and achieved an average gain of **+12.8%** in data generation success rates across 26 robotic manipulation task variants.
- Policies trained on MinInter-augmented data demonstrated an average **+4.8%** improvement in success rates, with gains up to **+20.7%** in challenging contact-rich and long-horizon tasks.
- The method outperformed a state-of-the-art baseline (SkillGen) on common benchmarks, achieving a higher average policy success rate improvement with a simpler, more integrable design.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly minimizing non-expert interpolations during trajectory-level data augmentation is critical for producing higher-fidelity synthetic demonstrations.
> - A simple, quantifiable metric for interpolation can effectively guide the selection of expert trajectories, leading to better augmented data without complex additional learning or planning modules.
> - Reducing non-expert segments not only enhances the quality of data for training downstream policies but also significantly increases the success rate of the data generation process itself.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning (IL) is a methodology that enables robots to acquire complex skills by observing human or expert demonstrations. While IL avoids the extensive online interaction typically required by reinforcement learning, its effectiveness is substantially contingent upon the availability of large-scale and high-quality demonstration data. The process of collecting such data can be resource-intensive and time-consuming, posing a significant limitation to the scalability and generalization capabilities of IL policies.

To address this data scarcity challenge, data augmentation (DA) has emerged as a critical technique in imitation learning. Existing data augmentation approaches can be broadly categorized into image-level and trajectory-level methods. Image-level augmentation focuses on transforming raw visual observations through techniques like rotation, cropping, color jitter, or generative models. While beneficial for improving visual robustness, these methods do not directly alter the temporal or physical structure of the actions within a trajectory.

This research specifically operates within the domain of trajectory-level data augmentation. Within this area, some methods generate novel trajectories through motion planning, kinematic retargeting, generative models, or counterfactual reasoning. These approaches can expand the training data but often involve additional modeling efforts or high computational costs. A parallel line of work, which is directly relevant to this paper, involves recomposing or stitching together segments from existing expert demonstrations. Frameworks such as MimicGen and DexMimicGen fall into this category, aiming to generate new trajectories under varied initial conditions by combining subtask segments.

A prevalent issue in these trajectory recomposition methods is the necessity of inserting synthetic transition segments, often in the form of linear interpolations, to connect misaligned parts of the transformed segments. These interpolated segments are not derived from expert demonstrations and can introduce unnatural or suboptimal motions into the synthetic trajectories. Such non-expert segments have the potential to degrade the overall quality of the augmented data, which in turn can negatively impact the performance and robustness of downstream policies trained on this data.

Previous research has explored various ways to handle these transition segments, including graph-based searches for feasible paths, model-based synthesis using learned dynamics, or recovery-targeted interventions. However, these methods often increase system complexity by requiring additional learning models, planning algorithms, or manual interventions. While approaches like CyberDemo, MimicGen, and DexMimicGen explicitly use interpolation, they do not directly address the potentially detrimental effects of these non-expert transitions on data quality.

MinInter positions itself within this context by identifying the unaddressed problem of excessive non-expert interpolation in trajectory-level data augmentation. It aims to improve the quality of synthetic data generated through recomposition by explicitly minimizing these interpolations. This approach seeks to provide a practical solution that remains compatible with existing data generation frameworks while enhancing the fidelity of augmented demonstrations without significantly increasing system complexity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent challenge in imitation learning: the dependence on large quantities of high-quality demonstration data, coupled with the high cost and labor required for its collection. Data augmentation offers a means to alleviate this dependency by synthetically expanding the dataset. However, current trajectory-level data augmentation techniques, particularly those that recombine segments from existing demonstrations, introduce a specific drawback: the necessity of interpolating between disconnected segments.

The key objectives and motivations behind the MinInter framework are as follows:

1. **Address the Degradation Caused by Non-Expert Interpolations**: Existing trajectory recomposition methods frequently insert synthetic transition segments, such as linear interpolations, to connect transformed subtask segments. These interpolations do not originate from expert demonstrations and can introduce unnatural or suboptimal motions. The core motivation is to mitigate the negative impact of these non-expert segments on the quality of generated data. The paper hypothesizes that such interpolations can degrade downstream policy performance.

2. **Develop an Effective Trajectory Selection Mechanism**: The objective is to design a method that, for a given initial configuration, can identify and select an original expert demonstration whose segments, when transformed and reconnected, require the minimal amount of interpolation. This selection process aims to ensure that the synthetically generated trajectories are as faithful as possible to expert behavior by reducing the proportion of non-expert transitions.

3. **Enhance the Quality of Synthetic Demonstrations**: By explicitly minimizing interpolations, the research aims to produce higher-quality synthetic demonstrations that more accurately reflect expert performance. This is expected to lead to more robust and generalized policies.

4. **Improve Data Generation Efficiency and Success Rates**: Large interpolations can lead to infeasible or unsuccessful composite trajectories. A key objective is to increase the success rate of the data generation process itself, meaning a higher proportion of sampled initial states will yield valid, augmentable trajectories. This reduces wasted computational effort on generating unusable data.

5. **Maintain Compatibility and Simplicity**: The method is motivated by the desire to integrate seamlessly with existing data generation frameworks (such as MimicGen) without introducing substantial additional complexity in terms of model learning, planning, or intricate policy architectures. The goal is to offer a straightforward yet impactful improvement.

6. **Boost Downstream Policy Performance**: The ultimate objective is to demonstrate that policies trained on MinInter-augmented data exhibit consistently higher success rates and improved performance across a variety of manipulation tasks, especially in challenging scenarios involving contact-rich interactions, long horizons, or high environmental variability.

In summary, MinInter is motivated by the need to refine existing trajectory-level data augmentation techniques by directly tackling the issue of non-expert interpolations, thereby producing higher-fidelity synthetic data and ultimately improving the capabilities of robots learning through imitation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MinInter proposes a trajectory selection method that minimizes the interpolation introduced during the data augmentation process. The methodology is built upon the foundational setup of recomposing expert trajectory segments under new initial conditions, as commonly employed in frameworks like MimicGen.

**Problem Formulation:**
The data generation setup begins with a set of `N` expert trajectories, `T_exp = {τ_1..., τ_N}`. Each expert trajectory `τ_i` is pre-segmented into `n` object-centric segments, `τ_j_i`, where each segment corresponds to a subtask involving a static object interaction. An end-effector pose at timestep `t` in segment `j` of source trajectory `i` is denoted `E_j_t,src`. The centric object associated with segment `j` has an initial pose `O_j_src` in the source trajectory, which is transformed to `O_j_transf` for the current subtask based on a new sampled initial state `s_0`.

To generate a new trajectory for a given `s_0`, each segment `τ_j_i` is transformed to `˜τ_j_i(s_0)` by preserving the relative pose between the end-effector and the centric object. The transformed end-effector pose `E_j_t,transf` is calculated as `E_j_t,transf = O_j_transf * (O_j_src)^-1 * E_j_t,src`. After transformation, consecutive segments `˜τ_j_i` and `˜τ_j+1_i` are often misaligned at their endpoints. The standard practice is to insert linear interpolation in both position and orientation to connect these disconnected segments, thereby forming a complete, continuous trajectory. MinInter aims to minimize these interpolated segments.

**Interpolation Metric:**
To quantify the amount of interpolation between two end-effector poses, `E_1` and `E_2`, MinInter introduces a metric `δ(E_1, E_2)`. An end-effector pose `E ∈ SE(3)` comprises a position `p ∈ R^3` and a rotation `R ∈ SO(3)`. The metric is defined as:
`δ(E_1, E_2) = (1 - w)||p_1 - p_2||_2 + w||R_1^-1 R_2 - I||_F`
Here, `||·||_2` is the Euclidean norm for positional differences, and `||·||_F` is the Frobenius norm for rotational differences (specifically, for the difference between the relative rotation and the identity matrix `I`). The weight `w ∈` balances the contributions of translational and rotational interpolation. The authors empirically set `w = 0.5` based on ablation studies indicating it optimizes downstream policy performance.

The "total interpolation" for a complete generated trajectory `τ`, composed of `n` segments, is calculated as the sum of interpolations between all consecutive segments:
`Inter(τ) = Σ_(j=0)^(n-1) δ(E_j_H_j,transf, E_j+1_1,transf)`
Where `E_j_H_j,transf` is the end pose of segment `j`, and `E_j+1_1,transf` is the start pose of segment `j+1`. For the first segment, `E_0_H_0,transf` represents the initial end-effector pose provided by the environment.

**MinInter Selection Process:**
The MinInter method integrates into the existing data generation pipeline, specifically at the trajectory selection stage, as illustrated in Figure 1 of the paper.
1. **Initial State Sampling**: A new initial object configuration `s_0` is sampled from the environment's distribution.
2. **Candidate Trajectory Generation**: For this sampled `s_0`, the system generates a candidate augmented trajectory for *each* source demonstration `τ` available in `T_exp`. This involves transforming all segments of `τ` according to `s_0` and linearly interpolating the gaps between them. It is crucial that all segments for a single generated trajectory originate from the same source demonstration; segments from different expert trajectories are not mixed.
3. **Interpolation Calculation**: The total interpolation, `Inter(Γ(τ, s_0))`, is computed for each candidate trajectory `Γ(τ, s_0)`, where `Γ(τ, s_0)` represents the trajectory formed by adapting source `τ` to `s_0`.
4. **Optimal Trajectory Selection**: MinInter then selects the source trajectory `τ*` that results in the minimal total interpolation among all candidates:
 `τ* = argmin_(τ∈T_exp) Inter(Γ(τ, s_0))`
5. **Task Success Validation**: The selected optimal trajectory `Γ(τ*, s_0)` is executed in a simulation environment. If it successfully completes the task according to predefined task-specific success criteria, it is added to the training dataset. If the trajectory fails, it is discarded, and a new initial state `s_0` is sampled, repeating the process.

This approach ensures that the augmented trajectories included in the training dataset are those that require the least amount of non-expert-demonstrated motion, thereby aiming to maximize their fidelity to true expert behavior. The method's design emphasizes compatibility, allowing its integration into existing data augmentation frameworks without requiring substantial architectural changes or additional learning modules beyond the core selection logic.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MinInter was conducted across 12 robotic manipulation tasks with 26 variants from the MimicGen benchmark, utilizing the robosuite environment with MuJoCo. Each task variant was tested under three initial state distributions (D0, D1, D2), progressively increasing in difficulty due to more challenging object placements. For each task variant, 10 expert demonstrations served as source data to generate 1,000 successful trajectories, which were then used to train BC-RNN policies with RGB observations and end-effector/gripper states. Policies were trained for 1200 epochs and evaluated over 50 rollouts per checkpoint, averaged across three random seeds.

**Interpolation Reduction:**
MinInter consistently demonstrated a reduction in the average interpolation required in the generated trajectories compared to the baseline method, as visualized in Figure 3. While slight increases in interpolation were noted for Kitchen D1 and Coffee Preparation D1, these instances were attributed to MinInter's ability to generate successful demonstrations in configurations where the baseline typically failed due to larger interpolations, indicating a trade-off for increased data generation success.

**Data Generation Success Rate:**
Table II presents the detailed data generation success rates, defined as the proportion of generated trajectories that satisfy task-specific success criteria. MinInter achieved an average gain of +12.8% over the baseline across all task variants. Substantial improvements were observed in contact-rich and long-horizon tasks, such as Hammer Cleanup D0 (+50.1%) and Nut Assembly D0 (+31.1%), where generating valid trajectories is particularly challenging. This indicates that MinInter reduces the number of failed attempts during trajectory composition, leading to a more efficient data generation process and an increased yield of high-quality demonstrations.

**Policy Success Rate:**
The policies trained on data augmented using MinInter generally outperformed those trained on baseline-generated data, as detailed in Table I and Figure 4. Out of 26 task variants, 19 showed higher success rates or already achieved 100% success. The average policy success rate improvement across all tasks was +4.8%. The most significant gain was recorded in Nut Assembly D0, with a +20.7% improvement. Tasks like Stack D0 and Threading D0, which already achieved high baseline success rates, exhibited marginal or no further improvement, indicating a ceiling effect. The observed improvements in policy success rates aligned with the reduction in interpolations, suggesting a correlation between less interpolated data and more effective learned policies.

**Comparison with State of the Art (SkillGen):**
MinInter was compared against SkillGen, a contemporary data augmentation method, on a subset of tasks reported in SkillGen's original paper (Table I). On this common benchmark, SkillGen showed an average improvement of +3.8% over the baseline. In contrast, MinInter achieved a higher average improvement of +4.9% on the same tasks, setting a new state-of-the-art performance within this comparison. The paper also highlights MinInter's conceptual simplicity and ease of integration compared to SkillGen, which requires more complex fine-grained segmentation and separate skill/motion policies.

**Performance Across Task Difficulties:**
Further analysis of MinInter's performance across different task difficulties revealed specific trends (Figure 5):
- **Initial Distributions:** The policy success rate improvements of MinInter over the baseline were more pronounced in more challenging initial state distributions (D1 and D2), compared to the default D0 distribution.
- **Task Groups:** MinInter yielded larger gains in contact-rich and long-horizon tasks, which typically demand more precise and sequential manipulation, compared to basic manipulation tasks.

These findings collectively suggest that MinInter is particularly effective in complex and challenging scenarios where policies must learn from diverse and intricate data, and where the fidelity of augmented demonstrations plays a crucial role.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "MinInter: Minimizing Trajectory Interpolation During Data Augmentation for Imitation Learning" offers several significant contributions and has the potential for considerable impact on the field of robotic imitation learning.

**Identification of a Critical Problem:** The work effectively highlights a previously understated issue in trajectory-level data augmentation: the degradation of synthetic demonstration quality due to the insertion of non-expert, interpolated transition segments. By explicitly identifying these interpolations as a source of "unnatural motions," the paper draws attention to a fundamental limitation of common data augmentation strategies.

**Simple yet Effective Solution:** MinInter proposes a straightforward and computationally efficient solution to this problem. Instead of requiring complex model learning, planning, or additional policy architectures, MinInter's core innovation lies in a strategic trajectory selection mechanism based on a quantifiable interpolation metric. This simplicity makes the method highly practical and easy to integrate into existing data generation pipelines (such as MimicGen), without significantly increasing system complexity or computational overhead.

**Enhanced Data Quality and Learning Performance:** The experimental results consistently demonstrate that minimizing interpolations leads to higher-quality synthetic demonstrations. This directly translates into improved performance for downstream imitation learning policies, as evidenced by the gains in policy success rates across a wide range of tasks and variants. The largest improvements in challenging, contact-rich, and long-horizon tasks underscore the method's value in scenarios where precise and reliable execution is critical.

**Improved Data Generation Efficiency:** Beyond improving policy performance, MinInter also significantly boosts the data generation success rate. By selecting source demonstrations that intrinsically require less interpolation, the method reduces the number of failed attempts during trajectory composition. This efficiency gain means that a higher proportion of sampled initial conditions result in valid, usable synthetic trajectories, optimizing the overall data collection process.

**Advancement in State-of-the-Art:** MinInter's ability to outperform a recent state-of-the-art method like SkillGen, particularly with a simpler design, suggests that the principle of interpolation minimization holds substantial merit. This demonstrates that focusing on the intrinsic quality of generated data, rather than solely increasing diversity or complexity, can lead to superior learning outcomes.

**Broad Applicability and Future Directions:** The general principle of "interpolation minimization" is not restricted to the specific framework used in this paper. The authors suggest that this approach could be incorporated into other data augmentation frameworks, indicating its potential to serve as a fundamental guiding principle for enhancing data quality across various imitation learning applications. This could lead to more robust, generalizable, and efficient robot learning systems in diverse real-world scenarios.

In summary, MinInter provides both a deeper understanding of limitations in current data augmentation and a practical, effective method to overcome them. Its impact is likely to be seen in the development of more reliable and performant robotic skills learned through imitation, particularly as these systems are deployed in increasingly complex and unconstrained environments.
