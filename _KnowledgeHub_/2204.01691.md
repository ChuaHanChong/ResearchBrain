# Do As I Can, Not As I Say: Grounding Language in Robotic Affordances

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from Robotics at Google and Everyday Robots introduces SayCan, a framework that enables robots to follow complex natural language instructions by combining the high-level semantic understanding of Large Language Models (LLMs) with learned robotic affordance functions. The system achieves 74% execution success on 101 diverse real-world tasks in a mock kitchen and demonstrates that robot performance scales directly with the size of the underlying LLM.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) lack grounding in the physical world, making them prone to generating physically impossible or nonsensical actions when applied directly to robotic control.
- Traditional robot learning and control methods struggle to scale to high-level, abstract, and long-horizon natural language instructions, often requiring meticulous programming or extensive task-specific training.
- Bridging the gap between the semantic knowledge of LLMs and the physical capabilities of robots for real-world task execution remained a significant challenge.

## Method

- The **SayCan** framework utilizes an ==LLM== to score the likelihood of predefined low-level skills (e.g., "pick up the sponge") being a valid next step towards a high-level user instruction (the "Say" component).
- Learned robotic ==value functions== act as ==affordance functions== that quantify the probability of successfully executing each low-level skill from the robot's current state (the "Can" component).
- The system selects the next action by ==multiplying the LLM's semantic likelihood for a skill with its physical feasibility== from the affordance function, iteratively choosing the most probable and feasible skill until the task is complete.

## Results

- SayCan achieved an **84%** planning success rate and **74%** execution success rate in a mock kitchen, with **81%** planning and **60%** execution success in a real office kitchen across **101** diverse tasks.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that both the LLM's semantic understanding and the robotic affordance functions are critical; removing either component severely degraded performance (e.g., **0%** success without LLM's high-level planning, significantly lower without affordance grounding).
- Robot performance improved with larger LLMs (e.g., **PaLM 540B** achieved higher success than smaller PaLM models or FLAN), showing a direct correlation between LLM capability and robot task completion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining a Large Language Model's (LLM) high-level semantic understanding ("Say") with learned robotic affordance functions ("Can") enables robots to execute complex, multi-step natural language instructions.
> - Robot performance directly correlates with the size and capability of the underlying LLM, indicating a scalable path for embodied AI where advancements in LLMs translate to more capable robots.
> - The modular SayCan framework provides interpretability by verbalizing the LLM's planned steps and allows for zero-shot generalization to novel tasks without explicit task-specific training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: Large Language Models (LLMs), embodied AI, robot learning, and natural language processing (NLP).

**Large Language Models (LLMs):** Recent advancements in LLMs, such as GPT-3, PaLM, and FLAN, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generating coherent text, answering complex questions, and even performing reasoning tasks based on the vast text corpora they are trained on. This has led to a critical question: how can this semantic knowledge be leveraged by physical agents like robots? The fundamental challenge, as the paper highlights, is that LLMs are "not grounded in the physical world." They lack real-world experience and do not observe the consequences of their linguistic outputs on physical processes, leading to responses that may be nonsensical or unsafe for an embodied agent.

**Embodied AI and Robot Learning:** A long-standing goal in robotics is to enable robots to understand and execute high-level human instructions. Traditional robot control often relies on meticulously programmed or learned low-level policies for specific tasks. While significant progress has been made in learning language-conditioned behaviors (e.g., via imitation learning or reinforcement learning) for simple primitives like pick-and-place, scaling these methods to complex, long-horizon, and abstract tasks expressed in natural language remains a significant hurdle. Existing methods for language grounding in robotics often involve training end-to-end models that map language to actions, or using language embeddings to condition policies. However, these rarely leverage the rich, general semantic knowledge embedded in very large, pre-trained LLMs.

**Task and Motion Planning:** This field traditionally deals with sequencing abstract tasks and ensuring their feasibility given physical constraints. Classical approaches rely on symbolic planning and explicit models, which can be brittle or difficult to scale to complex, dynamic environments. More recent machine learning-based approaches aim to generalize primitives and relax constraints, but few directly integrate the high-level common-sense reasoning abilities of LLMs with real-time physical feasibility checks.

SayCan addresses the critical gap between the high-level semantic understanding of LLMs and the low-level physical capabilities of robots. While concurrent work (e.g., Huang et al., 2022) explored using LLMs as zero-shot planners through prompt engineering, they lacked a mechanism for grounding these plans in the robot's actual capabilities and the current environmental state. SayCan's novel contribution lies in its explicit integration of LLM-derived task likelihoods with learned "affordance functions" (value functions) that quantify the physical feasibility of skills. This "Do As I Can, Not As I Say" paradigm allows the robot to leverage LLM intelligence while ensuring its actions are practical and contextually appropriate, moving beyond mere linguistic competence towards embodied common sense.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the SayCan research can be summarized as follows:

1. **Grounding Large Language Models in the Real World:** The central motivation is to overcome the inherent "ungroundedness" of LLMs when applied to embodied agents. While LLMs excel at semantic understanding and generating plausible narratives, they lack real-world physical experience. This makes their direct application to robotic decision-making problematic, as they might propose actions that are physically impossible, unsafe, or nonsensical for a given robot or environment (e.g., suggesting a robot use a vacuum cleaner when none is present or it lacks the ability).
2. **Enabling Robots to Follow High-Level, Abstract Natural Language Instructions:** The goal is to allow users to command robots using everyday, complex, and temporally extended natural language instructions (e.g., "I spilled my drink, can you help?"). This moves beyond simple, primitive commands to more abstract, goal-oriented tasks that require multi-step planning and contextual understanding.
3. **Leveraging LLM Semantic Knowledge for Robotic Decision-Making:** The authors aim to harness the vast semantic knowledge encoded within LLMs (e.g., common sense about tasks, objects, and procedures) to inform high-level task planning for robots. This would allow robots to infer appropriate sequences of actions for abstract goals without explicit, hand-coded task graphs.
4. **Ensuring Feasibility and Contextual Appropriateness of Actions:** To counteract the LLM's lack of physical grounding, the system must constrain the proposed natural language actions to be both feasible for the robot's capabilities ("Can") and contextually appropriate given the current environment state.
5. **Achieving Zero-Shot Generalization:** A key aspiration is for the system to perform complex, long-horizon tasks in a zero-shot fashion, meaning without requiring additional training specifically for each new high-level instruction. This emphasizes the generalization capabilities derived from the pre-trained LLMs and learned skills.
6. **Providing Interpretability:** The proposed method aims to generate an interpretable sequence of steps (a plan expressed in language) that the robot intends to execute, fostering user trust and enabling easier debugging.

In essence, the motivation is to create a symbiotic relationship: the LLM provides the high-level "brain" for semantic understanding and procedural knowledge ("Say"), while the robot's learned skills and their associated value functions provide the "hands and eyes" for physical interaction and feasibility assessment ("Can").

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of SayCan is a novel framework that combines the high-level semantic reasoning of Large Language Models (LLMs) with the real-world grounding provided by learned robotic affordance functions. The method's name, "SayCan," directly reflects this duality: what the LLM "says" is useful for a task, and what the robot "can" physically do.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system receives a natural language instruction `i` from a user. The robot is equipped with a predefined set of low-level skills `Π`, each associated with:
- A short natural language description `l_π` (e.g., "pick up the sponge," "go to the table").
- An affordance function `p(c_π|s, l_π)`, which quantifies the probability of successfully completing skill `π` with description `l_π` from the current state `s`. This is essentially an answer to "if I ask the robot to do `l_π`, will it do it?"

The objective is to select a sequence of skills that successfully completes the high-level instruction `i`.

#### 3.2. Connecting Large Language Models to Robots

- **LLM for Task Grounding ("Say"):** The LLM's role is to assess the likelihood that a given skill's language description (`l_π`) is a valid next step toward completing the user's instruction (`i`). This is expressed as `p(l_π|i)`. Instead of relying on generative outputs (which can be ungrounded or non-actionable), SayCan uses the LLM in a "scoring" mode. Given a prompt (including the user instruction and examples of desired response structure), the LLM outputs a probability for each candidate skill `l_π` from the predefined set. This constrains the LLM's output to only the robot's available primitives. The planning process is structured as a dialogue, where the LLM iteratively suggests the next step, appending the chosen skill to the instruction context for subsequent steps until a "done" token is selected. Prompt engineering is crucial here to guide the LLM's output format and content.
- **Value Functions for World Grounding ("Can"):** To address the LLM's lack of physical grounding, SayCan incorporates "affordance functions," which are value functions learned through reinforcement learning (RL). In an undiscounted, sparse reward setting (reward 1 for success, 0 for failure), a value function `Q_π(s,a)` or `V_π(s)` quantifies the probability of successfully completing a task (skill `π`) from a given state `s`. These value functions provide `p(c_π|s, l_π)`, indicating what actions are physically possible and likely to succeed in the current environment. This makes the LLM "aware of the current scene."

#### 3.3. Skill Selection and Execution (SayCan Algorithm)

The core idea is to combine the LLM's semantic judgment with the robot's physical reality. For each available skill `π`, SayCan computes a combined probability:
`p(c_i | i, s, l_π) ∝ p(c_π | s, l_π) * p(l_π | i)`

This product represents the probability that a given skill `π` will both successfully execute AND make progress toward the high-level instruction `i`.
The algorithm iteratively selects the skill `π_n` with the maximum combined probability:
`π_n = arg max_{π∈Π} [p(c_π | s_n, l_π) * p(l_π | i, l_{π_{n-1}}, ..., l_{π_0})]`

Once a skill is selected, its corresponding low-level policy is executed in the environment, updating the state `s_n+1`. The selected skill's description (`l_π_n`) is then appended to the historical context provided to the LLM for the next iteration, enabling sequential planning. This process continues until the LLM selects a termination token ("done").

#### 3.4. Implementing SayCan in a Robotic System

- **Language-Conditioned Robotic Control Policies:** SayCan requires a set of pre-trained low-level skills. These skills (e.g., picking, placing, navigating) are "language-conditioned," meaning their policies and value functions take a language description as input. This allows a single multi-task model to handle variations of a skill (e.g., "pick up the coke can," "pick up the apple").
- **Training Low-Level Skills:**
 - **Behavioral Cloning (BC-Z):** Some skills are trained via imitation learning from a large dataset of teleoperated demonstrations (68,000 demos, expanded with autonomous episodes). A pre-trained Universal Sentence Encoder (USE) provides language embeddings for conditioning a Resnet-18-based policy network.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (MT-Opt):** Value functions (affordance models) are primarily trained using TD-based reinforcement learning with MT-Opt, a multi-task RL algorithm, in a simulated environment. Sim-to-real transfer techniques (RetinaGAN) are used to bridge the reality gap. Sparse rewards (1 for success, 0 for failure) are used, making the learned value functions directly correspond to affordances.
- **Robotic System and Skills:** The experiments use a mobile manipulator robot from Everyday Robots in an office kitchen environment. A diverse set of 551 skills are defined across seven families (e.g., picking, placing, navigation) involving 17 objects. For practicality, a subset of these skills most amenable to composition are utilized. Specific calibration and heuristic adjustments are applied to the learned value functions (e.g., for "pick" skills) and for certain deterministic skills (e.g., "place" is assumed always possible, "go to" uses distance heuristics).

This modular architecture allows SayCan to leverage powerful, separately developed components (LLMs and robot skills) while providing a robust mechanism for their interaction in physically grounded scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

SayCan was rigorously evaluated on 101 diverse real-world robotic tasks, encompassing varying time horizons, language complexities, and embodiment challenges, performed on a mobile manipulator in both a mock kitchen and a real office kitchen.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

- **Mock Kitchen:** SayCan achieved an **84% planning success rate** (correct sequence of skills chosen by the model) and a **74% execution success rate** (robot successfully completes the task).
- **Real Kitchen:** Performance saw a slight reduction, with **81% planning success** and **60% execution success**. This indicates reasonable generalization to a less controlled, real-world setting, though also highlights the remaining challenges in robust robot execution.

#### 4.2. Performance Across Instruction Families

- **NL Single Primitive, NL Verbs, Structured Language:** Generally high planning success rates (93-100%), indicating the LLM effectively parses explicit or synonymous commands into appropriate skills.
- **NL Nouns:** Performed worse (67% planning success), attributed to the larger number of possible nouns (15 objects, 5 locations) making semantic disambiguation harder for the LLM.
- **Embodiment Tasks:** Achieved 64% planning success. Failures often stemmed from affordance function misclassifications, indicating challenges in precisely understanding the current state.
- **Crowd-Sourced Queries:** Performed on par with other families (87% planning success), demonstrating SayCan's ability to handle natural, unstructured user requests.
- **Long-Horizon Tasks:** The most challenging, with 73% planning success. The primary failure mode was premature termination by the LLM (e.g., bringing only one of two requested items). SayCan also struggled with negation (e.g., "bring me a snack that isn't an apple") and ambiguous references, an inherited limitation from underlying LLMs.

**Error Analysis:** Overall, 65% of failures were attributed to LLM errors (e.g., incorrect planning logic, early termination) and 35% to affordance errors (e.g., skill reported as feasible but failed, or infeasible when possible).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Ablating Language (No LLM):**
 - `BC NL` (feeding full instruction directly to policy): Achieved **0% success** across all tasks, demonstrating that low-level language-conditioned policies alone cannot handle high-level, multi-step instructions.
 - `BC USE` (projecting instruction to nearest known skill via Universal Sentence Encoder): Achieved 60% for single primitives but **0% for all other complex tasks**, highlighting the critical role of the LLM's semantic knowledge and planning capabilities beyond simple mapping.
 These results underscore the absolute necessity of the LLM for high-level task understanding and decomposition.

- **Ablating Value Functions (No Affordance Grounding):**
 - `No VF` (choosing skill based solely on LLM score): 67% planning success.
 - `Generative` (using LLM's generative output, then projecting to nearest skill): 74% planning success.
 Both methods performed significantly worse than full SayCan (84% planning success). This clearly demonstrates that while LLMs can propose plausible sequences, they critically lack the physical grounding to ensure feasibility and context-appropriateness. The value functions are indispensable for connecting the linguistic plan to the robot's embodied reality.

- **Ablating the Language Model (LLM Size):**
 - SayCan was tested with different PaLM models (8B, 62B, 540B parameters) and FLAN (137B).
 - **PaLM 540B (SayCan)** achieved 84% planning and 74% execution success.
 - **FLAN-SayCan** achieved 70% planning and 61% execution success.
 This is a highly significant finding: **robot performance improved with larger, more capable underlying language models.** This establishes a direct correlation between advancements in LLMs and improvements in robot capabilities, suggesting a scalable path forward for embodied AI.

#### 4.4. Case Studies of New Capabilities

- **Adding Skills (Drawer Manipulation):** SayCan readily integrates new skills (open/close drawer, put/take object from drawer) by simply adding their descriptions as options for the LLM and providing associated value functions/heuristics. This showed 100% planning success for drawer tasks, though execution was limited by current policy robustness (33%).
- **Chain-of-Thought Reasoning:** By incorporating Chain-of-Thought prompting, SayCan demonstrated improved reasoning for complex tasks, including handling negation (e.g., "bring a fruit-flavored drink without caffeine") and multi-step conditional logic. The LLM generates an explicit "Explanation" step, making its reasoning transparent.
- **Multilingual Queries:** Due to the multilingual training of PaLM, SayCan could handle queries in Chinese, French, and Spanish with almost no performance drop, demonstrating an inherent and powerful generalization capability.

The results strongly validate SayCan's core hypothesis: combining the high-level semantic reasoning of LLMs with learned, real-world robotic affordances is crucial for robustly executing complex, long-horizon instructions in physical environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SayCan framework represents a significant step forward in the field of embodied AI and human-robot interaction, offering several profound implications:

1. **Bridging the Language-Reality Gap:** SayCan effectively addresses one of the most critical challenges in applying large language models to robotics: grounding abstract linguistic knowledge in the concrete realities of a physical robot and its environment. By explicitly combining the "Say" (LLM's semantic understanding) with the "Can" (robot's physical affordances), it enables robots to leverage vast textual knowledge while ensuring actions are feasible and contextually appropriate.
2. **Enabling Complex, Long-Horizon Tasks:** The ability to execute multi-step, abstract, and temporally extended natural language instructions (e.g., cleaning up a spill, preparing a snack) in a real kitchen environment is a major achievement. This moves beyond simple pick-and-place tasks towards truly helpful, general-purpose robotic assistants.
3. **Scalability with LLM Advancements:** A groundbreaking finding is the direct correlation between the performance of the underlying LLM and the robot's success rate. This suggests a powerful symbiotic relationship where improvements in large language models can directly translate into more capable robots, potentially accelerating progress in embodied AI. This "co-improvement" paradigm is highly impactful for future research directions.
4. **Enhanced Interpretability and Trust:** SayCan's explicit planning process, where the LLM's proposed steps are verbalized, offers a high degree of interpretability. Users can understand *why* the robot is choosing certain actions, fostering trust and enabling more effective human oversight and debugging of autonomous systems.
5. **Zero-Shot Generalization:** The system's ability to handle novel, complex instructions in a zero-shot manner (without specific task training) demonstrates strong generalization. This is crucial for deploying robots in dynamic, unpredictable real-world environments where every possible instruction cannot be pre-programmed or demonstrated.
6. **Integration of Diverse Capabilities:** SayCan's modular design allows for the flexible integration of various low-level skills, learned through different methods (BC, RL) and calibrated via heuristics. This makes the system extensible and adaptable as new robotic capabilities emerge.
7. **New Avenues for LLM Improvement:** The authors highlight a compelling future research direction: using real-world robotic experience and feedback to improve LLMs themselves. This could lead to LLMs with a more robust common-sense understanding of physics and causality, going beyond purely text-based learning.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Inherited LLM Biases:** SayCan will inherit biases and limitations present in the LLMs it uses, including issues with negation or ambiguous references, and dependence on training data.
- **Bottleneck of Underlying Skills:** The system's capabilities are ultimately bounded by the range and robustness of the pre-trained low-level skills. Failures often stem from the robot's inability to execute a planned skill perfectly, despite the LLM generating a correct sequence. Future work must focus on expanding the skill repertoire and improving their robustness.
- **Reactive Planning:** The current system does not easily react to failures of individual skills or unexpected environmental changes. While follow-up work like "Inner Monologue" (Huang et al., 2022) builds on SayCan to address closed-loop planning with environmental feedback, this remains a challenge for the base SayCan system.

Despite these limitations, SayCan provides a robust, interpretable, and scalable framework for integrating linguistic intelligence with embodied action, setting a strong foundation for the next generation of truly intelligent and helpful robots.
