# JEPA-WAM: Stage-Level Joint-Embedding Prediction for World-Action Models in Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> JEPA-WAM adds a stage-level semantic future to a Motus-based world-action model. A frozen V-JEPA2 encoder plus a goal-conditioned JEPA predictor estimates the latent state of the next task stage, injected via a bounded scalar gate into the WAM's video tokens to steer short-horizon video and action generation. Across 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks it reaches 90.25% overall success and cuts successful-rollout length by 5.97%.

## Problem

- Vision-language-action policies map observations directly to actions, leaving future environment states and task progress implicitly encoded, so they cannot reason explicitly about what state comes next.
- World-action models predict only a dense short-horizon video-action chunk, capturing local motion and contact but leaving the next task-relevant object-state or relation change implicit.
- Hierarchical subgoal methods rely on explicit pixel-level visual subgoals or reference trajectories fed to a separate low-level controller, emphasizing appearance over task completion.

## Method

- **Stage-pair construction**: frozen ==V-JEPA2== clip and token representation changes score transitions, ==temporal NMS== keeps at most five keyframes, pairing every frame densely with its next stage boundary.
- **Stage-JEPA**: a frozen ==V-JEPA2== encoder plus trainable goal-conditioned predictor and ==instruction cross-attention== adapter, trained with a ==cosine alignment loss== between predicted and target next-stage latents.
- **Gated conditioning interface**: mean-pooled stage tokens pass an ==MLP== and a bounded ==global scalar gate== for additive injection into WAM video tokens, with magnitude-ratio and gate regularizers.

## Results

- On **50** RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, JEPA-WAM scores **91.42%** clean and **89.08%** randomized success, beating Motus (**88.66%**/**87.02%**), X-VLA (**72.88%**), and GO-1 (**37.86%**).
- Ablations: removing the JEPA branch drops overall success from **90.25%** to **87.23%**; blocking latent injection gives **89.89%**; skipping Stage-I training gives **89.98%**.
- Mean episode length falls from **156.28** to **132.80** steps overall (**15.02%**) and **79.57** to **74.82** on successes (**5.97%**); real-robot progress score **48.00** vs **41.50**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating a semantic stage-level future from the short-term physical future gives complementary supervision; latent targets sidestep pixel prediction's bias toward appearance over task completion.
> - Gains concentrate on tasks with explicit relational progress like handover and packing, and shrink where success hinges on grasp geometry or precise contact timing.
> - Pretrained latent predictors already carry much of the useful structure: task-specific stage training added little, while the conditioning pathway itself mattered most.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of generalist robot policies, which aim to enable robots to perform diverse tasks in varied environments by mapping multimodal observations and linguistic instructions to actions.

Current approaches in this field broadly fall into two categories:
- **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models**: These models directly map observations and task instructions to executable actions. While they leverage semantic representations from pre-trained vision-language models, they often do not explicitly model how the environment should change as a task progresses. The future task states and environmental evolution are implicitly encoded within the policy. Examples include RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, and π0.5.
- **World Action Models (WAMs)**: WAMs address the limitations of VLAs by jointly predicting future visual observations and robot actions. This provides dense supervision for object motion, contact, and local scene evolution. However, existing WAMs typically represent the future as a short, dense temporal chunk, focusing on immediate video-action trajectories. This short-term prediction can capture local dynamics but often leaves the "next task-relevant object-state or relation change" implicit, meaning they do not explicitly model the stage-level progress of a task. Examples include UVA, UWM, and Motus.

Hierarchical and stage-guided manipulation approaches have been explored to address long-horizon tasks by incorporating explicit intermediate goals, visual subgoals, or structured supervision. These methods often guide a separate low-level controller or use explicit visual targets.

The work presented in this paper, JEPA-WAM, positions itself by identifying a gap between the short-term future modeling of WAMs and the need for anticipating "stage-level semantic future" for task progress. It aims to bridge this gap by integrating Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs), which predict latent representations of target states rather than pixel-level details, into the WAM framework. This approach seeks to provide explicit anticipatory guidance about the next meaningful task stage, complementing the WAM's ability to model local scene dynamics and actions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enhance generalist robot policies by explicitly modeling two complementary temporal scales of the future: a "short-term physical future" for immediate video-action evolution, and a "stage-level semantic future" for task progress.

The motivation stems from the limitations observed in existing robot policy frameworks:
- **Implicit Task Progress in VLAs**: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models convert observations and language instructions directly into actions. While effective for many tasks, this direct mapping does not explicitly represent how the environment should evolve or what state the task should reach next. This implicit encoding can limit explicit reasoning about task progress, making it challenging for robots to understand and execute complex, multi-stage manipulation tasks.
- **Short-Term Focus of WAMs**: World Action Models (WAMs) predict both future visual observations and robot actions, offering a more explicit model of environmental dynamics than VLAs. However, the future is typically represented as a fixed, short video-action chunk. While sufficient for local scene evolution, contact, and object motion, this short-term horizon does not explicitly capture the "stage-level future"—i.e., how a task should progress from its current state to a distinct, meaningful next stage (e.g., specific object-state changes or relation changes like alignment or interaction). Predicting a plausible video-action trajectory does not necessarily imply an understanding of the next task milestone.
- **Need for Semantic Task Guidance**: Directly predicting distant target images for stage-level guidance is challenging because pixel-level reconstruction can overemphasize appearance details rather than the semantic completion of a task. The authors argue for a mechanism that can encode task-relevant object-state and relation changes without committing to pixel-level specifics or exact transition durations.

To address these motivations, the authors propose distinguishing between two types of future:
1. **Short-term physical future**: Describes immediate video-action evolution, essential for executing local actions and understanding immediate scene changes.
2. **Stage-level semantic future**: Specifies the next object or relation state to reach, guiding the overall task progression.

The key objective is to integrate this stage-level semantic future into a WAM framework. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) are identified as a suitable mechanism for modeling this semantic future, as they predict latent representations of target states, preserving semantic and physical structure while being less sensitive to low-level appearance details. The proposed JEPA-WAM aims to couple a JEPA predictor, which estimates the latent representation of the next task stage, with a WAM that models the local scene and action dynamics required to achieve that stage. The predicted latent representation serves as an internal progress target that conditions short-term world and action generation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

JEPA-WAM employs a two-stage training process and a receding-horizon closed-loop inference mechanism.

**Overall Architecture:**
JEPA-WAM augments a Motus-based World Action Model (WAM) with Stage-JEPA, a goal-conditioned Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) predictor. Stage-JEPA takes the current observation and task instruction as input, uses a frozen V-JEPA2 encoder to extract the current-state representation, and predicts the latent target of the next inferred stage. This predicted stage representation is then injected into the WAM as a denoising condition to guide both video prediction and action generation.

**Training Stages:**

- **Stage I: Training the JEPA Predictor (Stage-JEPA)**
 - **Objective**: To learn a goal-conditioned JEPA predictor that infers the next-stage future. This stage focuses on representation-space supervision to capture semantic changes.
 - **Stage-Pair Construction**: Robot trajectories are processed to automatically detect key stages without manual labels.
 1. Candidate centers are sampled every eight frames within an episode.
 2. A 64-frame clip centered at each candidate is encoded using a frozen V-JEPA2 encoder, yielding clip-level (mean-pooled) and token-level representations.
 3. A "transition score" is calculated for each candidate, measuring the maximum Euclidean distance between representations in a local window (e.g., between `j-2` and `j+2` positions) at both clip and token levels. Scores are min-max normalized per episode.
 4. Temporal Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) is applied to these scores, retaining at most K=5 transition keyframes.
 5. These keyframes, along with the initial and terminal frames, define an ordered sequence of stage boundaries.
 6. For every stage interval `[b_i, b_i+1)`, each current frame `t` is paired with its next boundary `b_i+1`. This creates dense `(current_frame, next_boundary)` pairs for supervision.
 - **Goal-Conditioned Stage Prediction**:
 1. The future prediction model `f_θ` is realized as Stage-JEPA, consisting of a frozen V-JEPA2 encoder and a trainable goal-conditioned predictor (initialized from a V-JEPA2 predictor) and an instruction adapter.
 2. For a given dense pair `(t, b_i+1)`, a 64-frame current observation slice `S_t` (centered at `t`) and a 64-frame target observation slice `S_target_t` (centered at `b_i+1`) are formed.
 3. The frozen encoder `E` independently encodes `S_t` into `z_t` and `S_target_t` into `z_target_t`.
 4. The predictor, conditioned on `S_t` and the task instruction `l`, produces token-level next-stage predictions, which are pooled and normalized as `bz_target_t`.
 5. The `L_stage` loss, a cosine similarity loss, is optimized to align `bz_target_t` with `z_target_t` in representation space. Only the predictor and instruction adapter are trained.

- **Stage II: Training the Stage-Conditioned WAM**
 - **Objective**: To train a WAM to generate short-horizon visual future and action chunks, conditioned on the predicted next-stage latent from the frozen Stage-JEPA.
 - **WAM Instantiation**: The WAM is based on Motus, a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture. Motus comprises:
 - A video-generation expert (initialized from Wan2.2) for local visual evolution.
 - An action expert for robot state and dense action chunk prediction.
 - A vision-language understanding expert (with a frozen Qwen3-VL encoder) for instruction and observation-dependent features.
 - These experts interact through Tri-modal Joint Attention at each transformer layer, while maintaining modality-specific functions.
 - **JEPA-WAM Augmentation (Conditioning Interface)**:
 1. The Stage-JEPA branch (now frozen) generates the predicted next-stage latent `bz_target_t`.
 2. This `bz_target_t` is mean-pooled and passed through an MLP `P_ψ` to match the dimension of the WAM's video tokens.
 3. The resulting condition vector `h_t` is additively injected into the WAM's noisy video tokens `X_v_t` using a global scalar gate `α = 0.2σ(β)` (where `β` is a trainable parameter). This `h_t` is broadcast to all video tokens.
 - **Loss Function**: The WAM's native visual and action objectives (`L_video`, `L_action`) are used. Optionally, regularization terms `L_reg` are added for the conditioning interface, including a term to bias the gate `α` towards a small residual update and another to keep the relative magnitude of the injected feature `ρ` close to a target `ρ0`.

**Closed-Loop Inference:**
During deployment, the policy maintains a causal 64-frame observation buffer. At each query:
1. The frozen Stage-JEPA (`f_θ`) predicts the next-stage latent `bz_target_t` from the current observation buffer and task instruction.
2. The WAM (`W_ϕ`) then predicts the short-term visual future `bV_short_t` and action chunk `bA_t`, conditioned on the current observation, robot state, instruction, and the predicted stage latent `bz_target_t`.
3. The environment executes the action chunk, updates the observation buffer, and the cycle repeats. Stage boundaries and target observation slices are only used for training supervision and are not available during inference.

**Implementation Details:**
Experiments used NVIDIA A800 GPUs. Stage-JEPA utilized a facebook/vjepa2-vitl-fpc64-256 model, with its encoder frozen (FP16) and the predictor/adapter trained in FP32 for 1,000 steps. The Motus policy was trained in BF16 for four epochs, with specific learning rates and weight decays for each stage. The conditioning interface used a global scalar gate initialized to 0.02, with specific regularizer weights.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of JEPA-WAM was conducted on 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, covering diverse manipulation categories in both clean and randomized environments.

**Overall Performance on RoboTwin 2.0:**
- JEPA-WAM achieved an overall success rate of **91.42%** in clean environments and **89.08%** in randomized environments.
- This represents an improvement over the strongest baseline, Motus, which achieved 88.66% in clean and 87.02% in randomized settings.

**Comparison with Baselines (Table 1):**
JEPA-WAM consistently outperformed other baselines (GO-1, π0.5, X-VLA, and Motus) across most semantic manipulation categories:
- **Categories with significant improvement over Motus**: Handover, Targeted placement, Container packing, Arrangement & stacking, and Tool use & dynamic manipulation. For example, in Handover tasks, JEPA-WAM achieved 96.50% (clean) and 93.50% (randomized), compared to Motus's 82.00% and 68.00%.
- **Categories with smaller or no improvement**: Acquisition & lifting (JEPA-WAM slightly lower than Motus in clean, slightly lower in randomized), and Articulated/device interaction (JEPA-WAM slightly lower than Motus in clean, slightly higher in randomized).
- The authors attribute the strong results in categories like Handover and Container Packing to tasks whose execution heavily relies on explicit changes in task progress, which directly aligns with JEPA-WAM's two-scale design. Tasks like Acquisition & lifting, primarily depending on immediate grasp geometry, showed less complementary stage structure.

**Ablation Study (Table 2):**
An ablation study was conducted to determine the contribution of key components:
- **Removing JEPA entirely ("w/o JEPA")**: This resulted in the largest performance degradation (87.23% overall success compared to JEPA-WAM's 90.25%), indicating the significant value of the stage-conditioning pathway.
- **Removing future-latent injection ("future guidance on current-frame tokens only")**: This also lowered success (89.89%), suggesting that injecting the predicted next-stage latent into the WAM video tokens is more effective than using it only to modify the current observation.
- **Removing Stage-I training ("w/o Stage-I training")**: The model remained competitive (89.98%), implying that the pre-trained V-JEPA2 predictor provides a useful generic visual structure. However, specific training on current-to-next-stage pairs still yielded the best overall results (90.25%).
- **Removing gate regularizers ("w/o gate regularizers")**: A smaller performance drop (89.88%) was observed, indicating that while helpful, the regularization terms primarily refine the bounded gate's behavior rather than being foundational.

**Trajectory Analysis (Qualitative - Figure 3):**
Qualitative comparison showed that JEPA-WAM often reaches success with fewer actions and steps compared to Motus. In a representative case study, JEPA-WAM quickly progressed to the next task-relevant state, while Motus exhibited repeated local corrections and oscillations of the manipulated object before task completion. This suggests that the predicted stage latent guides local actions towards task progress and reduces redundant motions.

**Execution Efficiency (Figure 4):**
- JEPA-WAM reduced the mean episode length across all rollouts from 156.28 to 132.80 steps (15.02% reduction) compared to Motus.
- For successful rollouts only, JEPA-WAM reduced the mean length from 79.57 to 74.82 steps, a 5.97% reduction, indicating more direct progression towards task targets.

**Real-World Evaluation (Supplementary - Tables 3, 4, Figures 5-7):**
On an in-house dual-arm LIFT2 platform for two tasks (Cube_Grasp and Power_Strip), JEPA-WAM achieved higher average task-progress scores compared to π0.5. For Cube_Grasp, JEPA-WAM scored 45.00 vs. π0.5's 33.50. For Power_Strip, JEPA-WAM scored 51.00 vs. π0.5's 49.50. This provided evidence of the approach's effectiveness beyond simulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of JEPA-WAM contributes to the field of robot manipulation by formalizing and addressing the distinction between short-term physical future and stage-level semantic future. This dual-scale approach to future modeling represents a conceptual advancement in how robot policies can anticipate and act upon task progression.

**Significance:**
- **Explicit Task Progress Modeling**: JEPA-WAM demonstrates a method for policies to explicitly reason about meaningful task stages rather than relying solely on immediate visual or action predictions. This can lead to a more robust understanding of long-horizon tasks.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Efficiency**: The observed improvements in success rates across diverse semantic task categories on RoboTwin 2.0, coupled with increased execution efficiency (fewer steps for task completion), suggest that stage-level guidance can lead to more effective and purposeful robot behaviors.
- **Integration of Predictive Architectures**: The work shows a practical integration of Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) into World Action Models, leveraging JEPA's ability to predict latent representations of target states to provide high-level semantic guidance.
- **Automated Stage Detection**: The method for automatically constructing stage supervision from robot trajectories without manual labeling streamlines the data preparation process for learning stage-level guidance.

**Potential Impact:**
- **More Capable Generalist Robots**: By enabling robots to better anticipate and progress through distinct task stages, JEPA-WAM's principles could lead to generalist robot policies capable of handling more complex, multi-step manipulation tasks in unstructured environments.
- **Improved Long-Horizon Planning**: The ability to predict and condition on semantic future stages could significantly enhance hierarchical planning frameworks, providing high-level objectives to low-level controllers.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: This work lays a foundation for future investigations into multi-scale latent representations, which could jointly encode coarse task progress and fine-grained contact/geometric information for precise manipulation, addressing current limitations in articulated interaction tasks.
- **Efficiency in Real-World Deployment**: The demonstrated reduction in execution steps implies that robots adopting such methodologies could complete tasks more quickly and with fewer redundant movements, which is valuable in real-world applications where time and energy consumption are critical.

Future work outlined by the authors includes reducing inference latency for higher-frequency control and developing multi-scale latent representations that integrate both semantic task progress and fine-grained physical interaction details. These directions highlight the ongoing challenges and opportunities in advancing robot manipulation capabilities.
