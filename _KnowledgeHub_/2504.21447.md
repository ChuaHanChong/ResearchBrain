# Multimodal Language Models See Better When They Look Shallower

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers systematically analyzed visual layer selection in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), demonstrating that integrating features from shallow, middle, and deep Vision Transformer layers via a simple concatenation fusion outperforms conventional deep-layer reliance and more complex fusion strategies.

## Problem

- Current MLLMs primarily extract visual features from the final or penultimate layers of Vision Transformers (ViTs), a practice lacking systematic justification and potentially suboptimal.
- The direct impact of hierarchical information across different ViT layers on downstream MLLM performance, particularly for fine-grained visual tasks, has been largely underexplored.
- Existing multi-layer fusion methods often rely on heuristics or complex architectures without a principled understanding of optimal layer contributions for MLLM tasks.

## Method

- The authors systematically explored the impact of each of the **24** CLIP-ViT layers by individually connecting them to an MLLM and evaluating performance across **10** diverse benchmarks.
- They partitioned ViT layers into semantically coherent ==shallow, middle, and deep groups== based on ==Layer-wise Representation Similarity (LRS)== analysis.
- A lightweight ==fusion strategy== was proposed, concatenating features from selected shallow, middle, and deep layers (e.g., layers 23, 18, 3) and passing them through a single-layer MLP connector.

## Results

- Layer 23 consistently outperformed Layer 24 across nearly all benchmarks, especially on general tasks, OCR, and hallucination evaluation, validating its superiority among deep layers.
- Shallow and middle layers significantly improved performance on approximately **one-third** of fine-grained visual sub-tasks, with Layer 18 showing a **20%** improvement over the penultimate layer on MME 'position' tasks.
- The proposed simple linear fusion strategy, combining features from layers 23, 18, and 3, consistently achieved substantial performance gains over both single-layer baselines and state-of-the-art fusion methods across multiple benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The absolute final ViT layer (Layer 24) is suboptimal for MLLMs due to CLIP's contrastive training, with the penultimate layer (Layer 23) providing a better balance for general tasks.
> - Shallow and middle ViT layers contain rich, underutilized fine-grained visual information crucial for tasks requiring precise localization, counting, and fine-grained perception, often outperforming deep layers in these areas.
> - A simple concatenation of features from strategically chosen shallow, middle, and deep layers can achieve superior performance over both single-layer baselines and more complex, specialized fusion architectures, suggesting 'less is more' in fusion complexity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) represent a significant frontier in artificial intelligence, extending the capabilities of traditional Large Language Models (LLMs) to jointly process and reason over both visual and textual inputs. These models, exemplified by architectures like LLaVA, Qwen-VL, and Flamingo, are crucial for a wide array of applications, from robotic navigation and medical diagnostics to visual question answering.

A common architectural choice in MLLMs involves integrating a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT) (e.g., CLIP-ViT) to extract visual features, which are then projected into the LLM's embedding space. Despite rapid advancements in LLM capabilities and the sophisticated design of ViTs, a critical component of this pipeline—the selection of which ViT layers to use for visual representation—has largely remained underexplored. The prevailing practice is a "deep-layer bias," where MLLMs typically extract visual features from the final or penultimate layers of the ViT. This choice is largely empirical, driven by convention rather than systematic analysis, as noted by the authors and other related works (e.g., Yao et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023).

Previous research has established that different ViT layers encode a hierarchical spectrum of information: shallower layers capture fine-grained visual details (like edges and textures), while deeper layers progressively abstract this information towards higher-level semantic concepts that align more closely with textual semantics. However, the direct impact of this layer-wise variation on *downstream MLLM performance* has been poorly understood. Existing methods that explore multi-layer fusion (e.g., DenseConnector, MMFuser) often rely on heuristic-based strategies or primarily focus on the fusion mechanism itself, without a principled, in-depth analysis of why certain layers or combinations are beneficial.

This paper addresses this fundamental gap by providing the first comprehensive, principled study of visual layer selection for MLLMs. It moves beyond qualitative probing of ViT representations to systematically quantify how each layer contributes to diverse MLLM tasks. By challenging the "deep-layer bias" and rigorously analyzing the utility of shallower and middle layers, this work aims to inform more effective visual processing pipelines and fusion strategies in future MLLM designs, thereby advancing the field's understanding of multimodal integration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observed "deep-layer bias" in current MLLM designs, where visual features are almost universally extracted from the final or penultimate layers of a pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT). The authors highlight that this practice, though widespread, lacks systematic justification and might be suboptimal.

The key objectives of this study are:

1. **Systematically Investigate Visual Layer Impact:** To conduct the first comprehensive study into which specific ViT layers provide the most effective visual features for MLLM performance across a diverse range of tasks.
2. **Characterize Layer-wise Information:** To analyze Layer-wise Representation Similarity (LRS) across ViT hidden states to establish semantically coherent groupings of layers (shallow, middle, deep). This aims to understand the intrinsic information hierarchy within the ViT and how it relates to task performance.
3. **Challenge the Deep-Layer Bias:** To empirically demonstrate that while deep layers are crucial for semantic-rich tasks (like OCR), shallower and middle layers contain underutilized, valuable information that can significantly improve performance on fine-grained visual tasks (e.g., counting, positioning, object localization).
4. **Evaluate Generalizability:** To assess the robustness and generalizability of their findings across different MLLM scales (1.4B to 7B parameters) and varying training data sizes (665k to 1M samples).
5. **Propose an Effective Fusion Strategy:** To design and evaluate a lightweight feature fusion method that strategically combines features from shallow, middle, and deep layers, demonstrating consistent improvements over single-layer baselines and existing specialized fusion approaches.

In essence, the authors are motivated to move beyond empirical conventions towards a principled understanding of visual feature extraction in MLLMs, aiming to unlock the full potential of ViT backbones for multimodal understanding.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a multi-faceted, systematic approach to investigate visual layer selection and fusion in MLLMs:

1. **Problem Formulation and Base Architecture:**
 - MLLMs are conceptualized as comprising a vision encoder (pre-trained CLIP-ViT-L/14, 336px), a connector (a one-layer MLP), and a large language model (MobileLLaMA 1.4B for primary experiments, scaled to 2.7B MobileLLaMA and 7B Vicuna v1.5 for generalization studies).
 - The CLIP-ViT encodes an image `I` into layer-wise outputs `H(l)` where `l` ranges from 1 to `L` (24 layers). The standard practice of selecting a single layer (often penultimate or final) is highlighted as the core problem.
 - Training follows a two-phase LLaVA-style strategy with AdamW optimizer.

2. **Partitioning of Visual Representations:**
 - To establish a principled understanding of layer-wise information, the authors compute Layer-wise Representation Similarity (LRS) using cosine similarity between the hidden states of different ViT layers.
 - This analysis, inspired by similar studies in LLMs, revealed three distinct representation spaces (Fig. 1a):
 - **Shallow layers:** 1 to 12
 - **Middle layers:** 13 to 20
 - **Deep layers:** 21 to 24
 - This categorization provides a structured framework for subsequent layer selection and fusion experiments.

3. **Layer-wise Exploration:**
 - The core experimental method involved individually connecting *each* of the 24 CLIP-ViT layers to the language model (via the MLP connector).
 - For each layer, a corresponding MLLM was trained and benchmarked across a comprehensive suite of tasks. This contrasts with prior work that primarily "probes" for information presence without assessing downstream utility.

4. **Evaluation Benchmarks:**
 - To ensure a thorough evaluation, 10 benchmarks encompassing over 60 tasks were categorized into four groups:
 - **General tasks:** MME (Perception & Cognition), MMBench, SEEDBench, GQA.
 - **OCR tasks:** TextVQA, OCRBench.
 - **Vision-centric tasks:** CVBench (2D/3D), RealWorldQA, MMVet, RefCOCO (visual grounding).
 - **Hallucination tasks:** POPE.
 - Metrics included dataset-defined scores, direct accuracy, and CIDEr for RefCOCO.

5. **Effect of Data and Model Scale:**
 - To validate the generality of findings, experiments were extended by varying:
 - **Training data size:** LLaVA 665K, Cambrian-1 737K (with additional OCR data), and a custom 1M dataset (incorporating vision-centric data).
 - **LLM size:** MobileLLaMA 1.4B, 2.7B, and Vicuna v1.5 7B.
 - Due to computational costs, these scaling experiments focused on representative layers (3 from shallow, 18 from middle, 23 and 24 from deep layers) rather than a full 24-layer analysis.

6. **Visual Feature Fusion Strategy:**
 - Based on the insights from layer-wise exploration, the authors proposed a simple yet effective fusion strategy:
 - Features from selected shallow, middle, and deep layers `H(i)` are concatenated along the feature dimension: `f = Concat(H(i) | i ∈ L)`.
 - This concatenated feature `f` is then passed to the one-layer MLP connector.
 - Ablation studies were conducted on different layer combinations (`L`) to identify optimal fusion sets.
 - Their proposed fusion (layers 23, 18, 3) was compared against single-layer baselines and more complex, state-of-the-art fusion methods like DenseConnector and MMFuser.

This systematic methodology allowed the authors to not only uncover the distinct contributions of different ViT layers but also to demonstrate how these insights can be leveraged to design more effective MLLMs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, challenging conventional practices and offering actionable insights for MLLM design:

1. **Deep-to-Deep Layer Comparison:**
 - **The Final Layer is Suboptimal:** Contrary to widespread practice, the absolute final layer (Layer 24) of CLIP-ViT consistently underperformed the penultimate layer (Layer 23) across nearly all benchmarks, especially on general tasks, OCR, and hallucination evaluation (POPE) (Table 1, Fig. 2). This degradation is attributed to CLIP's contrastive training, which optimizes the final `[CLS]` token for image-text matching, potentially suppressing crucial local image features needed for MLLM tasks.
 - **Penultimate Layer as Optimal in Deep Space:** Layer 23 (the penultimate layer) consistently achieved the best performance among deep layers. Its superiority stems from an optimal balance of retaining rich visual information and strong alignment with textual representations, making it well-suited for diverse multimodal tasks. This advantage persisted across all tested LLM scales (1.4B, 2.7B, 7B) (Fig. 4).

2. **Deep-to-Shallower Layer Comparison:**
 - **Deep Layers are Essential for OCR:** Shallow layers provide negligible text-related information, showing a sharp performance gain only from the middle layers onwards for OCR tasks (Fig. 1b). This highlights the need for both rich visual features *and* strong textual alignment for OCR, which shallow layers inherently lack.
 - **Potential of Middle Layers for Vision-Centric Tasks:** Shallow and middle layers, often overlooked, significantly outperformed deep layers on approximately one-third of sub-tasks, particularly those requiring fine-grained visual perception, localization, and counting (Fig. 3). For instance, Layer 18 showed a 20% improvement over the penultimate layer on "position" tasks in MME (Fig. 9). Similar trends were observed in MMVet and SEEDBench for tasks like "spatial relations," "physical relations," and "instance localization."
 - **Limited Impact of Visual Quality on Cognitive Tasks:** Surprisingly, even shallow layers, which generally provide lower-quality visual representations, could rival or surpass deeper layers on certain MME-Cognition tasks like "Code Reasoning" and "Numerical Calculation." This suggests that for some cognitive tasks, the bottleneck is not solely visual feature quality but also the LLM's reasoning capabilities.
 - **Hallucination Mitigation in Middle Layers:** While hallucination was most pronounced in shallow layers, it was effectively mitigated in the middle layers, which often outperformed the penultimate layer on POPE. This indicates that the problem in this task is more about visual perception than high-level semantic comprehension.

3. **Effect of Data and Model Scale:**
 - **Generalizability of Findings:** The core conclusions remained consistent across different training data sizes (665k, 737k, 1M) and LLM scales (1.4B, 2.7B, 7B) (Table 2, Fig. 4).
 - **Scaling Benefits Deep Layers More:** As training data and LLM size increased, the proportion of subtasks where the penultimate layer achieved optimal performance also grew (Fig. 5). This implies that while fine-grained details in middle layers are readily utilized, extracting such information from deep layers requires more data to activate the LLM's full potential.
 - **Persistent Limitations for Shallow OCR:** Increasing OCR-specific training data or LLM size did not significantly improve OCR performance for shallow layers, reaffirming their inherent lack of textual alignment.

4. **Visual Feature Fusion:**
 - **Simple Fusion Outperforms Complex Methods:** The authors proposed a straightforward linear-layer-based fusion strategy, combining features from selected shallow, middle, and deep layers (specifically, layers 23, 18, and 3). This minimalist approach consistently achieved substantial performance gains over both single-layer baselines and more complex, specialized fusion designs (e.g., DenseConnector, MMFuser) across multiple benchmarks (Table 3, Table 8). The finding "Less is more" suggests that complex fusion architectures might be unnecessary if the right combination of layer information is supplied.

In summary, the paper provides compelling evidence that the conventional deep-layer bias in MLLMs is suboptimal for many tasks and that strategically incorporating shallower and middle layer features, even with a simple fusion method, can lead to significant performance improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work marks a significant contribution to the field of multimodal AI, offering both a principled understanding and practical implications for the design and optimization of MLLMs.

**Significance:**

1. **First Principled Study:** The paper presents the first comprehensive and systematic analysis of visual layer selection for MLLMs, moving beyond empirical heuristics and qualitative observations. By quantifying the utility of different ViT layers for downstream tasks, it establishes a foundational understanding of the hierarchical information flow in multimodal visual encoders.
2. **Challenges Deep-Layer Bias:** It effectively challenges the long-standing "deep-layer bias" in MLLM architectures, demonstrating that relying solely on the final or penultimate ViT layers leads to suboptimal performance on a significant subset of fine-grained visual tasks. This paradigm shift encourages researchers to reconsider fundamental architectural choices.
3. **Highlights Underutilized Information:** The study convincingly shows that shallower and middle layers of ViTs contain rich, underutilized fine-grained visual details crucial for tasks like counting, positioning, and object localization. This uncovers a valuable resource that current MLLMs often discard.
4. **Simplicity in Fusion:** The discovery that a simple linear concatenation of features from carefully selected layers (shallow, middle, deep) outperforms more complex, specialized fusion architectures is highly significant. It advocates for efficiency and parsimony in model design, suggesting that the key lies in *what* information is fused rather than the complexity of *how* it's fused.
5. **Generalizability across Scales:** The validation of findings across various LLM sizes and training data scales enhances the robustness and applicability of the conclusions, suggesting they are not artifacts of specific model configurations but fundamental properties of ViT-MLLM integration.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **Improved MLLM Performance:** By guiding the selection of optimal visual layers and promoting effective fusion strategies, this research can directly lead to the development of MLLMs with enhanced perception and reasoning capabilities, especially for tasks requiring fine-grained visual understanding.
2. **More Efficient MLLM Design:** The "less is more" finding regarding fusion methods can simplify MLLM architecture design, potentially reducing computational overhead and making advanced multimodal capabilities more accessible for deployment.
3. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a strong foundation for future research in several directions:
 - **Dynamic Layer Selection:** Exploring adaptive mechanisms for MLLMs to dynamically select or weigh visual features from different layers based on task requirements or input characteristics.
 - **Visual Encoder Architecture:** Informing the design of future vision encoders specifically optimized for multimodal tasks, possibly by rethinking their internal information hierarchy and alignment with language models.
 - **Connector Architectures:** Understanding how different connector architectures interact with multi-layer features to maximize their utility.
 - **Broader Generalization:** Extending the analysis to other ViT backbones beyond CLIP-ViT-L/14 and larger LLMs to validate the generalizability further.
4. **Applications in Specialized Domains:** MLLMs with improved fine-grained visual perception could have a profound impact on domains such as medical imaging (e.g., precise lesion localization), robotics (e.g., accurate object manipulation), autonomous driving (e.g., detailed scene understanding), and scientific image analysis.

The paper acknowledges limitations, such as focusing mainly on CLIP-ViT-L/14 and external fusion methods. However, these limitations also define exciting avenues for future work, reinforcing the foundational nature of this study in advancing the understanding and development of highly capable multimodal AI systems.
