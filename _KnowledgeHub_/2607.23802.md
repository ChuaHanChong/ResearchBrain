# From RLVR to RLSVR: Task Transformation Induces Self-Verifiable Rewards for Open-Ended LLM Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> Reinforcement Learning with Self-Verifiable Rewards (RLSVR) transforms open-ended language tasks into structured environments, generating internal, rule-based reward signals. Its instantiation, SpyRL, consistently outperforms existing self-improvement methods on diverse benchmarks, including summarization, creative writing, and mathematical reasoning, by enabling verifier-free self-improvement through information-asymmetric multi-agent self-play.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is limited to tasks with deterministic correctness checks (e.g., math, coding), preventing its use for open-ended tasks.
- Open-ended tasks, such as summarization or creative writing, typically rely on costly human feedback, biased learned reward models, or external LLM judges, leading to scalability issues, evaluation bias, and increased inference costs.
- Existing LLM self-improvement methods for open-ended generation struggle to provide scalable, unbiased, and cost-effective supervision for continuous learning.

## Method

- Introduces Reinforcement Learning with Self-Verifiable Rewards (**RLSVR**), a paradigm that transforms an original open-ended task into a ==proxy environment== where rewards become automatically verifiable.
- Instantiates RLSVR through **SpyRL**, a multi-agent, ==information-asymmetric self-play== framework inspired by social deduction games, which consists of a Performing Stage and a Detection Stage.
- In SpyRL, agents (one 'spy' with degraded input, others 'civilians' with full input) generate task outputs, and then vote to identify the spy, with ==rule-based rewards== derived from these votes to drive continuous improvement without external verifiers.

## Results

- Demonstrated a strong positive correlation between SpyRL's rule-based suspicion votes and output quality as ranked by GPT-4o, confirming reward alignment for non-verifiable tasks.
- Achieved superior performance on open-ended tasks (text summarization, creative writing) and verifiable tasks (mathematical reasoning), outperforming baselines and base models (e.g., **74.6%** GPT-4o win rate on GovReport for Qwen3-4B; GSM8K accuracy improved from **84.5%** to **93.4%**).
- Showcased cost-efficiency by achieving competitive or superior performance to expensive rubric-as-reward baselines (Qwen3.5-27B-RaR, GPT-4o-RaR) at significantly lower operational costs, as it requires no external verifiers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Verifiability for reward generation can be an engineered property of a task environment, not just an intrinsic one, through strategic task transformation and latent variable injection.
> - An information-asymmetric multi-agent game can generate self-verifiable, high-quality reward signals that align with actual output quality for open-ended tasks.
> - Coupled, alternating optimization between performer and detector policies, combined with Role-Advantage Estimation (RAE) to mitigate information asymmetry, is critical for stable and effective co-evolution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language model (LLM) training has seen significant advancements through Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), particularly in domains where the correctness of an output can be deterministically checked, such as mathematics and coding. Examples include models like OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek-R1. This approach benefits from scalable, unbiased, and low-cost supervision.

However, the applicability of RLVR is limited in open-ended tasks like text summarization or creative writing, where evaluation often relies on subjective judgment. Current methods attempting to bridge this gap typically employ learned preference signals (e.g., Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)), or proxy feedback mechanisms such as LLM-as-a-Judge or self-rewarding language models. These approaches, while expanding RL's scope, introduce challenges such as evaluation bias, limitations in the judge's capability, and increased inference costs due to the need for external evaluation.

This paper positions itself by drawing a parallel to self-supervised learning (SSL), where pretext tasks are constructed to derive supervisory signals automatically from the data itself, thereby circumventing the need for human annotations. The proposed Reinforcement Learning with Self-Verifiable Rewards (RLSVR) paradigm aims to extend this task-transformation principle to RLVR. By creating proxy environments that generate verifiable rewards internally, RLSVR seeks to enable scalable RL-based self-improvement for open-ended tasks without external human or model supervision. This distinguishes it from other self-play methods for LLMs, which often focus on inherently verifiable domains or risk performance degeneration when applied to open-ended generation. The work builds on the success of multi-agent self-play in domains like games (e.g., AlphaGo, AlphaZero) but adapts it to general language tasks by engineering verifiability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to extend the benefits of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to open-ended language tasks, where traditional, deterministic correctness verification is not feasible. The authors are motivated by several limitations of current LLM self-improvement and reinforcement learning approaches for such tasks:

1. **Verifiability Bottleneck in RLVR:** Existing RLVR methods, while effective and scalable in domains like mathematics and coding, are constrained to tasks where a clear, deterministic verifier (e.g., an answer checker, a unit test) exists. This fundamental requirement prevents their direct application to open-ended tasks that inherently lack such a ground truth.

2. **Limitations of Approximate Evaluation Methods:** To address the verifiability gap in open-ended tasks, prior research has employed various approximation strategies:
 - **Human Preference Signals (e.g., RLHF, DPO):** These methods require extensive and costly human annotation, which is not scalable for continuous self-improvement.
 - **Learned Reward Models:** These models are prone to evaluation bias, can be limited by the quality of the data they are trained on, and introduce additional inference costs.
 - **LLM-as-a-Judge or Self-Rewarding Mechanisms:** While reducing human effort, these approaches face bottlenecks in the judge's capability, as the LLM judge's competence can bound the policy's improvement. They also incur additional inference costs for every evaluation rollout. Furthermore, model-based feedback can introduce its own forms of bias or lead to "policy stagnation" where the model learns to optimize for the judge's preferences rather than genuine task quality.

3. **Scalability and Cost Concerns:** The aforementioned approximate evaluation methods are often resource-intensive, either through human labeling costs or increased computational overhead for external evaluators. The authors aim to find a scalable solution that does not rely on these external, costly, or potentially biased signals.

4. **Inspiration from Self-Supervised Learning (SSL):** The core motivation draws from the success of SSL, which constructs "pretext tasks" (e.g., masked language modeling, jigsaw puzzles) that automatically generate supervisory signals from the data itself. The authors posit that if a proxy objective can automatically generate supervision while maintaining substantial capability overlap with the target task, learning can proceed without specific human annotation or external judges.

Therefore, the key objective is to propose a novel training paradigm, RLSVR, that transforms open-ended tasks into structured environments capable of generating inherently self-verifiable rewards. This transformation aims to enable scalable, robust, and cost-effective self-improvement for LLMs on a broad spectrum of open-ended generation and reasoning tasks, by deriving reward signals directly from the environment's internal rules and interactions, rather than external judgments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on Reinforcement Learning with Self-Verifiable Rewards (RLSVR), a task-transformation paradigm. This is instantiated through a concrete framework called SpyRL.

#### 3.1. RLSVR Framework

RLSVR conceptualizes the problem of extending RLVR to open-ended tasks by adopting the task-transformation principle from self-supervised learning. It transforms an original open-ended task into a *proxy environment* (E) where rewards become automatically verifiable. The core idea is that reward verifiability is derived from the environment's internal rules or interaction outcomes, rather than external evaluators. This process involves four key steps:

1. **Latent-Variable Injection:** The environment samples an input `x` and injects a latent variable `z`, which is recorded as the ground truth but not directly revealed to the policy. `z` can represent perturbed inputs, withheld information, or conditions for output generation.
2. **Conditioned Task Execution:** The policy performs the original target task on observations `o` derived from `(x, z)`, producing outputs `y`. This ensures that the capabilities being trained align with the target task.
3. **Verifiable Interaction:** The environment's rules pose a question about `z` that must be answered using only the task outputs from step 2. The design ensures that correctly answering this question depends on the quality of the outputs.
4. **Rule-Based Reward:** The environment computes a reward `R` by checking the interaction outcomes against the recorded `z`. This reward is "self-verifiable" because `z` is known by construction.

#### 3.2. SpyRL Instantiation

SpyRL implements RLSVR as a multi-agent, information-asymmetric self-play framework inspired by social deduction games like "Who Is the Spy?". It transforms open-ended generation tasks into a two-stage adversarial game: a **Performing Stage** and a **Detection Stage**.

#### 3.2.1. Information-Asymmetric Performing Stage

- **Capability-Oriented Task Design:** The tasks (text summarization, creative writing, mathematical reasoning) are chosen to align the capabilities exercised in the game with the target task objectives. Tasks are designed to use cheap document-level information.
- **Asymmetric Information Allocation:** In each round, `n` players are involved. One player is designated as the "spy" (index `u`), and the remaining `n-1` are "civilians."
 - Civilians receive the complete task input `x`.
 - The spy receives a degraded version of the input, `g(x)`, where `g(·)` is an information-degradation operator (e.g., continuous span masking, partial context removal) tailored to the domain. This ensures the spy has a disadvantage.
- **Output Generation:** All `n` agents, using a shared performer policy `π_P_θ`, generate an output `y_i` for the target task based on their respective observations `o_i`. The objective for all players is to perform well on the task, with the spy also trying to blend in.

#### 3.2.2. Detection Stage with Verifiable Rewards

- **Verifiable Identity Detection:** All players inspect one another's outputs `Y = {y_1, ..., y_n}` and vote on which player is the spy (`v_i`). Crucially, the spy's identity (`u`) is predetermined by the environment.
- **Rule-Based Reward for Detectors:** The reward for a detector `i` is defined as `r_D_i = I[v_i = u]`, an indicator function that is 1 if the vote is correct and 0 otherwise. This reward is deterministically verifiable against the known spy identity.
- **Group-based Advantage:** To stabilize the training signal, a GRPO-style group relative advantage (`A_D_i`) is calculated based on the mean (`μ_D`) and standard deviation (`σ_D`) of rewards within the group.

#### 3.2.3. Two-Stage Coupled Optimization

The training involves a dynamic interplay between the performing and detection stages:

- **Zero-Sum Reward for Performers:** The rewards for the performer policy (`r_P_u` for the spy, `r_P_c_j` for civilians) are derived from the voting outcomes of the detection stage.
 - The spy receives a penalty proportional to the number of votes it receives.
 - Civilians receive a reward based on the spy receiving votes and a penalty if they themselves receive votes. This creates a zero-sum game, driving continuous co-evolution where performers are incentivized to produce high-quality outputs that do not reveal their information status.
- **Role-Advantage Estimation (RAE):** To mitigate bias from the inherent information asymmetry, RAE is employed. This mechanism maintains separate exponential moving average (EMA) baselines for spy and civilian roles, ensuring that the gradient signal for the performer reflects relative performance within its role, rather than being confounded by structural difficulty differences.
- **Iterative Optimization:** The performer policy `π_P_θ` and detector policy `π_D_ϕ` are updated separately using GRPO-style clipped objectives with KL regularization. A dynamic alternating schedule is used: only one stage is updated in each training epoch based on the current performance (e.g., if detection accuracy is high, focus on performer improvement; if detection fails, focus on detector improvement). This prevents premature convergence and ensures stable learning pressure.

This integrated approach aims to transform the qualitative judgment of open-ended tasks into a quantitative, verifiable reward signal, fostering continuous self-improvement without external human or model supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across various tasks and ablation studies, demonstrating the effectiveness and properties of RLSVR instantiated as SpyRL.

#### 4.1. Self-Verifiable Rewards Alignment

A crucial finding is the alignment between the rule-based rewards generated by SpyRL and actual output quality. Experiments on text summarization (GovReport) and creative writing (WritingPrompts) showed a positive correlation: outputs receiving more suspicion votes in the detection stage were consistently ranked lower in quality by GPT-4o. Conversely, higher-quality outputs received fewer votes. This directly supports the claim that the vote-based reward in SpyRL's performing stage is aligned with task performance without needing an external verifier.

#### 4.2. Performance on Non-Verifiable Tasks

- **Text Summarization (GovReport, Multi_News, QmSum, VcSum, SamSum):** SpyRL consistently outperformed existing self-improvement methods (R-Zero, Absolute Zero) and the base models on all five summarization benchmarks for both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B. SpyRL achieved the highest ROUGE-L scores and high GPT-4o A/B win rates (e.g., 74.6% win rate against the Qwen3-4B backbone on GovReport). The baselines showed only marginal gains or even degradations against their respective backbones in supplementary analyses.
- **Creative Writing (WritingPrompts, WritingBench):** SpyRL also demonstrated superior performance in creative writing, as evaluated by GPT-4o A/B testing. It achieved higher win rates across all fine-grained dimensions (novelty, emotion, coherence, consistency, overall) compared to the base models and baselines. Gains were particularly pronounced in novelty and emotion, suggesting improvements beyond surface fluency.

#### 4.3. Performance on Verifiable Tasks

- **Mathematical and General Reasoning (GSM8K, Math500, AIME, Minerva, MMLU-Pro, GPQA-D):** SpyRL achieved the best performance across all five mathematical benchmarks and two general reasoning benchmarks for both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B. For instance, on Qwen3-4B, SpyRL improved GSM8K accuracy from 84.5% to 93.4% and AIME 2025 accuracy from 6.7% to 20.0%. This indicates that SpyRL's advantages extend even to domains where conventional RLVR is applicable, suggesting that its training mechanism provides a finer-grained and more stable learning signal.

#### 4.4. Human Evaluation

A blinded human evaluation on creative writing confirmed the GPT-4o findings. Human evaluators consistently preferred SpyRL's outputs across all dimensions, including novelty and emotion, over the base model and baseline methods, with overall win rates of 80.0% against Qwen3-4B on WritingPrompts.

#### 4.5. Comparison with Rubric-as-Reward Baselines

SpyRL outperformed Qwen3.5-27B-RaR and remained competitive with GPT-4o-RaR, despite the latter methods incurring substantial additional verifier costs (approximately $200 and $900, respectively). This highlights SpyRL's cost-efficiency in achieving strong performance without external verifiers.

#### 4.6. Generalization and Transferability

- **Domain-Specific Summarization:** SpyRL demonstrated generalization by successfully applying to scientific article summarization (PubMed corpus) and achieving significant gains on arXiv, PubMed, and BillSum benchmarks, indicating its applicability beyond the initial government report domain.
- **Cross-Task Transfer:** Positive transfer was observed between summarization and creative writing tasks. However, models trained on mathematical reasoning showed negative transfer to writing tasks, suggesting task-specific capability development for domains with distinct requirements (symbolic manipulation vs. stylistic generation).

#### 4.7. Ablation Studies

- **Alternating Optimization:** Ablations revealed that alternating optimization between performing and detection stages is critical. Training solely on one stage or simultaneously updating both led to performance plateaus or degradation, emphasizing the importance of dynamic stage-wise updates for stable co-evolution.
- **Group Size:** Increasing the number of players from 3 to 5 yielded the most significant performance gains, with diminishing returns for larger groups (6 or 8 players). This suggests an optimal game complexity for effective self-improvement.
- **Role-Advantage Estimation (RAE):** Removing RAE significantly degraded performance, sometimes even below the base model, confirming its importance in calibrating the reward signals to account for the inherent information asymmetry between spy and civilian roles.
- **Degradation Operator Sensitivity:** The performance was found to be largely insensitive to the specific masking ratio (20% vs. 40%) used by the information-degradation operator for summarization, indicating robustness in the design of information asymmetry.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Reinforcement Learning with Self-Verifiable Rewards (RLSVR) as a significant conceptual and methodological contribution, with substantial potential impact on the development of advanced language models.

**Addressing a Core Limitation of RLVR:** The most significant contribution is the extension of scalable RLVR to open-ended tasks. Previously, RLVR's reliance on deterministic verifiers severely limited its application. RLSVR, through its task transformation principle, demonstrates a method to engineer verifiability for tasks that intrinsically lack it, thereby broadening the scope of scalable reinforcement learning for LLMs.

**Enabling Verifier-Free Self-Improvement:** By deriving rewards from environment-assigned latent variables and rule-based interaction outcomes, RLSVR, as instantiated by SpyRL, eliminates the need for expensive human annotations, learned reward models, or external LLM judges. This reduces the evaluation bias, judge capability bottlenecks, and inference costs associated with prior methods for open-ended tasks. This self-contained learning loop could enable more autonomous and continuous self-improvement for LLMs.

**Enhanced Model Capabilities for Open-Ended Tasks:** The experimental results show that SpyRL not only outperforms existing self-improvement methods on open-ended tasks like summarization and creative writing but also yields consistent gains on already-verifiable reasoning tasks. This suggests that the information-asymmetric self-play and coupled optimization foster more rigorous, coherent, and creative generation capabilities, even when a direct verifier is not available. The human evaluations further corroborate these observed improvements in subjective quality metrics.

**New Paradigm for Verifiability:** The paper's core insight—that verifiability can be an engineered property of a task environment rather than an intrinsic one—represents a potential paradigm shift. This principle could inspire novel approaches for designing learning environments across various AI domains, particularly where complex, nuanced, or subjective outputs are required.

**Cost-Efficiency and Scalability:** By making reward generation internal and automated, RLSVR offers a more cost-effective and scalable training solution compared to methods dependent on human feedback or large external LLM judges. This could accelerate research and development in areas requiring extensive training iterations for LLM self-improvement.

**Future Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for future research into other forms of task transformations and multi-agent game designs that can induce verifiable rewards for a wider array of complex, open-ended AI capabilities, including those in multimodal domains (as hinted by Vision-Zero). It also encourages further exploration into the dynamics of alternating optimization and role-advantage estimation in adversarial self-play.

In summary, RLSVR provides a robust, scalable, and cost-effective framework for LLM self-improvement on open-ended tasks, addressing a fundamental challenge in current reinforcement learning paradigms for language models.
