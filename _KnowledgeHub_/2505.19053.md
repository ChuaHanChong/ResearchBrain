# Structured Reinforcement Learning for Combinatorial Decision-Making

> [!summary] Summary
> Structured Reinforcement Learning (SRL), an actor-critic framework, integrates combinatorial optimization layers into neural networks to address large combinatorial action spaces in real-world industrial problems. SRL demonstrated superior performance, stability, and convergence over unstructured deep reinforcement learning and expert-dependent methods across various static and dynamic environments.

## Problem

- Standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms struggle with the exponentially large action space in Combinatorial Markov Decision Processes (C-MDPs), making action selection computationally intractable and hindering efficient exploration.
- Current neural architectures often fail to inherently leverage the combinatorial structure of action spaces, leading to poor generalization and unstable learning dynamics.
- Existing CO-augmented Machine Learning (COAML) approaches for C-MDPs often rely on costly expert demonstrations or introduce non-differentiability challenges that impede stable end-to-end gradient-based learning.

## Method

- **SRL** ==integrates a combinatorial optimization== (CO) layer directly into the actor's architecture, transforming a state-dependent latent score vector into a feasible combinatorial action.
- It employs Fenchel-Young (FY) losses with Gaussian perturbations to create a convex and differentiable surrogate objective, enabling stable gradient propagation through the non-differentiable CO-layer.
- SRL estimates target actions online without expert data by perturbing the actor's score, generating candidate actions via the CO-layer, and evaluating them with the critic network to form a softmax-weighted average.

## Results

- SRL consistently outperformed unstructured deep reinforcement learning (PPO) by up to **92%** across dynamic combinatorial problems (DVSP, DAP, GSPP), exhibiting substantially lower variance (up to 80 times less).
- In the Dynamic Assortment Problem (DAP), SRL outperformed Structured Imitation Learning (SIL) by **8%**, and in the Gridworld Shortest Paths Problem (GSPP), it surpassed SIL by **78%** and the online optimum by **79%**.
- SRL demonstrated competitive convergence rates compared to SIL while learning purely from interaction, and was significantly more stable and efficient than PPO in complex combinatorial environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly embedding a CO-layer into the actor allows for principled handling of combinatorial action spaces, ensuring feasibility, scalability, and leveraging inherent structure for better generalization.
> - The use of Fenchel-Young losses provides a robust mechanism to enable end-to-end differentiable learning through discrete combinatorial optimization layers, resolving a key challenge in COAML.
> - Online, critic-guided exploration of the combinatorial action space for target action estimation eliminates the need for expert demonstrations, making the approach applicable to problems without pre-existing optimal solutions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has seen considerable success in various domains, but its application to real-world industrial problems, such as vehicle routing, inventory planning, and machine scheduling, presents specific challenges. These problems frequently involve large-scale combinatorial decision-making, which the paper refers to as Combinatorial Markov Decision Processes (C-MDPs). Standard RL algorithms often encounter difficulties in these settings due to:
1. **Exponential action space size:** This renders action selection computationally intractable and impedes efficient exploration.
2. **Limited structure exploitation:** Standard neural architectures struggle to leverage the inherent combinatorial structure of the action spaces, leading to poor generalization and unstable learning dynamics.

The broader research landscape includes several communities addressing learning and decision-making over structured spaces. These include structured prediction, differentiable optimization layers, and hierarchical reinforcement learning. While these fields provide tools for embedding structure, they do not consistently offer direct solutions for end-to-end reinforcement learning in environments with combinatorial action spaces.

Prior approaches to solving C-MDPs have encompassed:
- **Handcrafted decision rules:** These often lack the ability to model complex dynamics or constraints.
- **Predict-and-optimize algorithms:** Typically relying on imitation learning, these methods separate prediction from optimization, which can impair performance in dynamic settings.
- **CO-augmented Machine Learning (COAML) pipelines:** These integrate combinatorial optimization directly into model architectures to enable end-to-end learning. A key challenge in COAML is differentiating through the non-differentiable combinatorial optimization (CO) layer. Existing strategies include problem-specific relaxation schemes and general methods like Fenchel-Young losses. In C-MDPs, prior COAML work has either used offline expert imitation, requiring expert solutions and potentially limiting exploration, or treated the CO-layer as an action mask in unstructured RL, which can lead to unstable gradients.
- **Policy-gradient algorithms (e.g., REINFORCE, PPO, SAC):** While effective in conventional MDPs, these are challenged by the scale and structure of C-MDP action spaces. Problem-specific neural network designs often lack generalizability. Value-based methods typically assume decomposable critics, limiting their applicability in structured domains.
- **Offline RL:** Often relies on imitation learning, differing from online approaches that update from on-policy targets.

This work addresses the limitations of existing methods by proposing Structured Reinforcement Learning (SRL), an actor-critic paradigm designed to integrate CO-layers into neural actors for C-MDPs. SRL aims to enable end-to-end learning within these structured environments without requiring expert data and while maintaining stable learning dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel reinforcement learning paradigm, termed Structured Reinforcement Learning (SRL), that can effectively solve Combinatorial Markov Decision Processes (C-MDPs). This objective is driven by several key motivations stemming from the inadequacies of current RL methodologies when applied to real-world industrial problems:

1. **Overcoming Combinatorial Action Space Challenges:** Industrial problems such as routing, scheduling, and assortment planning are characterized by action spaces that grow exponentially with problem size. Standard RL algorithms struggle with the computational intractability of selecting actions from such vast spaces, which also hinders efficient exploration. A core motivation is to develop an approach that can manage these large action spaces effectively.

2. **Leveraging Combinatorial Structure:** Current neural network architectures often fail to inherently exploit the combinatorial structure of action spaces. This deficiency can lead to poor generalization capabilities and unstable learning dynamics in C-MDPs. The research seeks to embed combinatorial optimization directly into the RL agent's architecture to leverage this structure, thereby improving generalization and learning stability.

3. **Enabling End-to-End Learning without Expert Dependence:** Many existing COAML approaches for C-MDPs rely on imitation learning, which necessitates access to expert demonstrations. Obtaining such expert data can be costly or impossible in real-world scenarios. A crucial motivation for SRL is to enable end-to-end training of COAML pipelines using only collected experience, thereby removing the dependency on expert supervision.

4. **Addressing Differentiability Issues:** Integrating combinatorial optimization layers into neural networks often introduces non-differentiability, making gradient-based end-to-end learning challenging. Prior methods use various approximations or relaxations, which can lead to unstable gradients or suboptimal performance. A key objective is to devise a robust mechanism, specifically using Fenchel-Young losses, to facilitate smooth and stable gradient propagation through the CO-layer.

5. **Improving Performance and Stability in Dynamic Settings:** While some structured learning approaches perform adequately in static tasks, dynamic C-MDPs with evolving states and uncertainties (both exogenous and endogenous) pose greater challenges. The research aims to develop an RL framework that demonstrates superior performance, stability, and convergence speed compared to unstructured RL and imitation learning, particularly in these complex dynamic environments.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between advanced RL techniques and complex industrial decision-making problems by developing a structured, end-to-end learning framework that directly addresses the unique challenges of combinatorial action spaces and dynamic uncertainties.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Structured Reinforcement Learning (SRL) is introduced as a novel actor-critic framework specifically designed for environments with combinatorial action spaces. Its core principle is the integration of a combinatorial optimization (CO) layer into the actor's architecture, transforming it into a COAML (Combinatorial Optimization-augmented Machine Learning) pipeline.

#### 3.1. Combinatorial Actor

The SRL agent extends the standard actor-critic paradigm.
- **Architecture:** The actor comprises a statistical model, typically a neural network ($\phi_w$), which maps the current state ($s$) to a latent score vector ($\theta$). This score vector is then passed to a CO-layer ($f$).
- **Action Selection:** The CO-layer solves a combinatorial problem, $a = \operatorname{argmax}_{\tilde{a} \in A(s)} \langle \theta | \tilde{a} \rangle$, where $A(s)$ is the set of feasible actions. This ensures that selected actions are both scalable in dimension and valid. The resulting policy, $\pi_w(\cdot|s) := \delta_{f(\phi_w(s),s)}$, is a Dirac distribution centered on the output of $f$.
- **Benefits:** The statistical model handles high-dimensional states and contextual information, enabling generalization. The CO-layer enforces combinatorial feasibility, facilitates structured exploration, and improves scalability.

#### 3.2. End-to-End Actor Learning

A significant challenge is training the actor's parameters ($w$) using gradient-based methods, as the CO-layer ($f$) is piecewise constant and non-differentiable.
- **Problem:** Geometrically, the CO-layer maps score vectors ($\theta$) to vertices of the action polytope $C(s) = \operatorname{conv}(A(s))$. Each vertex corresponds to a normal cone, and crossing cone boundaries results in abrupt changes in action, leading to uninformative derivatives.
- **SRL's Solution - Fenchel-Young (FY) Loss:** SRL employs FY losses to define a surrogate objective that is convex and differentiable with respect to $\theta = \phi_w(s)$. The loss takes the form $L_{\Omega}(\theta; \hat{a}) = \operatorname{max}_{a \in A(s)} \theta^T a - \theta^T \hat{a}$. To address non-smoothness, a Gaussian perturbation $Z \sim N(0, \epsilon)$ is introduced: $L_{\Omega}(\theta; \hat{a}) = E_Z[\operatorname{max}_{a \in A(s)} (\theta+Z)^T a] - \theta^T \hat{a}$. This enables smooth gradient propagation via a pathwise estimator.
- **Target Action ($\hat{a}$):** Unlike imitation learning, SRL estimates its target action online without expert demonstrations.
 1. The actor's score vector $\theta$ is perturbed using a Gaussian distribution $Z \sim N(\theta, \sigma_b)$ to sample $m$ perturbed scores ($\eta$).
 2. Each $\eta$ is passed through the CO-layer $f$ to generate candidate actions ($a'$).
 3. These $a'$ actions are evaluated by the critic network ($\psi_{\beta}$).
 4. A softmax-weighted average of these candidate actions, based on their Q-values, determines the target action $\hat{a}$:
 $\hat{a} = \sum_{a'} a' \frac{\exp(\frac{1}{\tau} \cdot Q_{\psi_{\beta}}(s, a'))}{\sum_{a''} \exp(\frac{1}{\tau} \cdot Q_{\psi_{\beta}}(s, a''))}$, where $\tau$ is a temperature parameter.
 - **Exploration:** SRL uses three distinct Gaussian perturbations of $\theta$:
 - $\sigma_f$: for exploration of the state space ($S$) during the forward pass.
 - $\sigma_b$: for exploration of the combinatorial action space ($A(s)$) during target action selection.
 - $\epsilon$: for regularizing the CO-layer $f$ within the Fenchel-Young loss.

#### 3.3. Critic Architecture and Stability

- **Q-value Estimation:** SRL uses a critic network ($\psi_{\beta}$) to estimate Q-values, $Q_{\pi}(s, a) = Q_{\psi_{\beta}}(s, a)$.
- **Critic Update:** The critic is updated using standard temporal-difference (TD) learning, minimizing the loss $(Q_{\psi_{\beta}}(s_t, a_t) - y_t)^2$, where $y_t = r_t + \gamma Q_{\psi_{\bar{\beta}}}(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1})$. Target networks ($\psi_{\bar{\beta}}$) are used to stabilize training, and double Q-learning techniques are adopted in complex environments to mitigate overestimation bias.
- **Distinction from other methods:** SRL does not require the Q-function to be decomposable or directly optimizable over actions, which is often intractable for large combinatorial $A(s)$. Instead, its sampling-based approach for $\hat{a}$ allows for a tractable estimate of the update direction.

#### 3.4. Geometrical Insights (Primal-Dual Interpretation)

The paper provides a geometric interpretation of SRL, particularly for static contextual stochastic combinatorial optimization (a simplified case relevant to how value functions are learned).
- **Connection to Primal-Dual Algorithms:** The actor update in SRL is formalized as a variant of a primal-dual algorithm previously introduced for empirical risk minimization, enhanced with an additional sampling step.
- **Policies as Regularized Optimization:** Policies are viewed as solutions to regularized optimization problems over the distribution simplex, mapping a state $s$ to a direction vector $\theta$ and then to a distribution over actions $q = \nabla \Omega^*_{\Delta_{A(s)}}(A(s)^T \phi_w(s))$.
- **Tractable Updates:** The sampling step, which evaluates multiple candidate actions using the critic, addresses the intractability of direct optimization over a potentially non-linear critic function in combinatorial spaces. This approach ensures tractable updates for the actor through the Fenchel-Young loss.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of SRL was evaluated across six diverse environments, including three static and three dynamic problems, representing common industrial applications with combinatorial action spaces.

#### 4.1. Studied Environments

- **Static Environments:**
 - Warcraft Shortest Paths Problem (WSPP)
 - Single Machine Scheduling Problem (SMSP)
 - Stochastic Vehicle Scheduling Problem (SVSP)
- **Dynamic Environments (Focus of detailed discussion):**
 - Dynamic Vehicle Scheduling Problem (DVSP) with exogenous uncertainty.
 - Dynamic Assortment Problem (DAP) with endogenous uncertainty.
 - Gridworld Shortest Paths Problem (GSPP) with endogenous uncertainty.

#### 4.2. Experimental Setup

SRL was compared against:
- **Structured Imitation Learning (SIL):** A structured learning approach using Fenchel-Young losses but relying on expert supervision.
- **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):** A widely used unstructured RL algorithm.
- **Performance References:** An expert policy (optimal offline or best possible online) and a greedy policy.
All algorithms utilized identical COAML pipelines and shared critic architectures (for RL methods) to ensure a controlled comparison. Training was conducted for the same number of episodes, with hyperparameters tuned per algorithm and environment. Each algorithm was retrained using ten random seeds to assess robustness.

#### 4.3. Numerical Results (Dynamic Environments)

- **Performance:**
 - **DVSP:** SRL performed comparably to SIL. SRL outperformed PPO by 16%.
 - **DAP:** SRL outperformed SIL by 8% and PPO by 77%.
 - **GSPP:** SRL significantly outperformed SIL by 78% and PPO by 92%. In this environment, SRL also surpassed the online optimum by 79%.
 - **General Observations:** SRL consistently outperformed unstructured deep reinforcement learning (PPO) across all dynamic problems. PPO generally struggled, often failing to reach the performance level of greedy policies. While SIL performed well, its reliance on expert supervision constrained its performance to that of the expert policy and limited its exploration in multi-stage problems.

- **Convergence Speed:**
 - PPO exhibited the slowest convergence, requiring approximately 200 episodes in DVSP and 160 in GSPP to plateau.
 - SRL and SIL showed similar convergence rates in DVSP. In DAP, SIL converged roughly 150 episodes faster, and in GSPP, 10 episodes faster than SRL, which is attributed to SIL's access to expert demonstrations. SRL's learning from interaction naturally resulted in a slightly slower initial convergence.

- **Stability:**
 - PPO displayed the highest variance across all dynamic environments, up to 80 times higher than SRL and 40 times higher than SIL. This highlights the challenges of unstructured RL in combinatorial settings.
 - SRL and SIL demonstrated consistently low variance, indicating the robustness of structured approaches in these problems.

- **Computational Cost:**
 - SRL required approximately 30 minutes of training per environment.
 - SIL and PPO generally had shorter runtimes.
 - This difference is attributed to the frequency of CO-layer invocations: PPO used it twice per update, SIL 20 times, and SRL up to 61 times. Runtime also varied with the complexity of the CO-layer in each environment.

#### 4.4. Numerical Results (Static Environments - Appendix E)

- SRL's performance was competitive with SIL, with both algorithms closely approaching the expert policy.
- SRL consistently outperformed PPO by 5% to 54%. PPO tended to converge to near-greedy policies and struggled significantly in the Stochastic Vehicle Scheduling Problem.
- SRL and SIL exhibited low variance, contrasting sharply with PPO, which showed up to 400 times higher variance, especially in more stochastic and complex environments.
- Runtimes followed a similar pattern, with SRL incurring higher computational costs when CO-layers were more complex, albeit remaining within reasonable limits for most static tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces Structured Reinforcement Learning (SRL) as a method addressing the challenges of combinatorial decision-making in real-world industrial applications. The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

1. **Addressing Core C-MDP Challenges:** SRL provides a concrete approach to overcome the limitations of traditional RL algorithms in Combinatorial Markov Decision Processes (C-MDPs). It directly tackles the issues of exponential action space size and the difficulty of exploiting combinatorial structure, which are prevalent in complex routing, scheduling, and resource allocation problems.

2. **Enabling End-to-End Learning without Expert Dependence:** A notable impact of SRL is its capability to train COAML pipelines end-to-end using only collected experience. This eliminates the need for expert demonstrations, which are often costly, scarce, or impossible to obtain in real-world settings. This contrasts with Structured Imitation Learning (SIL), which relies heavily on such expert data.

3. **Enhanced Performance and Stability in Dynamic Settings:** The consistent outperformance of SRL over unstructured RL baselines (PPO) in dynamic environments, with performance gains up to 92%, demonstrates its practical utility. Its improved stability and convergence speed compared to unstructured RL are critical for deploying robust decision-making systems in uncertain and evolving real-world scenarios. The ability to surpass even online optimal policies in some dynamic environments (e.g., GSPP) further highlights its effectiveness.

4. **Robustness in Varied Environments:** SRL's competitive performance against SIL in static environments, and its superior performance in dynamic settings, indicates its versatility. The consistently low variance observed across environments for SRL, in contrast to the high variance of PPO, underscores the inherent robustness introduced by its structured learning approach.

5. **Theoretical Advancements:** The geometric interpretation of SRL as a sampling-based primal-dual algorithm contributes to the theoretical understanding of structured learning in RL. This connection provides insights into how continuous optimization techniques can be leveraged for discrete decision problems, opening avenues for further theoretical development in this interdisciplinary field.

6. **Practical Applicability for Industrial Problems:** By offering a scalable and effective solution for problems with large combinatorial action spaces and various forms of uncertainty (exogenous and endogenous), SRL holds significant potential for real-world deployment in areas like logistics, manufacturing, and supply chain management. While it incurs a higher computational cost compared to baselines due to CO-layer invocations, this overhead is presented as a reasonable trade-off for the substantial performance and stability gains in complex scenarios.

In conclusion, SRL represents an advancement in the application of reinforcement learning to complex combinatorial problems, offering a principled method for learning effective and robust policies from interaction, thereby expanding the applicability of RL in industrial contexts where structured decision-making is paramount.
