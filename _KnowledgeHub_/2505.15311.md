# Trajectory Bellman Residual Minimization: A Simple Value-Based Method for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UW-Madison and MIT present Trajectory Bellman Residual Minimization (TBRM), a value-based reinforcement learning algorithm for enhancing Large Language Model reasoning. TBRM achieves competitive or superior performance on mathematical reasoning benchmarks compared to policy-based methods, while offering improved computational efficiency and simpler implementation.

## Problem

- Policy-based reinforcement learning methods, commonly used for LLM reasoning, incur high computational costs due to the requirement for on-policy rollouts.
- Current policy-based approaches demand complex architectures, including auxiliary critic models, advantage estimation, and clipping mechanisms, increasing implementation complexity.
- Assigning credit for sparse, end-of-trajectory rewards (e.g., in mathematical problem-solving) is challenging for existing methods, often leading to imprecise token-level attribution.

## Method

- The paper introduces Trajectory Bellman Residual Minimization (**TBRM**), an algorithm that minimizes a single ==Bellman residual== over entire trajectories instead of per-step errors.
- TBRM directly leverages the LLM's own logits to parameterize Q-values and derived policy probabilities, eliminating the need for a separate critic model.
- The method enables fully off-policy optimization, allowing efficient learning from diverse data sources without the computational overhead of frequent on-policy rollouts or importance sampling ratios.

## Results

- TBRM consistently matched or surpassed the accuracy of PPO and GRPO across multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks, including AIME24/25, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva-Math, and OlympiadBench.
- Using a single rollout per prompt (`TBRM_n=1`), the method achieved higher accuracies than `PPO_n=1` and performed comparably to `GRPO_n=4` while reducing GPU memory usage by **33.3%** and achieving a **1.3x** speedup.
- Ablation studies confirmed that directly applying classical, token-level Bellman Residual Minimization to LLMs resulted in unstable training and reward collapse, validating TBRM's trajectory-level adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Value-based reinforcement learning can be effectively adapted for LLM reasoning tasks by reformulating Bellman Residual Minimization to operate at the trajectory level.
> - An improved change-of-trajectory-measure analysis demonstrates that trajectory-level objectives in TBRM incur only state-wise distribution shift costs, avoiding the 'curse of horizon' and facilitating efficient off-policy learning.
> - TBRM's objective fosters the emergence of sophisticated, human-like reasoning patterns in LLMs, such as problem decomposition, systematic enumeration, verification, and backtracking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of LLM reasoning has seen rapid advancements, primarily driven by larger pre-training corpora and sophisticated fine-tuning techniques. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for enhancing LLM reasoning, especially in domains like mathematical problem-solving where objective correctness can be verified.

**Dominance of Policy-Based Methods:** The current landscape for LLM post-training, particularly for reasoning, is overwhelmingly dominated by policy-based RL variants such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017b) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). These methods optimize policies to maximize objective rewards, leveraging the clear evaluability of mathematical tasks.

**Challenges with Policy-Based Methods:** Despite their empirical success, policy-based methods present several practical challenges:
1. **Computational Demand:** They typically require fresh on-policy rollouts from the current model, which is computationally expensive for large models.
2. **Complexity:** They often necessitate additional components like critic models (for value estimation), advantage normalization, and clipping mechanisms, increasing implementation complexity and tuning effort.
3. **Credit Assignment:** For token-level decisions in LLMs, policy-based methods often simplistically attribute outcome-based rewards (e.g., correctness of the entire response) to individual tokens, potentially assigning credit primarily to the final token.
4. **Bootstrapping Issues:** When critic models are used, advantages are bootstrapped with truncated rollout horizons, which can compromise effective credit assignment.

**Underexplored Value-Based Approaches:** In contrast, value-based methods, which learn an action-value function Q and derive a policy from it, have been largely unexplored in the LLM context. The paper highlights a natural alignment: LLM logits can inherently serve as a parametric family for Q-values within the KL-regularized RL framework (Schulman et al., 2017a). The authors conjecture that this under-exploration is partly due to the perceived difficulty in adapting traditional iterative bootstrapping algorithms (like Q-learning) to the scale and stability requirements of LLM training.

TBRM directly addresses this gap by revisiting classical Bellman Residual Minimization and adapting it to the trajectory level for LLMs. It positions itself as a principled, efficient, and theoretically grounded alternative, aiming to offer the advantages of value-based learning without the complexities of policy-based counterparts. The work thus contributes to diversifying the RL methodologies applied to LLMs, potentially opening new avenues for simpler and more scalable alignment and reasoning enhancement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop a simple, effective, and theoretically sound value-based reinforcement learning algorithm to enhance the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models**, particularly for multi-step tasks like mathematical problem-solving.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several key observations and challenges in the current LLM-RL landscape:
1. **Addressing Policy-Based Complexities:** The authors are motivated by the practical challenges inherent in prevalent policy-based methods (PPO, GRPO), which include high computational costs due to on-policy rollouts, reliance on auxiliary critic models, complex advantage estimation, and policy clipping. They seek a simpler, more streamlined approach.
2. **Leveraging LLM Architecture for Value-Based RL:** LLMs inherently produce logits for token generation, which naturally align with the concept of Q-values in a KL-regularized RL framework. The motivation is to exploit this natural fit to design a value-based method that seamlessly integrates with LLM architectures.
3. **Overcoming Perceived Instability of Value-Based Methods:** The authors acknowledge a prevailing belief that traditional iterative value-based methods (like Q-learning) might be less stable or harder to scale than optimization-style policy-based methods when applied to large-scale LLM training. A key motivation is to demonstrate that a well-designed value-based approach can overcome these perceived limitations.
4. **Improving Credit Assignment:** Policy-based methods often struggle with precise credit assignment when only sparse, end-of-trajectory rewards are available (e.g., a mathematical solution being correct or incorrect). TBRM aims to provide a more effective and principled way to propagate these rewards throughout the entire reasoning trajectory.
5. **Achieving Off-Policy Efficiency:** A core motivation is to enable fully off-policy optimization, which would remove the need for frequent, expensive on-policy rollouts, thereby reducing computational and memory overhead.
6. **Providing Theoretical Guarantees:** Beyond empirical performance, the authors are motivated to provide strong theoretical guarantees for their algorithm, particularly convergence to a near-optimal KL-regularized policy from arbitrary off-policy data, building on recent advances in theoretical RL.

In summary, the paper aims to introduce an algorithm that is computationally efficient, conceptually simple, robust, and theoretically sound, offering a compelling alternative to the current policy-based paradigm for LLM reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method is **Trajectory Bellman Residual Minimization (TBRM)**, a novel value-based RL algorithm adapted specifically for LLMs.

#### 3.1. Bellman Residual Minimization Foundation

The starting point for TBRM is the classical idea of **Bellman Residual Minimization (BRM)** (Schweitzer and Seidmann, 1985; Baird et al., 1995). In deterministic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), BRM aims to fit a Q-function by directly minimizing its Bellman residual—the difference between the current Q-value estimate and the target Q-value derived from the Bellman equation.
The classical BRM objective ($L_{BRM}(θ)$) minimizes the squared per-step Bellman error:
$L_{BRM}(θ) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{\tau \in D} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \left[ \text{logit}_{\theta}(s_h, a_h) - \left( \frac{r(s_h, a_h)}{\beta} + \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(s_h, a_h) + \log \sum_{a \in A} \exp(\text{logit}_{\theta}(s_{h+1}, a)) \right) \right]^2$
However, directly applying this per-step BRM to LLMs is challenging because token-level rewards $r(s_h, a_h)$ are typically sparse or unavailable, with only a final outcome reward $r(\tau)$ being provided. This makes effective credit assignment at each token problematic for classical BRM.

#### 3.2. Trajectory-Level Adaptation for LLMs (TBRM)

The key innovation of TBRM is to lift the Bellman residual minimization from the step (token) level to the **trajectory level**. It minimizes a single residual that spans the entire rollout. The TBRM loss ($L_{TBRM}(θ)$) is defined as:
$L_{TBRM}(θ) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{\tau \in D} \left[ \text{logit}_{\theta}(s_1, a_1) - \left( \frac{r(\tau)}{\beta} - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau) + \sum_{h=2}^{H} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_h | s_h) \right) \right]^2$
This formulation has several critical advantages for LLMs:
- **Outcome-Based Rewards:** It only requires the trajectory outcome reward $r(\tau)$, eliminating the need for dense, token-level rewards. The credit assignment is implicitly handled by the trajectory-level objective.
- **Critic-Free Design:** The algorithm uses the LLM's own logits as Q-values and derived policy probabilities ($\pi_\theta(a_h | s_h)$) directly in the objective. This removes the need for a separate critic model, simplifying the architecture and reducing memory overhead.
- **Off-Policy Optimization:** TBRM is inherently an off-policy algorithm. This means it can learn from data generated by *any* behavior policy (potentially older versions of the model or other data sources) without requiring computationally expensive importance-sampling ratios or clipping mechanisms. It can operate effectively with just one rollout per prompt.
- **Autoregressive Function Approximation:** The paper leverages the autoregressive nature of LLMs, where the model's logits for the next token ($Q_\theta(s,a)$) naturally parameterize the Q-function. The policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ and value function $V_\theta(s)$ are derived directly from these logits via a Boltzmann distribution: $\log \pi_{\theta}(a | s) = Q_{\theta}(s, a) - V_{\theta}(s)$, where $V_\theta(s) = \log \sum_{a'} \exp(Q_\theta(s,a'))$.

**Algorithm 1: Training Loop:**
The training process for TBRM (Algorithm 1) is straightforward:
1. Initialize the LLM policy parameters $\theta$ from a reference policy $\theta_{\text{ref}}$.
2. In each iteration:
 - Sample a batch of prompts.
 - For each prompt, generate a trajectory (response) using the current policy $\pi_\theta$.
 - Collect these trajectories into a dataset $D_t$.
 - Update $\theta$ via gradient descent to minimize the $L_{TBRM}(θ)$ loss.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Underpinnings

A significant contribution of the paper is its theoretical analysis, which proves convergence properties for TBRM.
- **Realizability Assumption:** The analysis assumes that an optimal Q-function ($Q^{\star}$) can be represented by the model's parameterization ($\theta^{\star}$).
- **Population Minimizer:** The authors show that the optimal Q-function $Q^{\star}$ (or $\theta^{\star}$) is a deterministic minimizer of the population TBRM loss (i.e., $L_{TBRM}(\theta^{\star}) = 0$).
- **Performance Difference Lemma:** They leverage a "soft performance difference lemma" (Corollary 4) to bound the sub-optimality of the learned policy ($\pi_{\hat{\theta}}$) in terms of the expected trajectory Bellman residual.
- **Improved Change-of-Trajectory-Measure Analysis:** Crucially, the paper addresses concerns about the "curse of horizon" in trajectory-level objectives. Building on (Jia et al., 2025), they provide an improved "change-of-trajectory-measure lemma" (Lemma 1). This lemma demonstrates that the trajectory-level objective in TBRM only incurs a *state-wise* distribution shift cost, rather than an exponential trajectory-level shift, thus avoiding the curse of horizon and enabling efficient convergence with off-policy data.
- **Convergence Guarantee (Theorem 2):** Under standard assumptions, the algorithm is proven to converge to a near-optimal KL-regularized policy, with a sub-optimality bound that depends polynomially on the horizon $H$, the concentrability coefficient of the data distribution, and the optimization error.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of TBRM on standard mathematical reasoning benchmarks demonstrates its effectiveness and efficiency compared to existing policy-based methods.

#### 4.1. Performance on Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks

- **Benchmarks:** The models were evaluated on AIME24/25, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva-Math, and OlympiadBench.
- **Model Sizes:** Experiments were conducted with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B.
- **Comparison with Baselines:** TBRM was compared against PPO and GRPO, which are widely used policy-based methods for LLM reasoning.
- **Key Results (Table 1):**
 - **Competitive or Superior Accuracy:** TBRM consistently matched or surpassed PPO and GRPO across all benchmarks.
 - **Efficiency with Single Rollout:** `TBRM_n=1` (one rollout per prompt) achieved higher accuracies than `PPO_n=1` on most benchmarks and performed comparably to `GRPO_n=4` (which uses four times as many samples), especially with the 1.5B model.
 - **Scaling with More Rollouts:** `TBRM_n=4` (four rollouts) further improved performance, achieving up to 30.5% accuracy on AIME24 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B, surpassing `GRPO_n=4` by 1.6% absolute on average.
- **Convergence Rate (Figure 1a):** TBRM exhibited a superior convergence rate and achieved higher absolute performance earlier in training compared to its counterparts.

#### 4.2. Training Dynamics and Efficiency

- **Reward Curves (Figure 2a):** TBRM showed comparable or faster reward acquisition during training, particularly noticeable with the smaller 1.5B model, where it attained significantly higher rewards than PPO and GRPO.
- **Response Length (Figure 1b):** Consistent with improved reasoning, TBRM encouraged the model to generate longer, more elaborate responses during training (when using the DeepSeek-R1 prompt template), suggesting it learns to explore and refine reasoning steps more deeply.
- **Computational and Memory Overhead (Figure 2b):**
 - **Simplicity Advantage:** TBRM's design eliminated the need for critic models, advantage estimates, importance ratios, and clipping, leading to a much simpler implementation.
 - **Resource Savings (n=1):** `TBRM_n=1` used 33.3% less GPU memory and achieved a 1.3x speedup compared to `PPO_n=1`.
 - **Comparable to GRPO (n=4):** `TBRM_n=4` demonstrated comparable resource usage to `GRPO_n=4`.
 - **Significant Speedup (TBRM_n=1 vs GRPO_n=4):** `TBRM_n=1` achieved a 3.1x training speedup over `GRPO_n=4` while yielding similar performance (average performance gap of only 0.8-1.27%).

#### 4.3. Qualitative Analysis of Emergent Reasoning Patterns

- **Human-like Reasoning:** Analysis of responses revealed that TBRM models learned emergent reasoning patterns common in human problem-solving:
 - **Decomposition:** Breaking down complex problems into smaller, manageable subproblems.
 - **Enumeration:** Systematically iterating through possibilities.
 - **Verification:** Self-monitoring by checking intermediate results.
 - **Backtracking:** Reassessing and revising steps upon encountering errors.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies and Generalizability

- **Classical BRM Instability:** An ablation study showed that directly applying classical, token-level BRM to LLMs led to unstable training and reward collapse, underscoring the importance of TBRM's trajectory-level objective for sparse reward settings.
- **Beyond Math:** TBRM was evaluated on non-mathematical graph-based reasoning tasks from reasoning-gym, demonstrating its ability to generalize and perform competitively with GRPO in diverse reasoning domains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Trajectory Bellman Residual Minimization (TBRM) carries significant implications for the field of LLM reasoning and the broader application of reinforcement learning to language models.

1. **Validation of Value-Based RL for LLMs:** TBRM provides compelling evidence that value-based RL, often sidelined in favor of policy-based methods for LLMs, is a powerful and viable alternative. It demonstrates that the inherent structure of LLMs (logits as Q-values) can be effectively leveraged, potentially catalyzing renewed interest and research into value-based paradigms for LLM alignment and reasoning.

2. **Simplified and Efficient RL Training:** One of the most impactful aspects of TBRM is its inherent simplicity and efficiency. By eliminating the need for separate critic models, importance-sampling ratios, and policy clipping mechanisms, it dramatically reduces implementation complexity, memory footprint, and computational overhead. Its ability to operate effectively with a single rollout per prompt further enhances its efficiency, making advanced RL fine-tuning more accessible and scalable, especially for resource-constrained environments or larger models. This directly addresses some of the major practical bottlenecks of current methods like PPO and GRPO.

3. **Strong Theoretical Foundations:** The paper's robust theoretical analysis, particularly the convergence proof to a near-optimal policy and the improved change-of-trajectory-measure lemma, provides a solid scientific grounding for TBRM. This theoretical rigor, which demonstrates efficient learning from off-policy data without incurring the "curse of horizon," distinguishes TBRM from many ad-hoc or empirically driven LLM-RL techniques and could inspire more theoretically sound approaches in future work.

4. **Enhanced Reasoning Capabilities:** Empirically, TBRM achieves competitive or superior performance on challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Furthermore, the qualitative analysis reveals that TBRM encourages LLMs to develop sophisticated, human-like reasoning patterns such as decomposition, verification, backtracking, and enumeration. This suggests that value-based methods can not only achieve high accuracy but also foster more robust and interpretable reasoning processes in LLMs.

5. **Broadened Applicability:** The demonstration of TBRM's effectiveness beyond mathematical problems, on diverse graph-based reasoning tasks, indicates its generalizability. This suggests that the principles behind TBRM could be applicable to a wider array of multi-step decision-making problems faced by LLMs, including complex planning, strategic interactions, and long-horizon tasks.

In conclusion, TBRM offers a principled, efficient, and simple framework for enhancing LLM reasoning. Its success could lead to a paradigm shift, complementing or even challenging the dominance of policy-based methods, and paving the way for more resource-efficient and theoretically sound approaches to developing advanced reasoning capabilities in large language models.
