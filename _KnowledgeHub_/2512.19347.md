# OMP: One-step Meanflow Policy with Directional Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> OMP introduces a generative policy framework for robot manipulation that achieves high-fidelity, single-step action generation by addressing spectral bias and gradient starvation in MeanFlow, using a novel Directional Alignment mechanism and an efficient Differential Derivation Equation (DDE). The system improves average success rates by 9.4% over MP1 on medium Meta-World tasks and 10-15% on real-world tasks while maintaining real-time inference.

## Problem

- High inference latency in diffusion models for robot control due to multi-step iterative denoising.
- Theoretical pathologies in direct MeanFlow application, including spectral bias hindering precise directional adjustments and gradient starvation in low-velocity regimes.
- Prohibitive memory complexity from computing Jacobian-Vector Products (JVPs) for total derivatives in MeanFlow, limiting model size and batch sizes.

## Method

- Introduced Directional Alignment Learning (LDAL_{DA}LDA) to decouple magnitude and direction, maximizing cosine similarity between predicted and true velocities, thereby countering spectral bias and gradient starvation.
- Implemented a Differential Derivation Equation (DDE) approximation using finite differences to estimate time derivatives, which eliminates the need for memory-intensive JVP computations.
- Developed a composite loss function combining MSE, Dispersive Loss, and the new Directional Alignment Loss for robustly learning both ballistic transit motions and high-precision contact phases.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **82.3%** across Adroit and Meta-World tasks, outperforming MP1 (**78.9%**) and FlowPolicy (**71.6%**), with up to **10.6%** improvement over MP1 on 'Very Hard' Meta-World tasks.
- Demonstrated superior robustness in real-world tasks, showing **10%** improvement in 'Place Bottle' and 'Clean Table' and **15%** improvement in 'Slip Ring' compared to the strongest baseline, MP1.
- The OMP-DDE variant provided significant GPU memory savings (e.g., ~7.5GB for a prediction horizon of 16) compared to OMP-JVP, while maintaining competitive performance with an **80.8%** average success rate.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MeanFlow's integral formulation inherently acts as a low-pass filter (spectral bias) and suffers from gradient starvation in low-velocity regimes, necessitating explicit directional learning for precise robot manipulation.
> - Approximating Jacobian-Vector Products (JVPs) with a finite difference-based Differential Derivation Equation (DDE) can significantly reduce memory overhead, making it feasible to train complex networks for high-dimensional inputs like point clouds.
> - Explicitly aligning the direction of predicted velocities, even when magnitudes are small, is crucial for stable and high-fidelity single-step generative policies, particularly in tasks requiring fine movements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation has transitioned towards data-driven generative policy frameworks to model complex, multimodal action distributions. This shift is broadly categorized into two main approaches: diffusion models and flow-based methods.

Diffusion models, such as Diffusion Policy (DP) and its 3D extension DP3, operate by formulating control as a probabilistic denoising process, iteratively refining action trajectories. These models generally achieve high fidelity in action generation. However, their reliance on multi-step iterative denoising leads to high inference latency, which can be a limiting factor for real-time, high-frequency robot control.

Flow-based methods and Consistency models, including approaches like AdaFlow, FlowPolicy, and ManiFlow, aim to mitigate the latency issue. They typically leverage Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) or consistency distillation to learn vector fields that transform noise into actions, enabling rapid, often single-step, inference. While effective in reducing delays, these methods frequently require complex training pipelines involving segmented flows or auxiliary consistency constraints. These architectural demands can impose significant constraints on the model, affecting its generalizability across novel scenarios.

A recent theoretical development, MeanFlow, offers an alternative by modeling interval-averaged velocities directly, thereby circumventing the need for ODE solvers entirely. Its application in robotics, exemplified by MP1, demonstrates single-step inference with low latency while achieving competitive success rates.

This paper, OMP, positions itself within this landscape by building upon the MeanFlow paradigm. It identifies specific theoretical and computational pathologies that impede the direct and robust application of MeanFlow to high-precision robotic manipulation. OMP proposes solutions to these identified limitations to combine the efficiency of single-step generation with high-fidelity and robustness, aiming to overcome the persistent trade-off between latency and architectural complexity in current generative policy frameworks. The work also leverages 3D point cloud representations, a common approach in modern robot learning to resolve spatial ambiguity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel generative policy framework for robot manipulation, termed One-step MeanFlow Policy (OMP), that achieves both high-fidelity action generation and real-time, single-step inference. This objective is driven by the recognized limitations and trade-offs in existing data-driven generative policy frameworks for robotics.

Specifically, the motivations stem from addressing three critical pathologies identified when attempting to apply the MeanFlow paradigm directly to robotic manipulation tasks:

1. **Overcoming High Inference Latency of Diffusion Models**: Diffusion models, despite their high-fidelity outputs, require numerous function evaluations (NFEs) during inference due to their iterative denoising process. This multi-step requirement results in latency that is often prohibitive for real-time robot control, where actions must be generated at high frequencies. Flow-based and consistency models address this, but often introduce architectural complexities or constraints. MeanFlow offers a path to single-step inference, making it an attractive foundation for low-latency control.

2. **Addressing Theoretical Pathologies in MeanFlow's Direct Application to Robotics**:
 - **Spectral Bias**: The MeanFlow objective, which models interval-averaged velocity through an integral formulation, acts inherently as a low-pass filter. This attenuates high-frequency directional adjustments, which are crucial for the rapid and fine-grained movements required in high-precision manipulation tasks. The integral averages out the dynamic nuances, providing coarse guidance but hindering the learning of precise, fast adjustments.
 - **Gradient Starvation in Low-Velocity Regimes**: In tasks demanding slow, precise movements (where target velocities approach zero), the standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function for MeanFlow suffers from "gradient starvation." The gradient responsible for correcting the direction of the predicted velocity is multiplicatively coupled to the target magnitude. As the target magnitude approaches zero, the directional gradient also diminishes, leading the optimization process to prioritize collapsing the predicted velocity's magnitude rather than accurately aligning its direction. This results in policies that fail to execute necessary fine-grained directional corrections.

3. **Mitigating Memory Complexity of Higher-Order Differentiation**: The MeanFlow Identity requires computing total derivatives that involve Jacobian-Vector Products (JVPs). In standard automatic differentiation frameworks, computing the gradients of a JVP requires nesting reverse-mode AD over forward-mode AD, which necessitates storing a significantly larger computational graph. This increased memory complexity scales with tangent dimensionality and becomes a prohibitive bottleneck for training large-scale neural network backbones (e.g., for processing high-dimensional point clouds) with sufficient batch sizes or temporal horizons on typical hardware.

By resolving these three limitations, OMP aims to enable robust single-step action generation that combines the precision typically associated with diffusion models with the speed of flow-based methods, all while maintaining a computationally efficient training process suitable for complex robotic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The One-step MeanFlow Policy (OMP) framework is designed to generate single-step trajectories from 3D point-cloud inputs, building on the MeanFlow paradigm. The core of OMP involves addressing the identified theoretical and computational limitations through two primary mechanisms: Directional Alignment Learning and an efficient Differential Derivation Equation (DDE).

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: The MeanFlow Framework

OMP utilizes the MeanFlow framework, which estimates interval-averaged velocities $u(z_t, r, t)$ directly, rather than instantaneous velocities. This formulation bypasses multi-step numerical integration, enabling single-step inference (NFE=1). The true mean velocity $v_0$ is defined as the vector connecting the initial noise distribution $z_T$ to the target state $z_0$. The model $u_\theta(z_t, r, t|c)$ is optimized to predict the average velocity between randomly sampled timesteps $t$ and $r$, based on the MeanFlow Identity. The standard objective minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the prediction and a target velocity $u_{tgt}$, and previous work (MP1) augments this with a Dispersive Loss $L_{Disp}$.

#### 3.2. Addressing Theoretical Pathologies

##### 3.2.1. Directional Alignment Learning

To counter the spectral bias and gradient starvation issues, OMP introduces a Directional Alignment mechanism. This mechanism explicitly decouples the learning of magnitude from the learning of direction.
- **Mechanism**: The Directional Alignment loss, $L_{DA}$, is formulated to maximize the cosine similarity between the predicted velocity $u(z_t, r, t|c)$ and the true mean velocity $v_0$. Specifically, $L_{DA} = -\log\left(\frac{\cos \alpha + 1}{2}\right)$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between $v_0$ and $u$.
- **Benefit**: This objective ensures that a non-vanishing directional correction gradient is maintained even when the magnitude of the true mean velocity $||v_0||$ approaches zero. A small constant $\epsilon_{dir}$ is added to $||v_0||$ for numerical stability. By directly aligning the predicted mean velocity's direction with the true mean velocity, OMP aims to bypass the spectral bias, allowing for the learning of precise directional adjustments across the frequency spectrum.
- **Composite Loss Function**: The overall OMP training objective combines three components: the standard MSE reconstruction loss ($L_{mse}$), the Dispersive Loss ($L_{Disp}$) from MP1 (encouraging latent space feature discrimination), and the new Directional Alignment Loss ($L_{DA}$). The final loss is $L = L_{mse} + \lambda_{Disp} \cdot L_{Disp} + \lambda_{DA} \cdot L_{DA}$, where $\lambda_{Disp}$ and $\lambda_{DA}$ are weighting hyperparameters. This composite loss is designed to robustly learn both ballistic transit motions (driven by $L_{mse}$) and high-precision contact phases (enhanced by $L_{DA}$).

##### 3.2.2. Efficient Optimization via Differential Derivation Equation (DDE)

To overcome the prohibitive memory costs associated with computing the exact Jacobian-Vector Product (JVP) operator required by the MeanFlow Identity, OMP implements a Differential Derivation Equation (DDE) approximation.
- **Mechanism**: Instead of relying on analytical differentiation to compute the total derivative $\frac{d}{dt} u$ (which involves a JVP and thus nested automatic differentiation), the DDE approximates this time derivative using a finite difference method. Specifically, $\frac{du_\theta(z_t, t, r|c)}{dt} \approx \frac{u_\theta(z_{t+\epsilon}, t+\epsilon, r|c) - u_\theta(z_{t-\epsilon}, t-\epsilon, r|c)}{2\epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is a perturbation constant.
- **Benefit**: This approximation effectively decouples the forward and backward passes of the neural network. Consequently, it eliminates the need to store the intermediate computational graph required for exact Jacobian calculations, significantly reducing memory overhead. This memory efficiency enables the training of deeper neural networks and the use of longer context windows or larger batch sizes, which would otherwise be computationally intractable on standard hardware, particularly when dealing with high-dimensional inputs like point clouds.

#### 3.3. Input Representation

For processing visual information, OMP utilizes 3D point-cloud data. This data is downsampled using the Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) strategy to manage computational load while retaining essential spatial structure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an extensive evaluation of OMP against state-of-the-art baselines across simulated and real-world robot manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Evaluations on Simulation Environments

Evaluations were conducted on 3 tasks from the Adroit benchmark and 34 tasks from the Meta-World benchmark (categorized into Easy, Medium, Hard, and Very Hard).
- **Overall Performance**: OMP, specifically its JVP variant (OMP-JVP), achieved an average success rate of 82.3% ± 1.6% across all tasks. This represents an improvement over the previous state-of-the-art method, MP1, which achieved 78.9% ± 2.1%, and FlowPolicy (71.6% ± 3.5%).
- **Performance on Challenging Tasks**: The improvement was particularly notable in more complex Meta-World tasks. OMP-JVP showed a 9.4% improvement over MP1 on Medium tasks, 4.4% on Hard tasks, and 10.6% on Very Hard tasks. The DDE variant (OMP-DDE) also yielded substantial improvements (8.4% on Medium, 2.9% on Hard, and 3.4% on Very Hard) compared to MP1 for these challenging categories.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Evaluations on Real-world Tasks

OMP was evaluated on three physical robot manipulation tasks: Place Bottle, Clean Table, and Slip Ring.
- **Superior Robustness**: OMP consistently outperformed all baselines (DP3, FlowPolicy, MP1). It achieved success rates of 80.0% for Place Bottle, 75.0% for Clean Table, and 70.0% for Slip Ring.
- **Significant Gains**: Compared to the strongest baseline, MP1, OMP demonstrated a 10% improvement in Place and Clean tasks and a 15% improvement in the more challenging Slip Ring task, indicating superior capability in handling complex real-world dynamic manipulation.

#### 4.3. JVP vs. DDE Comparison

A direct comparison between OMP-JVP (exact Jacobian-Vector Product) and OMP-DDE (Differential Derivation Equation approximation) revealed a trade-off.
- **Performance**: OMP-JVP generally achieved a slightly higher average success rate (82.3% vs. 80.8% for OMP-DDE), with more pronounced advantages in certain complex scenarios like Meta-World "Very Hard" tasks (77.8% vs. 70.6%).
- **Memory Efficiency**: OMP-DDE consistently offered substantial reductions in GPU memory usage. For example, in the "Place Bottle" task with a prediction horizon of 16, memory consumption decreased from 26.71GB (JVP) to 19.19GB (DDE), a saving of approximately 7.5GB. This efficiency gain is critical for larger inputs or longer action horizons, where JVP's memory overhead would become prohibitive.

#### 4.4. Training Efficiency and Stability

Training stability curves demonstrated that OMP maintained consistently high and stable training trajectories with rapid convergence and lower variance across different random seeds, particularly when compared to the significant volatility observed in baselines like FlowPolicy and DP3.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

Ablating the components of OMP's loss function clarified their contributions:
- **Directional Alignment (L_DA)**: The removal of $L_{DA}$ resulted in a substantial drop in average success rates for both OMP-JVP (to 78.9%) and OMP-DDE (to 77.2%). This indicates that $L_{DA}$ is critical for providing the necessary fundamental guidance for stable, high-fidelity single-step generation.
- **Dispersive Loss (L_dis)**: Removing $L_{dis}$ led to a minor decline in overall average success, with a more noticeable impact on complex tasks like Meta-World Hard tasks.
- **Combined Removal**: Eliminating both $L_{dis}$ and $L_{DA}$ further degraded performance significantly (e.g., on Adroit Pen, success rates dropped from 60% to 48% for JVP and from 64% to 55% for DDE), underscoring their synergistic importance.

#### 4.6. Sensitivity Analysis

- **Horizon Length**: OMP-JVP performed optimally at short and medium horizons, with performance degrading at longer horizons, suggesting susceptibility to compounding errors in extended open-loop sequences. Conversely, OMP-DDE showed enhanced robustness and improved performance at longer horizons, indicating better stability for long-range trajectory planning.
- **DDE Parameter $\epsilon$**: The parameter $\epsilon=0.005$ for the DDE approximation yielded optimal performance. Decreasing $\epsilon$ below this value led to a monotonic and significant degradation in success rates, particularly in dexterity-heavy tasks, highlighting the importance of an adequate lower bound for stable optimization.

#### 4.7. Failure Cases

Identified failure modes in real-world experiments included visual occlusions (due to a single depth camera and robotic arm self-occlusion) and limitations of open-loop action chunking, where the robot could not dynamically adjust to minor errors mid-trajectory.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The OMP framework presents a notable advancement in data-driven generative policies for robot manipulation, addressing long-standing trade-offs between generation fidelity and inference latency.

**Technical Contributions**:
- **Resolution of MeanFlow Pathologies**: The paper rigorously identifies and provides solutions to critical theoretical limitations (spectral bias and gradient starvation) and computational bottlenecks (memory complexity of JVP) inherent in applying the MeanFlow paradigm to robotics. This systematic approach enhances the robustness and precision of single-step generative policies.
- **Efficient High-Fidelity Control**: By introducing Directional Alignment, OMP enables the policy to learn and execute precise movements, even in low-velocity regimes, which are crucial for fine manipulation. Simultaneously, the Differential Derivation Equation (DDE) approximation significantly reduces the memory footprint, making it feasible to train and deploy complex neural networks for real-time control without requiring specialized hardware for higher-order differentiation.
- **New Performance Standard**: OMP establishes a new benchmark for efficient, single-step generative policy learning, consistently outperforming leading state-of-the-art baselines like MP1 and FlowPolicy in both simulated and real-world environments. Its superior performance, particularly in complex and high-precision tasks, while maintaining real-time inference speeds, marks a step forward in balancing these critical performance metrics.

**Potential Impact**:
- **Enabling Real-time Dexterous Manipulation**: The capability for high-fidelity, single-step inference at high frequencies can unlock new possibilities for real-time dexterous manipulation in various applications. This includes industrial automation requiring precise object handling and assembly, and potentially household robotics where responsive and accurate control is essential.
- **Increased Scalability and Accessibility**: The memory efficiency provided by the DDE approximation allows for the training of larger and more complex policy networks and longer action horizons on standard GPU hardware. This could make advanced generative policy learning more accessible to researchers and practitioners with limited computational resources, fostering further innovation.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: OMP's methodological innovations, particularly the Directional Alignment and DDE, provide a robust foundation for future research in one-step generative modeling for continuous control. It opens avenues for exploring even more complex tasks, diverse sensor modalities, and enhanced generalization capabilities in robot learning.

While the primary focus is on advancing embodied AI, the improvements in robust, high-frequency robot control contribute to the broader discourse on automation. The work emphasizes foundational algorithmic improvements, without introducing specific new ethical concerns beyond those generally recognized in machine learning and robotics regarding data, bias, or surveillance.
