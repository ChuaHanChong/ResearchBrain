# Ego-Vision World Model for Humanoid Contact Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Michigan, UC Berkeley, and The Chinese University of Hong Kong developed an ego-vision world model combined with value-guided sampling Model Predictive Control, enabling humanoid robots to perform diverse physical contact tasks using only ego-centric depth images. This approach improves data efficiency and demonstrates robust real-time performance on a physical robot across multiple contact-rich scenarios.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots face challenges in purposefully engaging in physical contact with their environment, often limited to collision avoidance rather than active interaction.
- Existing contact planning methods, including traditional optimization-based and on-policy reinforcement learning, suffer from computational intensity, model inaccuracy sensitivity, sample inefficiency, and limited multi-task generalization.
- Developing robust vision-based contact planning is difficult due to partial observability, sensor noise, and sparse rewards in real-world scenarios.

## Method

- A scalable ego-vision world model was developed, trained solely on a demonstration-free offline dataset, to predict future outcomes in a compressed latent space rather than raw pixel data.
- A value-guided sampling ==Model Predictive Control (MPC)== framework was introduced, utilizing a learned surrogate value function as a heuristic for robust planning from pixel-based observations.
- The framework integrates the world model with MPC to enable real-time, reactive contact planning on a physical humanoid robot using ego-centric depth images and proprioceptive feedback.

## Results

- The framework achieved robust real-time performance on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid robot, successfully executing tasks like wall bracing, object blocking, and arch traversal at 25 Hz using ego-centric depth images.
- It demonstrated superior sample efficiency, requiring approximately 0.5M data steps, significantly outperforming online reinforcement learning methods in complex visual tasks such as 'Traverse the Arch'.
- A single multi-task model exhibited comparable or improved performance across three distinct contact tasks and generalized to out-of-distribution scenarios, such as blocking a previously unseen object.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training a visual world model on a demonstration-free offline dataset is sufficient for learning complex contact dynamics, leading to significantly enhanced data efficiency compared to online reinforcement learning methods.
> - Utilizing a learned surrogate value function within a sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) provides robust planning signals, effectively guiding the robot in environments characterized by sparse rewards and sensor noise.
> - An N-step surrogate optimization objective within MPC can reduce the variance of value estimates, enhancing planning stability and performance even when using an imperfect learned value function.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a critical challenge in robotics: enabling humanoid robots to engage in purposeful physical contact with their environment, rather than merely avoiding collisions. This capability is considered essential for achieving greater autonomy and robustness in unstructured settings.

Historically, **traditional optimization-based planning methods** have been employed for robot control. These methods, which explicitly model physical interactions like friction and impacts (e.g., using Linear Complementarity Problems (LCP) or Cone Complementarity Problems (CCP) within trajectory optimization frameworks, or Hybrid Zero Dynamics (HZD)), face several limitations. They are often computationally intensive, sensitive to model inaccuracies, and struggle with the complexity of real-time contact scheduling, particularly for general, aperiodic whole-body contact scenarios.

More recently, **learning-based contact planning**, particularly through on-policy reinforcement learning (RL), has shown promise for dynamic robot skills. The advent of parallelized simulation environments has facilitated the training of such policies for various legged robots, including humanoids. However, on-policy RL methods typically suffer from significant sample inefficiency, which is exacerbated when incorporating visual inputs. They also exhibit limited multi-task adaptability, making it challenging to generalize across diverse interactions or varying task definitions. Furthermore, the sparse and discontinuous nature of contact events can complicate exploration for model-free methods, and some approaches rely on simplified environmental representations like 2.5D elevation maps.

This research situates itself within the emerging field of **planning with robotic world models**. World models provide a learned internal representation of an environment, allowing a robot to predict future outcomes in an abstract latent space. Integrating these models with model-based planning techniques offers a pathway to enhance generalization and sample efficiency in contact planning. While early world model frameworks aimed to accelerate policy learning, contemporary models have evolved into generative systems capable of simulating complex physical dynamics. Efforts to leverage neural dynamics models [27–30] within sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) for adaptive control are also part of this landscape. A persistent challenge for robotic world models, especially for contact planning, is their generalization capability, given that whole-body contact states are often partially observable and difficult to infer and predict from noisy, partial sensory data. This paper seeks to advance the integration of learned world models with MPC to address the aforementioned limitations for humanoid contact planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the aspiration for humanoid robots to advance beyond basic dynamic locomotion to intelligent interaction within complex, unstructured environments. To achieve this, humanoids must be able to exploit physical contact with their surroundings in a purposeful manner, mirroring human capabilities such as bracing against a wall for balance, blocking incoming objects for safety, or traversing height-limited obstacles. The existing limitations of traditional optimization-based methods (which struggle with computational cost, model inaccuracies, and generalization) and on-policy reinforcement learning (which suffers from sample inefficiency and limited multi-task capabilities, especially with visual inputs) necessitate novel approaches for robust and agile contact planning.

The key objectives of this work are articulated through three main contributions:
1. **To develop a scalable visual world model for dynamic robots:** This model is designed to capture the complex dynamics of diverse contact tasks. A central aspect is its training methodology, which relies entirely on a demonstration-free offline dataset, aiming to improve data efficiency and avoid the need for costly expert demonstrations. The model is specifically engineered to predict future outcomes in a compressed latent space rather than raw pixel data, which addresses issues like compounding errors over long horizons in pixel-based prediction.
2. **To introduce a planning framework that operates from pixel-based observations using value-guidance:** This involves a sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) system that is guided by a learned surrogate value function. This surrogate function aims to provide dense and robust planning signals, particularly in environments characterized by sparse contact rewards and sensor noise, where direct reward prediction can be challenging. The value function is intended to act as a powerful heuristic, rather than an optimal oracle, to mitigate issues arising from incomplete offline datasets and partial observability.
3. **To validate the proposed framework through agile and robust real-world visual contact planning:** The ultimate objective is to demonstrate the practical applicability and effectiveness of the framework on a physical humanoid robot. This validation focuses on showcasing novel contact planning capabilities, such as supporting a wall after perturbation, blocking incoming objects, and traversing archways, utilizing only ego-centric depth images and proprioceptive feedback. The goal is to prove that the system can achieve real-time performance and exhibit reactive, context-dependent behaviors in various contact-rich scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates a learned world model with sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) to enable vision-based contact planning for humanoid robots.

**A. Low-Level Controller**
The system operates on a hierarchical control structure. A pre-trained low-level controller handles motor commands. This controller is designed to track a command vector `c = [v^T, p^T_ee, h_body]^T`, which specifies the desired linear velocity, end-effector position, and body height, respectively. It relies solely on proprioceptive observations, including angular velocity, projected gravity vector, command, joint positions, and joint velocities. The controller is trained in simulation using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, following established methods.

**B. Data Collection**
To train the visual world model, an offline dataset `D` of object-interaction trajectories is collected in a simulated environment. This dataset is "demonstration-free," meaning it does not rely on expert or optimal robot behaviors. Instead, it is generated by applying randomly sampled high-level actions to the simulated humanoid, which is equipped with the trained low-level policy.
The data collection process involves:
- Simulating the robot across three distinct object types: a ball, a wall, and an arch.
- At each timestep `t`, the robot receives an observation `o_t` composed of a downsampled 64x48 ego-centric depth image and proprioceptive signals.
- The high-level action `a_t` consists of desired end-effector positions `p_ee` and body height `h_body`. The desired linear velocity `v` is intentionally excluded from the planner's action space to encourage the robot to solve contact-rich problems through postural adjustments.
- Actions are sampled as finite differences: `a_t = a_{t-1} + η ⋅ δ`, where `δ` is uniformly sampled from `(-1, 1)`, and `η = 0.32` controls the magnitude of the update. This method aims to generate varied but not entirely random or jittery behavior, making the data more suitable for learning without requiring demonstrations.
- Collected transition tuples `{o_t, a_t, r_t, d_t}` (observation, action, reward, termination signal) are stored as a trajectory dataset `[Batch, Time, Data]`.

**C. Ego-Vision Humanoid World Model**
The world model learns the environment's dynamics by predicting abstract latent states of future observations, rather than directly predicting pixels. This approach, inspired by models like Dreamer and JEPA, is designed to mitigate compounding errors common in long-horizon pixel predictions and to handle scenarios where defining pixel-space goals is difficult.
The world model's architecture (illustrated in Fig. 2) includes:
- **Recurrent Neural Network (RNN):** Maintains a deterministic dynamics latent state `h_t`. This state is updated autoregressively based on the previous latent state `h_{t-1}`, the previous stochastic latent state `z_{t-1}`, and the previous action `a_{t-1}` (Equation 1).
- **Stochastic Latent State `z_t`:** Inferred from the current observation `o_t` and the deterministic latent `h_t` via an encoder `q_ϕ` (Equation 2). This `z_t` acts as a compressed, abstract representation of the current observation. Both `z_t` and the reconstructed observation `ˆo_t` are modeled as Gaussian distributions.
- **Observation Decoder `p_ϕ`:** Reconstructs the original observation `o_t` as `ˆo_t` from `h_t` and `z_t` (Equation 3). This reconstruction objective compels `z_t` to encode salient features.
- **Latent Predictor:** An additional model that predicts the stochastic latent `ˆz_t` directly from `h_t` (Equation 4), enabling rollouts in the latent space without requiring an actual observation.
- **Specialized Heads:** To address challenges like partial observability, sensor noise, and sparse contact in robotics, the model includes two dedicated prediction heads conditioned on `(h_t, z_t)` and a candidate action `a_t`:
 - **Termination Probability `ˆd_t`:** Predicts the likelihood of robot failure (e.g., falling) (Equation 4).
 - **Surrogate Value `ˆQ_t`:** Estimates the expected cumulative return (Equation 5). This value function allows the robot to infer the current task context from observations and adapt its objective, enabling training on mixed multi-task datasets.
The model uses a CNN for image feature extraction and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) for other components.
The world model is optimized by minimizing a total loss `L_total = L_rec + L_jep + L_ˆQ` (Equation 6):
- `L_rec` (Reconstruction Loss): Combines Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) for `ˆo_t` and Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) for `ˆd_t` (Equation 7).
- `L_jep` (Joint-Embedding Predictive Loss): Consists of two KL divergence terms to ensure a consistent and non-collapsing latent space (Equation 8).
- `L_ˆQ` (Surrogate Value Loss): A Mean-Squared Error (MSE) term that trains `ˆQ_t` to estimate a Monte Carlo (MC) target `Q_target` (Equation 9). MC estimation was empirically found to provide more stable results than Temporal Difference (TD) error in this context.

**D. Value-Guided Sampling MPC**
The core planning mechanism is a Value-Guided Sampling MPC framework. This approach acknowledges that a learned value function `ˆQ` from finite offline data may be imperfect due to incomplete dataset coverage, partial observability, sensor noise, and action delays. Instead of treating `ˆQ` as an optimal oracle, it is used as a heuristic to guide a robust, receding-horizon planning process.
The framework performs an N-step surrogate optimization (Equation 11) to find the optimal action sequence `A*_t` that maximizes the sum of predicted surrogate Q-values over a planning horizon `N` (Equation 17). This objective `ˆJ_N` is defined as the average of `Q_ϕ(h_{t+k}, ˆz_{t+k}, a_{t+k})` over `N` steps, where future states are rolled out using the learned world model dynamics.
A theoretical analysis is presented (Equations 12-16) to argue that this N-step surrogate objective can reduce the variance of value estimates, especially when the correlation between `ˆQ` estimates at different time steps is weak, a condition likely met by using data generated from random actions. This variance reduction can improve performance even if the objective introduces some bias.
The planning process (Fig. 3) involves:
- Encoding the current observation `o_t` into `z_t`.
- Sampling a batch of `M=1024` candidate action sequences for a planning horizon of `N=4` steps (empirically determined to be optimal).
- Using the world model to recursively predict future latent states `(h_{t+k}, ˆz_{t+k})` and evaluate actions with `ˆQ_{t+k}`.
- A termination signal `ˆd_{t+k}` predicts robot failure; if its probability exceeds 0.9, subsequent values for that trajectory are zeroed.
- The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) is used to optimize the sampled trajectories and find the optimal action sequence `A*_t`.
- Only the first action `a*_t` from the optimal sequence is executed, and the process re-plans at the next timestep (receding horizon control), allowing continuous feedback incorporation and reaction to disturbances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed framework through extensive experiments conducted in a simulated environment and validated on a physical humanoid robot, the Unitree G1 equipped with a RealSense D435i camera.

**A. Evaluation Tasks**
Three distinct contact-rich tasks were designed to assess the model's capabilities:
1. **Support the Wall:** The robot must brace against a wall with its hands to maintain balance against external perturbations.
2. **Block the Ball:** The robot must intercept a flying object using its hands.
3. **Traverse the Arch:** The robot must pass through a low-clearance arch while avoiding head contact.

**B. Advantages of Offline Data and Sample Efficiency**
The proposed method, which leverages an offline, demonstration-free dataset, demonstrated significant sample efficiency compared to online on-policy reinforcement learning (PPO).
- The proposed method achieved task completion using approximately 0.5M data steps.
- In contrast, PPO required substantially more data and computational time, particularly in simulations involving visual rendering.
- While PPO could match the efficiency of the proposed method in tasks with simple visual features and minimal robot viewpoint changes (e.g., "Block the Ball"), the proposed method exhibited superior performance in tasks with complex visual representations and significant robot postural changes (e.g., "Traverse the Arch"), where the robot's perspective shifts dramatically.

**C. Multi-Task Capability**
The framework's ability to handle multiple tasks simultaneously was evaluated by training a single model on a combined dataset from all three tasks.
- Unlike online RL methods like PPO, which often struggle with multi-tasking due to challenges in reward engineering, catastrophic forgetting, and curriculum design, the offline approach circumvented these issues.
- The multi-task model achieved performance comparable to, or improved upon, specialized single-task models for two of the three tasks, with a minor decrease in performance for the "Block the Ball" task.
- Visualizations using t-SNE (Fig. 6b,c) confirmed that the model learned distinct latent space clusters for each task. The deterministic dynamics latent state `h_t` showed significant temporal evolution, indicating its role in encoding unique task dynamics, while the stochastic observation latent state `z_t` provided a compressed representation of the current environmental observation.

**D. Analysis of Key Design Choices**
Ablation studies were conducted (Table I) to assess the impact of core design elements:
- **Planning Horizon (N):** A short planning horizon (N=1), representing a greedy maximization of value, proved infeasible as it resulted in myopic behavior. Longer horizons (e.g., N=6) led to performance degradation, likely due to increased prediction bias. An intermediate horizon of N=4 was identified as the optimal "bias–variance sweet spot" across tasks.
- **World Model Training (ARWM):** Incorporating auto-regressive prediction, as in ARWM baselines, was found to be unnecessary and sometimes detrimental. It could lead to overfitting of the value function by overemphasizing precise prediction.
- **Objective Function:**
 - **Rew-MPC** (using direct reward prediction as the objective) yielded suboptimal results. This was attributed to the difficulty in predicting rewards accurately due to partial observability, sensor noise, action lag, and sparse contact signals.
 - **TD-MPC** (using TD-targets for trajectory evaluation) produced unstable results. The authors attributed this to TD-error methods potentially converging to "deceptive solutions" that satisfy the Bellman equation on incomplete offline datasets, even if the actual value estimates are inaccurate.
 - The proposed objective function based on summing predicted surrogate Q-values (`ˆJ_N`) demonstrated robustness.

**E. Model Interpretation and Visualization**
The research provided visualizations to offer insight into the model's internal decision-making:
- **Physical Intuition:** Long-horizon open-loop rollouts (up to 16 steps) demonstrated that the world model retained physically meaningful structure, such as predicting the parabolic motion of a ball (Fig. 7b), suggesting it learned genuine environment dynamics. This "physical intuition" did not, however, imply that a longer MPC planning horizon would automatically improve control performance.
- **Contact-Directed Planning:** Visualization of the Q-value map for the hand's target position (Fig. 7a) showed a dynamic and interpretable planning process. Initially, the value map encouraged a default, energy-efficient hand position. As a task-relevant event (e.g., a ball approaching) occurred, a high-value region dynamically emerged and sharpened, guiding the robot's hand towards the optimal contact point. This demonstrated the model's contact-directed reasoning.

**F. Real-World Validation**
The method was successfully deployed on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, achieving real-time planning at 25 Hz.
- Both single-task and multi-task models performed their assigned tasks robustly in the physical world.
- The policy demonstrated generalization capabilities to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, such as blocking a previously unseen box.
- The robot exhibited reactive, context-dependent behavior, for example, only bracing against a wall when perturbed and returning to a neutral stance once balance was recovered, indicating that the learned policy was not overfitting to fixed action patterns.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses key limitations in humanoid robot autonomy, particularly concerning physical interaction with the environment. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Robust Contact Exploitation:** The work demonstrates a method for humanoid robots to effectively exploit physical contact for stability and task completion, moving beyond collision avoidance. This capability is critical for robots operating in complex, unstructured real-world environments where active physical interaction is often necessary.
- **Improved Data Efficiency:** By leveraging a scalable ego-centric visual world model trained on a demonstration-free offline dataset, the framework significantly reduces the data requirements compared to traditional online reinforcement learning approaches. This is a substantial advantage, as collecting expert demonstrations or extensive online interaction data for complex humanoid tasks is typically expensive and time-consuming.
- **Enhanced Multi-Task Capability and Generalization:** The ability of a single world model to manage diverse contact tasks and generalize to unseen objects (e.g., blocking an unknown box) highlights a step towards more versatile and intelligent robotic systems. This contrasts with the challenges faced by online RL in multi-task scenarios, such as catastrophic forgetting and complex reward engineering.
- **Robust Real-time Vision-Based Planning:** The successful deployment on a physical humanoid robot, operating in real-time using ego-centric depth images and proprioception, validates the practical applicability of the approach. This demonstrates that robust control can be achieved directly from sensory inputs, addressing the difficulties of partial observability and sensor noise in real-world robotics.
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Methods:** The research systematically investigates and mitigates the shortcomings of both traditional optimization-based planners (computational cost, model sensitivity) and modern on-policy RL (sample inefficiency, multi-task limitations) by proposing a hybrid learning and planning architecture. The detailed analysis of the value-guided sampling MPC, particularly its variance reduction properties and robustness compared to reward-based or TD-error-based objectives, contributes to the understanding of planning with imperfect learned models.

Overall, this work contributes to advancing data-efficient, vision-based planning for real-world robotic interaction, offering a promising direction for future developments in humanoid robot autonomy and their deployment in complex human-centric environments.
