# Interventional Causal Circuits for Safe Robot Action Testing and Failure Recovery

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for robot action testing and failure recovery integrates interventional causal circuits with Joint Probability Trees, enabling causal diagnosis and targeted corrections for failed action proposals. This approach reduces failed attempts by up to 37% and recovery time by 2.2x, ensuring robust task completion even with weakened planning distributions.

## Problem

- Robot actions require computationally expensive pre-execution safety testing to ensure physical safety.
- Current systems typically resort to inefficient blind resampling of actions when a proposed plan fails a safety test.
- The lack of a principled failure diagnosis mechanism leads to wasted computational effort and no systematic path to convergence for failed plans.

## Method

- An **Interventional Causal Circuit** is constructed by extending a ==Joint Probability Tree (JPT)== with a ==Marginal-Deterministic Variable Tree (MdVtree)==, modeling the joint distribution of robot action parameters.
- When an action fails a safety test, the circuit causally diagnoses the primary failed parameter by computing ==interventional success probabilities==.
- Targeted corrective values for the identified parameter are recommended to maximize the probability of the revised action passing, which is then used for a ==one-shot re-attempt==.

## Results

- In experiments with a PR2 robot, the Causal Circuit reduced failed attempts by **10.3%** and average recovery time by **41%** when using a high-quality planning distribution.
- With a degraded planning distribution, the system reduced total failed attempts by **37%**, accelerated average recovery time by **2.2x**, and increased the task success rate from **99%** to **100%**.
- The framework consistently produced structured, interpretable causal reports for each rejected plan, detailing the primary cause, its observed value, and recommended corrective regions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating causal inference directly into robot planning-testing loops enables efficient and principled failure recovery from rejected action proposals.
> - Causal diagnosis and targeted corrections can be achieved by extending existing planning distributions (Joint Probability Trees) with a specific causal structure, without needing new training data or separate failure models.
> - Guaranteeing marginal determinism in the causal circuit ensures tractable and exact computation of interventional queries before robot operation begins, providing pre-runtime assurances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work addresses a core challenge in the deployment of physically safe autonomous systems: enabling robots to operate reliably within a continuous loop of proposing actions, testing them against safety constraints, and adapting when proposals fail. This "hypothesize, test, debug" paradigm is common in modern robot architectures, where a planner generates candidate actions and a separate testing stage evaluates their compliance with constraints such as collision, reachability, or stability.

The paper identifies a limitation in existing approaches where, upon a failed test, a new action is often blindly resampled from the planning distribution. This naive response is characterized as wasteful, uninformative, and lacking a mechanism for convergence. The authors argue that rejection should instead trigger a more principled causal diagnosis.

This research distinguishes itself from several related areas:
- **Classical model-based methods:** These often detect symbolic precondition violations and trigger replanning from scratch, discarding the continuous parametric structure of the failed plan. The proposed method aims to leverage this continuous structure for targeted correction.
- **Data-driven approaches (e.g., reinforcement learning, vision-language pipelines):** While these can learn corrective behaviors, they typically require extensive offline failure datasets or online interaction. The present work seeks to achieve diagnosis and correction using only the fitted planning distribution and tester outputs, without a separately trained failure model.
- **Causal inference in robotics:** While causal models are increasingly used for policy generalization and sim-to-real transfer, this work applies causal reasoning at runtime specifically for diagnosing individual failed plans, rather than for training a policy.
- **Tractable probabilistic circuits:** The paper builds on the theoretical foundation established by Wang and Kwiatkowska (2023), who demonstrated that imposing a Marginal-Deterministic Variable Tree (MdVtree) on a probabilistic circuit enables exact, polytime computation of interventional distributions. This work provides a practical application and experimental validation of this theory within a robot planning context, by extending a Joint Probability Tree (JPT) into a Causal Circuit.
- **Task and motion planning under uncertainty:** Existing work in this area often focuses on learning feasibility classifiers to predict plan success before execution. In contrast, this research is complementary, focusing on diagnosing failures *after* rejection and producing targeted corrections to reduce subsequent testing calls.

In summary, this research positions itself at the intersection of safe robot AI, causal inference, and tractable probabilistic modeling, offering a method for informed failure recovery that aims to be more efficient, interpretable, and less data-intensive than some alternative approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the necessity for robots to operate safely and efficiently in physical environments, particularly when proposed actions must undergo pre-execution safety testing. The paper highlights a critical inefficiency in current robot planning-testing loops:
1. **Computational Cost of Formal Testing:** Formal methods for testing motion parameters can provide strong safety guarantees but are computationally expensive, with costs escalating rapidly with environmental complexity and the dimensionality of the action space.
2. **Inefficiency of Blind Resampling:** When a proposed action is rejected by a safety tester, the common practice of blindly resampling new candidates from the planning distribution is inefficient, provides no insights into the cause of failure, and offers no systematic path to convergence. Each rejection, however, contains implicit information about regions of the action space that are incompatible with safe execution.

Given these limitations, the key objectives of this work are to:
- **Develop a principled failure diagnosis mechanism:** The primary objective is to move beyond blind resampling by enabling a robot to causally diagnose a failed action proposal. This involves identifying which specific action parameter caused the failure.
- **Recommend targeted corrections:** Following diagnosis, the system aims to determine the corrective value for the identified causal parameter that would maximize the probability of the revised action passing testing, leveraging an interventional probability distribution.
- **Integrate causal reasoning into the planning-testing loop:** The research seeks to directly embed causal reasoning into the robot's action generation, evaluation, and adaptation cycle, thereby extending the "hypothesize, test" loop into a "hypothesize, test, debug" framework.
- **Leverage existing models without additional training:** A key motivation is to achieve this causal diagnosis and correction using only the robot's already-fitted planning distribution (specifically, a Joint Probability Tree) and tester outputs, thereby avoiding the need for a separately trained failure model, additional data collection, or costly retraining.
- **Ensure tractability and provide guarantees:** The framework is motivated by the need to guarantee the tractability of all interventional queries before robot operation begins, and to automatically detect and exclude out-of-support candidates from correction to prevent unreliable extrapolations.
- **Enhance interpretability and operator oversight:** The system is designed to produce structured, interpretable causal reports that identify the primary cause, its observed value, and the recommended corrective region, thus supporting human operator understanding and autonomous recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework establishes a closed-loop system for robot action testing and failure recovery, integrating a Joint Probability Tree (JPT) with a Causal Circuit derived from a Marginal-Deterministic Variable Tree (MdVtree). The methodology can be broken down into several stages.

#### 3.1. System Overview and Components

- **Task-Agnostic Parameterization:** The framework applies to any robot action representable as a continuous vector **x** of parameters (e.g., approach positions, joint configurations).
- **Inputs:** A dataset of successful robot executions, typically stored as Narrative Enabled Episodic Memories (NEEMs), and a designation of "cause" variables (**C**) and an "effect" variable (**Y**).
- **Joint Probability Tree (JPT):** A JPT is fitted to the successful training executions. This model recursively partitions the parameter space into axis-aligned regions and fits simple, independent distributions within each region. The resulting piecewise structure encodes the joint distribution p(**x**) over all action parameters, captures inter-variable correlations, and supports fast, exact marginal and conditional inference. This fitted JPT serves as the planning distribution for generating candidate actions. JPTs have finite support, enabling exact detection of out-of-support parameter combinations.

#### 3.2. Causal Circuit Construction

- **Cause and Effect Variable Designation:** A subset of action parameters are designated as cause variables **C** ⊆ **x**, and one variable (e.g., a success metric) is designated as the effect variable **Y**.
- **Causal Priority Ordering:** A causal priority order among the variables in **C** is established using normalized average treatment effect (ATE_norm) analysis. This ranks variables based on their influence on **Y** across the training set.
- **MdVtree Imposition:** The ATE_norm-ranked order is used to construct an MdVtree over **C**. An MdVtree is a binary tree where leaves are variables in **C**, and internal nodes recursively split them. By ordering these splits according to ATE_norm (most influential first), this structure is imposed onto the JPT.
- **Causal Circuit Extension:** The JPT is extended into a Causal Circuit by ensuring that every SumUnit (which represents a mixture distribution) at each causal level partitions its children's support disjointly on the corresponding cause variable. This property, known as marginal determinism (or Q-determinism), is crucial for causal inference.

#### 3.3. Support Determinism Verification

- Before deployment, the Causal Circuit undergoes a verification step. This validates whether the circuit satisfies marginal determinism (i.e., support disjointness) for every declared cause variable.
- If this condition holds, it provides a pre-runtime guarantee that all interventional queries will be tractable and exact, as the backdoor adjustment sum reduces to a weighted sum over non-overlapping leaf regions, preventing double-counting.

#### 3.4. Runtime Operation (Hypothesize-Test-Debug Loop)

1. **Hypothesize:** A candidate action **x̂** is sampled from the fitted JPT.
2. **Test:** The sampled **x̂** is submitted to a safety tester.
3. **Execute/Reject:**
 - If the plan passes, the robot executes it, and the loop resets.
 - If the plan is rejected, the Causal Circuit initiates diagnosis.
4. **Causal Diagnosis:**
 - For each cause variable x_i ∈ **C**, the circuit computes the interventional success probability π_i(v) = P(Y ≥ τ | do(x_i = v), **C**_{-i} = **x̂**_{-i}). This represents the probability of success if x_i were *forced* to value *v*, rather than merely observed.
 - These probabilities are computed exactly and in polytime using the backdoor adjustment formula, leveraging the support determinism of the Causal Circuit.
 - **Primary Cause Identification:** The variable x_i whose observed value x̂_i minimizes π_i(x̂_i) is identified as the primary cause of failure; it is the value least plausible under the interventional distribution.
 - **Corrective Value Recommendation:** The circuit identifies v* = arg max_v π_i(v) as the optimal corrective value, often returned as a recommended region R*.
5. **Causal Sampling Correction:**
 - For the next planning cycle, the identified primary cause variable is constrained to a narrow interval around v* (e.g., [v* - δ, v* + δ]).
 - All other parameters are resampled from the conditional distribution p(x_{-i} | x_i = v*), preserving learned correlations.
 - **One-shot and Non-chaining:** This correction is applied only once. If the corrected attempt succeeds, the system reverts to unconstrained JPT sampling. If it fails again, the correction is discarded to prevent committing to a poor recommendation.
 - **Out-of-Support Detection:** Plans where the observed parameters lie entirely outside the training support (ρ_i(x̂_i) = 0 for all i) are automatically detected and excluded from the correction loop, as no reliable recommendation can be made.
- **Interpretable Output:** Every rejected plan produces a "Failure Diagnosis Result" detailing the primary cause, its observed value, interventional probability, and the recommended corrective region, aiding operator oversight.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The framework was evaluated through experiments conducted in a ROS2 simulation environment using a PR2 robot performing a pick-and-place task. The task involved grasping a milk carton from a kitchen counter and placing it on a dining table, constrained by a narrow passage. The action parameters included five continuous variables: counter approach (x, y), table approach (x, y), and arm selection. The effect variable (Y) was the milk's final placing height (z), used as a task-success proxy.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- A JPT was trained on 1,742 successful executions in an open-world environment and transferred to the constrained apartment world via coordinate remapping, without retraining.
- The Causal Circuit was constructed from this JPT. The causal priority order, determined by ATE_norm analysis, was: counter approach x, table approach x, arm selection, counter approach y, and table approach y.
- The support determinism verification step passed before all experimental runs.
- A one-shot corrective re-attempt strategy was employed for failed plans; if the corrected attempt also failed, the system reverted to unconstrained JPT sampling.
- Two variants of the JPT were used to assess robustness:
 - **High-Quality JPT:** Trained on the full 1,742 samples with fine precision (δ=0.005, n_min=25), resulting in 53 leaves and dense coverage.
 - **Degraded JPT:** Fitted with coarser precision (δ=0.15, n_min=600) and introduced moderate approach-coordinate noise (σ=0.18), deliberately leading to systematic sampling failures.

#### 4.2. Results with High-Quality JPT (Efficiency)

- Over 5,000 iterations, both the baseline JPT and the JPT + Causal Circuit achieved a 100% success rate, indicating the high-quality planning distribution was sufficient for task completion.
- The Causal Circuit primarily contributed to *efficiency*:
 - It reduced the total number of failed attempts from 97 to 87 (a 10.3% reduction).
 - Average attempts per recovery dropped from 2.04 to 1.02.
 - Average recovery time was reduced from 24.0 seconds to 14.1 seconds.
 - Worst-case attempts per iteration decreased from 3 to 2.
- Out-of-support cases were automatically excluded from correction.
- **Conclusion:** The causal circuit enhanced efficiency by reducing recovery costs and the number of re-attempts, even when the underlying planning distribution was robust.

#### 4.3. Results with Degraded JPT (Correctness and Efficiency)

- Under the degraded JPT, the causal circuit demonstrated a more substantial impact, addressing systematic failures caused by coarser precision and noise near leaf boundaries.
- The baseline JPT required 4,280 failed attempts across 2,283 iterations that needed resampling, averaging 2.86 attempts and 35.5 seconds per recovery, with a worst case of 10 attempts per iteration. It also resulted in 3 failed iterations (99% success rate).
- The JPT + Causal Circuit significantly improved performance:
 - Reduced total failed attempts to 2,674 (a 37% reduction).
 - Average attempts per recovery dropped to 1.13.
 - Average recovery time was reduced to 16.3 seconds (a 2.2x speedup).
 - Worst-case attempts per iteration decreased to 3.
 - Crucially, it achieved a 100% success rate over 5,000 iterations, indicating that it converted previous failures into successes.
- **Conclusion:** The causal circuit provided both efficiency and correctness, enabling targeted corrections that steered the planner toward safe parameter regions, preventing exhaustive blind resampling, and ensuring task completion under a weaker planning distribution.

#### 4.4. General Findings

- The system consistently produced structured, interpretable causal reports for every rejected plan, identifying the primary cause, its observed value, and the recommended corrective region. This supports operator oversight and autonomous recovery without requiring a separately trained failure model.
- The automatic detection and exclusion of out-of-support plans ensured that the system did not extrapolate corrective recommendations beyond observed data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for integrating causal reasoning into robot action testing, offering several potential impacts on the development and deployment of autonomous systems:

- **Enhanced Robot Safety and Reliability:** By enabling robots to causally diagnose and specifically correct failed actions, the framework contributes to improved system reliability and safety. It reduces the incidence of repeated failures that stem from the same underlying cause, leading to more robust operation in real-world environments.
- **Increased Efficiency in Robot Planning and Recovery:** The ability to move beyond blind resampling to targeted, one-shot corrections significantly reduces the number of wasted test calls and recovery time. This efficiency gain is evident even with high-quality planning distributions and becomes substantial when planning distributions are degraded, directly impacting the speed and cost of task execution.
- **Reduced Development and Data Requirements:** A notable aspect of this work is its capacity to perform causal diagnosis and recommend corrections using only an existing, fitted planning distribution and tester outputs. This eliminates the need for collecting large, specific failure datasets, training separate failure models, or conducting extensive simulations for debugging, potentially simplifying the development and deployment pipeline for complex robot tasks.
- **Improved Interpretability and Operator Oversight:** The framework's generation of structured, human-readable causal reports provides critical insights into why an action failed and what corrective measure was taken. This transparency is vital for operator understanding, auditing, and ensuring accountability in safety-critical autonomous systems, fostering trust in robot behavior.
- **Foundational Validation of Causal Circuit Theory:** The work serves as a practical application and experimental validation of theoretical advancements in compositional probabilistic and causal inference using tractable circuit models. It demonstrates how such theoretical constructs can be leveraged in a real-world robotics context.
- **Pre-Deployment Tractability Guarantees:** The inclusion of a support determinism verification step prior to deployment offers a formal guarantee that all interventional queries are tractable. This pre-runtime assurance increases confidence in the system's operational reliability and predictability.
- **Addressing Limitations of Current AI in Robotics:** By providing a structured "debug" stage, the research offers a more sophisticated approach to failure recovery compared to simple replanning or data-intensive learning methods, thereby enhancing the intelligence and adaptability of robot systems.

In the long term, this methodology could be extended to more complex manipulation tasks involving diverse physical parameters (e.g., gripper force, object weight), sequential actions where failures propagate causally, and integration with continuous safety testers. Successful validation on real robot hardware would further solidify its practical significance for industrial and service robotics applications.
