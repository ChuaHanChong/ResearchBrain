# Learning Object-Centric Spatial Reasoning for Sequential Manipulation in Cluttered Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Oklahoma State University and Google DeepMind developed Unveiler, a decomposed framework for sequential robot manipulation that retrieves occluded objects from dense clutter by separating high-level spatial reasoning from low-level action execution. This approach achieved up to 90.0% success in fully occluded simulation scenes and demonstrated zero-shot transfer to a real robot while maintaining near real-time inference at approximately 260 milliseconds.

## Problem

- End-to-end visuomotor policies and large vision-language models (VLMs) struggle with data efficiency, scalability, and interpretability in densely cluttered environments, as they conflate object selection and action execution.
- Developing robust methods for identifying optimal obstacle removal sequences when target objects are occluded is challenging, often leading to suboptimal actions or scene destabilization.
- The high computational overhead and inference latency of large models limit their deployability on resource-constrained robotic hardware for real-time operations.

## Method

- ==Unveiler== employs a ==decomposed architecture== with a ==Spatial Relationship Encoder (SRE)== for high-level object selection and an ==Action Decoder== for low-level push-grasp execution.
- The SRE is a transformer encoder trained in a two-stage process: imitation learning from a geometric heuristic, followed by ==reinforcement learning (PPO)== fine-tuning to discover complex removal strategies.
- The Action Decoder is a fully convolutional network that generates rotation-invariant push-grasp actions based on a heuristic expert, operating on depth heightmaps of the selected object.

## Results

- Unveiler achieved **90.0%** task completion in fully occluded scenes with 6-9 objects and **53.8%** in 9-12 object scenes, consistently outperforming baselines like GPT-4o and VILG in simulation with fewer planning steps.
- The framework demonstrated favorable computational efficiency with an estimated inference time of **260 milliseconds**, making it suitable for real-time robotic deployment.
- Unveiler successfully transferred to a real Dofbot-Pro manipulator in a zero-shot manner, requiring only geometric calibration, and showed improved spatial reasoning accuracy on real-world scenes compared to VLM baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decoupling high-level spatial reasoning for obstacle selection from low-level action execution enhances interpretability, improves data efficiency, and allows for specialized component design.
> - Combining heuristic-driven imitation learning with reinforcement learning fine-tuning enables robust and sample-efficient policy learning, leveraging structured prior knowledge while discovering advanced strategies.
> - Object-centric inductive biases and rich visual representations (object crops) are critical for effective spatial reasoning and improved performance in cluttered environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation in densely cluttered environments is a persistent challenge in automation. Existing approaches broadly fall into two categories: end-to-end visuomotor policies and approaches leveraging object-centric representations or large vision-language models (VLMs).

Traditional end-to-end methods, such as TransporterNet and its successors, map raw visual input directly to grasp actions. While these methods have demonstrated capabilities in various manipulation tasks, they often struggle with data efficiency and scalability as scene complexity increases, particularly when objects are heavily occluded. They typically conflate the decision of *what* to remove with *how* to remove it, leading to a single model attempting to solve multiple complex problems simultaneously.

A related area is push-grasping synergy, where pushing actions are combined with grasping to manage clutter and improve successful object retrieval. Methods in this domain, like MPGNet, aim for more integrated action selection but often still operate within frameworks that may not explicitly reason about long-horizon spatial dependencies.

Object-centric representation learning aims to build models that understand individual objects and their relationships within a scene, offering improved generalization and exploration. Prior works like VIMA and VIOLA use imitation learning with language prompts and object proposals, while others construct 3D object-centric representations for robustness to viewpoint changes or learn object-centric world models for planning. However, many of these focus on generalizability rather than tailoring reasoning specifically to sequential obstacle removal in dense clutter.

More recently, the integration of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) has led to models like ThinkGrasp, OVGNet, and VILG. These leverage semantic understanding from VLMs for high-level reasoning and more intuitive control. While powerful for tasks requiring semantic understanding, these models often incur significant computational overhead and may lack the fine-grained spatial understanding necessary for robust low-level physical interaction, especially in dense clutter where geometric relationships are paramount. Their inference latencies can also be prohibitive for real-time robotic systems.

This work introduces "Unveiler," a framework that positions itself within this landscape by arguing for a paradigm of specialized, decoupled systems. It explicitly separates high-level spatial reasoning (determining *which* objects to remove and in *what order*) from low-level action execution (*how* to physically remove them). This approach contrasts with monolithic end-to-end or large-model-based solutions by focusing on computational efficiency, modularity, and interpretability through explicit object-centric spatial reasoning. The authors contend that such a decomposed architecture, specifically designed for sequential obstacle removal, can surpass the performance of both classic end-to-end policies and modern large-model baselines in dense clutter, particularly regarding data efficiency and deployment on resource-constrained hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, efficient, and interpretable robotic manipulation system capable of retrieving occluded target objects from densely cluttered environments through a sequence of obstacle removal actions. This objective is driven by several key motivations stemming from the limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Addressing Limitations of End-to-End Models:** Current large-scale, end-to-end visuomotor policies, including those based on vision-language models (VLMs), often conflate the complex sub-problems of object selection (which object to remove, in what order) and action execution (how to grasp or push it). This conflation leads to poor scalability as scene complexity increases, requiring extensive data and making failures difficult to diagnose. The authors observe that these models can struggle with dense clutter, where precise spatial reasoning about inter-object dependencies is crucial.

2. **Improving Data Efficiency:** Training large end-to-end models, especially VLMs, requires massive datasets and significant computational resources. The motivation is to achieve strong performance with a more data-efficient approach, particularly for the spatial reasoning component, by leveraging structured learning and imitation from simpler demonstrations.

3. **Enhancing Modularity and Interpretability:** Monolithic systems can be opaque, making it difficult to understand the robot's decision-making process. The researchers aim for a decomposed architecture where each component solves a simpler, more specialized problem. This modularity is intended to make failures independently diagnosable, increase system interpretability by providing clear insights into *why* an object was selected, and facilitate debugging and trust in robotic systems.

4. **Achieving Computational Efficiency and Deployability:** Large models typically demand substantial computational resources for inference, leading to long latencies that are unsuitable for real-time robotic operations. A key motivation is to develop a lightweight system that maintains near real-time inference capabilities, making it deployable on resource-constrained hardware commonly found in robotic platforms.

5. **Learning Optimal Obstacle Removal Sequences:** The work is specifically motivated by the need to move beyond simple proximity-based obstacle removal. The goal is to enable the robot to reason about long-horizon spatial dependencies, considering factors like grasp accessibility, workspace boundaries, and potential cascading effects (scene stability) to identify the *optimal* sequence of objects to remove to clear a path to the target. This includes handling both partially and fully occluded targets.

By addressing these motivations, the authors propose "Unveiler" as a solution that prioritizes specialized, object-centric reasoning to create a more effective and practical system for sequential manipulation in complex, cluttered real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Unveiler framework employs a decomposed architecture that explicitly separates the task of sequential target retrieval into two primary components: a Spatial Relationship Encoder (SRE) for high-level spatial reasoning and an Action Decoder for low-level action execution. This factorization is designed to simplify the learning problem for each component and enhance interpretability.

**Problem Formulation:**
The system operates on an RGB scene image ($I$) and a corresponding depth heightmap ($I_g$). Instance segmentation provides object masks ($M$) and the target object's index ($t$). The robot executes a push-grasp primitive under fixed end-effector height, constant aperture during pushing, and workspace boundary constraints. At each step, the robot must select an object to remove and then execute a push-grasp action. This is factorized as $\pi(a | s) = \sum_{i \in \{1...,N\}} \pi_{SRE}(i | s,t) \pi_{AD}(a | s, i)$, where $i$ is the selected object index and $a$ is the push-grasp action.

**A. Spatial Relationship Encoder (SRE):**
The SRE is a transformer encoder model tasked with identifying the most critical obstacle for removal. It focuses on spatial dependency modeling to predict the optimal sequence of objects to remove, considering two spatial criteria:
1. **Distance to workspace boundary (periphery):** A proxy for grasp accessibility, as peripheral objects are generally less occluded.
2. **Distance from the target:** Prioritizes objects closest to the obstructed target region.
This implicitly promotes scene stability by prioritizing peripheral objects, reducing disturbance to deeper objects.

- **Inputs:** The SRE takes as input the top-camera view image of the scene, a cropped view of the target object, and cropped views of all visible objects. Inputs are normalized and replicated across three channels.
- **Architecture:**
 - Visual features are extracted using a ResNet18 backbone, pretrained on ImageNet, from each $1 \times 144 \times 144$ image (target and $N$ object crops) into $1 \times 1024$ feature maps.
 - These features are flattened and processed by Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) to obtain 1024-dimensional latent representations.
 - A multi-head attention mechanism computes attention scores between the target (query), scene image (key), and surrounding objects (value).
 - Further processing by an MLP on the $N \times 512$ attention output yields $1 \times N$ removal weights for each object.
 - A stack of transformer blocks models inter-object dependencies and target relevance, capturing higher-order relationships like indirect occlusions.
- **Output:** A probability distribution over the $N$ object indices, selecting the object with the highest removal probability. This discrete decision is passed to the Action Decoder.
- **Training:** The SRE is trained in a two-stage process:
 1. **Imitation Learning (IL):** The policy is initialized by imitation learning from demonstrations generated by a heuristic (Algorithm 1). This heuristic scores objects based on normalized boundary and centroid distances, prioritizing objects that are both peripheral and close to the target. This stage provides a sample-efficient starting point, encoding geometric removal logic into visual representations.
 2. **PPO Fine-tuning:** The IL-pretrained weights are fine-tuned using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), treating obstacle selection as a discrete Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state is the SRE's visual input, and the action is the index of the selected obstacle. The reward function (Eqn. 2) includes terms for successful target grasping, occlusion shaping (rewarding direct target exposure and path clearance), and a penalty for unnecessary actions. RL fine-tuning enables the SRE to discover more complex removal strategies in dense, fully occluded regimes where the heuristic might degrade. Training is performed entirely in simulation.

**B. Action Decoder:**
Once the SRE selects an object, the Action Decoder generates the continuous push-grasp action parameters ($[p_x, p_y, \theta, a_{apt}]^\top$) required for its removal.

- **Inputs:** A depth heightmap of the full scene ($I_g$) and a cropped depth heightmap of the selected object ($O_i$).
- **Architecture:** A fully convolutional network (FCN) with separate convolutional pathways for the two inputs. These streams are fused to produce grasp heatmaps across 16 discrete orientations (22.5$^\circ$ increments), providing rotation invariance.
- **Training:** Trained using a supervised cross-entropy loss over grasp maps. The supervision labels are generated by an expert heuristic (Algorithm 2), which samples grasp centers and push targets on object boundaries, then discretizes the push direction and samples gripper aperture.
- **Success Criteria for Data Collection:** Demonstrations for both SRE and Action Decoder are collected in PyBullet. Strict success criteria are applied: 1) single-object interaction (gripper grasps only one object), and 2) grasp stability (grasped object remains secure when lifted).

**C. Data Collection:**
All policy training data is generated using heuristic functions in the PyBullet simulation environment, specifically Algorithms 1 (Obstacle Selection Heuristic) and 2 (Grasp Pose Heuristic). Only successful manipulation attempts are included in the demonstrations.

**D. Properties of the Decomposed Policy:**
The factorization offers two key properties:
1. **Object-centric inductive bias:** The SRE's architecture (target as cross-attention query, per-object crops) anchors learning to target-relative spatial context and makes the model equivariant to instance ordering, enhancing generalization.
2. **Additive error decomposition:** Failures in selection (SRE) and execution (Action Decoder) are separate, leading to an additive error bound (Eqn. 3: $J(\pi^*) - J(\pi) \leq H \cdot \Delta \cdot \epsilon_{SRE} + \epsilon_{exec}$). This implies that errors from one component do not multiplicatively compound errors from the other, aiding in diagnosis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding Unveiler's performance across various metrics and scenarios, including computational efficiency, simulation performance, spatial reasoning, ablation studies, and real-robot deployment.

**A. Computational Efficiency:**
Unveiler demonstrates favorable computational efficiency. With 83.03 million parameters, it achieves an estimated inference time of approximately 260 milliseconds. This contrasts with large-model-based baselines like VILG (156.26 million parameters, ~29,300 ms inference) and API-dependent models like GPT-4o and ThinkGrasp (inference times exceeding 6,000 ms), indicating Unveiler’s near real-time capability and suitability for resource-constrained systems.

**B. Simulation Experiments:**
Evaluations were conducted in PyBullet using objects from YCB and KIT datasets, categorized into seen and unseen sets, across varying clutter densities (2–12 objects) and occlusion levels (partial and full). Each condition involved 30 episodes.
- **Task Completion and Action Efficiency:** Unveiler consistently achieved higher task completion rates and required fewer planning steps compared to all baselines (GPT-4o, CLIP-Grounding, VILG, ThinkGrasp, ACT, PPG, Heur).
 - In partially occluded scenes with 6–9 objects, Unveiler reached 97.6% success with 1.43 steps, outperforming the next best (GPT-4o, 80.0% with 1.81 steps).
 - In fully occluded scenes with 6–9 objects, Unveiler achieved 90.0% success with 3.31 steps, significantly higher than GPT-4o (60.0% with 4.22 steps) and other baselines that showed considerable performance drops (e.g., ACT, PPG failing completely in some dense scenarios).
 - Even in the most challenging scenarios (9–12 objects, full occlusion), Unveiler maintained a 53.8% success rate with 3.71 steps, while most baselines dropped below 35% or failed.
- **Baseline Limitations:** The evaluation highlighted specific limitations of baselines: GPT-4o showed planning inefficiency due to suboptimal removal sequences; the heuristic baseline (Heur) degraded significantly as scene complexity increased, despite reasonable performance in low clutter.

**C. Spatial Reasoning and Interpretability Analysis:**
Visualizations of SRE selection probabilities indicate that the model learns to prioritize obstacles based on spatial relationships beyond simple proximity. The SRE demonstrated an ability to avoid objects difficult to grasp due to workspace boundaries or neighbors and showed sensitivity to potential cascading effects from object removal, thereby minimizing scene destabilization. The interpretable selection probabilities are presented as providing transparency in decision-making.

**D. Ablation Studies (at 6–9 object density):**
The ablation studies revealed the contribution of Unveiler's components:
- **SRE w/o RL finetuning:** Removing the PPO fine-tuning stage resulted in a 3.3% performance drop compared to the full Unveiler system at 9–12 object density, indicating that RL enables discovery of strategies beyond the initial heuristic.
- **No SRE:** Removing the Spatial Relationship Encoder led to a 13.6% reduction in task completion under full occlusion (at 6–9 object density), while showing a slight 3.4% improvement in partial occlusion, underscoring its role in complex, occluded scenarios.
- **Multi-Obstacle Selection:** An approach attempting to predict all removable obstacles simultaneously degraded performance by 25.2% (partial occlusion) and 33.3% (full occlusion), suggesting the importance of sequential, single-object removal reasoning.
- **Binary Mask Input:** Replacing rich object crops with binary masks caused substantial performance drops (42.1% for partial and 53.3% for full occlusion), validating the necessity of detailed visual representations for the SRE.

**E. Demonstration on a Real Robot:**
Unveiler was validated on a Dofbot-Pro manipulator with an Intel RealSense camera, demonstrating zero-shot transfer capabilities. The transfer was facilitated by operating on depth heightmaps (reducing color/texture domain shift), matching camera geometry (preserving spatial scale), the Action Decoder's rotation-invariant predictions, and encoding arm kinematics in non-learned workspace bounds. The system outperformed other approaches in real-world trials, even with a less complete 3D point cloud from a single camera compared to the dual-camera setup in simulation. The only adaptation was geometric recalibration of workspace bounds.

**F. Scene Reasoning Evaluation on Real Images:**
A decoupled evaluation of the SRE's object-selection accuracy on 35 real tabletop scenes (Table IV) showed:
- In sparse scenes (2–4 objects), SRE (60%) performed comparably to CLIP-Grounding (65%).
- In denser scenes (5–8 objects), the SRE (54%) substantially outperformed CLIP-Grounding (37%) and GPT-4o (26%), confirming that the SRE's geometric reasoning about occlusion and periphery is critical in cluttered conditions, rather than just appearance similarity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Unveiler framework, with its decomposed architecture, presents several points of significance and potential impact in the field of robotic manipulation:

1. **Advancement in Clutter Manipulation:** The work provides an effective solution for sequential target retrieval in densely cluttered environments, a long-standing challenge in robotics. By explicitly modeling spatial relationships and optimal removal sequences, Unveiler moves beyond simple proximity-based obstacle clearing, enabling more robust planning in complex scenarios where objects are partially or fully occluded.

2. **Validation of Decomposed Architectures:** The research makes a case for the continued relevance and efficacy of specialized, decomposed systems. By separating high-level spatial reasoning (SRE) from low-level action execution (Action Decoder), each component can specialize, leading to a simpler learning problem for each part. This contrasts with the trend toward monolithic end-to-end models, suggesting that decomposition can be a powerful inductive bias for specific complex tasks.

3. **Computational Efficiency and Deployability:** Unveiler's lightweight architecture (83.03M parameters) and near real-time inference latency (approximately 260ms) represent a significant advantage over large-scale vision-language models (VLMs) that often require substantial computational resources and exhibit long inference times. This efficiency is critical for deploying sophisticated manipulation capabilities on resource-constrained robotic systems in real-world industrial or domestic settings.

4. **Enhanced Interpretability and Diagnosability:** The explicit nature of the SRE's spatial reasoning and its interpretable selection probabilities provide transparency into the robot's decision-making process. This interpretability is valuable for debugging, validating system behavior, and building trust in autonomous agents, particularly in applications where understanding the "why" behind an action is important. The additive error decomposition further aids in diagnosing failure modes.

5. **Robust Learning via Two-Stage Training:** The two-stage training approach for the SRE, combining imitation learning from heuristics with PPO fine-tuning, demonstrates a method for achieving both sample efficiency and performance beyond the capabilities of the initial heuristic. This suggests a pathway for developing robust policies that leverage structured prior knowledge while simultaneously allowing for discovery of more complex strategies through reinforcement learning.

6. **Zero-Shot Transfer to Real-World Scenarios:** The demonstrated zero-shot transfer of the learned spatial reasoning component to physical robot scenes, requiring only geometric calibration, is significant. This capability is attributed to the use of depth heightmaps, top-down camera geometry, and rotation-invariant action generation, indicating robustness to domain shift and practical applicability without extensive real-world data collection or fine-tuning.

7. **Complementary Role with Foundation Models:** The authors propose that Unveiler's specialized spatial reasoning could complement large-scale vision-language models (VLMs) by serving as a dedicated data generator for complex cluttered manipulation scenarios or as a modular component that enhances VLA performance on fine-grained sequential tasks. This suggests a collaborative future where specialized modules address specific challenges within broader AI systems.

Overall, the work underscores the benefits of purpose-built architectures for complex robotic tasks, offering a path to achieve high performance, efficiency, and interpretability in challenging manipulation environments.
