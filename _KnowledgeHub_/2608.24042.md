# Hierarchical Skill Retrieval for Data-Efficient Adaptation of Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Hierarchical Skill Retrieval (HSR) adapts pretrained VLA policies to data-scarce tasks by decomposing a long-horizon instruction into subtasks via an LLM planner scored by semantic plausibility and skill reliability, then retrieving demonstrations per subtask with language plus behavior-feature reranking, followed by two-stage pretrain-then-finetune adaptation. HSR beats strongest baseline by 10.3% on LIBERO and 21.3% on real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Existing retrieval methods for VLA adaptation rely on visual similarity, state-action features, or subtrajectory matching, ignoring hierarchical task structure where reusable skills recur but full task matches rarely exist.
- Task-level language retrieval fails for long-horizon composite instructions since complete matches are rare in prior datasets even though constituent skills are common and well represented.
- LLM-based task planners are not grounded in a policy's actual execution capability, so generated decompositions may include subtasks the policy cannot reliably perform.

## Method

- **Hierarchical Task Decomposition** clusters prior-dataset instructions into skill clusters via K-means, then an LLM planner proposes candidate subtask sequences for the target task.
- ==Skill Confidence Score== ranks candidate plans using held-out behavior-cloning validation loss and trajectory length per skill cluster, combined multiplicatively with an LLM semantic-plausibility score.
- **Hybrid Skill Retrieval** first pulls top language-similarity episodes per subtask, then reranks candidate frames with a VAE-based behavior-feature distance to the target demonstrations before two-stage pretrain-finetune adaptation.

## Results

- On LIBERO long-horizon tasks **HSR** reaches **43.5%** average success rate versus **33.2%** for the strongest baseline Behavior Retrieval and **31.9%** for STRAP.
- On real-world xArm tasks HSR achieves **48/80** overall successes versus **27/80** for BC and IWR, with the largest gain on the four-subtask Cup-Tea task (**9/20** vs **3/20** for BC).
- Ablations show removing task decomposition, feature reranking, or the two-stage scheme each degrades performance, while manually chosen decompositions (**44.9%**) nearly match HSR's automated plan scoring (**43.5%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing long-horizon tasks into reusable skill-level queries exploits hierarchical structure that flat language or motion similarity retrieval misses, especially for composite manipulation.
> - Rollout-free proxies like held-out BC loss can ground LLM planning in actual policy competence without costly environment evaluation, mitigating ungrounded plan generation.
> - Separating pretraining on broadly retrieved skills from finetuning on reranked task-aligned data outperforms single-stage co-training, since noisy retrieved data can otherwise bias optimization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements with the emergence of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which extend large pre-trained vision-language representations to robot control. These models leverage general visual and linguistic understanding acquired from large-scale multimodal datasets to perform downstream manipulation tasks. A common strategy for adapting VLA models to specific tasks involves finetuning them on target-task demonstrations. However, a fundamental limitation in this approach is data scarcity; collecting high-quality robot demonstrations is an expensive and time-consuming process, often resulting in small target datasets insufficient for fully adapting large policy models for reliable real-world deployment.

A complementary line of research focuses on improving adaptation by reusing extensive prior datasets as supplementary training data. Retrieval-based methods, in particular, have shown efficacy in identifying relevant demonstrations from large repositories such as Open X-Embodiment. Existing retrieval techniques typically rely on similarity metrics, often based on latent state-action spaces, optical flow, or subtrajectory-level visual or motion similarity. While these methods enhance downstream task performance by augmenting the effective training set, they frequently overlook higher-level task semantics and the hierarchical structure inherent in complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks. This limitation means that trajectories with visually similar motions might correspond to distinct underlying skills, potentially leading to ambiguous or less relevant retrieval outcomes.

VLA models, conversely, have demonstrated capabilities in following language instructions and transferring knowledge across related tasks. This suggests that language could offer a crucial semantic signal for improving task generalization. However, language-based retrieval often faces limitations when solely relying on coarse instruction similarity. For long-horizon tasks, direct retrieval using the complete task instruction (e.g., “make a cup of tea”) is often inefficient due to the rarity of exact matches in prior datasets. In contrast, the elemental skills comprising such tasks (e.g., “grasp a teapot,” “pick up a tea bag”) are frequently recurring and well-represented.

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the challenge of effectively leveraging the hierarchical structure of complex tasks and retrieving data at the reusable skill level. It aims to overcome the limitations of existing retrieval methods by combining semantic understanding from language with behavioral compatibility, facilitating data-efficient adaptation of VLA models for long-horizon manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable data-efficient adaptation of pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to novel manipulation tasks, particularly those involving long-horizon and compositional behaviors, when only a limited number of task-specific demonstrations are available.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges observed in the current robotic learning paradigm:

- **Data Scarcity for VLA Model Adaptation:** While VLA models, pre-trained on large datasets, offer a strong foundation for robot control, their performance often degrades when adapted to new tasks with limited task-specific demonstrations. Collecting sufficient high-quality robot data for every new task is resource-intensive and often impractical, limiting the real-world deployability of these models.
- **Limitations of Existing Retrieval Methods:** Current retrieval-based approaches, which aim to augment small target datasets with relevant demonstrations from large prior datasets, primarily rely on visual, motion, or latent state-action similarity. These methods often fail to adequately capture the semantic and hierarchical structure of complex manipulation tasks. For instance, visually similar motions might belong to different skills, or crucial task-level semantic relationships might be overlooked.
- **Ineffectiveness of Coarse Language-Based Retrieval:** Although VLA models possess strong language understanding capabilities, simply using a high-level task instruction (e.g., "make a cup of tea") for retrieval is often insufficient for long-horizon tasks. Complete matches for such complex instructions are rare in prior datasets, meaning that useful sub-skills or components of the task might be missed.
- **Need for Structured Skill Reuse:** Long-horizon tasks are typically composed of a sequence of reusable subtasks or "skills" (e.g., "pick up a cup," "open a drawer"). Effectively identifying and reusing demonstrations at this skill level, rather than the monolithic task level, could enable more precise and relevant data augmentation for policy adaptation.
- **Grounding LLM Planning with Robot Capabilities:** Large Language Models (LLMs) can generate task plans, but these plans may not be grounded in the robot's actual capabilities or the availability of relevant data. There is a need to guide LLM-based planning to produce subtasks that are not only semantically plausible but also reliably executable by the VLA policy based on existing data.
- **Optimizing Policy Adaptation with Retrieved Data:** Simply co-training the policy on both target and retrieved data can be suboptimal, especially if the retrieved data is noisy or less aligned with the target task. A more refined adaptation strategy is needed to effectively leverage the diverse nature of retrieved demonstrations.

To address these motivations, the research proposes the Hierarchical Skill Retrieval (HSR) framework, aiming to improve data-efficient VLA adaptation by explicitly leveraging the hierarchical structure of manipulation tasks through intelligent task decomposition and multi-faceted retrieval.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Hierarchical Skill Retrieval (HSR) framework is designed for data-efficient adaptation of VLA models by structuring the retrieval process around task decomposition and skill-level matching. The approach consists of three main stages: Hierarchical Task Decomposition, Hybrid Skill Retrieval, and Two-Stage Policy Adaptation.

#### 3.1. A. Hierarchical Task Decomposition

The methodology begins by addressing the limitation of coarse language-based retrieval for long-horizon tasks. Instead of matching complete tasks, HSR decomposes a target task into a sequence of semantic subtasks, which are then used as queries for retrieval.

1. **Skill Clustering and Reliability Estimation:**
 - The prior dataset ($D_{prior}$) is first processed to identify reusable skills. Task instructions within $D_{prior}$ are converted into text embeddings, and K-means clustering is applied to group them into $K$ semantically coherent skill clusters, $S = \{s_1, \dots, s_K\}$. Examples of such skills include "Find," "Pick up," or "Pour."
 - For each identified skill $s_i$, its reliability is estimated using the pre-trained VLA model. To avoid computationally expensive environment rollouts, a lightweight proxy based on held-out Behavior Cloning (BC) loss is employed. $D_{prior}$ is split into training and validation sets. The VLA model is finetuned on the training split to obtain $\pi_{\theta}'$.
 - The average trajectory-level BC loss ($L_{traj}(\tau)$) is computed for each validation trajectory $\tau$ associated with a skill cluster $s_i$. The validation loss for a skill $s_i$ ($L_{val}(s_i)$) is the average $L_{traj}(\tau)$ across its validation trajectories.
 - To account for varying task lengths and error accumulation, a Skill Confidence Score ($C(s_i)$) is defined as $C(s_i) = \exp \left( -\alpha \tilde{L}_{val}(s_i) - \beta \tilde{H}(s_i) \right)$, where $\tilde{L}_{val}(s_i)$ and $\tilde{H}(s_i)$ are min-max normalized validation loss and average trajectory length, respectively. Fixed weights $\alpha=0.6$ and $\beta=0.8$ are used. This score prioritizes skills that are easier to imitate and less prone to compounding errors.

2. **LLM-Based Plan Generation and Evaluation:**
 - A Large Language Model (LLM) (Qwen3-VL-4B in experiments) is prompted with the target task description and the set of available skills ($S$) to generate candidate decomposed plans. Each plan $p$ is an ordered sequence of subtasks $(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n)$.
 - For each subtask $u_j$, its nearest skill cluster $s(u_j)$ is identified based on text-embedding similarity.
 - The LLM also provides a semantic score ($S_{sem}(p) \in (0,1]$) for each plan, reflecting its task coverage, logical consistency, and semantic plausibility.
 - Each candidate plan is evaluated by combining its semantic score with the confidence scores of its constituent skills: $Score(p) = S_{sem}(p) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{n} C(s(u_j))$.
 - The plan with the highest score is selected as the final decomposition for subsequent retrieval.

#### 3.2. B. Hybrid Skill Retrieval

This stage aims to retrieve demonstrations that are not only semantically relevant but also behaviorally compatible with the target task, addressing the sensitivity of VLA policies to low-level environmental factors.

1. **Language-Based Initial Retrieval:** Given a subtask from the selected plan, the top M% of episodes from $D_{prior}$ are initially retrieved based on their language similarity to the subtask instruction.
2. **Behavior-Feature Reranking:**
 - The language-retrieved episodes are flattened into individual frames.
 - A VAE encoder, pre-trained on $D_{prior}$, is used to extract latent behavior features ($z_f$) for each candidate frame $f$.
 - Similarly, latent features ($\{z_{t_j}\}_{j=1}^{N_t}$) are extracted from the target dataset ($D_t$).
 - The behavior similarity score of a candidate frame $f$ is defined as $s(f) = \max_{1 \le j \le N_t} \left( -\left\|z_f - z_{t_j}\right\|^2 \right)$, corresponding to the negative squared Euclidean distance to the nearest target example in the latent space.
 - The top F% of candidate frames with the highest behavior similarity scores are retained as the final retrieved data for policy adaptation.

#### 3.3. C. Policy Adaptation

To effectively utilize the retrieved data, a two-stage policy adaptation scheme is employed, separating general skill acquisition from task-specific finetuning.

1. **Stage 1: Skill Pretraining:** The VLA policy is first pre-trained on $D_{lang}$, which consists of the training samples from the language-retrieved episodes. This stage aims to impart reusable behaviors and general skills from the broader prior dataset.
2. **Stage 2: Task-Specific Finetuning:** Subsequently, the policy is finetuned on the combined dataset of target demonstrations ($D_t$) and the behavior-reranked frames ($D_{rerank}$). This stage refines the policy for the specific target task, leveraging the most relevant and compatible retrieved data. This two-stage approach contrasts with single-stage co-training by explicitly mitigating potential issues arising from the scale and noise of retrieved data biasing the optimization away from the target task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HSR was conducted in both simulation and real-world environments, demonstrating consistent improvements over various baselines.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments (LIBERO Benchmark)

Experiments on the LIBERO long-horizon subset, comprising 10 composite manipulation tasks, revealed the following:

- **Overall Performance:** HSR achieved an average success rate of 43.5%, outperforming all baselines by a notable margin. The strongest competing method, Behavior Retrieval (BR), achieved 33.2%, indicating a 10.3% improvement in average success rate by HSR.
- **Baseline Comparisons:**
 - **Target-Only Finetuning (BC):** Performed poorly (22.7% average success), highlighting the insufficiency of small target datasets.
 - **Naive Data Mixing (Random, All Data):** Showed modest gains (26.0% and 29.0%, respectively), suggesting that merely increasing data volume without intelligent selection is inadequate.
 - **Language-Based Retrieval:** Improved slightly over random sampling (31.5%), but its limitations on complex tasks underscored the need for more nuanced semantic understanding.
 - **Behavioral/Motion Similarity Baselines (FR, STRAP, BR, IWR):** While these methods improved performance over simpler baselines, their gains were less pronounced on composite manipulation tasks. STRAP, which performs subtrajectory retrieval, reached 31.9%. BR and IWR, based on state-action latent space similarity, achieved 33.2% and 32.5%, respectively.
- **Performance on Complex Tasks:** HSR's advantage was particularly evident on complex composite tasks. For example, on the "Mug-M" task, HSR achieved 18.0% success, surpassing STRAP (17.3%) and BR (17.3%). On the "Bowl-C" task, HSR achieved 81.3% success, being the only method to substantially improve over pure BC (77.3%). HSR also significantly outperformed baselines on "Moka-M" (15.3% vs. 6.7% for STRAP) and "Mug-P" (26.0% vs. 16.7% for STRAP). These results suggest that HSR's ability to leverage hierarchical task structure is crucial for tasks requiring multiple, distinct skills.

#### 4.2. B. Real-World Experiments

Real-world manipulation tasks on a 7-DoF xArm robot, including "Drawer," "Trashcan," "Cup-Drawer," and "Cup-Tea," demonstrated HSR's effectiveness under significant data scarcity and cross-embodiment gaps.

- **Overall Performance:** HSR achieved 48/80 successful trials across all tasks, a substantial improvement compared to BC (27/80), BR (27/80), and IWR (31/80). This represents a 21.3% improvement in overall success rate compared to the strongest baseline.
- **Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks:** The advantage of HSR was more pronounced on long-horizon tasks requiring multiple steps. For the "Cup-Tea" task (4 subtasks), HSR achieved 9 out of 20 successful executions, whereas BC succeeded in only 3 trials. This indicates HSR's ability to facilitate learning and execution of complex, multi-step behaviors in real-world scenarios.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies on LIBERO tasks provided insights into the contributions of HSR's key components:

- **Task Decomposition:** Removing the task decomposition module, which meant directly retrieving data using the full task instruction, resulted in a noticeable performance drop, especially for complex tasks like "Mug-P" and "Moka-M." This suggests that breaking down complex instructions into semantically meaningful subtasks is critical for effective retrieval in long-horizon manipulation. Further analysis showed that incorporating the available skill set into the LLM prompt and then applying the plan evaluation module progressively improved performance, nearing human-level plan quality.
- **Feature Reranking:** Eliminating the behavior-feature reranking module, relying solely on language-retrieved data, led to a clear performance decrease, particularly on tasks requiring precise alignment with the target execution context (e.g., "Soup-S"). This confirms that language similarity alone is insufficient, and behavior-level compatibility filtering is necessary to select relevant demonstrations. An optimal reranking retention ratio was found, indicating that too much or too little data after reranking can degrade performance.
- **Training Method:** The proposed two-stage pretraining-finetuning strategy consistently outperformed single-stage co-training variants (with and without feature reranking). This indicates that separating general skill acquisition (via pretraining on language-retrieved data) from task-specific adaptation (via finetuning on target data and behavior-reranked samples) is more robust, especially when dealing with potentially noisy or large-scale retrieved data.

#### 4.4. D. Retrieved Data Study

A qualitative study of retrieved data for a representative task ("put the yellow and white mug in the microwave and close it") illustrated HSR's advantage. Unlike baselines that might retrieve demonstrations with similar motions but different goals, HSR, by decomposing the task, effectively retrieved diverse demonstrations covering distinct subtasks like object placing and microwave closing, leading to more task-relevant data for policy learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Hierarchical Skill Retrieval (HSR) framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in the domain of data-efficient adaptation for complex manipulation tasks. Its primary significance lies in providing a structured and multi-faceted approach to leverage vast prior datasets when target-task demonstrations are scarce.

The core innovations of HSR – hierarchical task decomposition guided by LLMs and skill reliability, hybrid retrieval combining semantic and behavioral similarity, and a two-stage policy adaptation strategy – collectively address critical limitations of existing VLA model adaptation methods. By moving beyond coarse instruction matching or purely visual/motion similarity, HSR enables more precise identification and reuse of transferable skills embedded within large, diverse datasets.

The consistent improvements in success rates observed across both the LIBERO simulation benchmark and real-world long-horizon manipulation tasks demonstrate the practical effectiveness of HSR. The particularly pronounced gains on complex, multi-step tasks highlight its capability to bridge the gap between general VLA models and specific, challenging robotic applications. This means that robots can learn to perform more intricate sequences of actions with substantially less new human demonstration data, accelerating deployment and reducing the cost associated with robot programming.

**Potential impacts include:**

- **Enhanced Data Efficiency:** HSR allows VLA models to achieve higher performance with fewer task-specific demonstrations, making robot learning more accessible and scalable in environments where data collection is expensive or difficult.
- **Improved Generalization for Long-Horizon Tasks:** By explicitly addressing the hierarchical nature of complex tasks and retrieving at the skill level, HSR enables VLA policies to compose known skills more effectively, leading to better generalization to novel long-horizon tasks.
- **More Robust Policy Adaptation:** The hybrid retrieval mechanism and two-stage adaptation strategy contribute to a more robust learning process by ensuring both semantic relevance and behavioral compatibility of retrieved data, while also mitigating potential negative transfer from noisy or misaligned demonstrations.
- **Advancement in Few-Shot Imitation Learning:** HSR pushes the boundaries of few-shot imitation learning in robotics by integrating advanced language understanding (LLMs for planning) with learning from prior experience (skill reliability, behavior reranking) in a synergistic framework.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge limitations, such as evaluation on a single VLA backbone and manipulation tasks, and the need for tighter integration of LLMs for online control and failure recovery. Addressing these areas could further enhance the framework's robustness and applicability. Nevertheless, HSR provides a valuable paradigm for creating more capable and adaptable robotic systems by intelligently unlocking the potential of large-scale pre-trained models and datasets.
