# Adapting by Analogy: OOD Generalization of Visuomotor Policies via Functional Correspondence

> [!summary] Summary
> Adapting by Analogy (ABA) enhances the ability of pre-trained visuomotor policies to perform tasks in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments by leveraging expert-provided natural language to find functional correspondences between OOD and in-distribution observations. This test-time intervention method improved task success rates by up to 76% for novel objects and backgrounds on real-world robotic manipulation tasks, requiring infrequent human feedback.

## Problem

- End-to-end visuomotor policies trained on in-distribution (ID) data exhibit significant performance degradation in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments with novel objects or backgrounds.
- Traditional approaches like collecting extensive new expert demonstrations for each OOD scenario are resource-intensive and time-consuming.
- Existing test-time intervention methods often lack a robust mechanism for identifying task-relevant functional correspondences between OOD and ID observations.

## Method

- Introduces '**Adapting by Analogy**' (**ABA**), a test-time observation intervention method that leverages expert natural language to establish functional correspondences.
- Detects OOD observations, then filters in-distribution data based on proprioceptive similarity and uses ==semantic segmentation==, guided by expert language, to find functionally corresponding ID observations.
- Refines functional correspondences interactively by analyzing the ==entropy== of retrieved action plans and requesting expert feedback until confidence in the behavior mode is high.
- Intervenes by averaging the latent embeddings of top-ranked functionally corresponding ID observations and feeding this averaged embedding to the pre-trained policy.

## Results

- **ABA** improved task success rates by up to **76%** for novel objects and backgrounds across two real-world manipulation tasks, compared to baseline policies.
- Baseline methods relying on general visual features or policy embeddings alone struggled significantly with OOD objects, achieving up to **43%** lower success rates than **ABA**.
- The method demonstrated efficiency in expert feedback, requiring an average of **3-5** queries per rollout, which corresponds to approximately **6%** of the rollout duration for challenging OOD objects.
- Successful baseline interventions implicitly correlated with retrieving functionally aligned observations, underscoring the critical role of functional correspondence for OOD generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learned in-distribution behaviors can be effectively repurposed and transferred to functionally similar out-of-distribution conditions without requiring entirely new policies.
> - Expert human knowledge, provided via natural language descriptions, can efficiently establish functional correspondences between novel and familiar objects or scenes for task-specific adaptation.
> - Intervening on a robot's observations with functionally analogous ID observations guides pre-trained visuomotor policies to generate appropriate actions based on existing knowledge.
> - Confidence in functional correspondences can be measured by the reduced diversity of retrieved action plans, enabling iterative refinement of expert guidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning aims to develop agents capable of performing tasks robustly across diverse, previously unseen environments. A significant challenge in this area is the generalization of visuomotor policies to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, which are prevalent in real-world deployments. While end-to-end visuomotor policies, often trained using behavior cloning, have demonstrated proficiency in generating complex, low-level robot behaviors within their training distribution (in-distribution, ID), their performance typically degrades significantly when confronted with OOD visual cues, such as novel objects, varying backgrounds, or altered environmental settings.

Previous approaches to address this OOD generalization problem have included interactive imitation learning, where human experts provide corrective demonstrations directly under the new OOD conditions. However, this method is often resource-intensive, requiring substantial human effort and time to collect new data for each novel scenario. Other lines of research focus on test-time policy interventions, which involve steering a policy's execution by supplying new knowledge. These interventions can occur in the policy's behavior space (e.g., through human feedback, Q-functions optimized on offline datasets, or predictive modeling) or directly on the policy's observations (e.g., using synthesized observations to mitigate known failure modes).

This paper introduces "Adapting by Analogy" (ABA), a method that positions itself within the domain of test-time observation interventions. It distinguishes itself by positing that successful task execution in OOD conditions does not invariably necessitate entirely novel robot behaviors. Instead, existing ID behaviors can often be repurposed and transferred to OOD conditions that share functional similarities with the training environments. For instance, a robot trained to interact with various pens might effectively apply similar behaviors to a visually distinct pencil if the functional properties (e.g., grasp points, handling characteristics) are analogous.

The primary gap this work addresses is the disambiguation of which ID observations functionally correspond to a given OOD observation for a specific task. Unlike prior work on functional correspondence for behavior transfer, which often relies on keypoint-based reasoning or pre-existing affordance datasets to adapt behaviors, ABA leverages expert human knowledge, provided through natural language, to establish these functional correspondences. This approach offers a novel mechanism for transferring learned skills by intervening on the robot's observations with functionally analogous ID observations, rather than directly modifying the policy's actions or requiring extensive new demonstrations and retraining for every OOD encounter. This makes the work a significant contribution to enabling more efficient and adaptable robot learning in unpredictable environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central goal of this research is to enhance the generalization capabilities of visuomotor policies, enabling robots to perform tasks successfully in environments not encountered during training. Visuomotor policies, which learn direct mappings from visual observations to robot actions, have achieved notable successes in controlled ID settings. However, their reliability diminishes sharply when deployed in OOD environments, characterized by variations in objects, backgrounds, or other environmental factors. This limitation stems from the inherent cost and time intensity of collecting expert demonstration data, leading to a scarcity of internet-scale datasets comparable to those available in vision and language domains. Consequently, robots often exhibit unpredictable failures when faced with novel conditions.

The core motivation for this work arises from the observation that overcoming OOD challenges does not always require the robot to learn entirely new behaviors. Instead, behaviors learned within the training distribution can often be effectively transferred to OOD conditions, provided that these conditions share functional similarities with the ID scenarios. For example, if a robot has learned to pick up various types of pens, it could potentially apply this learned behavior to a pencil, given their functional equivalence for the task of grasping and placement. The fundamental challenge, therefore, lies in accurately identifying which specific ID observations exhibit a functional correspondence with the current OOD observation in the context of the task.

To address this challenge, the paper proposes leveraging expert human knowledge to establish OOD-to-ID functional correspondences. This approach is motivated by the hypothesis that a human expert can articulate the functional relationship between novel and familiar objects or scenes, thereby guiding the robot's adaptation. The key objectives of the "Adapting by Analogy" (ABA) method are:

1. **Deployment-time Generalization:** To enable visuomotor policies, pre-trained on ID behaviors, to generalize their task performance to OOD environments without the need for collecting new expert demonstrations or extensive retraining for each new OOD instance.
2. **Efficient Expert Feedback:** To develop a system that efficiently solicits and utilizes expert feedback in the form of high-level functional correspondence descriptions. This feedback should allow the robot to disambiguate between potential behaviors and focus on the most relevant ID actions.
3. **Observation-based Intervention:** To intervene on OOD observations by substituting them with functionally corresponding ID observations, thereby allowing the pre-trained policy to generate appropriate actions based on its existing knowledge.
4. **Interactive Correspondence Refinement:** To incorporate a mechanism for interactively refining the functional correspondence description until the robot is confident in the retrieved behavior, acknowledging that correspondences may evolve throughout a task.
5. **Validation across Diverse Tasks:** To empirically validate the effectiveness of the proposed method across various real-world robotic manipulation tasks and demonstrate its ability to improve success rates in OOD environments induced by novel objects and background conditions with minimal expert intervention.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a practical and efficient paradigm for enhancing the robustness and adaptability of robots in unstructured, dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Adapting by Analogy" (ABA) method is designed for test-time adaptation of visuomotor policies to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, leveraging expert-provided functional correspondences. The approach operates in four sequential phases: OOD detection, establishment of functional correspondences, refinement of these correspondences, and intervention on observations.

**Problem Formulation:**
The robot operates in an environment `E`, capturing observations `o = (i, q)`, where `i` is an RGB image and `q` represents proprioceptive states (e.g., end-effector pose, gripper state). Actions are denoted by `a`. A visuomotor policy `π(a_t | o_t)` is pre-trained via imitation learning on a dataset `D_ID` collected from ID environments `E_ID`. The policy employs an encoder `E(o)` to map observations to latent state embeddings `z`. The goal is to generalize `π` to OOD environments `E_OOD`. The method assumes that `E_ID` and `E_OOD` differ primarily in objects or background color, the robot embodiment remains constant, and `D_ID` is accessible.

The core idea is to identify functional correspondences between an OOD scene and ID scenes. This is formalized by a functional correspondence map `Φ(i, î, l)`, which, given an ID image `i`, an OOD image `î`, and an expert language description `l`, returns a set of pairs of functionally corresponding image segments `(ω_j, î_ω_j)`. The functional alignment between two images is quantified by `f(Φ(i, î, l))`, defined as the sum of Intersection over Union (IoU) between all corresponding segment pairs. The method retrieves ID observations `O_f` from `D_ID` that are highly functionally aligned with the current OOD observation.

**Method Phases:**

1. **Detecting Out-of-Distribution Observations (Section 4.1):**
 At each timestep, the robot's current observation `ô` is first checked for anomalousness using a fast OOD detector. This is achieved by computing the `IDScore(ô, O_ID)`, which is the minimum cosine similarity between the latent embedding of the current OOD observation `E(ô)` and all latent embeddings `E(o)` of observations `o` in the training dataset `O_ID`. If the `IDScore` exceeds a predefined threshold `λ`, the observation is considered in-distribution, and the base policy `π(· | ô)` is executed directly. Conversely, if the `IDScore` falls below `λ`, the observation is flagged as OOD, and the system proceeds to solicit an initial natural language instruction `l` from an expert, describing the relevant functional correspondences.

2. **Establishing OOD-to-ID Functional Correspondences (Section 4.2):**
 Once an OOD observation `ô = (q̂, î)` is detected and an expert language description `l` is received, the method aims to identify functionally similar ID observations for intervention. A direct computation of functional alignment across the entire `D_ID` is computationally intensive and matching 2D image segments does not fully capture robot state correspondence. To mitigate this, two strategies are employed:
 - **Proprioceptive Filtering:** The demonstration dataset `D_ID` is filtered to `O_q`, a subset of observations whose proprioceptive states `q` are similar to the current robot's proprioception `q̂`. This pre-filters the search space to trajectories relevant to the robot's current configuration, reducing computational load.
 - **Semantic Segmentation and Language Grounding:** The method utilizes `Grounded Segment Anything` to semantically segment both the OOD image `î` and relevant ID images `i ∈ O_q`, yielding sets of image masks and semantic labels (`Ω̂` and `Ω`). The expert's language input `l` is then decoded into a functional feature set `ϕ_l`. This `ϕ_l` is applied to the segmented images to identify `K` functionally corresponding image segments `(ω_j, ω̂_j)`. For example, `l = "Match pencils with pens"` would result in `ϕ_l` pairing 'pencil' segments in `î` with 'pen' segments in `i`. Currently, `l` is templated, but the framework is adaptable.
 - Finally, the functional alignment `f` (sum of IoU) is computed for each `o ∈ O_q` with `ô`, ranking them to obtain the ordered set `O_f` of functionally corresponding ID observations.

3. **Refining Functional Correspondences Until Confident (Section 4.3):**
 Functional correspondences may not be static and can evolve during task execution. Therefore, the method incorporates an interactive refinement loop. The confidence in a given correspondence is assessed by the diversity of retrieved robot action plans. A well-established correspondence is expected to reduce this diversity, converging on a "correct" behavior mode.
 - Action plans `A_q` are generated by passing all observations in `O_q` through the policy `π`.
 - K-means clustering is applied to `A_q` to identify `n_c` distinct behavior modes.
 - The entropy over the cluster labels of the functionally-aligned action plans `A_f ⊆ A_q` is then measured.
 - If this entropy is high, indicating ambiguity in the behavior modes, the robot prompts the expert for a refinement of the language description `l`, providing context through current observations and their associated behaviors. This iterative process continues until the entropy in the retrieved action modes is sufficiently low, signifying confidence.

4. **Intervening on Observations to Generate Functionally-Corresponding Behavior (Section 4.4):**
 Once the functional correspondence description `l` is finalized and the uncertainty in the retrieved action mode is low, the robot intervenes on its observations. Instead of directly executing actions based on the OOD observation `ô`, the policy is guided by behaviors derived from the functionally corresponding ID observations.
 - The embeddings of the `M` highest-ranked observations in `O_f` (based on functional alignment) are averaged: `ẑ := (1/M) Σ_{o∈O_f} E(o)`.
 - This averaged embedding `ẑ` is then passed to the pre-trained diffusion policy network to generate the final T-step action plan, effectively "imagining" the OOD scene as its functionally corresponding ID counterpart.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated the Adapting by Analogy (ABA) method through a series of hardware experiments using a Franka Research 3 robotic manipulator equipped with a UMI gripper, a wrist-mounted RealSense D435 camera, and a third-person Zed mini 2i camera. The robot's observations consisted of concatenated images from both cameras and proprioceptive states.

Two real-world manipulation tasks were used:
1. **Sweep-trash:** The robot sweeps trash (e.g., crumpled paper for recycling, M&Ms for organic waste) towards designated goals. This task involved two sub-goals: (A) aligning a wiper with the trash and (B) sweeping it to the correct location. The base diffusion policy was trained on 100 demonstrations (50 paper, 50 M&Ms).
2. **Object-in-cup:** The robot picks up an object (e.g., marker, pen) and drops it into a mug, requiring different dropping behaviors based on the object's properties (pen: grasped above center-of-mass, dropped from bottom; marker: grasped below center-of-mass, dropped from front). This task had three sub-goals: (A) grasping the object, (B) picking the correct behavior mode based on the grasp, and (C) dropping the object into the cup. The base diffusion policy was trained on 200 demonstrations (100 markers, 100 pens).

For each task, the policies were evaluated in two in-distribution (ID) environments and five out-of-distribution (OOD) environments, including novel backgrounds (e.g., black cloth) and novel objects (e.g., Doritos, napkin, thumb tacks for sweep-trash; pencil, battery, Jenga block for object-in-cup).

Three baselines were compared:
- **Vanilla:** The base visuomotor diffusion policy without any intervention.
- **PolicyEmbed:** Intervenes by retrieving ID observations based on cosine similarity in the base policy's learned embedding space, without expert feedback.
- **DINOEmbed:** Intervenes by retrieving ID observations based on cosine similarity in the DINOv2 visual feature space (class token and patch features), also without expert feedback.

All intervention methods utilized proprioception-filtered ID observations (`O_q`). ABA generated functional correspondence features `ϕ_l` by decoding templated language inputs `l` (e.g., "match OOD object semantic label with ID object semantic label") using Grounded Segment Anything for segmentation. Evaluation involved 10 rollouts per environment condition, totaling 140 rollouts.

The main findings are summarized as follows:

1. **Improvement in Closed-Loop Performance (Section 5.1):**
 ABA consistently improved task success rates. In ID environments, ABA enhanced performance by 15% for `sweep-trash` and 25% for `object-in-cup` compared to the Vanilla policy. For OOD background conditions, while Vanilla was somewhat robust for `sweep-trash`, it degraded significantly for the more challenging `object-in-cup` task. ABA improved over Vanilla by 20% in `sweep-trash` and a notable 90% in `object-in-cup`, demonstrating robustness to task-irrelevant background changes. Most significantly, when faced with OOD objects from new semantic categories, ABA improved success rates by 76% on both tasks compared to Vanilla, indicating effective transfer of learned behaviors through functional correspondence. Sub-goal level analysis (Supplementary Sec. C.1) showed ABA maintained high success rates across individual stages, where Vanilla often failed.

2. **Effectiveness of Feature Types for Intervention (Section 5.2):**
 For ID environments, `PolicyEmbed` and `DINOEmbed` performed comparably to ABA for `sweep-trash`, but ABA still outperformed them by 15% for `object-in-cup`. In OOD background conditions, `DINOEmbed` and `PolicyEmbed` were also robust, with `DINOEmbed` sometimes improving, potentially due to DINOv2's capabilities in dense correspondence matching within the same semantic category. However, `PolicyEmbed` showed worse performance for `object-in-cup` with novel backgrounds. Crucially, when confronted with OOD objects, both `DINOEmbed` and `PolicyEmbed` struggled significantly, achieving only 36.67% and 33.34% success rates for `sweep-trash`, respectively, and largely failing for `object-in-cup`. Failures were observed at grasping and selecting the correct behavior mode. This indicates that general visual features or policy embeddings alone are often insufficient to capture the task-relevant functional similarities needed for OOD generalization across distinct object categories.

3. **Efficiency of Expert Feedback (Section 5.3):**
 ABA demonstrated efficiency in seeking expert feedback. For `sweep-trash` (80 timesteps per rollout), feedback requests averaged 3.8 ± 1.66 times for ID conditions, increasing to 4.5 ± 1.2 for OOD backgrounds, and peaking at 5.2 ± 1.16 for novel OOD objects (Doritos). This corresponds to feedback being requested approximately 6% of the rollout duration for OOD objects. For `object-in-cup` (120 timesteps per rollout), feedback was requested 2.1 ± 0.7 times for ID objects, increasing to 4.3 ± 1.73 for OOD backgrounds, and 3.4 ± 1.62 for novel OOD objects (battery). These figures indicate that ABA requires infrequent queries to the expert, even in challenging OOD scenarios.

4. **Criticality of Functional Alignment (Section 5.4):**
 An analysis of successful rollouts from the `DINOEmbed` and `PolicyEmbed` baselines in the `object-in-cup` task (ID and OOD-Background conditions) revealed a correlation between success and the retrieval of functionally aligned observations. When these baselines succeeded, the observations they retrieved exhibited high precision with the observations retrieved by ABA. This finding suggests that implicitly, even successful baseline interventions were likely leveraging observations that shared functional similarities, underscoring the importance of functional correspondence for successful OOD generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Adapting by Analogy (ABA), a method that significantly advances the capability of visuomotor policies to generalize in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments. The primary significance of this work lies in demonstrating that robust OOD generalization can be achieved at deployment time, without the computationally expensive and time-consuming process of collecting new expert demonstrations or retraining the policy for every novel scenario.

The core insight that learned in-distribution (ID) behaviors can be effectively transferred to functionally similar OOD conditions, guided by expert-provided analogies, is a substantial contribution. This functional correspondence approach allows robots to "repurpose" existing knowledge, treating novel objects (like a pencil) as functionally equivalent to familiar ones (like a pen) for a specific task. The empirical results, showing up to a 76% improvement in task success rate for novel objects and backgrounds with minimal human intervention, underscore the practical utility of this approach. This indicates that ABA provides a more efficient and scalable paradigm for robot adaptation than previous methods relying on extensive data collection or purely implicit feature matching.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Robustness:** By enabling robots to adapt to unforeseen objects and environmental changes, ABA can lead to more robust and autonomous robotic systems capable of operating reliably in unstructured real-world settings (e.g., homes, logistics centers, or dynamic industrial environments). This reduces the reliance on meticulously curated environments and extensive manual reprogramming.
- **Reduced Data Collection and Training Costs:** The method minimizes the need for new demonstration data for every OOD instance, thereby alleviating a major bottleneck in robot learning. This could accelerate the development and deployment of robotic applications by making them more economically viable.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** The interactive nature of ABA, where an expert provides high-level functional correspondence descriptions in natural language, represents a practical step towards more intuitive human-robot collaboration. It leverages human cognitive abilities for abstract reasoning and generalization, complementing the robot's learned motor skills.
- **Foundation for Future Generalization Research:** This work establishes a framework for observation-based intervention guided by semantic and functional understanding. It opens avenues for future research into autonomous identification of correspondence features (e.g., using advanced foundation models), more sophisticated natural language grounding into embodied representations, and improved OOD detection mechanisms.

Despite its contributions, the method has acknowledged limitations. It assumes an underlying functional behavior overlap between OOD and ID scenarios, implying that entirely new fundamental strategies are not required. Tasks demanding genuinely novel approaches might still necessitate new demonstrations. The reliance on expert input, while efficient, could be a barrier for novice users, motivating future work on more autonomous correspondence discovery. Furthermore, the precision of language decoding into functional features and the calibration of the OOD detection mechanism are areas for further refinement. Addressing these limitations will be crucial for further expanding the applicability and autonomy of robots using this paradigm.
