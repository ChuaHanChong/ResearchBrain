# Implicit Behavioral Cloning

> [!summary] Summary
> Implicit Behavioral Cloning (Implicit BC) from Google Robotics introduces a method for robot policy representation using energy-based models (EBMs), which define policies implicitly as minimizers of an energy function. This approach demonstrates improved performance across various robot learning tasks, including achieving comparable results to state-of-the-art offline RL methods on D4RL and significantly lower error rates in image-based regression with fewer training examples.

## Problem

- Traditional Behavioral Cloning (BC) policies, which explicitly map observations to actions, often suffer from compounding errors when deployed.
- Explicit policy representations struggle to accurately model complex relationships, such as discontinuous, multi-valued, or set-valued functions, leading to smoothed approximations.
- As task dimensionality increases, the performance of explicit BC models tends to drop drastically.

## Method

- Introduces **Implicit Behavioral Cloning (Implicit BC)** that represents robot policies using ==Energy-Based Models (EBMs)==.
- The policy is defined ==implicitly as the minimizer of an energy function== E_theta(o, a), which captures the compatibility between observations and actions.
- The energy function is trained using an ==InfoNCE-style contrastive loss==, optimizing for demonstrated actions while pushing away from sampled ==negative actions==.
- Action inference is performed by solving an ==optimization problem== to find the action that minimizes the learned energy function.

## Results

- Achieved performance comparable to state-of-the-art offline reinforcement learning methods on the D4RL benchmark, despite not using reward information.
- Consistently outperformed explicit BC across simulated particle environments, demonstrating particular robustness to increasing task dimensionality.
- Achieved orders of magnitude lower error rates than explicit models on image coordinate regression tasks, especially with fewer training examples.
- Successfully demonstrated on real robot hardware for complex manipulation tasks, including bi-manual sweeping and contact-rich object pushing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit policy representations using EBMs can accurately model discontinuous, multi-valued, and set-valued functions, overcoming limitations of explicit models.
> - The energy landscape of implicit models allows for the representation of much more complex relationships between observations and actions.
> - Rethinking policy representation, rather than complex algorithm changes, can yield substantial performance improvements in robot learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Behavioral Cloning (BC) is a straightforward approach to robot learning where a robot learns to mimic human demonstrations. Despite its simplicity, BC has found success in various applications but still faces limitations like compounding errors when deployed. The researchers from Google Robotics propose a fundamental shift in how we represent policies in BC—moving from explicit to implicit policy representations.

In the figure above, we can see the key difference between traditional explicit policies (left) and the proposed implicit policies (right). Explicit policies directly map observations to actions using a function approximator, while implicit policies define an energy function over both observations and actions, selecting actions that minimize this energy.

This paper introduces Implicit Behavioral Cloning (Implicit BC), which uses energy-based models (EBMs) to represent robot policies. Rather than completely changing the imitation learning algorithm, the authors demonstrate that simply changing how the policy is represented can significantly improve performance across a range of robot learning tasks.

### 2. Understanding Policy Representations

In traditional behavioral cloning, a policy is represented explicitly as a function that maps observations to actions: $\hat{a} = f_\theta(o)$. The policy parameters $\theta$ are typically optimized using supervised learning methods like mean square error (MSE) to match demonstrated actions.

Implicit BC, however, represents policies as the minimizer of an energy function:

$$\hat{a} = \arg\min_a E_\theta(o, a)$$

Where $E_\theta(o, a)$ is an energy function that captures the compatibility between observations and actions. This approach allows the model to represent much more complex relationships between inputs and outputs.

The figure above illustrates the fundamental difference in how implicit and explicit models handle function approximation. The top row shows how implicit models can accurately model sharp discontinuities, multi-valued functions, and random distributions. The bottom row demonstrates that explicit models struggle with these same tasks, often producing smoothed approximations that fail to capture the underlying structure.

### 3. Methodology

Implicit BC uses energy-based models to represent policies. The authors adopt an InfoNCE-style contrastive loss to train the energy function:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(o,a) \sim \mathcal{D}} \log \frac{e^{-E_\theta(o,a)}}{\sum_{a' \in \{a\} \cup \{a_j^-\}_{j=1}^N} e^{-E_\theta(o,a')}}$$

Where $(o,a)$ are observation-action pairs from the demonstration dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and $\{a_j^-\}_{j=1}^N$ are "negative" actions sampled from a proposal distribution.

For inference, the method uses stochastic optimization to find the action that minimizes the energy:

$$\hat{a} = \arg\min_a E_\theta(o, a)$$

The authors implement three different EBM training and inference approaches:
1. Derivative-free optimization
2. Auto-regressive derivative-free optimization
3. Gradient-based Langevin sampling

The network architectures used for both explicit and implicit models are carefully designed to ensure fair comparison. For image-based tasks, a convolutional neural network processes the visual input, which is then combined with action inputs in a multi-layer perceptron (MLP).

### 4. Theoretical Advantages of Implicit Models

The paper provides theoretical justification for why implicit models outperform explicit ones in certain scenarios. The key advantage lies in their superior approximation capabilities, particularly for:

1. **Discontinuous Functions**: Implicit models can represent sharp discontinuities by creating appropriate energy landscapes.

2. **Multi-valued Functions**: They can represent one-to-many mappings where a single input corresponds to multiple valid outputs.

3. **Set-valued Functions**: Implicit models can learn to represent entire sets of valid actions for a given observation.

The authors formalize these advantages by proving that implicit models can approximate a larger class of functions than explicit models, including discontinuous and set-valued functions. They demonstrate this through visualization of energy landscapes:

The figure above shows how implicit models can represent complex functions through their energy landscapes. Panel (a) shows a smooth function, while (b) illustrates a discontinuous function, with (c) and (d) demonstrating how level sets of these functions behave.

### 8. Limitations and Future Work

Despite its successes, Implicit BC has some limitations:

1. **Computational Cost**: Inference requires solving an optimization problem, which is more computationally expensive than forward passes in explicit models.

2. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: The performance can be sensitive to choices of optimization algorithms and hyperparameters for the energy function.

3. **Scalability to Very High Dimensions**: While Implicit BC outperforms explicit models in higher dimensions, extremely high-dimensional action spaces might still pose challenges.

Future work could explore:
- More efficient optimization techniques for inference
- Combining Implicit BC with reinforcement learning
- Extending to multi-modal sensory inputs
- Applications to more complex manipulation tasks

### 9. Conclusion

Implicit Behavioral Cloning represents a significant advancement in robot learning by fundamentally changing how policies are represented. By using energy-based models to implicitly define policies, robots can learn more complex behaviors from demonstrations, particularly those involving discontinuities and multi-modal action distributions.

The consistent outperformance of Implicit BC across various tasks—from simulated benchmarks to real-world robot manipulation—suggests that this approach addresses fundamental limitations in traditional behavioral cloning. Rather than developing increasingly complex algorithms, the research demonstrates that rethinking the underlying representation can lead to substantial improvements.

This work opens new avenues for robot learning research, suggesting that implicit models may be better suited for capturing the complex, discontinuous nature of many real-world tasks. The theoretical insights into function approximation capabilities provide a solid foundation for understanding why implicit models offer these advantages, potentially influencing future approaches to robot learning beyond behavioral cloning.
