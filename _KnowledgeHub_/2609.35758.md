# Statistical Learning of Contractive Dynamical Representations for Composite Adaptive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A representation-learning framework for composite adaptive tracking control under disturbances dynamically coupled to the plant, such as liquid sloshing. A neural, uniformly contractive latent dynamical system, trained by hard expectation-maximization with a Kalman smoother E-step, predicts the disturbance and feeds a Kalman-Bucy composite adaptive controller with provable exponential convergence. It beats fixed-decay, LTI disturbance-accommodating and PD baselines on hardware and simulation.

## Problem

- Last-layer adaptive disturbance-rejection methods treat the latent disturbance as exogenous with a fixed decay rate, ignoring disturbances dynamically coupled to the plant like liquid sloshing.
- Without learned predictive disturbance dynamics, controllers cannot compensate between sparse or noisy disturbance measurements, since numerical differentiation amplifies noise and actuator delays block instantaneous cancellation.
- Classical disturbance-accommodating control relies on hand-specified or LTI-identified disturbance models, which fail on highly nonlinear coupled dynamics, while fitting latent models faces unidentifiable unknown initial states.

## Method

- ==Contractive-by-construction latent dynamics==: the latent matrix combines a negative-definite symmetric part (-βI minus LL^T) with a ==skew-symmetric== term, guaranteeing uniform contraction, plus affine-in-latent disturbance decoding.
- ==Hard-EM== training with a ==Kalman (RTS) smoother== E-step: infer MAP latent trajectories per data window, then update noise covariances from residual statistics and network weights via AdamW.
- **Kalman-Bucy composite adaptive controller**: the learned model drives filtering of the latent state plus a tracking-error adaptation term, with theorems proving exponential convergence to a bounded ball.

## Results

- On a slippery tracked vehicle carrying a sloshing tank and pendulum, yaw-rate RMS error reached **0.407** rad/s versus **0.414** (LTI DAC), **0.449** (PD), **0.468** (fixed-decay).
- The vehicle test used an unseen **30%** tank fill level; the method also had lowest velocity error (**0.149** m/s) and tied for lowest position error (**0.078** m).
- On coupled Duffing oscillators with the disturbance proxy available at only **1** Hz, it cut tracking MSE **27%** versus the closest competitor; LTI DAC underperformed even model-based PD.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When disturbances have their own dynamics, learning a predictive latent model beats exogenous fixed-decay adaptation, especially when measurements are sparse relative to the disturbance timescale.
> - Building contraction into the architecture makes learned dynamics insensitive to unknown initial states and bounds filter covariance, turning neural models into components with provable stability.
> - Keeping neural models affine in the latent state preserves exact Kalman inference, letting classical smoothing and EM train them and supply principled noise covariances for gain tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A low-traction tracked vehicle makes consecutive double lane changes while carrying a pendulum and a partly filled water tank, and its maneuvers excite those coupled loads. A disturbance caused by something coupled to a robot has its own evolving dynamics. Treating it as an external effect that simply fades at a fixed rate can miss how the robot's motion excites it. Disturbance estimates can also be noisy or delayed. On-board estimates derived by differentiating sensor readings can amplify noise, while actuator delays make instantaneous cancellation difficult.

The authors learn a hidden state that evolves with measured plant features and control inputs. They then decode it into a disturbance estimate for the controller. This gives the controller a model it can use to predict between observations, rather than only react to the latest estimate. In the vehicle test, this learned controller had the best reported yaw-rate error, and a fixed-decay version did worse.

### 2. How the controller gets a memory

A compact hidden state summarizes disturbance-relevant dynamics. Learned functions evolve that state using measured features and control inputs, and decode it into a disturbance estimate. A Kalman–Bucy-style filter updates the state using the history of noisy disturbance-proxy measurements. The controller then combines this estimate with tracking-error feedback to compensate the disturbance. This approach is disturbance-accommodating control (DAC): the controller represents the disturbance as a dynamic state, estimates it online, and compensates for it. In the paper's interpretation, prior fixed-decay methods are the special case in which the latent state decays at a chosen rate and has no learned input term. The proposed method learns state- and control-dependent latent dynamics instead.

The network is built to enforce uniform contraction, meaning differences between latent trajectories shrink over time. As a result, an unknown initial latent state becomes less influential.

Training uses hard-EM. For each data window, the hard E-step uses a Kalman smoother to infer the most likely latent trajectory. The M-step then updates the noise covariances from residuals and updates the neural-network parameters with AdamW.

The training process, separate from the online controller. The hard E-step infers latent states $a_{0},\ldots,a_{L-1}$ for each data window. The M-step updates $R_{d}$, $Q_{d}$, and $\theta$, then loops back.

### 3. What the tests establish

The hardware test used a tracked vehicle, the GVR-Bot. It carried a pendulum and a water tank at approximately 30% capacity, a level absent from the training data, which used approximately 60% and 0% tank levels. Low-friction tape emulated slippery terrain, and the command was two consecutive double lane changes.

On this run, the root-mean-square (RMS) angular-rate tracking error was **0.407 rad/s** for the proposed method. The FixedDecay ablation scored **0.468 rad/s**. That ablation uses the same smoother-based hard-EM training but restricts the model to fixed decay, with no learned latent input term or direct offset. The other baselines were also strong in places. Model-based PD tracked forward velocity strongly, and N4SIDv-DAC remained competitive on position and yaw-rate tracking.

A second test used a simulated pair of coupled Duffing oscillators. Only the first oscillator's position and velocity are observed, and its hidden partner is coupled by a nonlinear spring. The disturbance proxy is provided at 1 Hz, and added Gaussian noise $\sigma$ is varied. The setting $\sigma=0$ still includes baseline measurement noise. At $\sigma=0$, the proposed method has 27% lower mean tracking MSE than the closest competitor, averaged over 30 reference trajectories of 20 seconds each. It also remains strongest across the noise sweep.

Mean tracking MSE, averaged over 30 trajectories of 20 seconds each, versus the standard deviation $\sigma$ of added Gaussian noise on the sparsely provided proxy. The curves compare Model-based PD, N4SIDv-DAC, FixedDecay, and Ours. The authors attribute the flat N4SIDv-DAC curve to their fitted model, whose compensation mostly came from a state/control-dependent mapping.

### 4. When prediction matters

When disturbance measurements arrive less often than the disturbance changes, the learned latent dynamics let the controller carry an estimate forward between observations. Under the paper's stated assumptions, tracking error converges exponentially to a bounded neighborhood, not necessarily to zero. The evidence comprises one hardware platform and maneuver plus a simulated testbed.
