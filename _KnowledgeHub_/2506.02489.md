# Grasp2Grasp: Vision-Based Dexterous Grasp Translation via Schrödinger Bridges

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Princeton University and Lockheed Martin Corporation developed Grasp2Grasp, a vision-based framework that translates dexterous grasp intent between robot hands with different morphologies using Schrödinger Bridges and unpaired grasp datasets. The system achieved a mean grasp success rate of up to 67.45% and a 6D Grasp Wrench Hull IoU of 14.63% when transferring grasps, outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Existing data-driven dexterous grasp generation methods are hand-specific, limiting their applicability to diverse robotic hardware and requiring extensive retraining.
- Transferring grasps between robots with significantly different hand morphologies is challenging, often resulting in kinematically infeasible or suboptimal grasps.
- Current grasp transfer techniques typically require costly paired demonstration data or fail to explicitly preserve the functional intent (e.g., contact patterns, forces) of a grasp.

## Method

- Frames vision-based dexterous grasp translation as a ==Schrödinger Bridge (SB)== problem to learn a ==probabilistic transport== between unpaired source and target grasp distributions.
- Implements a two-stage ==latent-space pipeline==: a ==Variational Autoencoder (VAE)== encodes visual grasp observations, and neural networks approximate the SB dynamics in this latent space.
- Employs ==physics-informed cost functions== (e.g., contact map similarity, grasp wrench space overlap, manipulability) to compute an ==entropic Optimal Transport== plan, guiding the SB without paired demonstrations.

## Results

- **Grasp2Grasp** achieved significantly higher grasp success rates (up to **67.45%**) compared to baselines in IsaacGym simulations, demonstrating robust physical validity for translated grasps.
- The method showed superior functional alignment, with GWH-based and Contact-based variants attaining the highest 6D Grasp Wrench Hull IoU (**14.63%** and **14.54%**), effectively preserving mechanical grasp intent.
- Inference time averaged **0.8 seconds** per grasp, offering a balance between computational efficiency and the generation of stable and diverse grasp configurations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating grasp translation as a Schrödinger Bridge problem enables learning a stochastic process that interpolates between different hand grasp distributions, preserving functional intent.
> - Physics-informed ground costs, particularly those derived from grasp wrench space overlap or Jacobian-based manipulability, are critical for achieving stable and functionally aligned grasp transfers without paired data.
> - A robust latent space, learned via VAE and incorporating both kinematic and geometric reconstruction, is essential for representing grasp features generalizable across diverse hand morphologies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic grasping, particularly with dexterous, multi-fingered hands, presents a significant challenge in autonomous manipulation due to high-dimensional configuration spaces and complex contact dynamics. While data-driven methods have advanced grasp generation, a persistent limitation is their hand-specific nature, often requiring extensive retraining or fine-tuning for new robotic hands. This specificity restricts the practical applicability of learned policies across diverse robotic hardware.

The broader research landscape therefore seeks methods to reuse grasp knowledge acquired from one hand for another, a problem often referred to as grasp transfer or retargeting. Traditional approaches in this area, such as teleoperation and imitation learning, typically aim for direct replication of joint trajectories or end-effector poses. These methods often struggle when confronted with manipulators of significantly different morphologies, as direct transfer can lead to kinematically infeasible or suboptimal grasps. More advanced retargeting techniques have explored positional optimization for key points or neural network-based mappings, but these frequently rely on pre-defined link correspondences or detailed simulation-based preprocessing, limiting their generalizability and flexibility. A notable limitation of some existing methods is their object-agnostic nature, failing to explicitly preserve the functional intent of a grasp, such as specific contact patterns or force application.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing a framework for vision-based dexterous grasp translation that operates at a distributional level. It aims to transfer the *functional intent* of a grasp, rather than just its kinematic configuration, between morphologically distinct robotic hands, using only unpaired, hand-specific grasp datasets. The work leverages the theoretical formalism of Schrödinger Bridges (SB), a framework from optimal transport that models the most likely stochastic process interpolating between two probability distributions. While optimal transport (OT) and SB have gained traction in machine learning for tasks like generative modeling and domain adaptation, their application to complex robotic tasks like grasp synthesis and translation remains relatively underexplored. This research bridges vision-based grasping with probabilistic generative modeling, specifically SB, to enable semantic grasp transfer grounded in physical properties. It positions itself as a solution to the challenge of generalization in dexterous grasping by offering a physics-informed, distribution-level translation mechanism that moves beyond hand-specific learning and direct kinematic retargeting.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel approach for vision-based dexterous grasp translation, enabling the transfer of grasp intent across robotic hands with differing morphologies. Specifically, given a visual observation of a source hand grasping an object, the goal is to synthesize a functionally equivalent and physically valid grasp for a target hand without requiring paired demonstrations or hand-specific simulations.

This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Hand-Specific Limitations:** Existing data-driven grasp generation models are predominantly hand-specific, meaning they require extensive retraining or fine-tuning when adapting to new robotic hands. This constitutes a practical bottleneck in the development and deployment of robotic systems, especially given the rapid evolution of robotic hardware. The authors aim to overcome this by enabling the reuse of learned grasp knowledge across heterogeneous manipulators.

2. **Overcoming Morphological Mismatches in Grasp Transfer:** Direct transfer methods, such as copying joint angles or end-effector poses from a source hand to a target hand, are often inadequate due to significant morphological differences between hands. Such naive transfers can result in physically invalid or unstable grasps. The research is motivated by the need for a more fundamental framework that can bridge this morphological gap.

3. **Eliminating the Need for Paired Demonstration Data:** A significant challenge in grasp transfer is the acquisition of paired demonstration data, where a grasp for a source hand is explicitly mapped to a functionally equivalent grasp for a target hand. Manually creating or algorithmically discovering such pairings at scale is prohibitively difficult. The authors are motivated to develop a framework that can learn grasp translation using only *unpaired*, hand-specific grasp datasets.

4. **Preserving Functional Grasp Intent:** The paper emphasizes that the objective is not to replicate the precise joint configuration or trajectory of the source hand. Instead, the motivation is to preserve the *functional intent* of the grasp, which is expressed through physical quantities such as contact locations, grasp wrenches, or stability measures. This ensures that the translated grasp is not only kinematically feasible for the target hand but also performs a similar interaction with the object.

5. **Leveraging Object Geometry and Physics:** To bridge the morphological gap and achieve functional equivalence, the motivation is to leverage the underlying object geometry and physics. This necessitates the integration of physical properties into the grasp translation process.

6. **Applying Probabilistic Generative Modeling:** The authors are motivated to cast the grasp translation task as a probabilistic transport problem between grasp distributions. By leveraging the Schrödinger Bridge formalism, they aim to frame grasp translation as learning a distributional flow from observed source grasps to compatible target grasps, guided by a physics-informed notion of grasp similarity. This approach allows for capturing the stochastic nature and variability of plausible grasps for different hands.

These motivations collectively drive the development of a vision-based, simulation-free, and physics-informed framework for dexterous grasp translation, designed to enhance the adaptability and generalization capabilities of robotic manipulators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Grasp2Grasp, formulates vision-based dexterous grasp translation as a Schrödinger Bridge (SB) problem between source and target grasp distributions. This is achieved through a two-stage latent-space learning pipeline that combines variational autoencoders (VAEs) with score and flow matching techniques, guided by physics-informed cost functions.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is defined as learning a stochastic transport task between grasp distributions associated with different robotic hands. Given a visual observation of a source hand's configuration (implicitly through `o_hand`) grasping an object (`o_obj`), the goal is to generate a corresponding grasp configuration (`g_target`) for a target hand. The source grasps are assumed to follow a distribution `q_0(g_source | o_obj)`, and feasible target grasps follow `q_1(g_target | o_obj)`. The task is to learn a mapping between `q_0` and `q_1` that preserves physical grasp properties while respecting target hand constraints. This is achieved by defining a stochastic process `g_t_target` interpolating between `q_0` and `q_1`, governed by an SB with appropriate reference dynamics.

#### 3.2. Latent Score and Flow Matching Pipeline

The core of the methodology is a two-stage latent-space learning pipeline:

1. **Stage 1: Latent Space Learning (VAE Training):**
 - A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is trained to encode segmented visual observations of a source hand grasping an object into a latent variable `z`.
 - The VAE consists of an encoder (`Enc: O → Z`) and a decoder (`Dec: Z → G`), where `G` is the grasp configuration space.
 - The decoded grasp configuration `ĝ = Dec(z)` is passed through a differentiable forward kinematics (FK) layer (`FK: G → R^3N`) to obtain the 3D mesh vertices of the hand.
 - The VAE is optimized using a combined loss function that includes the squared L2 norm for reconstructing the ground truth grasp configuration (`g`), the squared L2 norm for reconstructing the hand mesh vertices (`v̂` against `FK(g)`), and a small Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence term to regularize the latent space.
 - This stage aims to create a latent space `Z` that encodes semantically meaningful and physically grounded grasp features generalizable across hands.

2. **Stage 2: Latent Schrödinger Bridge Training:**
 - A Schrödinger Bridge is modeled between the latent source (`q_0(z_source | o_obj)`) and target (`q_1(z_target | o_obj)`) distributions.
 - The stochastic path `z_t ∈ Z` is governed by an Itô Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE), which induces corresponding deterministic probability flow ODEs.
 - Neural networks `v_θ(t, z)` and `s_θ(t, z)` are trained to approximate the conditional flow drift (`u_t_circ`) and score (`∇ log p_t(z)`), respectively, following the [SF]^2M framework.
 - Training involves drawing pairs `(z_0, z_1)` from an entropic Optimal Transport (OT) plan (`π*_ε`) between `q_0` and `q_1`. These pairs are used to construct conditional Brownian bridge samples `z_t` for regression targets.
 - The objective function combines flow and score matching terms, weighted by a time-dependent schedule `λ(t)` to ensure numerical stability.
 - **Inference:** Given a visual observation of a source grasp (`o_obj, o_hand`), the source hand is encoded to `z_0`. This `z_0` is then evolved forward using Euler-Maruyama integration of the learned SDE dynamics to obtain a translated latent code `z_1`. This `z_1` is subsequently decoded to produce the target hand configuration `g_target`.

#### 3.3. Grasp-Specific OT Cost Design

A central challenge is the absence of paired source-target grasp samples. To guide the Schrödinger Bridge and establish meaningful correspondences, the methodology introduces physics-informed ground costs for computing the entropic OT plan `π*_ε`. Unlike conventional OT which uses geometric distances, these costs reflect task-relevant physical properties:

1. **Base Pose Cost (`d_pose`):** Measures the squared L2 norm for translation and squared Frobenius norm for rotation between the global wrist poses (position `h` and rotation `R`) of the source and target hands. This encourages coarse spatial alignment.

2. **Contact Map Similarity (`d_contact`):** Computes the bidirectional Chamfer distance between 3D point clouds representing the contact maps on the object surface for both source and target grasps. This promotes the preservation of local interaction geometry.

3. **Grasp Wrench Space Overlap (`d_wrench`):** Defined as `1 - IoU` (Intersection-over-Union) of the volumes of the grasp wrench hulls (GWHs). The GWH approximates the convex hull of wrench vectors (forces and torques) that can be exerted on an object. This cost encourages similarity in the mechanical capabilities of the grasps. For efficiency during training, a 3D approximation of the GWH (focusing on forces) is used, while 6D IoU is reported in evaluation.

4. **Jacobian-based Manipulability (`d_jac`):** Calculates the squared L2 distance between 6D vectors representing the maximal object motion (translational and rotational) with respect to hand joint angles. This is derived from the Jacobian matrix of object motion, computed using a differentiable simulator. This cost encourages the translated grasp to retain similar object-level controllability.

To manage the computational cost of exact OT, minibatch entropic OT is employed, where local OT plans are estimated for minibatches of source and target latent samples using Sinkhorn's algorithm.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **VAE Architecture:** Employs a PVCNN-based backbone for the hand point cloud encoder and a pretrained LION VAE for object point cloud encoding. Five contact anchor tokens are integrated to provide physical grounding.
- **SB Architecture:** A U-ViT model serves as the backbone for both the score `s_θ` and flow `v_θ` networks.
- **Simulators:** The Warp differentiable simulator is used for computing the Jacobian matrix for the manipulability cost. IsaacGym is utilized for evaluating grasp success under perturbations.
- **Inference:** Latent samples are evolved using Euler-Maruyama integration with discretized steps. No test-time fine-tuning is performed.

This comprehensive methodology aims to translate grasp intent between diverse robotic hands by learning a probabilistic transport process guided by functionally relevant physical properties, without requiring explicit paired demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Grasp2Grasp was conducted on the MultiGripperGrasp dataset, comprising 30.4 million grasps across 11 robotic manipulators and 345 objects. The primary evaluation scenarios focused on translating grasps between an articulated human hand, the Allegro hand, and the Shadow hand. Performance was assessed using grasp success rate under physical perturbations (IsaacGym), diversity of generated grasps (average standard deviation of joint angles), and functional similarity (6D GWH IoU).

#### 4.1. Quality of Translated Grasps

The study compared four variants of Grasp2Grasp, each trained with a distinct physics-based OT cost (pose, contact, GWH, Jacobian), against several baselines: RobotFingerPrint, Dex-Retargeting, CrossDex, and a Diffusion-based model.

- **Grasp Success Rate:** Grasp2Grasp consistently outperformed all baselines in terms of grasp success rate. The Jacobian-based and GWH-based OT cost variants achieved the highest mean success rates (67.45% and 66.34% respectively), suggesting that transport costs aligned with wrench capabilities or manipulability are effective for stable grasp transfer. In contrast, IK-style optimization baselines (Dex-Retargeting, CrossDex) exhibited lower performance, often generating physically invalid grasps due to ignored collisions. Qualitative results indicated that Grasp2Grasp produces stable grasps even from suboptimal source poses where baselines fail.

- **Diversity:** The contact-based variant of Grasp2Grasp achieved the highest mean diversity among all methods, producing a wide range of plausible grasp configurations while maintaining physical feasibility. Other OT costs showed lower diversity but still achieved high success rates. The Diffusion baseline also demonstrated high diversity, but with generally lower stability compared to Grasp2Grasp.

- **Inference Time:** Grasp2Grasp demonstrated an average inference time of approximately 0.8 seconds per grasp (with 100 integration steps), comparable to the Diffusion baseline (0.5s) and substantially faster than the optimization-based RobotFingerPrint (>5s). This indicates a balance between computational efficiency and grasp quality.

#### 4.2. Physical Alignment between Translated Grasps

Functional alignment was evaluated using the 6D Grasp Wrench Hull (GWH) Intersection-over-Union (IoU) between source and translated target grasps.

- **6D GWH IoU:** Grasp2Grasp variants trained with the GWH-based and Contact-based OT costs achieved the highest mean IoU (14.63% and 14.54% respectively), significantly outperforming all baselines. This indicates that these variants are more effective at preserving the object-level mechanical capabilities and interaction patterns of the original grasp, rather than merely replicating coarse geometric aspects. A complementary analysis in the appendix using Chamfer distance for contact alignment further supported these findings, with the Contact OT model showing the best performance.

The results suggested a trade-off: the Jacobian-based cost yielded the highest success rate (indicating stability), while the GWH and Contact costs achieved superior functional alignment (indicating preserved mechanical intent).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Effect of Diffusion Rate (σ):** Experiments demonstrated that smaller diffusion magnitudes (e.g., σ = 0.01) generally led to improved grasp success and GWH IoU. Larger diffusion rates (e.g., σ = 1.0) resulted in noisier paths and degraded physical precision, despite yielding higher diversity, supporting the notion that excessive noise can hinder fine-grained physical alignment.

- **Effect of Discretization Steps:** Evaluating the number of Euler-Maruyama integration steps (10, 100, 200) during test-time inference showed that 100 steps offered a balance between accuracy and efficiency. More steps generally led to better physical alignment. Grasp2Grasp exhibited robustness even with as few as 10 steps, outperforming the Diffusion baseline, which degraded significantly under low discretization, suggesting more stable and sample-efficient generation dynamics for the SB-based formulation.

- **VAE Ablation (Appendix):** An ablation on the VAE architecture showed that the full VAE, incorporating both hand parameter reconstruction loss and mesh vertex reconstruction loss via a differentiable forward kinematics layer, significantly outperformed alternatives. This indicated the importance of combining kinematic and geometric reconstruction for learning robust latent representations.

#### 4.4. Additional Transfer Settings (Appendix)

Experiments on additional transfer settings (e.g., Allegro→Human, Shadow→Human, Allegro→Shadow) consistently showed Grasp2Grasp outperforming baselines in diversity and 6D GWH IoU. For the Allegro→Shadow task where success rates could be reliably computed, Grasp2Grasp achieved the highest success rate. These results suggested the robustness of the approach across various challenging transfer scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by formulating vision-based dexterous grasp translation as a Schrödinger Bridge problem. This approach allows for the transfer of grasp intent between robotic hands with diverse morphologies, a long-standing challenge in generalized robotic grasping.

The significance of this work lies in several key aspects:

1. **Enabling Semantic Grasp Transfer:** By framing grasp translation as a probabilistic transport problem guided by physics-informed cost functions, the method moves beyond direct kinematic retargeting. This allows for the generation of functionally equivalent grasps that preserve physical properties like contact patterns, wrench capabilities, and manipulability, rather than just geometric configurations. This capability is crucial for semantic grasp understanding and for situations where precise replication is impossible due to morphological differences.

2. **Addressing Data Scarcity Challenges:** The methodology is designed to operate on unpaired, hand-specific grasp datasets, thereby circumventing the difficult and costly requirement for paired demonstrations between different hand types. This makes the approach more scalable and applicable in real-world scenarios where diverse hand-specific data might be available but explicit pairings are not.

3. **Bridging Vision-Based Grasping with Probabilistic Generative Modeling:** The application of Schrödinger Bridges, a framework from optimal transport, to dexterous grasp translation represents an advancement in integrating sophisticated probabilistic generative models with robotic perception and action. This opens avenues for leveraging the strengths of these models in handling the inherent stochasticity and complexity of plausible grasp distributions.

4. **Demonstrated Robustness and Generalization:** Experimental results indicate that the approach generates stable, physically grounded, and diverse grasps. Its performance surpasses existing baselines in grasp success rate and functional alignment across various hand-object combinations, demonstrating strong generalization capabilities to different source and target hand morphologies.

5. **Computational Efficiency:** The generative nature of the model, once trained, allows for amortized inference, offering a substantial speed improvement over iterative optimization-based retargeting methods. This makes it more suitable for real-time or near real-time applications.

**Potential Impact:**
The ability to robustly translate grasp intent across heterogeneous manipulators could have several impacts:
- **Accelerated Learning:** It could accelerate the process of learning new manipulation skills by allowing robots to leverage knowledge acquired from human demonstrations or other robot platforms, regardless of kinematic differences.
- **Enhanced Robot Flexibility:** It can facilitate hardware substitution in manipulation pipelines, allowing for interchangeability of robotic hands and more adaptable robotic systems.
- **Advancements in Assistive Robotics:** By enabling more flexible and adaptable grasping behaviors, it could expand capabilities in assistive robotics, where diverse human and robotic hand morphologies interact.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
Despite its strengths, the paper identifies limitations that suggest avenues for future research:
- **Challenges with Complex Geometries:** Performance degrades for thin-shell objects or those with ambiguous contact regions, indicating a need for improved robustness in challenging geometric scenarios.
- **Hand-Specific Generalization:** The current framework does not generalize to *unseen* hand morphologies at inference time; retraining or fine-tuning is required for new target hands. Future work could explore methods for zero-shot or few-shot generalization to novel hand designs.
- **Dataset Quality Dependency:** Discrepancies in performance for hands with more complex kinematics (e.g., Shadow hand) suggest that dataset quality and coverage remain critical for enhancing generalization and translation fidelity.
- **Societal Implications:** The authors acknowledge the broader implications for safe deployment, failure detection, and responsible generalization in real-world systems, encouraging further research into integrating distributional grasp priors into transparent and human-aligned manipulation pipelines.
