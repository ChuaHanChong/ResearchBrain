# Learning to Localize Reference Trajectories in Image-Space for Visual Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> LoTIS, a visual navigation model, learns to localize pre-recorded reference trajectories in a robot's image space, providing robot-agnostic guidance. It significantly improves navigation success rates across diverse robots, camera settings, and challenging scenarios like backward traversal, surpassing state-of-the-art baselines.

## Problem

- Existing visual navigation policies are tightly coupled to specific robot kinematics and action spaces, limiting generalization across diverse robot embodiments.
- Current methods are sensitive to camera intrinsics mismatches, single subgoal selection errors, and primarily support forward traversal, struggling with off-trajectory initialization.
- State-of-the-art visual navigation solutions typically fail at tasks like robust backward traversal and navigation to arbitrary points along a trajectory.

## Method

- **LoTIS** (Learning to Localize Reference Trajectories in Image-Space) predicts a triplet for each reference frame in the robot's current image-space: 2D coordinates, visibility, and normalized distance.
- The model employs an ==asymmetric architecture== with an offline trajectory encoder and an online lightweight query encoder and decoder, utilizing a ==recurrent prediction head== for iterative refinement.
- A ==cross-trajectory training== strategy exposes the model to diverse camera parameters (FOV, aspect ratio, height) and viewpoint perturbations to foster robustness without action annotations.

## Results

- LoTIS achieved up to **98.5%** success rate in forward navigation in simulation, outperforming baselines by **24.3 to 32.8** percentage points, and reached **100%** with obstacle avoidance.
- It demonstrated high robustness to off-trajectory initialization (**88.2%** SR Gibson, **74.0%** SR HM3D) and camera mismatches (**83.6%** SR Gibson), maintaining performance where baselines significantly degraded.
- The model enabled robust backward traversal (**94.4%** real-world success) and navigation to arbitrary points along a trajectory, and successfully transferred to real-world quadrotor and quadruped robots using smartphone-recorded paths, showing resilience to occlusions and day-to-night changes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling visual perception (trajectory localization) from robot action enables the development of robot-agnostic guidance compatible with various classical motion controllers.
> - Jointly processing the full reference trajectory provides a richer, more consistent guidance signal compared to relying on single subgoal images, enhancing robustness to off-trajectory initializations and viewpoint changes.
> - A training regimen incorporating diverse camera intrinsics and viewpoints is crucial for achieving high generalization and robustness to real-world camera mismatches and environmental variations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Visual navigation enables autonomous robots to traverse environments using only camera observations. Historically, this field evolved from methods focusing on navigating to a single goal image towards more complex topological approaches that utilize visual reference trajectories—sequences of unposed RGB images recording a desired path. This latter approach allows routes to be defined through demonstration using readily available devices like smartphones, bypassing the need for metric maps or explicit camera calibration.

Current state-of-the-art methods in visual navigation, such as GNM, ViNT, NoMaD, PlaceNav, and FAINT, typically employ end-to-end learning. These methods train policies that directly map current visual observations and a goal image (often a subgoal extracted from the reference trajectory) to robot-specific actions. While these approaches have demonstrated capabilities in forward trajectory following, the authors identify several limitations:

1. **Coupled Perception and Action:** Policies are often tied to specific robot kinematics and action spaces (e.g., planar differential drive). This constrains generalization to diverse robot embodiments, such as aerial or legged robots, which possess different motion capabilities.
2. **Restricted Training Data:** Learning a policy from observations to actions typically requires training examples where the robot traverses from a current view to a goal image, generally sampled from the same trajectory. This implicitly assumes that the deployment camera shares characteristics with the recording device, making these methods susceptible to performance degradation when camera intrinsics or robot embodiment deviate.
3. **Sensitivity to Single Subgoal Selection:** Reliance on a single subgoal image makes the system vulnerable to selection errors. An incorrect subgoal can lead to navigation failure if the robot cannot visually match its current view to the target.
4. **Limited Traversal Capabilities:** These methods are primarily designed for forward trajectory following and often struggle with backward traversal, where the robot encounters viewpoints substantially different from those captured in the recorded trajectory.

The paper contrasts its approach with other related fields:
- **Visual Servoing (IBVS, PBVS):** Classical methods for translating visual feedback into control actions. However, they are noted for limitations such as local minima, sensitivity to calibration errors, and vulnerability to feature tracking failures under occlusions or appearance changes.
- **Concurrent Image-Space Local Navigation (e.g., VAMOS, VENTURA):** These methods focus on local navigation towards nearby goals (language or image-based) from the current observation. The authors position their work as complementary, providing long-range trajectory localization, which could feed into such local planners.
- **Learned Visual Geometry (e.g., DUSt3R, MASt3R, VGGT, Depth Anything 3):** These models excel at 3D reconstruction, dense mapping, and pose estimation. However, the authors observe that applying these models directly to navigation trajectories often yields inconsistent poses or failed reconstructions, suggesting that solving the full 3D reconstruction problem (which prioritizes global metric accuracy) may be computationally heavier and more complex than the relative trajectory localization needed for navigation.

This research aims to address the identified limitations of existing visual navigation methods by decoupling perception from action and developing a robot-agnostic guidance system that focuses on localizing the reference trajectory in the robot's current image space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to enable robust visual navigation to any point along a pre-recorded RGB reference trajectory, utilizing only RGB images from the robot's current viewpoint. This is intended to function without explicit assumptions about camera calibration or robot embodiment, allowing the reference trajectory to be recorded with a different camera or platform than the one used for navigation.

The detailed motivations and specific objectives are:

- **Develop Robot-Agnostic Guidance:** The motivation is to overcome the limitation of action-centric policies being tied to specific robot kinematics. The objective is to predict the visual appearance of the reference trajectory in the robot's current image-space, rather than outputting direct robot actions. This aims to provide guidance that is universally applicable across diverse robot embodiments (e.g., quadrupeds, drones) without requiring robot-specific training.
- **Decouple Perception from Action:** By separating the visual localization task from the motion control task, the model's predictions can be integrated with various downstream classical controllers or planners. This modularity is intended to simplify deployment and allow for more flexible system design.
- **Enhance Robustness to Environmental and Camera Variations:** Current methods struggle with mismatches in camera intrinsics, mounting heights, and off-trajectory starting points. The objective is to achieve robustness against these variations through a novel training strategy. This involves exposing the model to diverse camera parameters and viewpoints during training to increase its generalization capabilities.
- **Enable Challenging Navigation Capabilities:** A significant motivation is to enable functionalities largely absent in prior work, such as robust backward traversal and navigation to arbitrary points along the trajectory. Existing methods, primarily designed for forward following, fail when the robot's view significantly opposes the reference images.
- **Improve Localization Consistency and Accuracy:** The reliance of previous methods on selecting a single subgoal image leads to sensitivity. The objective is to process the full reference trajectory jointly to provide a richer, more consistent guidance signal, thereby improving localization accuracy, especially when initialized far from the trajectory.
- **Ensure Real-Time Deployment and Practicality:** The model is designed to be lightweight and efficient enough for real-time operation on embedded hardware. Furthermore, it aims to be user-friendly, allowing reference trajectories to be recorded with common devices like handheld phone cameras.

In essence, the research aims to provide a more general, robust, and versatile visual navigation solution that can be seamlessly integrated with existing robot control frameworks, extending capabilities beyond what is typically achieved by end-to-end action prediction policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, LoTIS (Learning to Localize Reference Trajectories in Image-Space), addresses visual navigation by decoupling perception from action. It learns to predict the image-space representation of a reference trajectory within the robot's current view.

**Guidance Representation:**
For each image $I_i$ in the reference trajectory $T = \{I_1, \dots, I_N\}$, the model predicts a triplet in the robot’s current image-space:
- **$p_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$**: 2D image-space coordinates of where the camera pose corresponding to $I_i$ would appear, normalized to $[-1, 1] \times [-1, 1]$.
- **$v_i \in \mathbb{R}$**: A visibility logit indicating whether the pose is visible, occluded, or out of view.
- **$d_i \in$**: A normalized distance to the pose from the current viewpoint, where the farthest visible point has a distance of 1.

**Model Architecture:**
LoTIS employs an asymmetric architecture designed for efficient real-time deployment. A large trajectory encoder processes the full reference trajectory offline once, while a lightweight query encoder and decoder run online for each incoming camera image.
- **Backbone:** All images (reference and query) are initially processed by a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/14 backbone, known for its performance on geometric vision tasks. Images are resized to $224 \times 224$.
- **Trajectory Encoder ($E_T$):** This component processes the sequence of DINOv3 features from the reference trajectory $T$. It projects features to a hidden dimension $D=256$ and prepends a learnable summary token per frame. $L=12$ transformer blocks alternate between global attention (across all frames and patches) and frame-wise attention (within each frame), incorporating temporal structure via Rotary Position Encodings (RoPE). The output, $F_T \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times (P+1) \times D}$, encapsulates spatio-temporal context.
- **Query Encoder ($E_q$):** The query image $I_q$ is processed similarly by the frozen DINOv3 backbone and projected. It then undergoes $L/2=6$ self-attention layers with spatial RoPE, followed by a linear adapter to align features with the trajectory encoder's space, yielding $F_q \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times D}$.
- **Query-Trajectory Fusion ($F_{qT}$):** This module fuses $F_q$ and $F_T$ through $L/2=6$ blocks. Each block alternates between cross-attention and frame-wise self-attention. During cross-attention, trajectory features (as queries) attend to query image tokens (as keys/values) to establish visual correspondences. The subsequent frame-wise self-attention aggregates these local correspondences into a global context within each frame's summary token.
- **Prediction Head ($P$):** A recurrent transformer regressor iteratively refines the predictions over $K=4$ iterations. It maintains a latent estimate for each trajectory frame, which is used to condition the summary tokens via Adaptive Layer Normalization (AdaLN). Self-attention across all $N$ frames enforces geometric consistency along the trajectory. Finally, linear layers predict residual updates, and the refined estimates are projected to the output space ($p_i$, $v_i$, $d_i$).

**Training:**
- **Cross-Trajectory Training:** A key aspect is the training strategy, which samples reference trajectories and query images from *different* trajectories within the same environment (for real-world data) or from random poses in the vicinity (for simulated data). This strategy exposes the model to camera mismatches, off-trajectory viewpoints, and environmental variations (e.g., lighting, dynamic objects), fostering robustness without requiring action annotations. In simulation, camera parameters (FOV, aspect ratio, mounting height) and rotational perturbations are randomized.
- **Ground Truth Generation:** Ground-truth image coordinates and distances are obtained by projecting trajectory camera centers into the query camera view using known camera poses. Visibility labels are generated by checking if the projected point is within the image bounds and not occluded (using depth maps, with PriorDepthAnything for real-world data).
- **Losses:** The model is optimized using a weighted sum of three objectives:
 - **$L_{pos}$ (L1 loss, $\lambda_{pos}=10.0$):** Applied to predicted 2D coordinates $p_i$, masked to penalize only visible points.
 - **$L_{vis}$ (Binary Cross-Entropy, $\lambda_{vis}=1.0$):** Applied to visibility logits $v_i$.
 - **$L_{dist}$ (L1 loss, $\lambda_{dist}=6.0$):** Applied to normalized distance predictions $d_i$.
 These losses are computed at each iteration of the prediction head with geometric temporal weighting.
- **Datasets:** Training utilizes a diverse mix of simulated (HM3D, HSSD, AI2-THOR) and real-world datasets (CODa, LILocBench, BotanicGarden, TartanGround), totaling approximately 25,000 reference trajectories and 850,000 query images across 500 environments. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 for roughly 4 days.

**Navigation:**
The model's predictions ($p_i, v_i, d_i$) are designed to interface with classical motion planning and control stacks. Two controllers are demonstrated:
- **Yaw Controller with Constant Forward Velocity:** A simple proportional controller identifies the closest visible point on the trajectory (using visibility and normalized distance) and steers the robot's yaw to align with this target, maintaining a constant forward speed. It includes a small lookahead for smoother navigation. If no points are visible, it can use non-visible points at the frame border to guide towards the trajectory.
- **Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) Control:** For more sophisticated control (demonstrated with a drone), an MPPI controller is used. It formulates a cost function to minimize deviation from the goal, ensure predictions remain in the robot's field of view, and proactively avoid collisions. Predicted depth maps from UniDepthV2 are used to ground image-space predictions in 3D and for collision avoidance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LoTIS was conducted through extensive simulation experiments and real-world deployments, comparing its performance against four state-of-the-art baselines (ViNT, PlaceNav, NoMaD, FAINT).

**Simulation Experiments (Gibson and HM3D Datasets):**
- **Forward On-Trajectory Navigation:** LoTIS achieved a 94.7% success rate (SR) on Gibson and 98.5% on HM3D, significantly outperforming the best baseline (ViNT) by 24.3 to 32.8 percentage points. When augmented with simple obstacle avoidance, LoTIS reached 100% SR. This performance is attributed to LoTIS's ability to localize the entire trajectory, providing a richer guidance signal compared to single-subgoal methods.
- **Off-Trajectory Initialization:** Baselines showed substantial performance degradation when initialized far from the trajectory (e.g., ViNT's SR on HM3D dropped from 65.7% to 24.0%). LoTIS maintained high robustness, achieving 88.2% SR on Gibson and 74.0% SR on HM3D, improving to 98.7% (Gibson) and 95.1% (HM3D) with obstacle avoidance. This indicates LoTIS's predictions remain accurate from distant viewpoints, unlike baselines whose subgoal localization accuracy decreased rapidly with increasing initialization distance.
- **Camera Mismatch Robustness:** All baselines experienced significant performance drops (e.g., ViNT's SR on Gibson fell from 70.4% to 21.1%) when camera intrinsics (FOV, aspect ratio) and mounting height mismatched between reference and query. LoTIS maintained high performance across these varied settings (e.g., 83.6% SR on Gibson, 98.0% with obstacle avoidance), a result attributed to its cross-trajectory training strategy. The model exhibited some sensitivity to large height mismatches.
- **Backward Navigation:** LoTIS demonstrated a substantial advantage in backward traversal, a task where baselines largely failed. On Gibson (On-Trajectory/Matched), LoTIS achieved 88.2% SR (97.4% with obstacle avoidance), while baselines ranged from 1.3% (ViNT) to 11.8% (FAINT). Similar trends were observed on HM3D. Even in the challenging Off-Trajectory+Cross-Camera setting for backward navigation, LoTIS maintained SRs between 65.2% and 86.8% (86.8% to 90.8% with obstacle avoidance), while baselines remained below 13%. This capability is achieved without explicit backward traversal demonstrations in training, relying on cross-trajectory training and joint trajectory processing.
- **Navigation to Arbitrary Goals (Any Point):** In this generalized task, requiring off-trajectory initialization and navigation to a random point on the trajectory, LoTIS maintained high SRs (85.5% on Gibson, 71.1% on HM3D), which increased to over 94% with obstacle avoidance.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- **Transfer to Diverse Embodiments:** LoTIS successfully transferred to real-world deployment on both a Crazyflie quadrotor (indoors with MPPI) and a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped (outdoors with a yaw controller), using reference trajectories recorded with a handheld Google Pixel 6 smartphone.
- **Navigation Performance:** LoTIS achieved 100% success on forward navigation across all four real-world environments, outperforming FAINT, which succeeded in only 27.8% of trials. For backward traversal, LoTIS maintained a 94.4% success rate (17/18 trials), whereas FAINT achieved 5.6%.
- **Robustness to Environmental Changes:**
 - **Crowded Environments:** LoTIS maintained high success (5/6 forward, 5/6 backward) in an indoor environment with people temporarily occluding the view, indicating accurate predictions even with substantial visual interference.
 - **Day-to-Night Transfer + Scene Changes:** The model achieved 6/6 forward and 5/6 backward success when navigating at night in an environment whose reference trajectory was recorded during daytime with different scene elements. While some prediction quality degradation was observed, the learned representation exhibited robustness to appearance and geometric variations.

**Runtime:**
LoTIS utilizes an asymmetric deployment pipeline where trajectory encoding is performed once offline. On a Jetson Orin AGX, offline encoding for a 40-frame trajectory took 120 ms, and online inference (query encoding and decoding) took 45 ms per frame (~22 Hz). On an RTX 5090 desktop, these times were 15 ms and 6 ms per frame (~160 Hz), respectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents LoTIS, a visual navigation model that introduces a shift from action-prediction to image-space trajectory localization, which has several implications for the field of robotics and autonomous systems.

**Significance:**
- **Decoupling of Perception and Action:** A primary contribution is the demonstration of effective decoupling between visual perception and robot action. By providing robot-agnostic guidance in image-space, LoTIS allows for flexible integration with existing, specialized local planners and controllers tailored for diverse robot kinematics. This addresses a fundamental limitation of end-to-end learning policies that are typically constrained to specific action spaces.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:**
 - **Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The ability to provide zero-shot guidance across diverse robot platforms (quadruped, quadrotor) from a single phone-recorded trajectory simplifies deployment and lowers the barrier for adopting visual navigation solutions in varied robotic applications.
 - **Robustness to Mismatched Cameras and Viewpoints:** The proposed cross-trajectory training strategy significantly improves the model's robustness to variations in camera intrinsics, mounting heights, and off-trajectory starting positions. This is a critical advancement for real-world scenarios where precise sensor matching and initial positioning cannot always be guaranteed.
 - **Resilience to Environmental Changes:** Demonstrated robustness to dynamic occlusions and significant day-to-night/seasonal appearance changes indicates improved adaptability to unconstrained environments.
- **Novel Navigation Capabilities:** LoTIS enables capabilities that previous state-of-the-art methods largely fail at:
 - **Backward Traversal:** The robust performance in navigating a trajectory in reverse direction significantly expands the utility of visual reference trajectories, allowing robots to traverse paths bidirectionally.
 - **Arbitrary Point Navigation:** The ability to navigate to any specified point along the trajectory, rather than just the start or end, offers greater operational flexibility.
- **Practicality and Accessibility:** The model's efficiency (real-time on embedded hardware) and its ability to utilize easily collected data (e.g., phone videos) enhance its practical applicability and accessibility for a broader range of users and scenarios.

**Potential Impact:**
The research could impact several domains:
- **Industrial Robotics:** Streamlining the deployment of autonomous mobile robots in factories and warehouses by allowing non-expert users to define navigation paths easily with a handheld camera.
- **Exploration and Inspection:** Enhancing the capabilities of inspection robots (e.g., drones inspecting infrastructure or ground robots exploring hazardous environments) to follow complex, predefined routes robustly, even in challenging visual conditions or when starting far from the path.
- **Service Robotics:** Improving the reliability and adaptability of robots operating in dynamic human environments, where pathways might be temporarily obstructed or lighting conditions change.
- **Research Platforms:** Providing a more versatile and generalized perception module for visual navigation research, allowing efforts to be concentrated on control strategies or higher-level task planning rather than re-training perception for each new robot or environment.

**Limitations Identified by Authors:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Visual Overlap Requirement:** The method fundamentally requires visual overlap between the robot's current view and some portion of the reference trajectory. Failures can occur if this overlap is lost, particularly around sharp corners or under extreme viewpoint differences, or with large camera height mismatches.
- **Real-World Evaluation Scope:** The real-world evaluation was limited to four environments, which may not encompass the full diversity of challenging scenarios (e.g., highly unstructured or repetitive environments).
- **Trajectory Length:** Systematically evaluated trajectories were up to approximately 100 meters (subsampled to 40 frames). While longer trajectories might be handled via chunking (demonstrated), this aspect was not systematically evaluated.
- **Pre-recorded Trajectories:** LoTIS is designed for navigation along pre-recorded paths and is not intended for exploring or reaching entirely unseen goals.

**Future Work:**
Future research directions proposed include integrating temporal memory to improve recovery from temporary loss of trajectory view, developing methods to provide guidance even without a clear line of sight to address height mismatch limitations, and combining image-space guidance with semantic understanding to enable navigation to functional goals (e.g., "the kitchen").
