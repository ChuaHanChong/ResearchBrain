# Geometric Entropy: When Trajectory Diversity Helps and Hurts in Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Geometric Entropy (H_G) is a task-agnostic, pre-training metric for trajectory-shape diversity in robot demonstrations. It isolates free-space transit segments, aligns them in a target-anchored frame that strips goal pose and workspace scale, then scores the covariance eigenspectrum via effective dimensionality plus total variance. Across simulation and real-robot manipulation, success versus H_G traces an inverted-U whose peak shifts lower as task mastery rises.

## Problem

- Data-scaling imitation learning work treats diversity coarsely as tasks, objects, or embodiments, never quantifying trajectory geometry as its own dataset attribute.
- Curation and synthesis methods optimize selection, robustness, or coverage, but offer no predictive criterion for whether to raise or lower geometric diversity.
- Naive spread measures on raw positions inflate diversity from extrinsic factors like randomized goal pose and workspace scale, mistaking placement variation for genuine strategy difference.

## Method

- Phase decomposition splits episodes at semantic gripper events into free-space transit segments versus contact-constrained fine manipulation, scoring only transit where strategy variation is learnable.
- Endpoint-anchored frame alignment: arc-length resample to 50 waypoints, translate terminal point to origin, rotate into target frame, divide by start-end displacement.
- Geometric Entropy sums log effective dimensionality (exponentiated Shannon entropy of the normalized covariance spectrum) and log total variance, aggregated across phases by trajectory-count weighting.

## Results

- H_G stabilizes within 50-100 successful demonstrations on StackCube-v1, and rises monotonically with approach radius (2.901 at r=0 to 3.667 at r=0.10) while single-mode k=1 lowers it to 2.997.
- Diffusion Policy shows inverted-U success on StackCube-v1 and PegInsertionSide-v1 with the peak shifting leftward as dataset size grows from 100 to 1000; fine-tuned pi-0.5 decays monotonically.
- Real ARX-arm ACT policies over 40 test episodes and three seeds peak at H_G around 3.3 on StackCube, while PlacePanda and OpenDrawer decline monotonically; variance, LogDet, participation ratio, and kNN entropy all alias or drift.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Match demonstration diversity to learner mastery rather than maximizing it; more data, easier tasks, or stronger pretrained priors all shrink the tolerable diversity budget.
> - Normalize away extrinsic context before measuring diversity, otherwise identical straight-line motions at randomized goal poses register as maximally diverse.
> - Spectral summaries that combine mode count with mode spread avoid the aliasing where marginal-variance proxies cannot separate many tight modes from one wide corridor.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern imitation learning (IL) increasingly relies on large, diverse datasets and high-capacity policy architectures, including diffusion policies and vision-language-action (VLA) models. Prior research in this area has focused on various aspects of data quality, such as observation fidelity, label accuracy, and task or scene coverage. Initiatives like RT-1, RT-2, Open X-Embodiment/RT-X, and DROID emphasize scaling up data and models for broad generalization, often treating "diversity" as a coarse attribute related to tasks, objects, embodiments, or modalities.

Concurrently, work on data curation and collection for IL addresses challenges like distribution shift, suboptimal demonstrations, and generating synthetic data to expand coverage. Techniques include DAgger for interactive learning, reweighting or filtering suboptimal data, self-supervised curation, and data synthesis methods like MimicGen and FieldGen. Another relevant area is the study of trajectory diversity as multimodality in conditional action distributions, where methods learn stochastic multi-skill policies or model multimodality via discretization. Benchmarks like D3IL also evaluate diversity, and some analyses acknowledge that varying certain factors can hinder learning.

This paper addresses an underexplored dimension within this landscape: the specific impact of *trajectory-shape diversity* in demonstrations. While others consider broad data diversity, this work isolates the geometric shape of motion paths, distinguishing it from extrinsic factors like goal pose or workspace scale. It introduces a metric, Geometric Entropy (H_G), to quantify this intrinsic shape diversity, and systematically investigates its non-monotonic relationship with IL performance. Unlike prior work that primarily optimizes data selection or robustness, this research provides a diagnostic tool and a guiding principle for *calibrating* geometric diversity, moving beyond a simple "maximize diversity" heuristic. It proposes an IL-specific inverted-U trend, indicating conditions under which geometric diversity aids or hinders learning, and how this trend shifts with increasing task mastery.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to systematically investigate how trajectory-shape diversity in demonstration datasets influences the performance of imitation learning models across various architectures, tasks, and data scales. The authors are motivated by the observation that while IL is becoming increasingly data-centric, the optimal level of diversity in the geometric shapes of demonstration trajectories remains poorly understood.

The core motivations and objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Understand the Dual Nature of Trajectory-Shape Diversity:** The authors recognize that trajectory-shape diversity presents a "double-edged sword." On one hand, varied approach geometries can enhance policy robustness by expanding the effective coverage of relevant state-action regions, thereby preventing overfitting to a narrow motion corridor. This is particularly crucial for handling variations in test-time initial conditions or environmental perturbations. On the other hand, excessive diversity can introduce "mode inconsistency," where multiple distinct strategies exist for achieving the same goal, but the conditioning signal available to the learner may be insufficient to reliably select among them. In contact-rich manipulation tasks, this ambiguity can lead to interpolation between incompatible strategies, resulting in infeasible motions and significant performance degradation. This suggests a non-monotonic relationship between geometric diversity and success, where both too little and too much diversity can be detrimental.

2. **Develop a Task-Agnostic Metric for Intrinsic Geometric Diversity:** To systematically study this relationship, a reliable pre-training metric is required. This metric must quantify the *intrinsic* between-trajectory geometric shape diversity, rather than superficial variability caused by extrinsic factors. Extrinsic factors include episode-specific elements like the target object's pose, its placement within the workspace, or the overall scale of the manipulation. A naive diversity measure that doesn't account for these factors would inaccurately inflate diversity estimates (e.g., a dataset of identical straight-line approaches executed at randomly varied goal locations would appear highly diverse).
 - **Objective:** Propose Geometric Entropy (H_G), a lightweight, task-agnostic scalar metric designed to isolate the learnable geometric signal. This involves a pipeline for normalizing trajectories by aligning them to a canonical target-anchored frame, resampling them to a fixed length, and scaling them to remove variations in physical extent. H_G then summarizes the intrinsic complexity of the resulting shape manifold using a stable spectral statistic.

3. **Characterize the Relationship Between Geometric Diversity and IL Performance:** The primary empirical objective is to experimentally determine how IL success rates evolve as a function of H_G. This involves systematic evaluation across:
 - **Multiple IL Architectures:** To assess if the observed trends are generalizable across different types of learning models (e.g., specialist models like Diffusion Policy and generalist models like VLA models).
 - **Diverse Manipulation Tasks:** Including both simulation-based and real-robot contact-rich tasks (e.g., StackCube, PegInsertionSide, PlacePanda, OpenDrawer) to observe effects under varying levels of task difficulty and constraints.
 - **Varying Data Scales:** To understand how the relationship changes as the amount of training data increases.

4. **Identify an Organizing Principle Governing Optimal Diversity:** Beyond merely observing trends, the authors aim to synthesize their findings into a unifying principle that explains when and why certain levels of geometric diversity are optimal or detrimental.
 - **Objective:** Formulate and support the "Mastery–Entropy Principle," which posits that the optimal geometric entropy (H∗G) is governed by the learner's task mastery, a concept influenced by training data volume, task difficulty, and model priors. This principle aims to provide actionable guidance for calibrating demonstration diversity.

5. **Provide Practical Guidance for Dataset Curation:** A key practical objective is to equip practitioners with a tool and guidelines for efficient dataset curation.
 - **Objective:** Demonstrate that H_G can serve as a fast pre-training audit signal for demonstration datasets, enabling practitioners to assess dataset quality and adjust collection strategies to achieve diversity levels that are conducive to learning, thereby avoiding suboptimal performance caused by either too little or too much geometric variation.

In summary, the work aims to move beyond generic notions of data diversity by rigorously defining, measuring, and empirically characterizing the specific role of *trajectory-shape diversity* in imitation learning, offering both a novel metric and a principled framework for its optimal calibration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology focuses on defining and computing Geometric Entropy (H_G) and then empirically evaluating its relationship with imitation learning performance across various experimental conditions.

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation and Phase Decomposition

The core challenge is to quantify the diversity of intrinsic *geometric strategies* (G) while marginalizing out *extrinsic context* (E, e.g., object pose, workspace scale) and *execution noise* (ϵ). The authors argue that raw end-effector trajectories are a mix of these three factors.

To achieve this, manipulation episodes are first decomposed into semantically meaningful segments:
- **Transit motion:** Free-space movement towards or away from a target. This phase is considered the primary carrier of demonstrator strategy diversity.
- **Fine manipulation:** Contact-rich actions (e.g., grasping, insertion) where trajectories are tightly constrained, offering little meaningful geometric variation.
The methodology exclusively focuses on *transit segments*, identifying phase boundaries via task-specific semantic events (e.g., gripper closure/opening).

#### 3.2. B. Endpoint-Anchored Frame Alignment

Each transit trajectory is transformed into a canonical shape descriptor $\xi \in R^{3T}$ through four steps:
1. **Arc-length resampling:** Trajectories, initially of variable length, are resampled to a fixed number of waypoints (T=50) at uniform arc-length intervals. This normalizes for execution speed and controller frequency, ensuring all descriptors have the same dimension.
2. **Endpoint anchoring (translation normalization):** Each resampled trajectory is translated so its terminal point (task target) is at the origin. This removes positional offsets due to randomized target locations.
3. **Target-frame alignment (rotation normalization):** The anchored trajectory points are rotated into a canonical local frame defined by the target object's orientation. This cancels global rotational variations (e.g., different object yaw), ensuring trajectories identical up to rotation map to the same descriptor.
4. **Displacement scaling:** The trajectory points are normalized by the Euclidean distance between the segment's start and end points. This removes workspace-scale effects, ensuring shape, not physical extent, is measured.

The resulting canonical descriptor $\xi$ is a flattened vector of the transformed 3D points. This pipeline aims to ensure that two trajectories produce the same $\xi$ if and only if they share the same intrinsic shape relative to the target frame, up to residual execution noise.

#### 3.3. C. Geometric Entropy (H_G) Calculation

Given M aligned descriptors $\{\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_M\} \subset R^{3T}$, H_G summarizes geometric shape diversity:
1. **Spectral decomposition:** To handle high-dimensional descriptors ($3T=150$) with moderate sample sizes (M ~ $10^2–10^3$), diversity is summarized via the covariance eigenspectrum. The mean-centered descriptor matrix's SVD yields eigenvalues $\{\lambda_j\}$ of the sample covariance, where each $\lambda_j$ represents variance along an orthogonal shape mode.
2. **Effective dimensionality ($d_{eff}$):** This metric quantifies the number of independent shape modes, defined as the exponentiated Shannon entropy of the normalized spectrum $p_j = \lambda_j / \sum_k \lambda_k$:
 $d_{eff} = \exp \left( - \sum_{j: p_j >0} p_j \ln p_j \right)$.
3. **Geometric Entropy (H_G):** H_G combines effective dimensionality and the overall spread of modes:
 $H_G = \ln d_{eff} + \ln \left( \sum_j \lambda_j \right)$.
 The first term increases with structural complexity (more independent modes), and the second with the magnitude of variation (modes spread further apart). This formulation is designed to be stable for robotics sample sizes, unlike methods like log det($\Sigma$) which can be ill-conditioned.
4. **Multi-phase aggregation:** For tasks with P transit phases, H_G is computed per phase and averaged, weighted by the number of successful trajectories for each phase:
 $H_{avg}^G = \frac{\sum_{p=1}^P n_p \cdot H_G^{(p)}}{\sum_{p=1}^P n_p}$.

#### 3.4. D. Experimental Setup

The experiments aim to answer three questions: H_G's stability, its relationship with IL performance, and an organizing principle.

**1. Simulation Experiments:**
- **Parameterized Demonstration Generation:** Built on ManiSkill3's PandaArmMotionPlanningSolver. Geometric diversity is controlled by introducing an approach waypoint sampled on a circle above the target.
 - **Approach radius (r):** Controls the curvature and lateral displacement of approaches. r=0 yields straight descents; larger r yields curved arcs.
 - **Discrete mode count (k):** Samples approach angles ($\theta$) uniformly (k=0) or from k equally spaced discrete values (k>0). This distinguishes magnitude from modality.
- **Datasets:** Generated for N $\in \{100, 200, 500, 1000\}$ demonstrations per (r, k) configuration.
- **Tasks:**
 - **StackCube-v1:** Pick and place, requiring robust grasping and precise placement.
 - **PegInsertionSide-v1:** High-precision lateral insertion with sub-centimeter clearance, serving as a "high mastery" contrast.
- **Imitation Learning Models:**
 - **Diffusion Policy (DP):** A specialist model trained from scratch.
 - **π$_{0.5}$:** A vision-language-action (VLA) model fine-tuned on StackCube-v1, representing a strong prior from large-scale pre-training.

**2. Real-Robot Experiments:**
- **Hardware:** ARX robot arm.
- **Tasks:** StackCube, PlacePanda, OpenDrawer.
- **Data Collection:** N=100 demonstrations per configuration, collected at five diversity levels by three collectors.
- **Policy:** ACT policies.
- **Evaluation:** Success rate over 40 test episodes per configuration, averaged over three independent seeds.

**3. Robustness of H_G (Metric Comparison):**
H_G is compared against four baselines on the same aligned descriptors:
- Mean Variance ($\sigma_{avg}^2$)
- LogDet Covariance ($\log \det(\Sigma + \alpha I)$)
- Participation Ratio ($(\sum_j \lambda_j)^2 / \sum_j \lambda_j^2$)
- kNN Differential Entropy (Kozachenko–Leonenko)

This comprehensive experimental design, combining controlled simulation with real-world validation and comparative metric analysis, aims to establish the utility and underlying principles of geometric entropy in IL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study consistently observed an inverted-U relationship between geometric diversity (H_G) and imitation learning performance across various models, tasks, and data scales. This relationship is moderated by what the authors term "task mastery."

#### 4.1. A. H_G Convergence and Stability

- **Sample Efficiency:** H_G demonstrated stability and converged within the first 50–100 successful trajectories across all (r, k) configurations on StackCube-v1. This indicates its reliability as a pre-training audit metric even with moderate demonstration counts.
- **Discriminative Power:** H_G monotonically increased with the approach radius (r) when approach angles were sampled uniformly (k=0), confirming it captures the expansion of the aligned shape manifold. Enforcing a single approach mode (k=1) significantly lowered H_G even at large 'r', reflecting reduced mode ambiguity.

#### 4.2. B. Simulation Results with Diffusion Policy: Inverted-U Pattern and Peak-Shift

- **Inverted-U Empirical Pattern:** For both StackCube-v1 and PegInsertionSide-v1, IL performance initially improved as H_G increased from low values, but then degraded when H_G became excessively large (Fig. 3).
 - **Low H_G:** Policies trained on low-diversity datasets were brittle, failing when test-time conditions deviated from the narrow motion corridor. Increasing H_G enhanced robustness by expanding geometric coverage.
 - **High H_G:** Beyond an optimal H_G$^*$, excessive diversity introduced mode ambiguity, where multiple distinct strategies for the same goal led the learner to execute incorrect modes or interpolate incompatible motions, particularly detrimental in contact-rich tasks.
- **Peak-Shift with Data Volume:** The optimal H_G$^*$ shifted towards lower values as the dataset size (N) increased. Larger datasets provided broader coverage of initial conditions intrinsically, diminishing the marginal benefit of higher injected geometric diversity and making the cost of ambiguity more prominent. This suggests that more data favors more consistent demonstrations.
- **Task Comparison:**
 - **StackCube-v1 (challenging):** Showed clear benefits from moderate H_G increases, as added coverage improved robustness.
 - **PegInsertionSide-v1 (easier):** Reached near-saturation performance faster. Here, additional geometric diversity provided little benefit and primarily acted as interference, leading to a sharper post-peak performance drop. This indicates that higher task mastery (from an easier task) reduces the tolerable diversity range.

#### 4.3. C. Generalist Foundation Model (π$_{0.5}$): Monotonic Decay

- When fine-tuned on StackCube-v1, the π$_{0.5}$ VLA model exhibited an approximately monotonically decreasing performance with increasing H_G (Fig. 4). This trend is consistent with a "high-mastery" regime where the model's strong pre-training already provides substantial geometric coverage. In this context, additional diversity in the fine-tuning set is redundant or acts as conflicting information, leading to degraded performance. The Inverted-U peak effectively shifted so far left that only the declining portion was observed.

#### 4.4. D. The Mastery–Entropy Principle

The collective findings suggest an organizing principle: the optimal geometric entropy (H_G$^*$) for imitation learning is governed by the *learner's task mastery*. Task mastery is a latent concept influenced by:
1. **Training data volume/coverage (N):** More data generally increases mastery.
2. **Task difficulty:** Easier tasks lead to higher mastery.
3. **Model priors:** Stronger priors (e.g., VLA models) impart higher mastery.
As mastery increases, the learner requires less geometric diversity for robustness, and its tolerance for conflicting modes decreases.

#### 4.5. E. Real-Robot Validation

Experiments on an ARX robot arm with ACT policies validated the simulation trends (Fig. 5):
- **StackCube:** Exhibited a clear Inverted-U trend, peaking at H_G $\approx$ 3.3. This mirrored the low-to-mid mastery regime in simulation, where both too little and too much diversity were detrimental.
- **PlacePanda and OpenDrawer:** Showed a monotonic decline in success rate with increasing H_G. These tasks were considered higher-mastery settings where low-entropy demonstrations were sufficient, and additional geometric variation acted as noise.
- **Sim-to-Real Consistency:** The qualitative agreement between simulation and real-robot experiments supports the Mastery–Entropy Principle and H_G's utility as a predictive diagnostic.

#### 4.6. F. Robustness of H_G: Overcoming Metric Aliasing

Comparison against baseline diversity metrics revealed H_G's advantages (Fig. 6):
- **Mean Variance:** Failed to distinguish subtle mode organizations, collapsing distinct (r, k) settings due to aggregating waypoint-wise spread without considering cross-time correlations.
- **LogDet Covariance and Participation Ratio:** While incorporating spectral information, they still compressed the dataset to a single covariance summary, leading to aliasing across distinct multimodal organizations. LogDet also required ad-hoc regularization for stability.
- **kNN Differential Entropy:** Showed pronounced sample-size drift for N $\leq$ 1000 in the 150-dimensional descriptor space, limiting its stability for practical auditing with moderate demonstration counts.
- **H_G's Behavior:** H_G, by combining effective dimensionality (number of independent modes) and overall spread (magnitude), was less prone to aliasing distinct strategy organizations and converged rapidly.

In summary, the results establish H_G as a stable and discriminative metric, confirm a consistent inverted-U relationship between geometric diversity and IL performance, and propose the Mastery–Entropy Principle as a framework for understanding and optimizing this relationship based on the learner's proficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a refined understanding of trajectory-shape diversity in imitation learning, moving beyond general notions of data variability to a specific, measurable aspect. The primary significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Challenging the "Maximize Diversity" Heuristic:** The consistent observation of an Inverted-U relationship between geometric diversity (H_G) and IL success challenges the common heuristic of simply maximizing data diversity. This work suggests that there is an optimal level of diversity, beyond which performance degrades due to strategy ambiguity or interference. This reframes data quality along the trajectory-shape axis, emphasizing calibration rather than unbounded expansion.

2. **Introduction of Geometric Entropy (H_G) as a Practical Diagnostic:** H_G provides a lightweight, task-agnostic, and computationally efficient metric for quantifying intrinsic trajectory-shape diversity. Its stability and sample efficiency (converging within 50-100 demonstrations) make it a practical tool for dataset auditing *before* any policy training. This allows practitioners to quickly assess the geometric quality of demonstration datasets, identifying regimes of too little or too much diversity.

3. **Formulation of the Mastery–Entropy Principle:** The synthesis of findings across models, tasks, and data scales into the "Mastery–Entropy Principle" offers a valuable theoretical and practical framework. This principle posits that the optimal H_G is directly tied to the learner's task mastery (influenced by data volume, task difficulty, and model priors). This provides actionable guidance:
 - **Low Mastery Regimes:** For challenging tasks, limited data, or models trained from scratch, moderate increases in H_G can improve robustness by expanding coverage.
 - **High Mastery Regimes:** For easier tasks, abundant data, or pre-trained models (like VLAs), additional diversity may be redundant or detrimental, indicating a need for more consistent demonstrations.
 This principle guides whether to increase or reduce geometric diversity during data collection or curation.

4. **Improved Data Curation Strategies:** The findings provide concrete guidelines for curating demonstration datasets. By using H_G, data collectors can tune their collection strategies (e.g., varying approach radius or discrete modes) to match the appropriate diversity level for their specific learning setup, rather than relying on trial-and-error. This can lead to more efficient and effective data collection, reducing the effort and resources spent on suboptimal demonstrations.

5. **Enhanced Understanding of Model Behavior:** The contrasting behaviors of specialist Diffusion Policies and the generalist $\pi_{0.5}$ VLA model, particularly the monotonic decay for the latter, shed light on how pre-trained models process and integrate new demonstration data. It suggests that strong priors from large-scale pre-training may already provide a high level of "mastery," making them more sensitive to perceived inconsistencies in fine-tuning data.

6. **Potential for Broader Application:** While evaluated on contact-rich manipulation, the underlying methodology of phase decomposition and target-anchored alignment could generalize to other robotic tasks involving distinct motion phases. The metric itself is agnostic to task semantics beyond the definition of transit segments.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the understanding of how geometric diversity impacts imitation learning, providing both a novel diagnostic tool (H_G) and a principled framework (Mastery–Entropy Principle) that can profoundly impact data collection and curation practices in robotics.
