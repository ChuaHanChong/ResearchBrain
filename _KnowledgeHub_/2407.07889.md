# AdaptiGraph: Material-Adaptive Graph-Based Neural Dynamics for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and Columbia University developed AdaptiGraph, a unified framework that combines graph-based neural dynamics with online physical property estimation to enable robots to manipulate diverse materials with unknown characteristics. The system dynamically infers material properties, leading to improved dynamics prediction and more effective manipulation across objects like ropes, granular media, and cloth.

## Problem

- Robots face difficulty manipulating diverse deformable objects whose physical properties (e.g., stiffness, granularity) are unknown and vary significantly.
- Prior learning-based dynamics models, including Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), are typically specialized for single material types, requiring extensive retraining for new materials or objects with varied physical properties.
- A lack of unified dynamics models and efficient online adaptation mechanisms limits robotic generalization to novel objects and materials in real-world scenarios.

## Method

- Introduces AdaptiGraph, a framework combining a material-conditioned Graph-Based Neural Dynamics (GBND) model with an online few-shot physical property estimation module.
- The GBND model is designed to predict object dynamics by incorporating both discrete material types and continuous physical properties as explicit inputs.
- An online, curiosity-driven inverse optimization process estimates unknown physical properties from limited interactions, enabling the model to adapt to novel materials in real-time.

## Results

- AdaptiGraph demonstrated improved forward dynamics prediction accuracy across rigid, rope, granular, and cloth materials, consistently outperforming non-adaptive GNN baselines.
- The system accurately estimated unknown physical properties like center of pressure, stiffness, and granularity within approximately 5 interaction steps, showing extrapolation capabilities for out-of-distribution values.
- In model-based planning tasks, AdaptiGraph, with adapted properties, achieved target configurations more efficiently and with higher success rates than non-adaptive models, proving enhanced manipulation proficiency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly conditioning Graph Neural Network dynamics models on both discrete material types and continuous physical properties allows a single model to capture diverse object behaviors.
> - Online few-shot adaptation, leveraging curiosity-driven interactions and inverse optimization, can effectively infer unknown material properties and generalize to unseen property values.
> - Incorporating estimated physical properties into a model-based planning framework significantly enhances the efficiency and success rate of robotic manipulation tasks across varied materials.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Predictive models are a foundational component in robotic systems, enabling robots to anticipate environmental changes and plan actions. Historically, classical methods for system identification have struggled with systems possessing high degrees of freedom, such as deformable objects, often necessitating explicit parameterization of system states. The past decade has seen the emergence of learning-based models, often leveraging visual observations to learn dynamics. These approaches have included models based on raw pixel data or latent representations of images. However, such representations can sometimes lack structural understanding of the environment, leading to poor generalization across variations in camera pose, object pose, robot, and object characteristics.

A notable advancement in this field has been the adoption of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which represent environments as 3D particles and model their pairwise interactions. GNNs have demonstrated efficacy in capturing relational biases and predicting the complex motions of deformable objects, including rigid bodies, plasticine, cloth, fluids, and granular matter. Despite these successes, most prior GNN-based dynamics models have typically focused on a single material type. This specialization often requires extensive retraining or significant data collection to adapt to new materials or objects with unknown physical properties. Consequently, a key challenge in the field remains the development of graph-based models that can generalize and adapt to diverse materials with varying physical properties, such as ropes of different stiffness or granular media of varying granularity.

AdaptiGraph addresses this limitation by proposing a unified framework that can model the dynamics of objects across diverse material types and within-class physical property variations. It aims to overcome the need for material-specific model training and enhance generalization to novel objects with unknown properties, thereby bridging a gap in the application of learned dynamics models for robust robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind AdaptiGraph is the inherent difficulty robots face in manipulating diverse deformable objects, particularly when their physical properties are unknown. Objects composed of different materials, or even the same material with varying physical parameters (e.g., stiffness), exhibit distinct behaviors under interaction. For example, a robot tasked with manipulating a cotton rope versus a stiff cable, or arranging coffee beans versus toy blocks, must contend with significantly different dynamic responses, even when initial configurations and actions are identical. Current robotic systems often lack the ability to adapt to such variations in real-time, necessitating online adaptation for effective manipulation.

The paper identifies several challenges that existing methods fail to fully address:
- **Modeling diverse materials:** A single dynamics model that can predict the behavior of various material types (e.g., rigid, granular, rope, cloth) in a unified manner is lacking.
- **Handling unknown physical properties:** Objects often possess intrinsic physical properties (e.g., stiffness, granularity, center of pressure) that are not directly observable or known a priori. A robust system needs to infer these properties online.
- **Adaptation without extensive retraining:** Traditional learning-based models often require significant retraining when encountering novel objects or materials, which is impractical for real-world deployment.
- **Real-time performance:** The adaptation process needs to be efficient enough to support real-time robotic control and planning.

To address these motivations, AdaptiGraph sets forth the following key objectives:
1. **Develop a unified physical property-conditioned Graph-Based Neural Dynamics (GBND) model:** This model should be capable of predicting the motions of diverse materials with varying physical properties without requiring retraining for each new object. It should encode both discrete material types and continuous physical property variables into the graph representation.
2. **Implement an online few-shot physical property estimation mechanism:** The system should be able to infer the unknown physical properties of novel objects through limited, curiosity-driven interactions during online deployment. This estimation process should optimize the model's fit to observed interaction data.
3. **Demonstrate enhanced prediction accuracy and task proficiency:** The adapted models, conditioned on estimated physical properties, should achieve superior dynamics prediction accuracy and facilitate more efficient and effective manipulation in downstream robotic tasks compared to non-material-conditioned or non-adaptive models.
4. **Validate the framework across a range of challenging deformable materials:** The approach should be evaluated on real-world manipulation tasks involving diverse objects like ropes, granular media, rigid boxes, and cloth, demonstrating its generalizability and robustness.

By achieving these objectives, AdaptiGraph aims to enable robots to adaptively manipulate a wide array of objects with unknown physical properties, thereby moving towards more versatile and intelligent robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AdaptiGraph proposes a unified, learning-based dynamics modeling framework that integrates a material-conditioned Graph-Based Neural Dynamics (GBND) model with an online few-shot physical property estimation module.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The core of the framework is a dynamics model, denoted as $f$, that predicts the next environment state $\hat{z}_{t+1}$ given the current state $z_t$, robot action $u_t$, a material type $M$, and a continuous physical property variable $\phi$:
$\hat{z}_{t+1} = f(z_t, u_t; \phi, M)$.
The material type $M$ classifies objects into discrete categories (e.g., rigid, granular, rope, cloth), while $\phi$ represents material-specific physical properties (e.g., stiffness for ropes, granularity for granular media, center of pressure for rigid objects).
For objects with unknown physical properties, an inverse optimization problem is formulated to estimate the optimal physical property $\phi^*$ by minimizing the discrepancy between predicted and observed states over a few interaction steps:
$\phi^* = \text{arg min}_{\phi} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{cost}(\hat{z}_{t+1}, z_{t+1})$,
where $T$ is the number of interactions and $\text{cost}(\cdot, \cdot)$ quantifies the difference between states.

#### 3.2. Material-Conditioned Graph-Based Neural Dynamics Model

The dynamics model $f$ is instantiated as a graph neural network (GNN).
- **Graph Representation:** The environment state $z_t$ is represented as a graph $G_t = (V_t, E_t)$, where $V_t$ is a set of vertices (particles representing object bits) and $E_t$ is a set of edges (representing interactions between particles). Object particle positions are determined by farthest point sampling from input point clouds to ensure coverage. Edges are constructed between particles within a spatial distance threshold $d$. Robot end-effector particles are also included, forming relations with object particles.
- **Feature Encoding:** A vertex $v_{i,t} \in V_t$ incorporates not only 3D position history ($h$ timesteps, implicitly encoding velocity) and a vertex attribute $c^v_{i,t}$ (indicating object or robot end-effector), but also the material type $M_i$ and the physical property variable $\phi_i$. Formally, $v_{i,t} = (x_{i,t-h:t}, c^v_{i,t}, \phi_i, M_i)$. Edge features $e_{k,t}$ include indices of receiver/sender particles and an edge attribute $c^e_{k,t}$ (indicating same/different objects).
- **GNN Architecture:**
 1. **Encoders:** Vertex features $v_{i,t}$ and edge features (derived from $v_{w,t}, v_{u,t}$) are passed through vertex encoder $f^{\text{enc}}_V$ and edge encoder $f^{\text{enc}}_E$ respectively, to obtain initial latent embeddings $h^0_{v_{i,t}}$ and $h^0_{e_{k,t}}$.
 2. **Propagation:** An iterative message passing process updates vertex and edge embeddings. For $L$ steps, an edge propagation network $f^{\text{prop}}_E$ updates edge embeddings, and a vertex propagation network $f^{\text{prop}}_V$ aggregates messages from neighboring edges to update vertex embeddings.
 3. **Decoder:** A vertex decoder $f^{\text{dec}}_V$ predicts the next state $\hat{v}_{i,t+1}$ from the final vertex embeddings $h^L_{v_{i,t}}$.
- **Translation Equivariance:** The model enforces translation equivariance by using position differences for edge encoding instead of absolute positions for vertex encoding, ensuring predictions translate identically with global 3D translations.
- **Training:** The model is trained to minimize the accumulated prediction loss over $K$ future steps (set to $K=3$). Mean Squared Error (MSE) on predicted object particle positions is used as the loss function:
 $L = \sum_t ||z_{t+1} - f(z_t, u_t; \phi, M)||^2_2$.
 Training data is generated at scale using physics-based simulators (NVIDIA FleX, Pymunk) by randomizing robot actions, object configurations, and most importantly, the material configuration (physical property $\phi$) over a wide range.

#### 3.3. Few-Shot Physical Property Adaptation

After training, the model is deployed on real-world objects with unknown physical properties. An inverse optimization pipeline is used for few-shot adaptation:
- **Curiosity-Driven Interaction:** The robot actively interacts with the object. In each iteration, it selects an action that maximizes the predicted displacement of the object using the Model-Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) trajectory optimization algorithm. This strategy aims to elicit rich dynamic responses that reveal more information about the object's properties.
- **Property Estimation:** After each interaction, the robot updates its estimate of the physical property $\phi$ by minimizing the accumulated dynamics prediction error from all prior interactions. This is achieved by solving the optimization problem:
 $\hat{\phi}_t = \text{arg min}_{\phi} \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} L_{\text{CD}}(\hat{z}_{i+1}, z_{i+1})$,
 where $L_{\text{CD}}$ is the Chamfer Distance (CD) between the predicted graph state $\hat{z}_{i+1}$ and the observed state $z_{i+1}$.
- **Optimizers:** For single-dimensional $\phi$, Bayesian Optimization (BO) is used. For multi-dimensional $\phi$, CMA-ES is employed.
- **Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Properties:** The search domain for $\hat{\phi}$ during adaptation is extended beyond the training data range (e.g., $\phi_{\text{min}}-0.2(\phi_{\text{max}}-\phi_{\text{min}})$ to $\phi_{\text{max}}+0.2(\phi_{\text{max}}-\phi_{\text{min}})$) to allow for generalization to extreme or unseen properties.

#### 3.4. Closed-Loop Model-Based Planning

With the estimated physical property $\hat{\phi}$, the adapted dynamics model $f(\cdot, \cdot; \hat{\phi}, M)$ is used for closed-loop planning within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework. MPPI trajectory optimization is again used to find an action sequence that minimizes a task-specific cost function. This cost function includes a term measuring distance to the target configuration and penalty terms for infeasible actions or collisions. The improved prediction accuracy from adaptation allows for more efficient and effective manipulation towards desired goal configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated AdaptiGraph across four material categories: rigid boxes, ropes, granular piles, and cloth, each with associated manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Materials and Corresponding Tasks

- **Rigid Box Pushing:** Task: push a box to a target position/orientation using point contact. Physical property: 2D normalized position of the center of pressure (CoP) on the top surface.
- **Rope Straightening:** Task: rearrange a rope to a target configuration. Physical property: normalized continuous stiffness variable $\phi \in (0,1)$.
- **Granular Pile Gathering:** Task: gather granular piles into a target region. Physical property: normalized continuous granular size (granularity) $\phi \in (0,1)$.
- **Cloth Relocating:** Task: grasp and drag cloth to a target configuration. Physical property: normalized continuous stiffness variable $\phi \in (0,1)$.

#### 4.2. Environment and Evaluation Setup

- **Simulation:** Datasets (1000 episodes, 5 interactions/episode) were generated using NVIDIA FleX for deformable/granular materials and Pymunk for rigid boxes. Object properties ($\phi$) were randomized across a wide range.
- **Real World:** Experiments used a UFACTORY xArm 6 robot with various 3D-printed end-effectors (flat pusher, cylindrical stick, gripper). Four RealSense D455 RGB-D cameras captured data at 15Hz. GroundingDINO and Segment Anything models were used for object detection and segmentation to extract point clouds.

#### 4.3. Baselines

- **GNN:** A graph neural network with the same architecture as AdaptiGraph, trained separately for each material category, but without conditioning on the physical parameter $\phi$.
- **Ours w/o Adaptation:** An ablated version of AdaptiGraph that uses only the mean physical property variable $\bar{\phi}$ as input during deployment, without online adaptation.
- **Additional Baselines (Appendix):** Compared against "Unified GNN" (single GNN for all materials without conditioning), fine-tuning unconditional GNNs, and adaptation with physics-based simulators (FleX/Pymunk).

#### 4.4. Forward Dynamics Prediction

- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 4):** AdaptiGraph, with estimated physical properties, demonstrated more accurate one-step dynamics predictions compared to "Ours w/o Adaptation". For instance, it successfully captured the distinct behaviors of yarn (low stiffness) and polymer rope (high stiffness), and the varied movements of rigid boxes with different CoPs, granular materials of different sizes, and cloths of varying stiffness. The baseline often failed to capture these property-dependent dynamics.
- **Quantitative Results (Fig. 5):** On a simulated test set of 200 objects with distinct physical properties per material, AdaptiGraph consistently outperformed both "GNN" and "Ours w/o Adaptation" in terms of prediction accuracy and stability across all material types. For rigid boxes, the model achieved near-perfect accuracy, significantly exceeding baselines.
- **Ablation Study (Appendix A.1):** "Unified GNN" (no material or property conditioning) performed the worst, highlighting the importance of material conditioning. "Separate GNN" (material-conditioned but no property conditioning) performed better than "Unified GNN" and comparably to "Ours w/o Adaptation", but still suffered from lack of property adaptability. AdaptiGraph showed the best performance across all categories.

#### 4.5. Physical Property Estimation

- **Rigid Box (Fig. 6a):** For boxes with weights placed at different CoP locations, AdaptiGraph accurately predicted the CoP positions, which converged to the ground truth after approximately 5 interaction steps. The error heatmap showed a single global minimum, indicating robust estimation.
- **Rope (Fig. 6b):** The model tested on 9 different ropes, including those with extreme stiffness/softness, showed the ability to extrapolate beyond the training data range. The mean Chamfer Distance (CD) for interaction observations yielded clear minimum points, and the estimated stiffness ranking aligned with human perception.
- **Granular (Fig. 6c):** On 9 types of granular objects (1cm to 3cm grains), the model correctly predicted the granularity ranking, identifying granola as the smallest and toy blocks as the largest grains.
- **Cloth (Fig. 6d):** For 5 different cloth instances, AdaptiGraph correctly estimated modal cloth as the softest and flannel as softer than cotton/microfiber. It also generalized to a plastic sheet, accurately predicting its high stiffness despite no plastic-like materials in training.
- **Multiple Properties (Appendix C.1):** An extension experiment with ropes demonstrated that the method can simultaneously recover multiple properties (stiffness and friction). The 2D model (stiffness + friction) generally showed lower dynamics prediction error than the 1D model (stiffness only), indicating improved accuracy with more relevant properties.
- **Comparison with Simulators (Appendix A.2):** AdaptiGraph with online adaptation exhibited the lowest dynamics prediction error compared to fine-tuning unconditional GNNs and simulator-based adaptation. It achieved significant error reductions (90.8% for rigid boxes, 7.9% for ropes, 4.0% for clothes, 9.0% for granular objects) compared to "Ours w/o Adaptation". The learned dynamics model's robustness to noisy visual inputs and significantly faster inference time were highlighted as advantages over simulator-based approaches.

#### 4.6. Model-Based Planning

- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 7):** AdaptiGraph, integrated into an MPC framework, consistently achieved target configurations within the maximum 10 planning steps across all four tasks. The baseline "Ours w/o Adaptation" frequently failed to reach goals due to its inability to account for physical properties. For example, in rigid box pushing, AdaptiGraph adapted CoP estimations to guide the box accurately, while the baseline struggled. For granular gathering, AdaptiGraph distinguished granular dynamics (stacking vs. pushing) to successfully gather piles.
- **Quantitative Results (Fig. 8):** AdaptiGraph demonstrated superior performance in terms of efficiency and error tolerance. It achieved lower errors within a constrained number of planning steps and attained higher success rates under stringent error margins across all four tasks, confirming its enhanced capability for model-based manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AdaptiGraph presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a unified framework for modeling and controlling diverse materials with unknown physical properties. Its primary significance lies in its ability to enable robots to adapt to novel objects in real-time without extensive retraining, a persistent challenge in developing versatile robotic systems.

The key aspects contributing to its significance include:
- **Unified Dynamics Modeling:** The framework provides a single, material-conditioned GBND model that can predict the dynamics of various material types (rigid, granular, rope, cloth) and their internal physical property variations. This reduces the need for specialized models for different materials.
- **Online Few-Shot Adaptation:** The integration of a curiosity-driven inverse optimization pipeline allows for rapid, few-shot estimation of unknown physical properties from real-world interactions. This capability is crucial for deploying robots in unstructured environments where object properties are often unobservable a priori.
- **Enhanced Prediction Accuracy:** By conditioning the dynamics model on estimated physical properties, AdaptiGraph achieves improved prediction accuracy, especially for objects with extreme or out-of-distribution characteristics. This directly translates to more reliable model-based planning.
- **Improved Robotic Manipulation Proficiency:** The adapted dynamics model, when integrated into an MPC framework, leads to more efficient and effective performance in complex manipulation tasks across diverse materials. This improvement is quantitative, demonstrating lower errors and higher success rates with fewer actions.
- **Interpretability and Generalizability:** The use of explicit physical property variables (e.g., stiffness, granularity, CoP) offers interpretability, while the model's ability to extrapolate beyond the training data range for these variables demonstrates robust generalization capabilities.

The potential impact of AdaptiGraph is substantial, particularly for applications requiring robots to interact with a wide range of everyday objects that exhibit varying and often unknown physical characteristics. This could include:
- **Logistics and Warehousing:** Robots handling packages of different materials and contents.
- **Domestic Robotics:** Robots performing tasks with deformable objects like laundry (cloth), food items (granular), or cables (ropes).
- **Manufacturing:** Robots manipulating components with varying material properties on an assembly line.
- **Hazardous Environments:** Robots operating in unknown or changing environments where direct knowledge of material properties is unavailable.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as extending to more object materials, a more comprehensive set of physical properties, and modeling heterogeneous object interactions using material-conditioned heterogeneous graphs, suggests avenues for further enhancing the framework's versatility and applicability. AdaptiGraph represents a step towards more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems capable of robust interaction with the physical world.
