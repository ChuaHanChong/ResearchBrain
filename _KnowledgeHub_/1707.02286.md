# Emergence of Locomotion Behaviours in Rich Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Agents are trained on procedurally generated obstacle courses (hurdles, gaps, platforms, slalom walls, variable terrain) with only a simple forward-progress reward, using a distributed variant of Proximal Policy Optimization with gradient averaging across parallel workers. Planar Walker, Quadruped, and Humanoid bodies spontaneously acquire running, jumping, crouching, and turning, plus gaits robust to unobserved terrain and body perturbations.

## Problem

- Continuous-control locomotion results rely on hand-crafted reward shaping or demonstration data, and behaviours break when the reward function is even slightly modified.
- Reward engineering sidesteps the core question of whether an agent can learn rich behaviour from a limited reward signal alone, in an impoverished flat-terrain setting.
- Prior policy-gradient work on continuous action spaces targets small problems with little distributed optimization, and trust-region methods scale poorly in wall-clock time.

## Method

- ==Distributed PPO== (**DPPO**): PPO workers collect data and compute gradients in parallel, a chief averages gradients once enough workers report, and applies synchronous updates via a parameter server.
- Adaptive KL trust region with K-step returns for advantage estimation and truncated backpropagation through time, plus an extra squared penalty and early stopping when KL exceeds twice the target.
- Two-pathway policy network splitting proprioceptive (joint angles, velocimeter, contact sensors) from exteroceptive (terrain heightfield samples) input, trained on procedurally generated single-type, mixed, and curriculum courses.

## Results

- DPPO matches TRPO returns on Planar Walker, Humanoid, and a recurrent Memory Reacher task, while scaling with worker count and beating A3C in wall-clock time at equal workers.
- **Planar Walker** learns to jump hurdles nearly as tall as its own body, clear gaps, and crouch under platforms, with skill emergence robust across random seeds.
- Curriculum hurdle courses with gradually increasing height improve faster on both easy and hard validation terrains; hurdle-trained walkers and quadrupeds beat flat-terrain policies under unobserved friction, rubble, actuator-strength, and incline changes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Environmental diversity can substitute for reward shaping: a harder, richer training distribution often makes learning easier rather than harder.
> - Training variation acts as regularization against reward overfitting, yielding gaits that transfer to perturbations never seen or observed during training.
> - High-DoF bodies remain fragile: humanoid outcomes varied strongly across seeds, termination conditions, and initialization, suggesting local optima that richness alone does not remove.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated success in various domains, including Atari games, 3D navigation, and board games. These applications typically involve well-defined reward functions that can be optimized directly. However, in many other tasks, particularly in continuous control problems like locomotion, defining an appropriate reward function is less straightforward. Optimizing a naively designed reward function can lead to behaviors that do not align with the designer's intentions.

Current practice in continuous control often involves careful hand-crafting of reward functions or deriving them from demonstration data. While this reward engineering has enabled successful demonstrations of locomotion, these solutions frequently exhibit brittleness, with slight modifications to the reward function leading to unexpected outcomes. Furthermore, designing suitable reward functions for more advanced or diverse behaviors can be non-obvious. This reliance on extensive reward engineering also arguably circumvents a fundamental challenge of reinforcement learning: enabling an agent to learn rich and effective behaviors directly from limited reward signals.

This paper positions itself by returning to this core challenge. It proposes an alternative approach: instead of meticulously designing reward functions for specific behaviors, complex and robust behaviors can emerge from simple reward functions if the agent is trained in an environment that provides sufficient richness and diversity. This approach contrasts with the prevailing methods by emphasizing environmental design over reward engineering as a primary mechanism for inducing sophisticated skills. The work also contributes to the development of scalable and robust deep reinforcement learning algorithms for continuous control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate whether sophisticated and robust locomotion skills can emerge spontaneously from simple, generic reward functions when agents are trained in diverse and challenging environments. This departs from the common practice in continuous control, where reward functions are often carefully engineered to incentivize specific desired behaviors.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several points:

- **Limitations of Reward Engineering**: Existing methods for learning locomotion often involve extensive hand-crafting of reward functions or leveraging demonstration data. While effective for some tasks, these approaches can lead to solutions that are brittle, failing under minor changes to the reward function or environment. Moreover, scaling this approach to more complex, multi-faceted behaviors becomes increasingly difficult, as the appropriate reward function may not be evident. This process also diminishes the autonomy typically associated with reinforcement learning, where agents ideally learn from minimal external guidance.
- **Hypothesis of Environmental Richness**: The authors hypothesized that a rich and diverse environment, even with a simple reward structure, could naturally promote the emergence of complex behaviors. This hypothesis is based on two premises:
 - **Implicit Curriculum**: An environment presenting a spectrum of challenges, varying in difficulty, could inherently guide the learning process. As an agent's capabilities improve, it tackles progressively harder obstacles, leading to the development of complex skills that might otherwise require explicit instruction or careful reward design.
 - **Robustness against Overfitting**: The brittleness observed in behaviors learned with finely tuned reward functions might be a form of overfitting to specific settings. Exposing an agent to a wider range of diverse challenges could increase the performance gap between idiosyncratic solutions and truly robust ones, thereby favoring the learning of behaviors that generalize across various conditions.
- **Focus on Locomotion**: The study specifically targets locomotion, a domain known for its sensitivity to reward function design, to demonstrate the applicability of their hypothesis.
- **Algorithmic Requirement**: To effectively learn in these rich and challenging simulated domains, the research was also motivated by the need for a reliable and scalable reinforcement learning algorithm capable of handling continuous state and action spaces. This led to the development and validation of a novel, scalable variant of policy gradient reinforcement learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a specific reinforcement learning algorithm and a procedurally generated, physically simulated environment to test its hypothesis.

**Reinforcement Learning Algorithm: Distributed Proximal Policy Optimization (DPPO)**
The core algorithm used was a novel, scalable variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), termed Distributed PPO (DPPO).

- **Robust Policy Gradients**: The algorithm builds on robust policy gradient methods like Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) and PPO. PPO is an approximate version of TRPO that uses first-order gradients, making it more compatible with recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and large-scale distributed setups. It enforces a trust region constraint on policy updates via a regularization term, whose coefficient is adaptively adjusted based on whether the policy change exceeded or fell below a target Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.
- **Scalable Architecture**: To handle the demanding environments, a distributed implementation of PPO was developed. Data collection and gradient computations are parallelized across multiple workers. The authors found that synchronous updates, where gradients from workers are averaged before updating global parameters, yielded better results than asynchronous updates.
- **RNN Compatibility**: For tasks involving memory or variable-length episodes, the algorithm uses truncated backpropagation through time with K-step returns for estimating advantages, similar to Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C).
- **Normalization and Synchronization**: The implementation incorporates several modifications from existing PPO works, including normalization of observations and rewards using statistics aggregated over the experiment, and per-batch normalization of advantages. Parameters, including the adaptive regularization coefficient (λ), and normalization statistics are shared and synchronized across workers via a parameter server. An additional penalty term and early stopping are used when KL divergence greatly exceeds the target.

**Simulation Environment and Models**
All experiments were conducted using the MuJoCo physics engine.

- **Procedural Terrain Generation**: A large number of diverse terrains with various obstacles were procedurally generated. A new, unique instance of terrain was generated for each episode, ensuring high environmental variability.
- **Simulated Bodies**: Three different torque-controlled bodies, representing increasing levels of complexity, were used:
 - **Planar Walker**: A 9 Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF), 6-actuated joint bipedal walker constrained to the xz-plane.
 - **Quadruped**: A three-dimensional quadrupedal body with 12 DoF and 8 actuated joints.
 - **Humanoid**: A more complex three-dimensional humanoid with 28 DoF and 21 actuated joints.
- **Reward Function Design**: A deliberate choice was made to use simple and consistent reward functions across tasks and terrains, primarily incentivizing forward velocity along the x-axis. A small term penalized high torques. Body-specific minor terms included box constraints on torso height/angle for the Planar Walker, penalties for deviating from the track center for the Quadruped and Humanoid, and a small constant reward for the Humanoid not falling. No behavior-specific rewards (e.g., for jumping or crouching) were engineered.
- **Terrain and Obstacle Types**: The environments featured a variety of obstacles:
 - **Hurdles**: Variable height and width obstacles requiring jumping or climbing.
 - **Gaps**: Openings in the ground necessitating jumps.
 - **Variable Terrain**: Diverse features such as ramps, hills, and uneven surfaces.
 - **Slalom Walls**: Obstacles requiring navigation around them.
 - **Platforms**: Elevated surfaces that could be jumped onto or crouched under.
 These terrain types were combined into single-type courses, mixtures of single types, or mixed terrains containing multiple features. Importantly, "curriculum" courses were also designed, where the difficulty (e.g., hurdle height) increased gradually over the length of the track.
- **Observations**: Agents received two categories of observations:
 - **Egocentric/Proprioceptive**: Joint angles, angular velocities, and for 3D bodies, velocimeter, accelerometer, gyroscope readings, and contact/torque sensor data.
 - **Exteroceptive**: Task-relevant information, including the agent's position relative to the track center and a heightfield representation of the terrain profile ahead, sampled at varying densities depending on distance.
- **Policy Parameterization**: The neural network policy architecture was structured into two subnetworks, one for proprioceptive information and one for exteroceptive information. This separation of concerns was found to improve learning speed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study's findings are categorized by the performance of the DPPO algorithm, the emergent behaviors across different bodies, and analyses regarding the impact of terrain structure on learning and robustness.

**Evaluation of Distributed PPO (DPPO)**
DPPO was benchmarked against TRPO and a continuous A3C implementation.
- **Performance Equivalence**: DPPO achieved performance comparable to TRPO on benchmark tasks such as Planar Walker, Humanoid, and a Memory Reacher task requiring recurrent networks.
- **Scalability**: DPPO demonstrated effective scaling with the number of workers, which significantly reduced wall-clock time required for training.
- **Recurrent Network Compatibility**: Its gradient-based nature allowed for direct use with recurrent neural networks, as demonstrated by successful learning in the Memory Reacher task.
- **Efficiency**: DPPO was observed to be faster in wall-clock time than the authors' A3C implementation when using the same number of workers.

**Emergence of Locomotion Behaviors in Rich Environments**
The core investigation focused on whether complex skills would emerge from simple rewards in diverse terrains.

- **Planar Walker**: When trained on various terrains (hurdles, gaps, platforms, variable terrain, and mixtures), the Planar Walker consistently acquired robust gaits. Crucially, without explicit reward shaping for specific actions, it spontaneously learned to jump over hurdles and gaps, and to walk over or crouch underneath platforms. These behaviors were observed to be robust across different training runs. The walker learned to clear hurdles nearly its own height.
- **Quadruped**: This body, operating in 3D, was trained on variable terrain, slalom walls, gaps, and an adaptable hurdles terrain. The Quadruped reliably learned to navigate most obstacles. It discovered strategies such as jumping (sometimes with high accuracy) to overcome hurdles and gaps, and turning appropriately to navigate slalom walls, despite the reward function only promoting forward movement. For the varied hurdles, it learned to differentiate between obstacles requiring climbing/jumping and those it could bypass. Even on variable terrain, where its body shape was not optimally suited, it learned reasonably robust traversal strategies.
- **Humanoid**: The 28-DoF Humanoid represented a significantly more complex learning challenge, exhibiting greater sensitivity to hyperparameters and a tendency to get stuck in local optima. Despite these challenges and using a simple forward-velocity reward, several high-performing agents were obtained across various terrains (gaps, hurdles, slalom walls, variable terrain, and mixtures). These agents developed robust gaits and emergent skills such as obstacle negotiation, gap jumping, and slalom course navigation. The learned behaviors, while sometimes diverse in strategy, demonstrated moderately large levels of qualitative robustness against unseen test courses with different statistics and unobserved perturbations like see-saws and random forces applied to the torso.

**Analyses on Terrain Nature and Learning**
The study also investigated the impact of terrain structure on learning.

- **Curriculum Training**: For the Planar Walker, training on "curriculum" courses, where hurdle height gradually increased, resulted in faster learning progress compared to training on "regular" courses with static, interleaved high and low hurdles. This acceleration was evident when evaluating policies on both easy (shallow hurdles) and difficult (tall hurdles) test terrains.
- **Robustness of Learned Gaits**: Policies trained on challenging terrains (e.g., hurdles) showed a trend of increased robustness compared to policies trained only on flat terrain. This was assessed by evaluating Planar Walker and Quadruped policies against unobserved variations in surface friction, rubble-strips, body actuator strength, and ground incline/decline. A notable advantage in robustness was observed in certain contexts for agents trained on more varied terrains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning and continuous control by demonstrating that complex and robust locomotion behaviors can emerge from simple, generic reward functions when agents are exposed to sufficiently rich and diverse training environments.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Re-evaluation of Reward Design Paradigms**: The findings challenge the conventional approach in continuous control that emphasizes extensive hand-crafting of reward functions. It suggests that investing in the design of diverse training environments, rather than complex reward signals, can be a more effective strategy for inducing sophisticated, multi-faceted behaviors. This could simplify the development process for complex agents and robots.
- **Promotion of Robustness and Generalization**: By demonstrating that agents trained in diverse conditions, especially with an implicit curriculum, acquire more robust policies that generalize better to unforeseen environmental variations and perturbations, the work addresses a critical limitation of many current RL solutions. This enhanced robustness is crucial for real-world applications where environments are inherently unpredictable.
- **Validation of Curriculum Learning**: The empirical evidence supporting the accelerated learning achieved through a gradually increasing difficulty curriculum highlights the practical benefits of this long-standing machine learning concept in the context of motor skill acquisition. This can guide future environment design for more efficient training.
- **Advancement in Scalable RL Algorithms**: The development and validation of Distributed PPO (DPPO) provide a robust and scalable algorithmic foundation for tackling high-dimensional continuous control problems. Its compatibility with recurrent neural networks further extends its applicability to tasks requiring memory and sequential processing, facilitating research in more complex and data-intensive simulations.
- **Broader Applicability to AI Development**: The principle that environmental richness can drive behavioral complexity extends beyond locomotion. It suggests a potential shift in focus for AI research towards designing rich and varied training experiences as a means to achieve more general and adaptable intelligent systems.
- **Impact on Robotics and Character Animation**: The ability to learn robust, adaptive locomotion skills without explicit reward shaping has implications for robotics, enabling more autonomous robots to navigate varied terrains, and for character animation, allowing for more realistic and dynamically responsive virtual agents in games and simulations.
