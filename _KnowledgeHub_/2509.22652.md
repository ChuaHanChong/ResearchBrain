# Pixel Motion Diffusion is What We Need for Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The DAWN framework, developed at Stony Brook University, employs a two-stage diffusion model to generate dense pixel motion as an intermediate representation for language-conditioned robot control. This architecture achieved competitive or state-of-the-art results on various simulation and real-world manipulation benchmarks, showcasing enhanced data efficiency and robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Problem

- Existing vision-language-action (VLA) models often struggle to balance broad generalization with interpretability in robotic manipulation tasks.
- Deriving motion from full RGB video generation is computationally intensive, while sparse motion cues may lack sufficient detail for precise control.
- Purely end-to-end VLA models can sometimes lack modularity, making debugging and independent component advancements challenging.

## Method

- **DAWN** utilizes a ==two-stage diffusion-based framework== consisting of a ==Motion Director== and an ==Action Expert==.
- The **Motion Director**, a ==latent diffusion model==, generates dense pixel motion fields based on visual observations and natural language instructions.
- The **Action Expert**, an enhanced ==diffusion policy==, translates predicted pixel motions into executable robot actions, leveraging pre-trained ==CLIP==, ==DINOv3==, and ==T5-small== backbones.

## Results

- **DAWN** achieved an average task length of **4.00** on the CALVIN ABC→D benchmark, outperforming prior state-of-the-art VPP (**3.93**).
- The framework achieved an overall success rate of **65.4%** across 11 MetaWorld tasks, surpassing LTM (**57.7%**), demonstrating improved semantic understanding and robot state awareness.
- In real-world single-arm manipulation, DAWN achieved high success rates (e.g., **20/20** for 'avocado' lift-and-place) and improved bimanual action prediction accuracy (**0.117 MSE** vs. **0.128** for baseline).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit, dense pixel motion serves as an effective and interpretable intermediate representation, simplifying the learning problem by directly predicting scene dynamics.
> - A modular architecture using diffusion models for both high-level motion planning and low-level action generation can achieve robust performance and enhance system transparency.
> - Strategic integration of pre-trained foundation models (CLIP, DINOv3, T5-small) significantly improves data efficiency and facilitates robust sim-to-real transfer for complex robotic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressively incorporating vision-language-action (VLA) models, which leverage extensive web and robotic datasets to achieve broad generalization in robotic manipulation tasks. Within this landscape, several distinct approaches are being explored, which DAWN (Diffusion is All We Need for robot control) positions itself relative to.

One line of research emphasizes integrating explicit spatial reasoning and motion awareness into action generation. This often involves multi-stage pipelines for pixel or point tracking to extract interpretable intermediate motion cues. Examples include methods that utilize sparse pixel trajectories or 3D flow, such as General Flow, FLIP, Im2Flow2Act, and Track2Act, which capture local pixel displacements or object flow. Some generative approaches like AVDC and Gen2Act synthesize future observations and then derive motion signals (e.g., optical flow or point trajectories) to guide robot policies. Other related works, such as GENIMA, LLaRA, RoboPoint, LLARVA, TraceVLA, Magma, and HAMSTER, also explore pixel trajectories, often using sparse representations or relying on components like object detectors and point selection mechanisms. DAWN distinguishes itself from these by predicting *dense* pixel motion using a latent diffusion model, rather than sparse trajectories or relying on additional detection mechanisms.

Another prominent direction involves predictive representations, particularly those based on video diffusion and GPT-style forecasting, to directly predict future observations. Models like SuSIE, UniPi, VidMan, and VPP finetune or pretrain generative video and image models to act as world models or planners, inferring actions from generated future frames or their feature embeddings. These methods primarily operate in the RGB pixel space, without explicitly generating or reasoning about motion-centric representations. DAWN contrasts with these approaches by directly predicting dense pixel motion, arguing for a reduction in complexity compared to generating entire RGB video frames.

Furthermore, a significant segment of current research focuses on end-to-end VLA models, exemplified by RT-2, OpenVLA, and the π series. These models directly map observations and language instructions to actions, often by treating robot actions as discrete tokens within a unified language modeling framework. They prioritize improving generalization and data efficiency, sometimes employing techniques like flow-matching for higher fidelity control. DAWN, while also a VLA framework, introduces an explicit intermediate pixel motion representation, aiming to bridge the benefits of hierarchical decomposition (interpretability, modularity) with the performance of end-to-end agents.

In summary, DAWN contributes to the landscape by proposing a novel, two-stage diffusion-based framework that directly predicts dense pixel motion as an intermediate representation. This design aims to provide a structured, interpretable interface between perception and control, differentiating it from methods that use sparse motion cues or indirectly derive motion from full video predictions. The work suggests that the perceived limitations of structured intermediate representations, compared to purely end-to-end VLA models, may stem from the capabilities of the underlying high- and low-level components rather than the architectural paradigm itself.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop DAWN, a unified diffusion-based framework for language-conditioned robotic manipulation. This framework aims to establish a structured connection between high-level motion intent and low-level robot actions through the explicit use of a pixel motion representation.

The motivation behind this approach is multifaceted:

1. **Simplifying the Learning Problem:** Prior work has indicated that deriving motion trajectories from generated videos can improve robotic control. However, generating entire RGB video frames for this purpose is computationally intensive and complex. DAWN proposes a simplified approach by directly predicting dense pixel motions. This is hypothesized to reduce the complexity of the underlying learning problem for the neural network, making it more tractable and efficient compared to indirect motion derivation from generated videos, object detectors, or keypoint tracking.

2. **Enhancing Interpretability and Modularity:** The framework is designed such that both high-level motion intent and low-level robot actions are modeled as diffusion processes, yielding an end-to-end system. A key aspect is the introduction of interpretable intermediate motion abstractions in the form of dense pixel motion. This explicit representation allows for a clearer understanding of the desired scene dynamics, acting as a structured interface between the perception (understanding the instruction and current state) and control (generating robot actions) components. This modularity potentially facilitates independent advancements or debugging of the high-level motion planning and low-level action execution stages.

3. **Leveraging Strengths of Diffusion Models:** Diffusion models have demonstrated considerable success in generating complex data, including images and sequences. By implementing both the high-level (Motion Director) and low-level (Action Expert) controllers as diffusion processes, DAWN aims to harness their generative capabilities for robust and coherent motion and action sequence generation.

4. **Achieving Data Efficiency and Robust Transfer:** A practical goal is to demonstrate strong performance on challenging benchmarks (CALVIN, MetaWorld) and real-world scenarios, even with limited training data and smaller model capacity. The researchers hypothesize that combining diffusion modeling with motion-centric representations, alongside the strategic leveraging of pretrained vision and language models, can facilitate high data efficiency and robust transfer from simulation to reality, requiring only minimal finetuning for real-world deployment.

In summary, the core objectives are to:
- Propose DAWN as a two-stage, diffusion-based framework that explicitly generates structured intermediate pixel motion for language-conditioned visuomotor policies.
- Demonstrate competitive or state-of-the-art performance on various benchmarks with limited data and smaller model capacity.
- Design DAWN to effectively utilize pretrained vision and language models, thereby enabling data-efficient domain transfer, interpretability, and modularity in robot control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DAWN (Diffusion is All We Need for robot control) is structured as a two-stage visuomotor framework, where both stages leverage diffusion models. The two stages, the Motion Director and the Action Expert, are connected through an explicit dense pixel motion representation.

#### 3.1. Motion Director (High-Level Controller)

The Motion Director is responsible for generating dense pixel motion fields based on current visual observations and a language instruction. It is implemented as a latent diffusion model, building upon a pre-trained model designed for RGB image generation.

- **Inputs:** The model takes multi-view images (e.g., a static third-person camera view $I_t$ and a gripper-mounted camera view $G_t$), a natural language instruction $L$, and a temporal offset $k$.
- **Pixel Motion Representation:** The pixel motion from frame $I_t$ to $I_{t+k}$ is defined as $F_{t,k} = [u, v]$, where $u$ and $v$ are the horizontal and vertical pixel displacements, respectively. For compatibility with pre-trained image models, this motion is encoded into a three-channel image $F'_{t,k} = [u, v, (u+v)/2]$.
- **Architecture:** It comprises:
 - A U-Net denoiser ($U_M$).
 - A text encoder ($T_M$), which utilizes a CLIP model for embedding language instructions.
 - A pre-trained VAE encoder-decoder pair ($V_{ME}$, $V_{MD}$) to operate in a compressed latent space.
 - A vision encoder ($E_M$), also utilizing a CLIP model, to extract embeddings from the gripper-mounted camera view.
- **Training:** During training, only the $U_M$ is updated, while other modules ($V_{ME}$, $V_{MD}$, $T_M$, $E_M$) are kept frozen to benefit from their pre-trained capabilities. Ground-truth pixel motion is generated using the RAFT optical flow model between $I_t$ and $I_{t+k}$ and then projected into the latent space via $V_{ME}$. The model is trained using a mean squared error (MSE) noise estimation loss.
- **Inference:** A Gaussian noise $N_{M,n}$ is concatenated with the latent encoding of the current frame $V_{ME}(I_t)$ to form a noisy latent representation. The U-Net $U_M$ iteratively denoises this representation, conditioned on the embedded language instruction $T_M(L)$, the visual embedding of the gripper view $E_M(G_t)$, and the temporal offset $k$. After multiple denoising steps, the final denoised latent tensor is decoded by $V_{MD}$ into the predicted three-channel pixel motion $F'_{t,k}$.

#### 3.2. Action Expert (Low-Level Controller)

The Action Expert translates the pixel motion predictions from the Motion Director into executable robot actions. It is designed as a transformer-based Enhanced Diffusion Policy.

- **Inputs:** This module receives the predicted pixel motion from the Motion Director, current visual observations (from both static and gripper cameras), the language instruction, and low-dimensional robot states.
- **Architecture:** It consists of:
 - A shared visual encoder ($V_A$), a ConvNeXt-S variant of DINOv3, for encoding pixel motion and visual observations.
 - A text encoder ($T_A$), a T5-small pre-trained model, for embedding language instructions.
 - A state encoder ($S_A$), a two-layer MLP, for processing low-dimensional robot states.
 - A denoising transformer ($U_A$).
- **Training:** $U_A$ and $S_A$ are initialized from scratch to allow adaptation to the specific task, while $V_A$ and $T_A$ are kept frozen to leverage their pre-trained representations. The Action Expert is trained with an MSE noise estimation loss, predicting action chunks in the robot's action space.
- **Inference:** The various input modalities (pixel motion, visual observations, language instruction, robot states) are processed by their respective encoders and transformed into token embeddings. These context tokens are concatenated and provided as conditioning to the $U_A$ via cross-attention. The action generation starts from a noisy action chunk sampled from a Gaussian prior, which the $U_A$ iteratively refines into a coherent sequence of executable robot actions.

#### 3.3. DAWN Training and Inference Overview

The two diffusion models, Motion Director and Action Expert, are trained with an MSE noise estimation loss. Motion Director learns to predict pixel motions in latent image space, while Action Expert learns to predict robot action chunks in the robot's action space.

During inference, observations are initially encoded. The Motion Director then produces a pixel-motion field, which subsequently conditions the Action Expert to generate a sequence of robot actions. This process is executed in a closed-loop fashion: actions are performed, and the system repeats the prediction and execution cycle with updated observations. The design allows for parallel training of the two modules, using optical flow as ground truth for pixel motion, and offers the flexibility to fine-tune the Action Expert with pixel motions generated by the Motion Director. The framework strategically utilizes pre-trained models (latent diffusion for Motion Director, CLIP, DINOv3, T5-small for various encoders) to enhance data efficiency and performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The DAWN framework was evaluated across a range of benchmarks, including challenging simulated environments (CALVIN and MetaWorld) and real-world robotic manipulation tasks, with specific ablation studies to assess component contributions.

#### 4.1. CALVIN Experiments (Simulation)

- **Without External Robotic Data (Table 1):** DAWN achieved an average task length of 4.00 on the CALVIN ABC→D benchmark, outperforming previous state-of-the-art methods like VPP (3.93) and Seer-Large (3.83). The Enhanced Diffusion Policy (DAWN's Action Expert without Motion Director) had an average length of 2.78, indicating the contribution of the explicit pixel motion.
- **With External Robotic Data (DROID) (Table 2):** When trained with the DROID dataset in addition to CALVIN, DAWN achieved an average task length of 4.10. This result was competitive with, though slightly lower than, top methods such as DreamVLA (4.44) and VPP (4.33), which leveraged substantially larger and more diverse pre-training datasets. DAWN surpassed several other recent works, demonstrating strong data efficiency.

#### 4.2. MetaWorld Experiments (Simulation)

- DAWN achieved an overall success rate of 65.4% across 11 challenging MetaWorld tasks (Table 3), surpassing prior state-of-the-art methods like LTM (57.7%). The framework demonstrated improved semantic understanding, particularly on visually similar but semantically distinct tasks (e.g., "open-door" vs. "close-door"), which the authors attributed to scalable language-video pretraining and enhanced language understanding. Performance gains in tasks like "basketball" and "assembly" were linked to the Action Expert's improved robot state awareness.

#### 4.3. Real-world Single-arm Manipulation (xArm7)

- On a single-arm lift-and-place task involving six types of toys, DAWN achieved the highest success rates across most object categories (Table 4). For instance, DAWN achieved 19 successful lift-and-place actions out of 20 episodes for "apple" and 20 out of 20 for "avocado," compared to 10 for "apple" and 6 for "avocado" for π0.
- DAWN demonstrated improved semantic grounding and more consistent object identification and manipulation compared to baselines like π0 and VPP. The Enhanced Diffusion Policy, without pixel motion, frequently grasped incorrect objects, highlighting the complementary benefits of the Motion Director.
- **Inference Efficiency:** DAWN exhibited higher inference latency (319.82 ms) compared to Enhanced Diffusion Policy (112.77 ms) and VPP (190.55 ms) due to its two-stage design. However, the system maintained practical closed-loop frequencies, with the observed benefits of explicit motion reasoning outweighing the increased inference time.

#### 4.4. Bimanual Manipulation (Galaxea R1-Lite)

- In a more complex bimanual manipulation setting, DAWN achieved a lower mean squared error (MSE) of action prediction (0.117) on a test set of 300 episodes compared to the Enhanced Diffusion Policy baseline (0.128) (Table 5). This result suggests that the structured pixel motion representation improves action prediction accuracy even in multi-arm coordination scenarios.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies (CALVIN Dataset) (Table 6)

- **Pixel Motion vs. RGB Goal and Pretraining:** Explicit pixel motion yielded the best performance (Avg. Len 4.00). Using an RGB goal image resulted in lower performance (3.21), and relying solely on the Action Expert (no motion) yielded 2.78. Pre-training the latent diffusion model for pixel motion generation contributed significantly to performance (4.00 with pre-training vs. 3.42 without).
- **Gripper View Conditioning:** Removing the egocentric gripper-mounted observation from the Motion Director led to a degradation in performance (3.74 vs. 4.00), confirming its importance for reasoning about occlusions and fine-grained hand-object interactions.
- **Number of Diffusion Steps:** The Motion Director showed the ability to capture meaningful motion even with 2 diffusion steps (Avg. Len 3.88). Performance improved up to 25 steps (4.00) but did not significantly increase further at 40 steps (3.95).
- **Additional Metrics (Supplementary Material):**
 - **Motion Director Prediction Error:** DAWN's Motion Director demonstrated lower pixel motion prediction error (MSE = 1.23 × 10^-4) compared to LTM (5.19 × 10^-4) on the CALVIN validation dataset (Table 9).
 - **Temporal Offset k:** Performance remained stable across different temporal offsets for pixel motion prediction, with k=20 yielding optimal results (Table 10).

In summary, the findings indicate that DAWN's two-stage diffusion-based approach, utilizing explicit dense pixel motion as an intermediate representation, achieves competitive or state-of-the-art performance across diverse robotic manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world environments. The detailed ablation studies support the contributions of pixel motion, pre-trained models, and multi-view observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DAWN framework introduces a method for language-conditioned robotic manipulation that demonstrates performance advantages and architectural characteristics with several implications for the field of robot learning.

One significant aspect is the re-emphasis on **structured intermediate representations**. By explicitly generating dense pixel motion as an interface between high-level intent and low-level action, DAWN offers an alternative to purely end-to-end visuomotor policies. This intermediate representation is designed to be interpretable, providing a visual understanding of the robot's planned movements, which could aid in debugging, analysis, and human-robot collaboration. The work suggests that previous limitations attributed to multi-stage pipelines might be overcome by using powerful generative models for each stage, rather than being an inherent flaw in the hierarchical approach itself.

The **modularity** of DAWN's two-stage diffusion model design is another potential impact. Separating the Motion Director (high-level motion planning) from the Action Expert (low-level action generation) allows for independent development and improvement of each component. This could facilitate faster iteration cycles for research and potentially enable the integration of advancements in vision (for motion prediction) or control (for action execution) more flexibly.

Furthermore, the framework demonstrates notable **data efficiency and robust transfer capabilities**. By leveraging strong pre-trained vision and language models (e.g., CLIP, DINOv3, T5-small) and applying diffusion models, DAWN achieves competitive performance on complex benchmarks with relatively limited task-specific data. Its successful transfer from simulation to real-world environments with minimal fine-tuning is crucial for practical robot deployment, addressing a common challenge in robotics where large-scale real-world data collection is expensive and time-consuming.

The achieved state-of-the-art or competitive results on challenging benchmarks like CALVIN and MetaWorld, alongside consistent performance in real-world single-arm and bimanual tasks, suggest that this approach can serve as a robust baseline for future robot learning research. The explicit pixel motion also appears to improve semantic grounding and fine-grained interaction capabilities compared to certain direct action prediction or RGB-based methods.

In conclusion, DAWN presents a compelling argument for the utility of explicit, dense pixel motion as a structured and interpretable intermediate representation in diffusion-based robot control. Its implications include potentially advancing the development of more interpretable, modular, data-efficient, and robust robotic systems capable of performing complex, language-conditioned tasks across various domains.
