# Learning Hip Exoskeleton Control Policy via Predictive Neuromusculoskeletal Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed a sim-to-real learning pipeline using predictive neuromusculoskeletal simulation to train a hip exoskeleton control policy. The policy, distilled for physical hardware, reduced simulated user effort metrics and achieved average sim-to-real torque profile agreement of r=0.82 ± 0.19.

## Problem

- Developing exoskeleton controllers traditionally demands extensive, time-intensive human experiments and data collection in controlled laboratory settings.
- Existing control strategies often struggle with generalization across diverse real-world locomotor tasks, varying terrains, speeds, and transitions.
- Prior simulation-to-hardware transfer efforts for exoskeletons lacked comprehensive evaluation of controller efficacy in simulation and quantitative characterization of the sim-to-real gap.

## Method

- A predictive neuromusculoskeletal walking simulation framework was developed to train a ==reinforcement learning== agent to coordinate muscle excitations and bilateral hip exoskeleton torques.
- A two-stage curriculum was used, first for stable unassisted locomotion, then for learning exoskeleton assistance while directly comparing assisted vs. unassisted conditions to quantify effort reduction.
- A privileged simulation-trained "teacher" policy was distilled into a deployable "student" policy using only a single-axis thigh IMU signal, enabling real-time control on physical hardware.

## Results

- Simulated sagittal-plane joint kinematics and kinetics demonstrated fidelity to human experimental data, with global mean RMSEs of **7.32** ± **2.21**0 for angles and **0.264** ± **0.078** Nm/kg for moments.
- Exoskeleton assistance in simulation reduced mean muscle activation by **1.1%** (level ground) to **3.4%** (ramp ascent) and mean positive hip-knee joint power by **4.8%** to **7.0%**.
- The sim-to-real transfer of assistance torque profiles showed good agreement between hardware and simulation (Pearson's r = **0.82** ± **0.19**, RMSE = **0.03** ± **0.01** Nm/kg), with strongest agreement on ramp ascent (r = **0.98** ± **0.01**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physics-based neuromusculoskeletal simulation can effectively front-load exoskeleton controller design, significantly reducing the experimental burden and accelerating development.
> - The learned assistance policy from simulation successfully transfers to physical hardware, with quantitative preservation of assistance torque waveforms, demonstrating the viability of the sim-to-real pipeline.
> - Exoskeleton assistance learned in simulation can reduce user muscle activation and positive joint power, with benefits increasing systematically with walking speed, serving as a predictive screening tool.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Lower-limb wearable robots, such as exoskeletons, are being developed to enhance human mobility by reducing metabolic cost or muscular effort, and to facilitate rehabilitation for individuals with motor impairments. Despite their potential, widespread real-world deployment of these systems faces challenges, primarily related to exoskeleton control. The optimal assistance strategy is not universally known and depends on the specific locomotor task.

Existing approaches to exoskeleton control have limitations. Human-in-the-loop optimization (HiLO) has yielded performance improvements by tuning parameterized assistance profiles to minimize objectives like metabolic cost. However, controllers developed using HiLO are typically task-specific and do not generalize well to diverse real-world conditions, such as varying terrains, speeds, and task transitions. Furthermore, HiLO requires extensive, time-intensive human experiments.

More recent advancements include task-agnostic control paradigms, which modulate exoskeleton assistance based on continuously estimated physiological states derived from onboard sensors. These controllers, often data-driven and supervised learning-based, can generalize across a wider range of activities. However, their performance is bounded by the coverage of the training data distribution and can degrade under out-of-distribution conditions. A critical limitation for these physiological state-estimation controllers is the need for ground-truth labels (e.g., joint moments), which necessitate synchronized three-dimensional kinematics and ground reaction force measurements, largely confining controller development to instrumented laboratory settings. This data collection bottleneck restricts scalability to diverse tasks and user populations.

To address the reliance on manual data collection and laboratory experimentation, reinforcement learning (RL) in physics-based simulation has emerged as a complementary approach. Simulation enables efficient generation of diverse locomotor experience, exploration of various gait patterns and terrains, and systematic evaluation under conditions impractical or unsafe for human participants. While simulation-trained controllers have shown success in robotic domains like legged locomotion, their application to closed-loop exoskeleton control policies for physical lower-limb hardware has been less common. Prior work often used musculoskeletal simulation to inform hand-designed assistance profiles, which were then experimentally validated, rather than learning and deploying end-to-end closed-loop policies.

One previous study demonstrated full sim-to-real transfer for a hip exoskeleton. However, it had two limitations: the simulation-side evaluation did not establish whether the learned controller reduced user effort in simulation or quantitatively benchmarked simulated biomechanics against experimental data. Additionally, the sim-to-real gap was not explicitly characterized through systematic comparisons of kinematics, kinetics, and assistance profiles between simulated and real-world conditions.

This research addresses these gaps by presenting a sim-to-real learning pipeline for hip-exoskeleton control that does not rely on motion-capture demonstrations. It aims to develop a predictive neuromusculoskeletal walking simulation framework to train an RL agent to coordinate muscle excitations and exoskeleton torques. The study explicitly quantifies user effort reduction in simulation, validates simulation biomechanical fidelity against human data, and quantitatively characterizes the sim-to-real transfer of assistance profiles. This approach seeks to establish physics-based neuromusculoskeletal simulation as a practical and scalable foundation for exoskeleton controller development, substantially reducing the experimental burden during the design phase.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop a scalable and practical methodology for designing hip-exoskeleton controllers that minimizes the need for extensive human experimentation. Specifically, the authors aimed to train a hip-exoskeleton control policy entirely within a predictive neuromusculoskeletal simulation environment, without requiring motion-capture demonstrations, and then successfully deploy this policy onto physical hardware via a process called policy distillation.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several challenges in current exoskeleton control development:
- **Experimental Burden:** Traditional methods, such as human-in-the-loop optimization or data-driven supervised learning controllers, demand extensive, time-consuming data collection using motion capture and force plates, often in controlled laboratory settings. This process is resource-intensive and limits the scope of conditions that can be explored.
- **Generalization Limitations:** Controllers optimized for specific tasks or trained on limited datasets often fail to generalize effectively to the diverse and dynamic locomotor conditions encountered in daily life (e.g., varying speeds, slopes, terrains, and transitions).
- **Unobservable Physiological States:** Many advanced controllers rely on estimating internal physiological states, such as joint moments, which are not directly measurable. Obtaining ground-truth labels for these states further complicates and constrains data acquisition.
- **Gaps in Prior Sim-to-Real Efforts:** While simulation-based approaches offer a promising alternative, previous attempts at full sim-to-real transfer for lower-limb exoskeletons have lacked comprehensive evaluation of the controller's effectiveness within simulation (e.g., reducing user effort) and a rigorous characterization of the discrepancy between simulated and real-world assistance profiles.

To overcome these challenges, the authors pursued the following specific objectives:
1. **Develop a Robust Simulation Framework:** Create a physics-based neuromusculoskeletal walking simulation capable of training an RL agent to coordinate muscle excitations and bilateral hip exoskeleton torques across a wide range of walking speeds and slopes.
2. **Demonstrate Simulated Efficacy:** Use a two-stage curriculum to allow direct comparison between exoskeleton-assisted and no-exoskeleton conditions, thereby quantifying the reduction in effort metrics (mean muscle activation, mean positive joint power) achieved by the learned assistance in simulation.
3. **Validate Simulation Fidelity:** Benchmark the simulated sagittal-plane joint kinematics and kinetics against open-source human biomechanics data to ensure the biological plausibility and accuracy of the simulation.
4. **Enable Hardware Deployment:** Distill the privileged simulation-trained "teacher" policy into a deployable "student" policy that can operate on limited, wearable sensor inputs (specifically, a single-axis inertial measurement unit, IMU) for real-time onboard control of a physical hip exoskeleton.
5. **Quantify Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Systematically compare assistance torque waveforms generated by the distilled policy on physical hardware against the original simulation profiles across matched speed-slope conditions to characterize the fidelity of the transfer.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to establish a more efficient, scalable, and practical methodology for exoskeleton controller design, shifting the primary development effort from costly human experiments to iterative simulation-based learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a neuromusculoskeletal simulation framework combined with reinforcement learning and policy distillation to develop and deploy a hip-exoskeleton control policy.

**Neuromusculoskeletal Simulation:**
Simulations were executed within the Hyfydy physics engine integrated with the SCONE framework. The control policy operated at 40 Hz, while forward dynamics were integrated at 200 Hz.
- **Human-Exoskeleton Model:** The H2190 full-body musculoskeletal model was utilized, modified by removing upper limbs, resulting in 21 degrees of freedom and 90 Hill-type musculotendon actuators (80 for lower limbs, 10 for pelvis/torso). Bilateral torque actuators were added to the hip joints to represent the exoskeleton, applying flexion-extension torques up to 12 Nm. The physical mass of the exoskeleton (4.3 kg total) was incorporated by augmenting the inertial properties of the thigh, pelvis, and torso segments. Raw torque commands were smoothed by a discrete-time first-order low-pass filter with a time constant (τ_LPF) of 0.1 s.
- **Policy Training and Curriculum:** Stochastic control policies were trained using the Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) algorithm. Training followed a two-stage curriculum:
 - **Stage 1 (0 to 5x10^7 environment steps):** The policy learned stable unassisted locomotion across target speeds (0.7–1.5 m/s) and slopes (−5° to +5°), with exoskeleton torques clamped to zero.
 - **Stage 2 (5x10^7 to 1x10^8 steps):** Training continued under two parallel conditions: (1) an exoskeleton-assisted condition, where the policy controlled hip torque actuators, and (2) a matched no-exoskeleton condition, where exoskeleton commands remained zero. This allowed for direct comparison of assistance effects.
- **Muscle Synergy Action Prior:** To manage the high-dimensional muscle actuation problem, a low-dimensional muscle synergy representation was derived. This involved tracking human overground walking motion using an OpenSim-converted H2190 model with OpenSim Moco to estimate muscle activations, followed by Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) on right-leg activation data to obtain a fixed synergy weight matrix. The policy's action space comprised 32 inputs: 20 lower-limb synergy coefficients (10 per leg), 10 direct torso/pelvis muscle activation signals, and 2 bilateral hip exoskeleton torque commands.
- **Action and Observation Space:** The teacher policy had privileged access to a rich observation space including muscle states (lengths, velocities, forces, excitations), joint positions and velocities, head orientation and angular velocity, foot positions, ground reaction forces, current hip exoskeleton torques (normalized), whole-body center-of-mass velocity, and target walking speed.
- **Domain Randomization:** To enhance policy robustness and generalization, ground slope and target walking speed were randomized at the start of each episode. Eleven discrete slopes were sampled with probabilities dynamically adjusted by per-slope difficulty scores (falls increased score, successes decreased). Target speed followed a cyclic curriculum, varying from 0.7 m/s to 1.5 m/s and back.
- **Reward Design:** The agent received a scalar reward composed of weighted component terms encouraging stable walking at target speed (`r_vel`), penalizing muscle effort (`r_eff`), enforcing physiological joint kinematics (`r_rom`, `r_knee`, `r_front`), promoting smooth exoskeleton torque commands (`r_sm`), and penalizing falls (`r_fall`).

**Policy Distillation:**
To transfer the simulation-trained policy to hardware with limited sensing, the privileged teacher policy was distilled into a student policy.
- **IMU Selection:** Thigh gyroscope signals from simulation (femur angular velocity in local frame) and hardware (IMU on distal thigh bar) were compared during level-ground walking at 1.2 m/s. The mediolateral component (gyro z) showed the strongest agreement (r = 0.55) and was selected as the sole input for the student policy.
- **Student Policy Training:** Training data consisted of teacher policy rollouts across various speeds and slopes. Each teacher's hip torque command was paired with a 0.95 s history window of the right femur mediolateral angular velocity. The student policy was implemented as a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) and trained for five epochs using a mean squared error loss.

**Robotic Hip Exoskeleton Hardware and Human Experiment:**
- **Hardware:** A custom-built robotic hip exoskeleton (4.5 kg mass, up to 18 Nm torque per hip) was used. It featured two quasi-direct-drive brushless DC motors and IMUs on the distal thigh bars. An embedded processor (Jetson Orin Nano) executed the TensorRT-converted student policy at 100 Hz, maintaining parallel I/O and inference processes. Torque amplitude was scaled by participant body mass, and the hardware torque filter time constant was set to 0.15 s (vs. 0.1 s in sim) with a rate limit (c = 0.5) to manage noise and inter-subject variability.
- **Human Experiment:** Five able-bodied participants completed a 5-minute acclimation walk. Main trials involved walking on a treadmill at three slope conditions (0°, +5°, -5°), with speeds increasing from 0.7 to 1.5 m/s in 0.2 m/s increments (30 s per speed). GRFs and exoskeleton hip-torque commands were recorded. Data from five consecutive gait cycles per speed-slope condition were extracted and time-normalized for analysis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings concerning biomechanical simulation fidelity, characteristics of the learned assistance profiles, effects of exoskeleton assistance in simulation, and the sim-to-real transfer of these profiles.

**Biomechanical Simulation Fidelity:**
Simulated sagittal-plane hip, knee, and ankle joint angles and net joint moments were compared against open-source human experimental data across ramp descent, level ground, and ramp ascent at speeds of 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2 m/s.
- Averaged across slopes and speeds, the root-mean-square errors (RMSEs) for joint angles were 5.83 ± 1.05° (hip), 9.81 ± 1.80° (knee), and 6.32 ± 0.92° (ankle).
- Corresponding moment RMSEs were 0.228 ± 0.063 Nm/kg (hip), 0.220 ± 0.052 Nm/kg (knee), and 0.344 ± 0.044 Nm/kg (ankle).
- Global mean RMSEs were 7.32 ± 2.21° for joint angles and 0.264 ± 0.078 Nm/kg for joint moments.
- Mean correlation coefficients were r = 0.845 for angles and r = 0.761 for moments. These metrics indicate that the simulation maintained biomechanical consistency with human walking.

**Learned Assistance Profile:**
The learned hip assistance torque profile from simulation (1.2 m/s, level ground) was compared with a spline-based controller and a biological-torque-based controller.
- The learned controller's hip extension peak occurred at 17% of the gait cycle, later than the spline (9%) and biological torque (13%) references.
- Its hip flexion peak occurred at 71% of the gait cycle, later than both references (61%).
- When compared to the biological hip net joint moment, the learned exoskeleton torque exhibited a phase lag: the extension peak lagged by 103 ms (18.6% vs. 6.9% gait cycle), and the flexion peak lagged by 166 ms (74.1% vs. 55.4% gait cycle).

**Effects of Exoskeleton in Simulation:**
Comparison between no-exoskeleton and exoskeleton-assisted conditions revealed the following:
- **Mean Muscle Activation Reduction:** When averaged over speed, assistance reduced mean muscle activation by 1.1% on level ground and 3.4% on ramp ascent. Ramp descent showed a slight increase of 2.2%.
- **Mean Positive Hip-Knee Joint Power Reduction:** Assistance reduced mean positive hip-knee joint power by 6.3% on level ground, 7.0% on ramp ascent, and 4.8% on ramp descent.
- **Speed Dependence:** The benefit of exoskeleton assistance increased systematically with walking speed. Pearson's correlations between walking speed and percent reduction were r = 0.76 for positive-power reduction and r = 0.98 for mean-activation reduction.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer of Assistance Profiles:**
The right hip exoskeleton torque waveforms from the physical device (distilled student policy) were compared against simulated profiles (teacher policy) across matched speed-slope conditions.
- Overall, the assistance profiles exhibited agreement (Pearson's r = 0.82 ± 0.19, RMSE = 0.03 ± 0.01 Nm/kg).
- Agreement varied by terrain:
 - Ramp descent: r = 0.66 ± 0.20, RMSE = 0.04 ± 0.01 Nm/kg.
 - Level ground: r = 0.83 ± 0.16, RMSE = 0.03 ± 0.01 Nm/kg.
 - Ramp ascent: r = 0.98 ± 0.01, RMSE = 0.02 ± 0.01 Nm/kg.
This indicates that the learned assistance strategy was generally preserved, with better agreement observed under locomotor conditions that were more prominently represented in the training distribution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic exoskeletons by establishing and quantitatively validating a simulation-to-hardware learning pipeline for hip-exoskeleton assistance without the need for motion-capture demonstrations or motion-mimicking objectives. The findings demonstrate a pathway to substantially reduce the experimental overhead typically associated with controller design.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Reduced Experimental Burden:** By front-loading controller development into a physics-based neuromusculoskeletal simulation, the approach enables rapid iteration, systematic comparison of design choices, and controlled evaluation across a wide range of locomotor conditions. This significantly decreases the reliance on time-intensive human experiments, allowing human trials to focus primarily on validation and safety testing rather than controller discovery.
- **Validated Simulation Fidelity:** The quantitative benchmarking of simulated joint kinematics and kinetics against open-source human biomechanics data confirms that the simulation generates physiologically plausible gait patterns. This validation is crucial for ensuring that the benefits observed in simulation are transferable and relevant to real-world human interaction.
- **Demonstrated Simulated Efficacy:** The observation that exoskeleton assistance consistently reduced muscle activation and positive joint power in simulation, with benefits systematically increasing with walking speed, supports the use of such predictive simulations as a screening tool. This allows researchers to identify conditions where real-world assistance is likely to be effective before committing to hardware development and extensive human trials.
- **Quantified Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The explicit characterization of the sim-to-real gap by comparing assistance torque waveforms between simulation and hardware provides interpretable, quantitative evidence of the preservation of the learned assistance strategy. This level of validation is important for building confidence in simulation-driven design.
- **Emergent Assistance Timing:** The policy distillation approach, which directly maps IMU signals to hip torque commands, allows the assistance timing to emerge from the learning process rather than being manually prescribed. The observed phase-dependent delays between biological joint moments and exoskeleton torques suggest a more nuanced control strategy that would be difficult to capture with fixed-delay heuristics.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Development of Exoskeletons:** This methodology could accelerate the design cycle for novel exoskeleton controllers, allowing for more rapid prototyping and evaluation of different assistance strategies.
- **Enhanced Generalizability:** By training policies across a diverse range of simulated conditions, future controllers developed using this framework may exhibit improved generalization capabilities to various real-world tasks and environments.
- **Application to Impaired Locomotion:** The framework's ability to operate without motion-capture demonstrations is particularly impactful for patient populations, where collecting extensive, diverse datasets is often constrained by fatigue, safety, and logistical challenges. By incorporating neurophysiological constraints of pathological gait in simulation, this approach could enable simulation-driven screening of assistance strategies to improve stability, symmetry, or functional performance in clinical settings.
- **Scalable and Personalized Control:** The approach lays a foundation for developing scalable sim-to-real controllers that could be more readily personalized to individual users by adapting simulation parameters, and potentially generalize beyond steady-state locomotion to more complex and unstructured activities.

While the current study focused on cyclic locomotion and used a single-axis thigh gyroscope, future work can extend this framework to broader locomotor contexts, richer sensing modalities, and systematic strategies for further narrowing the sim-to-real gap, such as matching sensor noise and mounting dynamics in simulation. Overall, this research represents a significant step towards making physics-based neuromusculoskeletal simulation a practical and efficient tool for designing advanced, deployable exoskeleton control systems.
