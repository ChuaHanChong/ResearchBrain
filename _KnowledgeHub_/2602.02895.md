# Moving On, Even When You're Broken: Fail-Active Trajectory Generation via Diffusion Policies Conditioned on Embodiment and Task

> [!summary] Summary
> DEFT is a diffusion-based joint-space trajectory generator for fail-active manipulation. It conditions a UNet denoiser on a per-joint embodiment vector encoding failure-induced position and velocity limits plus a one-hot task-constraint token, and enforces feasibility via start-goal inpainting and output clamping. On a 7-DoF arm it reaches 99.5% success for unconstrained motion versus RRT's 42.4%, and completes real-world multi-step tasks.

## Problem

- Safety standards mandate fail-freeze halting on fault detection, causing prolonged downtime and lost autonomy instead of continued reduced-capability operation without human intervention.
- Classical fault-tolerant planners assume a fixed subset of failures and rely on manual modeling, so they scale poorly across the combinatorial space of multi-joint degradations.
- Prior learning methods recover locomotion or reaching under known embodiments but need task-specific training loops, explicit policy switching, and handle only single manipulation primitives.

## Method

- ==Embodiment encoding==: a 4N vector of per-joint position and velocity min/max limits, passed through an MLP and injected into the diffusion denoiser via ==FiLM conditioning==.
- ==Constraint encoding==: a one-hot token distinguishing constrained (planar, orientation-preserving) from unconstrained motion, concatenated with the embodiment features to select motion primitives implicitly.
- Feasibility enforcement: ==start-goal inpainting== anchors endpoints and per-denoising-step ==clamping== to failure-induced joint limits, over 25 denoising steps, with an **RRT**-plus-IK minimum-jerk data pipeline.

## Results

- Across **4.7k** failure conditions and **4.7M** trajectories, **DEFT** satisfied constraints **74.51%** versus **36.85%** for classical baselines, a **37.66**-point gain with bootstrap confidence interval [**37.21%**, **38.10%**].
- Generalization held near parity between in-distribution (**78.33%**) and out-of-distribution (**73.61%**) failures; constrained motion reached **46.42%** versus **30.93%** for differential IK.
- Real-world drawer and erasing tasks scored **1.00** over 10 runs each, versus **0.00** and **0.35** for optimization and **0.60** and **0.93** for the unconditioned ablation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treating each actuation fault as a distinct embodiment turns fault tolerance into a conditional generation problem, letting one policy span unbounded failure spaces without switching.
> - Soft conditioning guides but never guarantees feasibility; hard mechanisms like clamping and endpoint inpainting are what actually enforce constraints during stochastic denoising.
> - Conditioning gains can be invisible in simulation metrics yet decisive on hardware, so ablations judged only in simulation may badly understate a component's real value.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The operation of robotic systems, particularly in long-duration or uncertain environments, is frequently disrupted by hardware failures. Current industrial safety standards often mandate "fail-freeze" behavior, where a robot must halt operations upon fault detection. While this approach prioritizes safety, it results in substantial downtime and limits autonomy. The paper positions its work in contrast to this conservative paradigm, advocating for "fail-active" operation, a more complex objective where robots maintain partial or full functionality even when experiencing faults. This concept draws parallels to human adaptation to injury or impairment.

The research specifically addresses actuation failures, such as joint locking, reduced range of motion, or diminished velocity, which directly alter a robot's kinematics and can induce non-holonomic end-effector motion. These failures present a significant challenge due to their unpredictable nature, potential for temporal evolution, and combinatorial complexity across multiple joints and failure types.

Existing approaches to failure-aware robot control predominantly fall into two categories: classical methods and learning-based strategies. Classical methods, including self-motion manifold planning and inverse kinematics-based redundancy exploitation, provide guarantees for specific, pre-defined failure subsets but typically lack generalization to arbitrary or unforeseen failure conditions. Learning-based strategies, often utilizing reinforcement learning or quality diversity search, have shown success in adapting to known embodiments for tasks like locomotion or point-to-point reaching. However, these methods often require task-specific training, explicit policy switching, or runtime optimization, making them less suitable for real-world scenarios where failures are diverse and adaptation must occur online without prior knowledge.

This paper identifies three key gaps in the existing literature: (1) a lack of generalization to arbitrary failure configurations, (2) an inability to complete multiple manipulation primitives seamlessly, and (3) insufficient solutions for multi-joint failures. By framing each failure mode as a distinct "embodiment," the authors propose that an adaptable system requires a diverse set of motion primitives to navigate the altered action and task spaces. The work suggests that diffusion models are particularly well-suited for this challenge due to their capacity to model complex, multimodal data distributions, which can encompass the range of actions required across numerous embodiments. This perspective, utilizing diffusion models to generate trajectories conditioned on both embodiment and task, represents an effort to bridge the identified gaps and advance the field towards robust fail-active robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable "fail-active manipulation," allowing robots to generate functional and feasible trajectories despite experiencing joint-level actuation failures. This overarching goal is motivated by the limitations of current robot safety protocols, which, by mandating a complete halt ("fail-freeze") upon fault detection, lead to significant operational downtime and reduced autonomy. The authors envision a future where robots can adapt and continue task execution under damage, akin to how humans perform tasks with physical impairments.

The specific motivations for developing the DEFT framework are rooted in the inherent challenges posed by actuation failures:
1. **Altered Kinematics and Dynamics:** Actuation failures (e.g., locked joints, reduced joint ranges or velocities) fundamentally change what a robot can physically accomplish. These changes redefine the robot's feasible workspace, degrade manipulability, and can cause end-effector motion to become non-holonomic, meaning the same control input no longer yields the same end-effector motion.
2. **Unbounded and Combinatorial Failure Space:** Failures can be arbitrary, evolve over time, and are not known a priori. Each joint can fail in multiple ways (locked, reduced range, reduced velocity), and this complexity scales combinatorially with the robot's degrees of freedom. Existing engineering or learning solutions often struggle to reliably perform across this effectively unbounded space of failure modes.
3. **Need for Adaptive Behaviors:** When a robot's physical capabilities change, its original motion plan or set of behaviors may become infeasible. This necessitates the ability to adapt task strategies and synthesize new motion primitives on the fly. For instance, if grasping an object becomes impossible due to a joint failure, the robot might need to switch to pushing it into a graspable state first.
4. **Limitations of Prior Work:** As previously identified, existing methods lack the generality to handle arbitrary failure configurations, the flexibility to execute multiple manipulation primitives within a unified framework, and the robustness to address multi-joint failures simultaneously. The authors explicitly state that, to their knowledge, no existing work comprehensively bridges all three of these gaps.

To address these motivations, the research proposes DEFT, a framework designed to:
- Generalize to arbitrary failure configurations.
- Complete multiple manipulation primitives.
- Handle multi-joint failures.

By interpreting failures as shifts in the robot's "embodiment," the work aims to create a system that can generate appropriate actions for an effectively infinite number of altered physical states and task requirements without needing explicit retraining or policy switching.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DEFT (Diffusion-based Embodiment-aware Fail-active Task-conditioned trajectory generation) framework is designed to generate functional and feasible joint-space trajectories for N-degrees-of-freedom robots under joint-level failure conditions. The core principle involves conditioning a diffusion model on both the robot's current failure-induced embodiment and the specific task constraints.

**A. DEFT Framework Overview**
The policy, denoted as π, infers a sequence of joint states (positions q and velocities ˙q) over a fixed number of steps T, conditioned on start and goal joint configurations (Q_{s,g}), an embodiment vector (ξ), and a task constraint vector (τ).

1. **Embodiment Conditioning (ξ):** Joint-level failures are modeled as constraints on the robot's actuation capabilities. The embodiment encoding vector ξ ∈ R^(4N) represents these per-joint limits: e_q,j = [q_min,j, q_max,j]^⊤ for joint angles and e_˙q,j = [˙q_min,j, ˙q_max,j]^⊤ for joint velocities. This vector captures a continuous spectrum of degradation severities, including locked joints (where min=max) or reduced ranges. This ξ is passed through a small MultiLayer Perceptron (MLP) to produce an embedding.
2. **Constraint Conditioning (τ):** Discrete task constraints (e.g., "unconstrained motion" or "constrained motion") are represented by a one-hot encoding vector τ ∈ {0,1}^K.
3. **Conditioning Vector (ζ):** The output embedding from the MLP of ξ is concatenated with τ to form a comprehensive conditioning vector ζ = [MLP(ξ), τ]^⊤. This vector modulates the diffusion model via FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation).
4. **Diffusion Model (UNet):** The core generative model is a UNet architecture. During the denoising process (K=25 steps), it receives a corrupted trajectory (initially sampled with Gaussian noise), the current time step, and the conditioning vector ζ. It then predicts a denoised trajectory.
5. **Start-Goal Inpainting:** To ensure adherence to specified start (q_s, ˙q_s) and goal (q_g, ˙q_g) configurations, these endpoints are inpainted into the noisy trajectory at each denoising step. This hard-enforces the boundary conditions.
6. **Output Clamping:** Both the noisy trajectory and the denoised prediction from the UNet are clamped to the joint angle and velocity limits defined by the embodiment vector ξ. This ensures that all generated joint values remain physically feasible under the robot's degraded state.

**B. Data Generation Approaches**
A dataset of failure-conditioned joint-space trajectories is generated for two distinct task constraints: unconstrained and constrained motions. Each trajectory consists of T joint configurations sampled at fixed intervals.

1. **Start and Goal Sampling:**
 - **Unconstrained:** Start and goal configurations (q_s, q_g) are sampled within nominal joint limits and then filtered for feasibility (e.g., self-collision checks, workspace bounds).
 - **Constrained:** Start and goal configurations (q_s, q_g) are selected such that they correspond to planar end-effector poses (p_s, p_g) at a fixed z-height.
2. **Trajectory Construction:**
 - **Unconstrained Trajectories:** Generated using an RRT-Connect joint-space planner with goal biasing.
 - **Constrained Trajectories:** Generated by interpolating end-effector poses along a straight Cartesian path. An optimization-based Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver with pose consistency constraints then solves for the corresponding joint configurations.
 - **Smoothing:** All trajectories undergo minimum-jerk optimization to ensure zero-velocity endpoints and continuous velocity profiles.
3. **Failure Condition Sampling:** A structured failure vector ξ is sampled per trajectory to simulate embodiment degradation.
 - **Number of Affected Joints:** Ranging from one to seven, sampled using a modified exponential decay distribution (50% probability for a single joint failure, remaining 50% distributed among multiple, with each additional joint failure half as likely as the one before it).
 - **Failure Types:** One of three types is sampled: joint angle range reduction, velocity limit reduction, or a combination.
 - **Modified Joint Limits:** Computed from the trajectory's observed maximum and minimum joint values, with additional uniform noise margins to create the reduced limits.
4. **Task Validity Filtering:** Trajectories are retained only if they obey constraint-specific requirements:
 - **Constrained Motions:** Validity determined by total change in end-effector alignment, manipulability above a specified threshold, and path efficiency (length ratio of straight line to generated path arc length).
 - **Unconstrained Motions:** Feasibility verified using manipulability and goal reachability checks.

This comprehensive methodology, integrating a conditional diffusion model with careful data generation and constraint enforcement, aims to enable flexible, zero-shot adaptation to diverse joint degradation and task requirements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DEFT was conducted in both simulation and real-world environments using a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda arm. The experiments aimed to assess DEFT's generalization to arbitrary joint failures (H1, H2) and its ability to produce both unconstrained and constrained motions (H3).

**A. Simulation Analysis**
The simulation involved 4,700 unique failure conditions, testing 100 start-goal pairs per condition, totaling 4.7 million trajectories. Failure conditions involved 1-7 joints being locked, range-limited, or velocity-restricted, with 22% being in-domain (ID) and 78% out-of-domain (OOD). Baselines included RRT for unconstrained motions and differential IK with optimization for constrained motions.

1. **H.1: Does DEFT obey embodiment constraints of arbitrary actuation failures more often than classical planners?**
 - DEFT demonstrated a significantly higher overall constraint satisfaction rate of 74.51% compared to the classical baseline's 36.85%. This represents an absolute improvement of 37.66 percentage points (95% CI: [37.21%, 38.10%], p < 10^-10).
 - Specifically, for angle failures, DEFT achieved 84.3% success versus the baseline's 48.2%. For velocity failures, DEFT achieved 70.8% versus the baseline's 32.5%.
 - These results indicate that DEFT effectively generalizes to diverse joint-level degradations by directly conditioning on the failure embedding, translating new failure specifications into feasible motion plans at substantially higher rates.

2. **H.2: Does DEFT generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) failures?**
 - DEFT maintained a high constraint satisfaction rate even for OOD failure conditions, achieving 73.61% for OOD compared to 78.33% for ID conditions.
 - The minimal performance difference between ID and OOD conditions suggests robustness to distributional shifts and indicates that DEFT extrapolates over joint space rather than memorizing seen cases. This capability enables the system to generate feasible, constraint-satisfying trajectories for previously unseen failure configurations, regardless of the affected joint or degradation magnitude.

3. **H.3: Does DEFT adhere to constraint conditions more than approaches specialized to a single class of constraints?**
 - DEFT significantly outperformed baselines for both unconstrained and constrained motion primitives.
 - For **unconstrained motions**, DEFT achieved a 99.58% satisfaction rate, vastly exceeding the RRT baseline's 42.40% (an improvement of 57.18 percentage points). Statistical analysis confirmed DEFT's significance in 95.24% of tested conditions.
 - For **constrained motions**, DEFT achieved 46.42% satisfaction compared to the baseline's 30.93%. A chi-squared test confirmed a significant association between planner choice and primitive constraint satisfaction (p < 10^-10). The lower absolute success rate for constrained motions is attributed to the inherent difficulty of these tasks and the evaluation methodology not guaranteeing the existence of any feasible action.
 - These findings indicate that DEFT is not specialized to a single manipulation mode but reliably synthesizes feasible trajectories for both unconstrained and constrained motions within a unified policy.

**B. Real-World Evaluation**
Real-world experiments assessed DEFT's end-to-end task success against a hybrid RRT–Differential-IK optimization approach and a DEFT ablation without conditioning (DEFT-NoConditioning).

1. **Drawer Task:** This multi-step task involved pulling, pushing, picking, and placing, integrating both unconstrained and constrained actions.
 - DEFT achieved a perfect 1.00 success rate over 10 runs.
 - The Optimization baseline failed completely (0.00 success rate), unable to find executable plans.
 - DEFT-NoConditioning succeeded in 6 out of 10 runs (0.60 success rate), failing due to surface-constraint violations, jerky motion, dropping objects, and an inability to open the drawer, highlighting the importance of embodiment conditioning and inpainting.

2. **Erasing Task:** This task involved grasping an eraser and performing constrained sweeps across a whiteboard.
 - DEFT again achieved perfect completion (1.00 success rate) over 10 runs.
 - The Optimization baseline had a 0.35 success rate, exhibiting unstable surface interaction and poor contact maintenance.
 - DEFT-NoConditioning achieved a 0.93 success rate but occasionally disobeyed contact constraints, leading to loss of the eraser, consistent with the absence of embodiment conditioning and inpainting.

The real-world results corroborate the simulation findings, demonstrating that DEFT, with its embodiment and task conditioning, coupled with inpainting and clamping, enables fail-active operation. It consistently recovered performance in scenarios where traditional optimization failed or produced unstable motions, and where an unconditioned generative policy exhibited brittleness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, DEFT, that addresses a critical bottleneck in the deployment of long-horizon autonomous robotic systems: their inability to maintain functionality in the presence of hardware faults. By proposing and validating "fail-active" operation as an alternative to the prevailing "fail-freeze" paradigm, the work contributes to the development of more resilient and continuously operating robots.

The significance and potential impact of DEFT stem from several key aspects:
1. **Enhanced Robot Reliability and Autonomy:** DEFT enables robots to adapt to diverse actuation failures and complete tasks without requiring constant human intervention or halting operations. This capacity is crucial for applications in remote, hazardous, or long-duration environments (e.g., space exploration, industrial automation, disaster response) where human access is limited or costly.
2. **Generality Across Failure Modes and Tasks:** A central contribution is DEFT's ability to generalize across arbitrary joint-level degradation, including single- and multi-joint failures of various types (locked, reduced range, reduced velocity), and to unseen (out-of-distribution) failure conditions. Furthermore, it supports the completion of multiple manipulation primitives, such as both unconstrained (e.g., pick-and-place) and constrained (e.g., pushing, pulling, surface tracing) motions, within a single policy. This broad applicability reduces the need for specialized, hand-engineered solutions for each potential failure scenario.
3. **Robustness Over Traditional Methods:** The demonstrated significant improvements in constraint satisfaction rates over classical motion planning techniques (RRT, differential IK) in simulation, and perfect task completion in complex real-world scenarios where baselines failed or exhibited unstable behavior, highlight DEFT's practical robustness. The ablation studies further underscore the critical role of embodiment and task conditioning, along with inpainting and clamping, in achieving this performance.
4. **Enabling Advanced Manipulation:** By re-conceptualizing failure as a shift in "embodiment," the framework allows robots to adapt their strategies dynamically. This means a robot can transition from a primary manipulation strategy (e.g., grasping) to an alternative (e.g., pushing) if its physical capabilities become impaired, expanding the effective action space in degraded states.
5. **Future Directions for Robotic Systems:** The work paves the way for future research in several areas, including the integration of real-time failure detection mechanisms, enabling cross-embodiment transfer of learned strategies to different robot platforms, and expanding the repertoire of adaptive manipulation skills (e.g., pivoting, throwing). These advancements could lead to highly adaptable and durable robotic systems.

In summary, DEFT represents a step towards robots that can not only detect faults but also actively respond to them by re-planning and executing tasks, thereby maintaining functionality and extending their operational lifetime in challenging and unpredictable environments.
