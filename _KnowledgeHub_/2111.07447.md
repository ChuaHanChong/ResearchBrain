# Learning Multi-Stage Tasks with One Demonstration via Self-Replay

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Imperial College London developed Self-Replay, a robot learning method that enables manipulators to learn complex, multi-stage tasks from a single human demonstration. It leverages autonomous data collection to train specialized neural networks, achieving 70-80% success rates across various tasks while drastically reducing human effort compared to existing imitation learning techniques.

## Problem

- Traditional Imitation Learning (IL) methods often require a large number of human demonstrations (tens to hundreds) to generalize effectively for robot tasks.
- Meta Learning and Reinforcement Learning approaches demand extensive prior data collection or continuous human supervision for environment resets and reward shaping, hindering practical application.
- Multi-stage manipulation tasks, common in real-world scenarios, exacerbate data and supervision requirements for existing robot learning techniques.

## Method

- A single human demonstration records '==bottleneck poses==' (precise interaction start points) and subsequent 'interaction actions' for each stage of a multi-stage task.
- The robot then autonomously collects training data through '**Self-Replay**' (==Random== or ==Active==) by navigating to bottleneck poses and replaying interactions, eliminating further human intervention.
- Three specialized convolutional neural networks (==stage-recognition==, ==bottleneck reaching==, ==bottleneck classification==) are trained using this self-generated data to enable robust task execution at test time.

## Results

- **Self-Replay** achieved high success rates (typically **70-80%**) on various multi-stage tasks from a single human demonstration, significantly outperforming baselines that often required **20 or more** demonstrations.
- **Active Self-Replay** consistently improved performance over **Random Self-Replay** on precision-demanding tasks, such as 'Cut' (**73%** vs. **57%** success rate).
- The method demonstrated robust scalability, maintaining high success rates (**80%** for **4** stages) as the number of task stages increased, and proved resilient to visual distractors with only minor performance degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex multi-stage manipulation tasks can be effectively decomposed into alternating phases of learned object-reaching ('bottleneck pose') and open-loop action replay for interaction.
> - Autonomous, self-supervised data collection through 'Self-Replay' drastically reduces human effort and data requirements, making robot skill acquisition more accessible.
> - Active sampling strategies (Active Self-Replay) enhance learning efficiency and robustness by prioritizing data from challenging or under-explored workspace regions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning aims to enable robots to acquire new skills from human demonstrations efficiently and with minimal human effort. Imitation Learning (IL) has gained prominence as a method for teaching robots, but existing techniques often encounter significant challenges, particularly when dealing with complex, multi-stage tasks.

Traditional Imitation Learning approaches, such as Behavioural Cloning (BC), typically demand a large number of human demonstrations (tens to hundreds) to generalize effectively, making the process time-consuming and laborious for operators. Meta Learning methods, while requiring fewer demonstrations for new tasks, necessitate extensive prior data collection on similar tasks, which is also a resource-intensive process. Furthermore, Meta Learning approaches often exhibit limited adaptability to tasks significantly different from those seen during meta-training. Reinforcement Learning (RL) from demonstrations can use expert data to bootstrap policy learning, but the subsequent autonomous exploration phase typically requires constant human supervision for environment resets and careful reward function engineering, escalating human involvement and experimental complexity, especially for sparse reward settings.

The challenge is exacerbated in multi-stage tasks, which involve a sequence of distinct actions or sub-goals, such as grasping an object and then using it, or arranging multiple items. For these tasks, the data and supervision requirements of existing methods tend to increase substantially. While various methods combine ideas from different fields for multi-stage tasks—including auto-regressive sequence modeling, neural task programming, spatial symmetry exploitation, hierarchical decomposition, and explicit task planning—they commonly necessitate considerable human effort in terms of data collection or system engineering.

This work builds upon the "Coarse-to-Fine Imitation Learning" paradigm introduced by Johns, which models single-stage manipulation tasks as a two-phase process: a learned object reaching phase followed by an open-loop replay of demonstrated actions. The current research extends this concept to handle multi-stage tasks, addressing the limitations of existing methods by proposing a system that requires only a single human demonstration and no prior object knowledge or extensive human intervention for data collection. The paper positions its contribution as a method that significantly reduces human effort and engineering requirements compared to contemporary robot learning techniques for multi-stage manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel methodology, named Self-Replay, that enables a robotic manipulator to learn and execute complex, multi-stage manipulation tasks from a single human demonstration. This is to be achieved without requiring any prior knowledge about the objects involved in the task or any additional human effort beyond the initial demonstration.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key challenges prevalent in the field of robot learning:
1. **Minimizing Human Effort:** A central motivation is to overcome the substantial human effort typically required by current imitation learning (IL), meta-learning (ML), and reinforcement learning (RL) techniques. These methods often necessitate numerous demonstrations, extensive pre-collected datasets, or continuous human supervision for environment resets and reward shaping, which hinders their widespread adoption outside controlled laboratory settings.
2. **Addressing Multi-Stage Task Complexity:** Multi-stage manipulation tasks, which are common in everyday scenarios (e.g., picking and placing, using a tool), inherently increase the complexity and data demands for robot learning. The researchers aim to develop a method that can effectively learn such tasks without the proportional increase in human input usually observed with traditional approaches.
3. **Generalizing Coarse-to-Fine Imitation Learning:** The work is explicitly motivated by and extends the Coarse-to-Fine Imitation Learning method. While effective for single-stage tasks by decomposing them into an object-reaching phase and an open-loop interaction phase, this prior method was not designed for sequential, multi-stage operations. A key objective is to generalize this decomposition to handle a series of alternating reaching and interaction phases required for multi-stage tasks.
4. **Enabling Autonomous Data Collection:** A significant objective is to allow the robot to autonomously collect all necessary training data after an initial single human demonstration. This "self-supervised" data collection, referred to as Self-Replay, is intended to eliminate the need for repeated human interventions like environment resets, which are common in RL-based approaches.
5. **Developing Robustness to Real-World Variability:** The research aims to design a test-time pipeline that can manage uncertainties inherent in real-world environments, including recovery from external disturbances or errors and robustness to visual distractors. This includes developing mechanisms for the robot to identify its current stage in a multi-stage task dynamically.
6. **Benchmarking Against State-of-the-Art:** The researchers seek to empirically evaluate the performance and efficiency of their proposed method against existing state-of-the-art robot learning techniques, specifically highlighting its advantages in terms of reduced human effort and improved success rates for multi-stage tasks.

The core underlying assumption motivating the approach is that most everyday manipulation tasks can be robustly decomposed into two distinct phases: a "coarse" phase focused on reaching the vicinity of an object (the "bottleneck pose"), and a "fine" phase involving precise interaction with that object. By accurately reaching the bottleneck pose, the robot can then simply replay the exact actions demonstrated by the human during the interaction phase, obviating the need to learn a complex interaction policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Self-Replay, extends the Coarse-to-Fine Imitation Learning framework to multi-stage tasks by decomposing each stage into a bottleneck-reaching phase and an action-replay interaction phase.

#### 3.1. Providing the Demonstration

The human operator provides a single, complete demonstration of the multi-stage task. For each stage (N), the operator first guides the robot's end-effector to a "bottleneck pose" (s_b,N), which is the precise starting point for interaction with an object. This pose, defined by 3D spatial coordinates and rotation around the z-axis, is recorded. Subsequently, the operator demonstrates the precise interaction with the object (e.g., grasping, cutting), and all executed actions (A_N) during this interaction are recorded. After completing all stages, the environment is reset once to its initial configuration.

#### 3.2. Self-Supervised Data Collection via Self-Replay

Following the single demonstration and environment reset, the robot autonomously collects training data without further human intervention. A wrist-mounted RGB camera captures observations (o_t), whose appearance is primarily a function of the relative pose between the end-effector and the objects, promoting generalization.

- **Random Self-Replay:** This approach involves the robot exploring its workspace by sampling random end-effector poses. From each sampled pose, the robot moves linearly back to the bottleneck pose (s_b,N) for the current task stage, recording a sequence of observations (o_t), end-effector poses (s_e,t), the target bottleneck pose (s_b,N), and the current stage (N). The ground-truth relative displacement between the current end-effector pose and the bottleneck is analytically computed, providing labels for training a model to predict optimal movements from RGB observations. Once data collection for a stage is complete, the robot reaches s_b,N and replays the recorded actions A_N to complete the interaction, thus transitioning to the next task stage (N+1). This cycle repeats for all stages.
- **Active Self-Replay:** This is an enhanced, more efficient variant. It begins with an initial dataset collected through `M` cycles of Random Self-Replay. The models are then trained on this initial data. Subsequently, the robot actively samples poses in the workspace that maximize the error of the trained networks. This strategy aims to collect more informative data from under-explored or challenging regions, rather than redundantly sampling already understood areas. This iterative process of data collection and network training continues until the network error is below a defined threshold (δ_err) or a maximum number of steps is reached.

#### 3.3. Overall Architecture and Test-Time Pipeline

The method employs a framework of three distinct convolutional neural networks (CNNs), all utilizing Deep Spatial Autoencoders (DSAE) to extract keypoints from 64x64 RGB images as a regularization mechanism. Different networks are trained for each stage, conditioned by the stage prediction.

- **Stage-Recognition Network (n(o_t) → j ∈ [0, N]):** This network predicts the current stage (j) of the task based on the current RGB observation. Predicting the stage at each time step enables robustness to external disturbances or failures, allowing the robot to return to a previous stage if, for example, an object is dropped.
- **Bottleneck Reaching Network (π(o_t) → a_t):** For a given stage j, this network takes the current RGB image and predicts the end-effector actions (spatial velocities vx, vy, vz; angular velocity vθ around z-axis; and a binary gripper command c) in the end-effector coordinate frame to move towards the bottleneck pose s_b,j.
- **Bottleneck Classification Network (r(o_t) → {0, 1}):** This network predicts whether the robot's end-effector has reached the target bottleneck pose s_b,j with sufficient precision (i.e., closer than a threshold).

**Test-Time Pipeline (Fig. 3):**
1. An RGB observation (o_t) is acquired.
2. The stage-recognition network identifies the current task stage (j).
3. The bottleneck classification network (r_j) evaluates if the bottleneck pose for stage j has been reached.
4. If not reached, the bottleneck reaching network (π_j) computes an action (a_t), which the robot executes. A new observation is then obtained.
5. If the bottleneck is classified as reached, the robot executes the pre-recorded object interaction actions (A_j) for stage j to complete that stage.
6. These steps are repeated for subsequent stages until the entire multi-stage task is completed.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setting and Tasks

Experiments were conducted using a TinkerKit Braccio 6-DOF manipulator with a parallel gripper and an uncalibrated wrist-mounted RGB camera (800x600 pixels, resized to 64x64). Eight "everyday-like" multi-stage manipulation tasks were designed, including pick-and-place, cutting, shape insertion, and stacking. Test sets involved 20 novel configurations for each task, with objects randomly positioned within a 30cm x 20cm workspace and rotated within a 120-degree range. The success of each task was judged by a human operator.

#### 3.5. Baselines for Comparison

The method was compared against several categories of existing techniques:
- **One Demonstration Methods:** FlowControl (optical-flow based visual servoing) and SIFT (non-learning based keypoint matching).
- **Multi-Stage Behavioural Cloning:** Latent Motor Plans (LMP), Relay Imitation Learning (RIL), and classic Behavioural Cloning (BC). These were tested with 1 and 20 demonstrations.
- **Reinforcement Learning from Demonstrations:** Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient from Demonstrations (DDPGfD), provided with 1 demonstration and human-supervised RL exploration with environment resets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation addressed the method's ability to learn multi-stage tasks, the efficiency of its data collection, its comparative performance against baselines, its scalability with increasing stages, and its robustness to distractors.

- **Learning Multi-Stage Tasks from a Single Demonstration:** Both Random Self-Replay and Active Self-Replay demonstrated the ability to solve a range of everyday multi-stage manipulation tasks based on a single human demonstration. The human demonstration phase typically took approximately one minute per task.
- **Active vs. Random Self-Replay Performance:** Active Self-Replay generally exhibited improved performance compared to Random Self-Replay (Table 1). It showed notable improvements on tasks requiring higher precision, such as "Cut" (73% vs. 57% success rate for Active and Random Self-Replay, respectively) and "Fork" (83% vs. 63%). While Active Self-Replay slightly underperformed on "Insert" (73% vs. 77%), the overall trend indicated its enhanced efficiency and robustness due to actively sampling more informative data.
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - **One-Demonstration Baselines:** Methods like FlowControl, SIFT, LMP-1, RIL-1, and DDPGfD showed very low success rates (often below 10%, with FlowControl reaching a maximum of 40%) when provided with only a single demonstration. This highlights Self-Replay's significant advantage in learning from minimal human input.
 - **Multi-Stage Behavioural Cloning (20 Demonstrations):** Even when provided with 20 demonstrations, methods like LMP-20, RIL-20, and BC-20 generally achieved lower success rates (e.g., LMP-20 max 62%, RIL-20 max 60%, BC-20 max 42%) compared to Self-Replay variants, which typically achieved 70-80% success rates with a single demonstration.
 - **Time and Sample Efficiency (Appendix Section 6.2, Figures 6 & 7):** Self-Replay exhibited superior time efficiency for the human operator, requiring orders of magnitude less human interaction time. RIL and LMP were found to require approximately 40-50 demonstrations to reach the performance level achieved by Self-Replay with just one demonstration, indicating a 40x to 50x increase in human effort. Furthermore, Self-Replay demonstrated better sample efficiency, achieving higher performance with the same amount of data due to its Coarse-to-Fine decomposition, which simplifies the learning task for the policy network to merely aligning with the bottleneck pose.
- **Performance with Increasing Number of Stages (Table 2):** Self-Replay maintained high success rates even as the number of task stages increased (e.g., 100% for 1 stage, 90% for 2 stages, 80% for 4 stages). In contrast, Relay Imitation Learning (RIL), even with 10 or 30 demonstrations, experienced a substantial performance degradation with more stages (e.g., RIL-30 dropped from 90% for 1 stage to 35% for 4 stages), demonstrating Self-Replay's superior scalability.
- **Robustness to Distractors (Table 3, Figures 8 & 9):** The method proved robust to visual distractors (unseen objects and lighting changes) at test time. The keypoint-based Deep Spatial Autoencoder architecture autonomously learned to focus on task-relevant objects, resulting in only minor performance reductions (e.g., for "Cup," 85% without distractors vs. 80% with distractors).
- **Replicating Interaction Trajectory vs. Learning a Policy (Table 4):** Comparative experiments showed no statistically significant performance benefit from learning a separate policy for the interaction phase over simply replaying the previously recorded human actions. If the bottleneck pose was accurately reached, open-loop action replay was sufficient for successful interaction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robot learning and robotic manipulation:

- **Dramatic Reduction in Human Effort:** The most substantial impact is the capability of robots to learn complex multi-stage manipulation tasks from a single human demonstration, coupled with a single environment reset. This drastically reduces the human effort compared to existing methods that demand numerous demonstrations, extensive pre-training, or continuous human supervision. This simplification makes robot skill acquisition more accessible and less laborious.
- **Enhanced Accessibility for Non-Experts:** By requiring only a single, straightforward demonstration from an operator without the need for expert knowledge of underlying algorithms or complex data engineering, the method lowers the barrier to entry for teaching robots. This could enable non-expert users to program robots for a variety of tasks, expanding the practical application of robotics in diverse settings.
- **Increased Autonomy in Data Collection:** The introduction of Self-Replay, particularly its active variant, empowers robots to autonomously collect their own training data after minimal initial human input. This self-supervised data generation reduces the reliance on tedious manual data collection and environment resets, accelerating the learning process and making it more scalable.
- **Improved Scalability for Multi-Stage Tasks:** The method demonstrates robust performance even as the number of task stages increases, a critical challenge for many imitation learning approaches. This scalability is essential for deploying robots in real-world scenarios that often involve sequential and complex tasks.
- **Robustness to Real-World Variability:** The demonstrated resilience to visual distractors and the ability to recover from external disturbances or errors at test time enhance the practical applicability of the method. This suggests that robots trained with Self-Replay could operate more reliably in less controlled and dynamic environments.
- **Efficiency in Learning:** Beyond reducing human effort, the approach exhibits high time and sample efficiency. The Coarse-to-Fine decomposition simplifies the learning problem, allowing the robot to achieve high performance with less data and computational resources than comparable state-of-the-art methods.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work extends the Coarse-to-Fine Imitation Learning paradigm to multi-stage tasks, providing a new foundation for future research in efficient and robust robot learning from demonstrations. It also reinforces the utility of open-loop action replay for interaction phases when precise pre-positioning is achieved, potentially simplifying policy learning.
- **Applicability to Low-Cost Hardware:** The successful implementation on an inexpensive TinkerKit Braccio robot with an uncalibrated wrist camera suggests that the method is not solely dependent on high-end industrial hardware, potentially broadening its adoption across a wider range of robotic systems.

In conclusion, Self-Replay represents a step towards more autonomous and human-friendly robot learning systems, addressing key limitations of current imitation learning approaches in the context of multi-stage manipulation tasks.
