# Automatic Prompt Optimization with "Gradient Descent" and Beam Search

> [!summary] Summary
> ProTeGi automatically improves LLM prompts using only API access and training data. It runs a minibatch of examples, has an LLM describe the prompt's flaws in natural language (a textual gradient), then edits the prompt in the opposite direction. Beam search with bandit selection picks the best candidates. Across four classification tasks it improves the initial prompt by up to 31% and beats prior prompt-editing baselines.

## Problem

- Soft-prompt and differentiable prompt tuning methods need access to the LLM's internal state, which practitioners using only an API do not have.
- Discrete prompt search with reinforcement learning or phrase-level edits often needs low-level model access and produces incoherent, scrambled prompts that humans cannot interpret.
- LLM-feedback methods such as Monte Carlo paraphrase search explore prompt space without semantic direction, so improvements are undirected, inefficient, and rely on evaluating many candidates.

## Method

- ==Textual gradients==: a feedback prompt takes the current prompt and its errors on a minibatch, and the LLM writes natural-language reasons the prompt failed, playing the role of a gradient.
- Gradient application: an editing prompt rewrites the current prompt to fix the criticized flaws, i.e. moves in the opposite semantic direction. ==Monte Carlo paraphrasing== of the edits adds local candidates.
- ==Beam search== with ==bandit selection==: prompts are expanded via gradients and pruned with ==best-arm-identification== algorithms (UCB, UCB-E, Successive Rejects/Halving) that cut the number of full-dataset evaluations needed.

## Results

- Across Jailbreak, Ethos, Liar and Sarcasm, ProTeGi beat Monte Carlo by **3.9%** and RL by **8.2%** on average, improved the initial prompt by **15.3%**, and beat AutoGPT by **15.2%**.
- Beam search ablation: ProTeGi scored **0.85**, **0.67** and **0.88** on Jailbreak, Liar and Sarcasm, versus 0.80, 0.63 and 0.87 for flat enumeration and 0.82, 0.63 and 0.85 for greedy search.
- Bandit selection beat uniform budget allocation (Jailbreak 0.77, Liar 0.59-0.61) with UCB reaching **0.83-0.85** and **0.66**. GPT-4 as base model gave the best Jailbreak F1 (**0.88**), versus 0.55 for GPT-3.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An LLM's natural-language critique of its own errors can serve as a usable gradient surrogate, letting discrete text optimization borrow the descent-step structure without any model internals.
> - Directed edits from error feedback produce more varied and meaningful prompt changes than undirected paraphrase or phrase-level search, though gradients can be unhelpful or drift the task, as in Jailbreak.
> - Treating candidate selection as best-arm identification saves evaluation cost, but the process overfits after about 3 steps, and stronger RLHF-tuned base models matter, so use early stopping.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has demonstrated their impressive capabilities across diverse natural language processing tasks. However, effectively harnessing these capabilities often hinges on the quality of the input "prompts" – the natural language instructions given to the LLM. The research community widely acknowledges that writing effective prompts is a manual, iterative, and often frustrating process that demands significant human effort and expertise. This paper positions itself as a solution to this critical bottleneck.

Prior work in automatic prompt optimization generally falls into two main categories, each with limitations that ProTeGi aims to overcome:

- **Differentiable/Soft Prompts:** Some methods involve training auxiliary models or creating differentiable representations of prompts (e.g., "soft prompts" or "prompt tuning"). While effective, these approaches typically require access to the internal state variables or gradients of the LLM, which is often not available to practitioners interacting with LLMs via black-box APIs (e.g., OpenAI's ChatGPT or Azure OpenAI Service). Furthermore, the resulting "optimized" prompts are often dense vectors or embeddings that are not human-interpretable.
- **Discrete Prompt Manipulations:** Other approaches apply discrete textual manipulations to prompts, often guided by Reinforcement Learning (RL) or LLM-based feedback. These include techniques like phrase-level operations (adding, paraphrasing, swapping, deleting text chunks) or Monte-Carlo search over prompt variations. The limitations here are that they may still require low-level LLM access for reward calculation, can produce incomprehensible or incoherent outputs, or rely on "directionless" search strategies that are inefficient and prone to getting stuck in local optima. Examples include AutoPrompt (Shin et al., 2020), GrIPS (Prasad et al., 2022), TEMPERA (Zhang et al., 2023), and the Monte-Carlo methods of Zhou et al. (2022).

ProTeGi distinguishes itself by proposing a **non-parametric and general-purpose solution that bridges the gap** between these two paradigms. It addresses the discrete nature of prompt optimization by mirroring the steps of numerical gradient descent within a natural language, Socratic dialogue framework. Crucially, it assumes only black-box LLM API access, making it highly practical for real-world applications. By generating "textual gradients" (natural language critiques of prompt errors) and using the LLM itself to "backpropagate" these gradients into improved prompt edits, ProTeGi introduces a *semantically directed* search strategy for discrete prompt space, unlike the often random or brute-force nature of prior discrete methods. The integration of beam search guided by bandit optimization further enhances its efficiency in exploring the vast space of possible prompts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automatic and efficient method for **optimizing natural language prompts for Large Language Models (LLMs)**. This optimization aims to enhance the LLM's task performance while minimizing the manual effort typically associated with prompt engineering.

The core motivations driving this work are:

- **Alleviating Manual Effort:** The current reliance on manual, trial-and-error prompt writing is highly "onerous" and requires significant human expertise. Automating this process would drastically reduce the time and resources spent by developers and practitioners.
- **Improving Task Performance:** Hand-tuned prompts often fail to extract the full potential of LLMs. An automated optimization process, driven by data, can discover more effective prompt formulations that lead to higher task accuracy and robustness.
- **Enhancing Interpretability:** Unlike methods that produce uninterpretable "soft prompts" or incoherent textual manipulations, ProTeGi aims to generate human-readable and semantically meaningful prompt improvements. The "textual gradients" themselves offer interpretable feedback on why a prompt is failing, providing insights into the cognitive decision process of the LLM and guiding further human refinement.
- **Addressing API-Centric Development:** A significant portion of LLM usage occurs via black-box APIs (e.g., ChatGPT, GPT-4). Existing advanced prompt optimization techniques often require access to internal LLM states or gradients, rendering them unusable in such common practical settings. ProTeGi specifically targets this limitation by operating solely through API interactions.
- **Directed Optimization in Discrete Space:** Previous discrete prompt optimization methods often suffer from being directionless, relying on Monte-Carlo sampling or primitive phrase-level operations. The authors are motivated to introduce a *semantically directed* approach to discrete prompt optimization, leveraging the LLM's own understanding of language to guide improvements.

In essence, the paper seeks to democratize effective prompt engineering, making it accessible and efficient even for those without deep expertise or access to underlying model architectures.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ProTeGi (Prompt Optimization with Textual Gradients) is a general-purpose, non-parametric algorithm designed to automatically refine an initial natural language prompt (`p0`) using training data (`D_tr`) and a black-box LLM API. The core idea is to mirror the iterative steps of gradient descent within a text-based Socratic dialogue, combined with an efficient beam search strategy.

The methodology can be broken down into two main components:

#### 3.1. Textual "Gradient Descent"

This component forms the inner loop of the optimization, simulating gradient descent using natural language. It involves three conceptual steps:

1. **Evaluation and Error Collection:** For a given prompt `p`, a small `minibatch` of training data (`D_mini`) is sampled. The LLM's performance with prompt `p` on `D_mini` is evaluated, and specific instances where `LLM_p(x)` produces incorrect outputs (`y_i`) compared to the ground truth are identified and collected as `errors (e)`.
2. **Gradient Generation:** A pre-defined static LLM prompt, referred to as `∇`, is used. This `∇` prompt takes the current prompt `p` and the collected `errors (e)` as input. It then instructs the LLM to generate natural language "gradients" (`g`). These `g` are textual summaries or criticisms describing the flaws of the current prompt `p` that led to the observed errors. Conceptually, these "gradients" point in the semantic direction that makes the prompt worse, similar to how numerical gradients point towards increasing error. The authors note that the same `∇` prompt (provided in the Appendix) is used across all tasks.
3. **Prompt Editing (Opposite Semantic Direction):** Another pre-defined static LLM prompt, `δ`, is employed. This `δ` prompt takes the current prompt `p` and the generated "gradient" `g` as input. It instructs the LLM to "edit the current prompt `p` in the opposite semantic direction of `g`." In other words, it asks the LLM to fix the problems identified by the gradient, thereby producing one or more improved candidate prompts (`p'`). This step acts as the "backpropagation" in the text-based gradient descent analogy.

#### 3.2. Beam Search Over Prompts

The textual "gradient descent" steps guide an outer beam search loop, exploring the space of coherent natural language prompts more efficiently.

1. **Initialization:** The process starts with an initial prompt `p0`, which forms the first beam `B0`.
2. **Iterative Optimization (Main Loop):** The algorithm proceeds for a specified number of `r` optimization steps (search depth). In each iteration `i`:
 - **Expansion Step (Algorithm 2):** For every prompt `p` currently on the beam `B_i`, new candidate successor prompts are generated:
 - Mini-batch evaluation and error collection are performed as described in the "Gradient Descent" section.
 - Multiple natural language "gradients" (`g_1, ..., g_m`) are generated using `LLM_∇` based on `p` and its errors.
 - For each `g_i`, the current prompt `p` is edited using `LLM_δ` to produce several primary candidate successors (`p'_i1, ..., p'_iq`).
 - To further explore the local semantic space, additional "Monte-Carlo" samples (`p''_ij1, ..., p''_ijm`) are generated by paraphrasing the primary candidates (`p'_ij`) using another LLM prompt `LLM_mc`.
 - All these generated candidates (from `p'` and `p''`) form a large set `C`. To manage computational load, only a random subset of `C` is typically considered before the next step.
 - **Selection Step (Algorithm 3 & 4):** From the large set of candidate prompts `C`, the algorithm selects the `b` most promising candidates to form the next beam `B_i+1`. This selection process is framed as a "best arm identification" problem in multi-armed bandit optimization, aiming to find the best performing prompts with the minimum number of evaluations (API calls).
 - **UCB Bandits (Upper Confidence Bound):** This strategy balances exploration (trying less-evaluated prompts) and exploitation (focusing on currently high-performing prompts). It estimates prompt performance (`Q_t`) and query counts (`N_t`), selecting arms (prompts) based on a confidence bound. Both standard UCB and UCB-E (an exploration-favored variant) are considered.
 - **Successive Rejects (SR):** This algorithm is provably optimal for best arm identification and requires no hyperparameters. It iteratively evaluates all currently surviving candidates on progressively larger subsets of data and eliminates the worst-performing one in each phase until only `b` candidates remain.
 - **Successive Halving (SH):** A more aggressive variant of SR that prunes the bottom half of candidates in each phase.
3. **Final Output:** After `r` iterations, the prompt (`p_hat`) with the highest performance from the final beam `B_r` is selected as the optimized prompt.

**Evaluation Setup:**
- **Tasks:** Four NLP classification tasks: Jailbreak detection (novel), Ethos (hate speech), Liar (fake news), and Sarcasm detection.
- **Data Split:** 50 examples for development, 150 for test (randomly sampled from larger datasets).
- **Metric:** Binary F1 score (maxpooling over the final beam of candidates).
- **LLM:** Primarily `gpt-3.5-turbo` (January 2023 version) via Azure OpenAI API. Temperature 0.0 for few-shot classification, 1.0 for gradient/editing generations.
- **Parameters:** Mini-batch size = 64, beam size = 4, 6 optimization steps. Few-shot examples were fixed. No hyperparameter tuning for baselines or ProTeGi.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of the ProTeGi framework across the four benchmark NLP classification tasks.

- **Overall Performance Superiority:** ProTeGi consistently outperformed all tested state-of-the-art prompt learning baselines: Monte-Carlo (MC), Reinforcement Learning (RL), and AutoGPT.
 - On average, ProTeGi improved over MC by 3.9% and over RL by 8.2%.
 - It achieved a significant 15.3% improvement over the initial, human-written prompt (`p0`) and 15.2% over AutoGPT.
 - This performance lead was robust across varying API query budgets (12 to 50 evaluations per prompt candidate).
- **Baseline Limitations:** The study revealed that while MC generally improved prompt performance, RL and AutoGPT sometimes fell short. For Ethos and Sarcasm, RL's performance was close to `p0`. Notably, for Jailbreak and Sarcasm, 6 rounds of AutoGPT feedback *reduced* the starting prompt's performance, suggesting that its AI-guided changes can be detrimental without a directed feedback mechanism. This underscores ProTeGi's advantage in providing semantically meaningful direction.
- **Impact of Query Budget:** All algorithms generally improved as the query budget (number of evaluations per prompt candidate) increased, confirming that more reliable scoring estimates lead to a more accurate and effective search sequence.
- **Beam Search Efficacy:** An ablation study confirmed the significant benefit of the beam search procedure. ProTeGi's beam search consistently outperformed single flat enumerate-then-select and greedy depth-first search strategies, particularly for the Jailbreak and Liar detection tasks, indicating its efficiency in navigating the prompt search space.
- **Bandit Algorithm Performance:** All bandit-style best arm identification algorithms (UCB, UCB-E, Successive Rejects, Successive Halving) performed better than a simple uniform selection baseline (spreading budget evenly). Surprisingly, UCB-style algorithms consistently outperformed successive rejects-style algorithms, contrary to theoretical expectations for best arm identification. The authors speculate this might be due to UCB's better practical balance of exploration and exploitation, especially with a sufficiently high exploration parameter.
- **Learning Curves and Overfitting:** Analysis of learning curves (performance over optimization steps) showed that performance often peaked early (around 3 steps) before potentially overfitting to the training data or getting stuck in local minima. This suggests that fewer, high-quality optimization steps might be more effective than extensive iterations.
- **Influence of Base LLM:** ProTeGi's effectiveness scales directly with the underlying LLM's capabilities. RLHF-tuned models (ChatGPT, GPT-4) dramatically outperformed GPT-3 and InstructGPT. GPT-4 achieved the best performance, especially on complex and novel tasks like Jailbreak detection, indicating ProTeGi effectively leverages the enhanced reasoning abilities of more advanced LLMs.
- **Qualitative Insights:** Qualitative examples highlighted that ProTeGi's textual gradients could successfully pinpoint subtle inconsistencies or biases in the initial prompt (e.g., for Ethos and Liar). The resulting ProTeGi-derived prompts exhibited greater syntactic and semantic variability compared to simple rephrasing (MC) or incoherent outputs (RL). However, the qualitative analysis also showed cases where gradients were less useful (e.g., too specific for Jailbreak) or where the LLM's editing process redefined a concept rather than fixing the original issue.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ProTeGi framework represents a significant step forward in the field of LLM operations and prompt engineering, offering several key areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Automation of a Manual Bottleneck:** ProTeGi directly addresses the labor-intensive and expert-dependent process of prompt engineering. By automating prompt optimization, it can drastically reduce the development time and cost associated with deploying LLM-powered applications, making LLM development more accessible to a broader range of practitioners.
- **Improved LLM Performance:** The demonstrated performance gains (up to 31% improvement over initial prompts, and significant leads over SOTA baselines) mean that ProTeGi can unlock greater accuracy and reliability from LLMs, allowing them to perform tasks more effectively. This is crucial for real-world applications where marginal performance improvements can have substantial impact.
- **Practicality for API-Driven Development:** A major strength of ProTeGi is its compatibility with black-box LLM APIs. This design choice makes it immediately applicable to the vast majority of LLM users and developers who interact with models like GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 through cloud services, without requiring access to internal model weights or gradients. This significantly lowers the barrier to entry for advanced prompt optimization.
- **Interpretable Optimization Process:** The use of "textual gradients" provides a human-interpretable audit trail of the optimization process. These natural language critiques of prompt flaws can offer invaluable insights for human developers, helping them understand *why* a prompt is failing and *how* it is being improved. This interpretability enhances trust and facilitates debugging and further refinement, distinguishing it from opaque "soft prompt" methods.
- **Generalizability Across Tasks:** The preliminary case study on diverse classification tasks (hate speech, fake news, sarcasm, and jailbreak detection) suggests ProTeGi's broad applicability. Its non-parametric nature implies that it can be adapted to various LLM tasks simply by defining an appropriate metric function and initial prompt.
- **Novel Algorithmic Contribution:** The concept of "textual gradient descent" – mirroring numerical optimization steps in a natural language dialogue – is a novel and innovative approach to discrete optimization problems. Combined with the efficient bandit-guided beam search, it provides a principled way to explore and optimize in discrete semantic spaces.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
Despite its promise, the paper acknowledges several limitations. The efficiency, while conceptually high, is constrained by LLM API rate limits and the number of calls required, potentially leading to long runtimes and high costs for large-scale applications. The evaluation was limited to four classification tasks, necessitating further testing across diverse problem types (e.g., generation, summarization). The observed early performance peaks suggest potential overfitting or local minima, indicating a need for future work on adaptive step sizes or more sophisticated stopping criteria.

In conclusion, ProTeGi offers a highly practical and conceptually elegant solution to a pressing challenge in LLM development. Its ability to automatically and interpretably improve prompts via black-box APIs, coupled with demonstrated performance gains, positions it as a valuable tool for anyone working with modern large language models.
