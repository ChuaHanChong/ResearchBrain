# Autonomous Obstacle Removal for Excavators through Policy Learning with Particle Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> An excavator obstacle-removal framework that learns in particle simulation and transfers to a real 12-ton machine. The policy maps RGB-D depth and obstacle-label maps to four excavation-trajectory parameters, trained under a burial-conditioned curriculum that raises burial depth and soil-particle count together. It exceeds 90% success within three days, while fixed-particle baselines stall near 20-30% after a full week.

## Problem

- Real-world excavator trial-and-error is too costly to collect the diverse state-dependent samples needed when each dig changes obstacle pose, terrain, and visibility.
- Analytical and continuum soil models are cheap but cannot resolve bucket-obstacle contact, soil-obstacle separation, local collapse, and progressive exposure of a rigid buried object.
- Particle simulators capture those discontinuities but cost scales with particle count, and prior curricula schedule task difficulty and simulator fidelity as separate, unlinked factors.

## Method

- ==Trajectory-level action interface==: the policy emits four parameters (position X, Y, depth D, drag length L) that a deterministic ==cubic-spline generator== expands into approach, penetration, drag, lift-out, and removal phases.
- Two-modal observation shared by simulation and hardware: a terrain depth map plus a dense obstacle-label segmentation map, produced on the real excavator by ==Faster R-CNN== with ==GrabCut== refinement.
- ==Burial-conditioned curriculum== of three stages, C1 particle-free surface obstacle, C2 minimal flat soil, C3 clustered deep burial, advancing on a success-rate threshold, trained with ==REDQ== and ==domain randomization==.

## Results

- The framework exceeded **90%** success in particle simulation, whereas Only-C3, curriculum-domain-randomization, and waypoint-trajectory baselines saturated near or below **30%** success.
- C3 costs roughly **20** times the per-step compute of C1; the curriculum reached peak performance in about **50%** of the wall-clock time of direct C3 training.
- Sim-to-sim and sim-to-real transfer succeeded in **14/15** trials versus **2/15** without main domain randomization; ablations show fixed-XY actions lose about **40%** and depth-only observations fall below **10%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Choose a curriculum variable physically coupled to both task difficulty and simulation cost, so one schedule buys learnability and wall-clock savings at once.
> - Parameterized trajectory actions ease exploration versus raw waypoint prediction, and spatially dense object masks beat centroid summaries or terrain geometry alone.
> - Feasibility and efficiency are separate milestones: waypoint-dense conservative motion buys reliability while roughly doubling task completion time relative to skilled human operators.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous earthwork tasks, such as excavation and material handling, are active areas of research, with particular challenges in tasks requiring adaptation to changing ground conditions or specific object interactions. Obstacle removal from the ground, which is frequently encountered in operations like excavating around buried pipelines or clearing construction sites, represents a complex variant of general excavation. This task demands state-dependent actions and sequential decision-making due to the repeated interaction with soil and the obstacle.

Previous research in learning excavation behaviors has explored several approaches:
- **Real-world learning:** Methods that directly learn or adapt skills on physical excavators are often costly and limited to narrow settings, such as fixed digging motions or specific force-related controllers. Applying these to state-dependent tasks like obstacle removal, which require diverse samples of changing obstacle and soil conditions, is generally impractical due to the expense and time involved.
- **Simulation-based learning with analytical or continuum models:** Many studies employ analytical, geometric, or continuum-based models for efficient simulation in bulk soil excavation and loading tasks. These models approximate soil behavior and excavation forces, making them computationally efficient. However, they typically struggle to represent discontinuous local phenomena critical for obstacle removal, such as intermittent bucket-obstacle contact, soil-obstacle separation, local collapse, and the progressive exposure of a rigid object. These fine-grained interactions are difficult to capture with simplified approximations.
- **Simulation-based learning with particle models:** Particle simulations represent soil as discrete particles and explicitly compute their interactions with rigid bodies and robots. This approach offers the expressiveness needed to model complex local motions, contacts, and friction, making it suitable for tasks like obstacle removal where precise interaction dynamics are crucial. However, particle simulations are computationally intensive. The computational burden increases significantly with the number of particles, which in turn is often necessary to accurately represent deeper or more complex burial conditions.

This research positions itself within the particle-simulation-based learning paradigm for excavation. It acknowledges the expressiveness of particle simulation as necessary for obstacle removal but directly addresses the computational cost challenge that has historically limited its practical application for complex, state-dependent tasks. The paper differentiates itself by proposing a method that not only leverages particle simulation for detailed interaction modeling but also introduces a strategy to manage its computational expense. Unlike prior work focused on general sample reduction or simulator acceleration techniques, this study integrates a curriculum learning approach where the training difficulty and simulation fidelity (via particle count) are jointly controlled by a physically meaningful variable: the obstacle's burial condition. This approach aims to make policy learning in high-fidelity particle simulations more time-efficient and practical for real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a policy learning framework that enables autonomous excavators to effectively remove obstacles from the ground. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and specific aims:

- **Addressing the complexity of obstacle removal:** Obstacle removal is a common but challenging earthwork task. Unlike bulk excavation, it requires the excavator to adapt its actions repeatedly based on the evolving state of the soil and obstacle. Excavating only the obstacle is often impossible in a single pass; instead, a sequence of actions is needed to gradually clear surrounding soil before the obstacle can be removed. This sequential, state-dependent nature makes it a complex control problem that is difficult to automate with conventional methods.
- **Need for detailed physical interaction modeling:** Successful obstacle removal involves discontinuous local phenomena such as bucket-obstacle contact, soil-obstacle separation, local terrain collapse, and the progressive exposure of a rigid object. Traditional analytical or continuum soil models used in many excavation simulations struggle to accurately represent these interactions. This necessitates the use of more expressive models, such as particle-based simulations, which can explicitly compute interactions among soil particles, the robot, and obstacles.
- **Mitigating the high computational cost of particle simulation:** While particle simulation provides the required fidelity, it is computationally expensive. The cost increases with the number of particles needed for accuracy, particularly for deeper or less visible obstacles that require more intricate soil-obstacle interactions to be reproduced. This high computational cost can render reinforcement learning impractical, as it requires numerous interaction samples.
- **Exploiting the relationship between task difficulty and simulation cost:** The authors observe that the burial condition of an obstacle (e.g., on-surface, partially buried, deeply buried) directly correlates with both the task's difficulty and the computational resources required for accurate particle simulation. Shallow burial conditions are easier to remove and require fewer particles for accurate modeling, thus being cheaper to simulate. Conversely, deep burial conditions are more difficult and necessitate more particles, increasing simulation cost. This coupled relationship motivates the development of a strategy that can simultaneously control task difficulty and simulation cost.
- **Developing a time-efficient learning approach:** Based on the aforementioned observation, the central hypothesis is that a "burial-conditioned curriculum learning" strategy can provide a more time-efficient method for particle-simulation-based policy learning. By progressively moving from easier, low-cost (shallow burial, fewer particles) to more difficult, high-cost (deep burial, more particles) stages, the learning process can accelerate the acquisition of skills while managing computational resources.
- **Ensuring real-world applicability:** The framework is designed for direct sim-to-real transfer. This requires a shared observation-action interface that can function in both the simulation environment and on a real excavator, ensuring that the learned policy is immediately deployable without extensive re-tuning.

In summary, the motivation is to automate a challenging earthwork task by leveraging the fidelity of particle simulation, while simultaneously addressing its computational drawbacks through an intelligent curriculum design tied to a physical characteristic of the task itself, and ensuring the practicality of the learned policy for real-world application.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates policy learning in a particle-based simulation with real-world policy execution through a shared observation-action interface. It is designed to handle sequential decision-making in obstacle removal, facilitate sim-to-real transfer, and ensure computational efficiency during training.

**Policy Execution Process:**
The autonomous obstacle removal operates in an iterative perception-action loop. At each step, the excavator observes the task field, generates a parameterized excavation trajectory, executes it, lifts the bucket, and discharges its contents (either to an obstacle-placement point or a soil-placement point). This cycle repeats until the obstacle is removed.

**Learnable Policy Interface:**
The framework defines a compact, shared observation-action interface.
- **Action Design:** Each action is a four-dimensional vector `a = [X, Y, D, L]⊤`. `(X, Y)` specifies the excavation position relative to the excavator's base, `D` is the excavation depth, and `L` is the drag distance. These parameters are converted by a deterministic trajectory generator into a full bucket motion sequence consisting of approach, penetration, dragging, lift-out, and removal phases. This low-dimensional representation aims to keep the exploration space small for efficient learning.
- **Observation Design:** The observation `s_t = [I_depth, I_label]` comprises two modalities: a depth map (`I_depth`) providing terrain geometry and a segmentation map (`I_label`) indicating the obstacle's location and extent. This two-modal input ensures that the policy receives both geometric and semantic information necessary for state-dependent excavation.

**Real-World Excavator System:**
The system comprises a Hitachi ZX120 hydraulic excavator equipped with an Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera and joint-mounted absolute encoders.
- **Observation Construction:** The RGB-D camera captures point cloud data, which is transformed to the global robot frame, filtered, and projected onto a 2D grid to create the `I_depth` map (max height value per cell). Missing depth data are filled via scattered-data interpolation. Obstacle detection utilizes a supervised perception pipeline: RGB images are preprocessed, Faster R-CNN detects object proposals, and GrabCut refines these regions to generate obstacle masks. The point cloud is then filtered by these masks to produce the binary `I_label` map. Both maps are downsampled to match the policy input resolution.
- **Low-Level Action Execution:** The policy's `a = [X, Y, D, L]⊤` command is fed to the deterministic trajectory generator, which outputs intermediate waypoints. These waypoints are converted to joint targets using inverse kinematics and then executed by onboard PID-based joint controllers. A timeout mechanism manages execution variability, ensuring progression to the next waypoint even if targets are not precisely met.

**Time-Efficient Learning in Particle Simulation:**
A particle simulation environment is developed to mimic the real excavator system under the shared interface.
- **Simulation Environment:** It models an excavator (swing, boom, arm, bucket joints), particle-based soil, and rigid obstacles. A virtual camera in Isaac Gym generates `I_depth` and `I_label`. Terrain is a hybrid representation of a global height field and confined particles in the task field, allowing detailed local soil-obstacle interactions while controlling computational cost.
- **Burial-Conditioned Curriculum:** This is a core methodological innovation. It organizes policy learning into three stages, `C1`, `C2`, and `C3`, based on obstacle burial depth, which simultaneously influences task difficulty and required particle count (simulation cost).
 - `C1` (On-Surface): The obstacle rests on a flat surface with no soil particles. Bucket-ground contact is disabled. This is an easy, computationally inexpensive stage.
 - `C2` (Partially Buried): A minimal set of soil particles forms a flat terrain with the obstacle on top. This introduces basic soil-obstacle interaction.
 - `C3` (Deeply Buried): Multiple soil clusters create diverse and complex burial conditions, often partially or deeply burying obstacles. This is the most difficult and computationally expensive stage, requiring more particles for fidelity.
 Training progresses to the next stage when the current policy achieves a predefined target success rate. This curriculum allows the policy to learn coarse behaviors efficiently before refining them in more complex, high-fidelity environments.
- **Policy Learning and Domain Randomization:** The policy is trained using a standard reinforcement learning (RL) framework, specifically Randomized Ensembled Double Q-Learning (REDQ).
 - **MDP Formulation:** The task is modeled as an MDP with `S` (observation space), `A` (action space), `p` (transition dynamics), `r` (reward function), and `γ` (discount factor). The policy `π(a_t | s_t)` maximizes expected discounted return.
 - **Reward Function:** `r(s_t, a_t) = ω_success * r_success_t + ω_distance * r_distance_t`. `r_success_t` provides a sparse reward (0 for success, -1 otherwise). `r_distance_t` offers dense reward shaping, penalizing actions that increase the distance between the bucket's reference point and the obstacle's center, encouraging obstacle manipulation.
 - **Domain Randomization (DR):** Applied at the beginning of each episode to bridge the sim-to-real gap. Randomized factors include soil and terrain properties (size, mass, friction of particles, number/position/height of soil clusters), camera parameters (pose, depth noise/bias), and obstacle properties (friction, mass, initial pose, and diverse geometries generated by connecting cuboid primitives).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the proposed framework across policy learnability, training-time efficiency, and sim-to-real transfer, with comparisons to baselines and human experts.

**Policy Learnability Compared with Existing Methods:**
- The proposed framework ("Ours") achieved success rates exceeding 90% in simulation, demonstrating its ability to learn an effective obstacle-removal policy.
- Baseline methods, including direct training in the final curriculum stage ("Only C3"), training with curriculum-based domain randomization ("Only C3 (CDR)"), or training in simplified stages ("Only C1", "Only C2"), achieved success rates of approximately 30% or less. This indicates that neither direct high-fidelity training nor simple domain randomization expansion alone was sufficient.
- A waypoint-based direct trajectory representation ("Only C3 (Trj.)") learned particularly slowly, suggesting that the proposed compact trajectory parameterization is more effective than directly learning high-dimensional waypoint sequences.

**Validation of Action and Observation Designs:**
- **Action Design Ablation:** Fixing any of the policy's predicted action parameters (`X, Y, D, L`) to values optimized by Bayesian optimization consistently degraded performance. Fixing the excavation position (`XY`) resulted in a significant ~40% drop in success rate, while fixing `L` or `D` led to >10% drops. This indicates the importance of the policy's ability to generate state-dependent trajectory-level actions.
- **Observation Design Ablation:** Using only depth information ("Depth-only") resulted in very low success rates (<10%). Using only obstacle-label information ("Label-only") performed better but was still ~20% below "Ours." Replacing the full label map with just obstacle centroid coordinates further reduced performance by ~20%. These results affirm the benefit of combining both terrain depth and spatially dense obstacle-label observations for robust policy performance.

**Training-Time Efficiency of Burial-Conditioned Curriculum:**
- A direct relationship was observed between particle count and simulation time: the calculation time per simulation step in `C3` (high particle count) was approximately 20 times that in `C1` (no particles).
- The burial-conditioned curriculum in "Ours" enabled the policy to reach peak performance in approximately 50% of the wall-clock training time compared to training directly in `C3` without the curriculum. This demonstrated that progressive particle scaling effectively reduces the computational burden during early learning stages, improving overall training efficiency.

**Policy Transferability from Training Simulator to Evaluation Simulator and Real-World Environment:**
- The policy trained with the proposed framework ("Ours") successfully transferred to both OperaSim (an independent evaluation simulator with more accurate soil modeling) and a real 12-ton excavator. It achieved success rates above 90% across various steel obstacles (I-shaped, L-shaped, logs, rocks).
- Baselines with limited domain randomization ("w/o main DR") or a centroid-based "Naive Controller" performed poorly (<20% success rates) in transfer experiments. Qualitative analysis revealed that "Ours" progressively excavated around obstacles to facilitate removal, while the "Naive Controller" often pushed obstacles out of the task region, and "w/o main DR" exhibited repetitive, ineffective excavation patterns due to insufficient state-action diversity during training.

**Comparison with Human Experts:**
- While "Ours" successfully performed autonomous obstacle removal in the real world, its efficiency lagged behind human experts. Human operators completed the task in an average of ~100 seconds, whereas the learned policy required ~230 seconds.
- The primary contributors to this time difference were identified as the "Dig" and "Throw" phases. The learned policy's "Dig" phase took approximately 40 seconds per excavation (longer than human experts), and its "Throw" phase took ~14 seconds (about twice as long as humans).
- Human experts tended to perform a greater number of shorter excavation actions. The learned policy's longer "Dig" duration was attributed to cautious bucket motion with many intermediate waypoints, designed to prevent obstacle drops and prioritize stability. The longer "Throw" duration was due to disposing of soil outside the task field to avoid re-deposition, enhancing reliability at the cost of speed. This comparison clarified that the current limitation is primarily execution efficiency rather than task feasibility.

**Application to Practical Excavation Scenarios:**
- The learned policy was integrated into a trench excavation task, demonstrating its capability to handle buried obstacles within a larger construction plan.
- Successful obstacle removal was also demonstrated under challenging rainy outdoor conditions, showcasing the robustness of the transferred policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous construction robotics and robotic manipulation of granular materials. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancing Autonomous Earthwork Automation:** The framework addresses a complex and frequent earthwork task – obstacle removal – that has been difficult to automate due to its state-dependent and sequential nature. By successfully learning and deploying a policy for this task, the work extends the capabilities of autonomous excavators beyond conventional bulk excavation, moving towards more adaptive and intelligent machine operations.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Complex Interactions:** The study demonstrates effective sim-to-real transfer for a task involving intricate physical interactions between a robot, granular soil, and rigid obstacles. The use of a shared observation-action interface and comprehensive domain randomization contributes to the robustness of this transfer, a critical aspect for practical robotic applications.
- **Improving Efficiency in Particle Simulation-based Learning:** The introduction of a burial-conditioned curriculum is a methodological advancement for reinforcement learning in high-fidelity particle simulations. By explicitly linking task difficulty and simulation cost through a physical variable (obstacle burial depth), the framework significantly reduces wall-clock training time. This strategy makes computationally intensive particle simulations more feasible for training complex robotic policies, potentially broadening their applicability to other domains involving granular media or deformable objects.
- **Providing a Foundation for Future Developments:** The research identifies specific areas for future improvement, such as optimizing execution speed, handling deformable or breaking obstacles, operating on sloped terrain, integrating mobility, and developing more generalized perception systems. These identified directions provide a clear roadmap for further research that could lead to more versatile and efficient autonomous excavation systems.
- **Potential for Increased Safety and Productivity:** Autonomous obstacle removal capabilities can enhance safety on construction sites by reducing human exposure to hazardous environments. It can also improve productivity and consistency of operations, especially in repetitive or difficult conditions, contributing to the broader goal of fully autonomous construction.
- **Contribution to Robotics with Granular Materials:** The detailed approach to simulating and learning interactions with granular materials offers insights for other robotic applications in agriculture, mining, or disaster response where manipulation in complex soil-like environments is required.

While the current framework is primarily demonstrated with rigid obstacles on flat terrain and with a specific perception pipeline, its success in addressing the learnability and computational challenges of particle simulation for a complex real-world task lays a substantial groundwork for the development of more advanced, adaptable, and efficient autonomous construction machinery.
