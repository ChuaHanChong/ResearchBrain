# What Do Latent Action Models Actually Learn?

> [!summary] Summary
> Latent action models compress frame-to-frame changes into latents hoped to encode control actions. This work introduces linear LAM, an analytically tractable linear inverse/forward dynamics pair trained by reconstruction, and proves it reduces to PCA over the mixture of controllable changes and exogenous noise. Latents therefore track whatever has the largest variance, and deterministic data policies, data augmentation, and 1% action supervision provably shift what is captured.

## Problem

- Prior latent action models are justified only intuitively and empirically, with no rigorous account of when latents encode true control signals rather than exogenous noise.
- Existing work disputes basic design choices, such as whether an information bottleneck is needed and whether discrete latents beat continuous ones, without analysis to settle them.
- How observation, action, and noise structure interacts with latent action learning, and how data-collection policy randomness affects it, was previously undocumented.

## Method

- **Linear LAM**: swaps the deep ==inverse dynamics== and ==forward dynamics== nets for linear maps z = Co + Do', o' = Ao + Bz, keeping the ==reconstruction loss== and low-dimensional bottleneck of practical models.
- **Linear LAM Objective**, a tractable ==mutual-information proxy== that fits linear probes from the latent to controllable change, exogenous noise, and observation, scoring an ideal latent at 2.
- Two remedies analyzed inside the same framework: shared random-crop-style ==additive data augmentation== applied separately to inverse and forward inputs, and an ==auxiliary linear action-prediction head== on the latent.

## Results

- Simulations confirm the theory: with no noise the latent is optimal once bottleneck dimension reaches action dimension, and it stays near-optimal for i.i.d. noise up to sigma **0.5**, half the signal scale.
- In a nonlinear VQ grid-world LAM, controllable loss rises **0.624** to **1.046** as noise goes none to high, and **0.781** to **1.997** when switching from a uniform to a **95%** deterministic policy.
- Augmentation cuts controllable loss **0.781** to **0.415** and raises stochastic loss **0.739** to **0.898**; **1%** action labels reach **0.295** and **0.986**, matching the proofs that both suppress noise.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unsupervised latent action learning is variance-driven, not causality-driven: any distractor louder than the action will dominate the latent, so denoise or stabilize data first.
> - Highly deterministic expert demonstrations are bad training data here, because the forward model absorbs the predictable state-to-action mapping and frees latent capacity for noise.
> - Augmentations that encode a designer's invariance prior, plus a sliver of action labels, are cheap and provably effective levers on latent semantics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental question within the rapidly evolving field of embodied AI and unsupervised representation learning: the actual semantic content learned by Latent Action Models (LAMs). LAMs are designed to infer controllable action changes from unlabeled video streams, a particularly valuable capability given the high cost of action-labeled data versus the abundance of unlabeled videos. This approach offers a promising route for embodied AI systems, such as robots, to acquire skills by pre-training policies using inferred latent actions, which can then be mapped to real control signals with minimal labeled data. LAMs have already shown success in diverse applications, including 2D video games, robotics, and analyzing broadcast footage.

The broader research landscape for action representation learning has historically included methods that rely on real action labels, such as PG-RA, LASER, TAP, and EAR. More recent advancements, like AD3, also extract latent actions but condition on real actions. In contrast, LAMs distinguish themselves by operating in a purely unsupervised setting during the initial learning phase, leveraging internet-scale video datasets that lack explicit action annotations. This capability aligns with the trend towards large-scale foundation models for embodied AI, where pre-training on vast, unlabeled datasets is key.

The specific "modern LAM template" (e.g., LAPO, Genie, LAPA, Moto, IGOR, Go-1) typically decomposes the learning process into an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) and a Forward Dynamics Model (FDM), trained via a reconstruction loss with an information bottleneck. While these models have demonstrated empirical success, a critical theoretical gap has persisted: an understanding of *what* these models actually learn. There has been an intuition, and some concern, that LAMs might simply compress *any* difference between consecutive frames, including exogenous noise or environmental stochasticity, rather than isolating changes specifically caused by controllable actions. This lack of rigorous investigation into LAMs' objective, learnability, and robustness, particularly how observations, actions, and noise structures influence learning, is precisely the gap this paper aims to fill. By providing a tractable theoretical framework, the authors seek to move beyond empirical motivations and offer precise insights into the mechanisms governing LAM learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to rigorously investigate and analytically characterize what Latent Action Models (LAMs) truly learn from unlabeled video streams. The authors are motivated by a critical, yet underexplored, concern in the LAM community: whether the inferred "latent actions" genuinely capture controllable changes induced by an agent's actions, or if they merely encode arbitrary differences between video frames, including irrelevant noise or environmental factors.

Specifically, the paper aims to answer several key questions:
1. **Distinguishing Action from Noise:** Do LAMs' latents effectively differentiate between changes caused by controllable actions and those arising from exogenous noise?
2. **Scope of Learning:** Are LAMs only successful in domains where frame changes can be almost entirely attributed to controllable actions, or can they handle more complex, noisy environments?
3. **Impact of Data Properties:** How do properties of the data, such as the correlation between observations and actions or the nature of noise, influence LAM learning?
4. **Effectiveness of Architectural Choices and Training Strategies:** What is the theoretical justification for common LAM architectural elements (like bottlenecks) and practical training strategies (like data augmentation and auxiliary action prediction)?

To address these questions, the authors propose a novel analytical approach using a simplified, linear version of LAM, which maintains the core architectural elements and learning challenge of practical LAMs while being mathematically tractable. This analytical framework allows them to derive precise insights that would be difficult or impossible to obtain from complex deep neural network models.

The paper outlines six key contributions that directly support these objectives:
1. **Introduction of Linear LAM:** Presenting a simplified, yet representative, linear model for analytical study.
2. **Connection to PCA:** Demonstrating that, under certain conditions, linear LAM reduces to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on a mixture of controllable changes and exogenous noise, providing a fundamental understanding of its learning mechanism.
3. **Analysis of Data-Generating Policy:** Investigating how the correlation between observations and actions, induced by the data-generating policy, affects LAM's ability to focus on controllable changes.
4. **Justification of Data Augmentation:** Proving that data augmentation can mitigate over-parametrization issues and improve the semantic quality of the learned latents.
5. **Validation of Auxiliary Action Prediction:** Showing that incorporating an auxiliary action-prediction head encourages LAMs to prioritize learning controllable changes over noise.
6. **Empirical Verification:** Validating the theoretical findings from the linear model through experiments in more realistic (non-linear, image-based) settings.

In essence, the motivation is to elevate LAM research from a largely empirical and intuitive domain to one grounded in rigorous theoretical understanding, thereby guiding future design choices and application strategies for embodied AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this research hinges on developing and analyzing a simplified "linear Latent Action Model" (linear LAM) that captures the essence of practical LAMs while remaining analytically tractable. This approach allows for a rigorous theoretical investigation into the factors influencing LAM learning, complemented by numerical simulations and experiments in more realistic, albeit still controlled, non-linear settings.

**Linear LAM Setup:**
1. **Problem Setting:** The authors model the environment as a Controlled Markov Process (CMP). Observations ($o \in \mathbb{R}^{d_o}$) and actions ($a \in \mathbb{R}^{d_a}$) are represented as vectors, with action dimensionality significantly lower than observation dimensionality ($d_a \ll d_o$).
2. **Dynamics:** The transition function is simplified to an additive model: $o' = o + q + \epsilon$, where $o'$ is the next observation, $o$ is the current observation, $q = Xa$ represents the controllable change induced by action $a$ (via an action effect matrix $X$), and $\epsilon$ represents exogenous noise or environmental stochasticity. This additive structure is crucial for tractability.
3. **Model Architecture:** The IDM and FDM are composed of linear mappings:
 - **IDM:** $z = Co + Do'$, where $C, D \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_o}$ are learnable matrices, and $z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ is the latent representation, acting as a bottleneck ($d_z \ll d_o$).
 - **FDM:** $\hat{o}' = Ao + Bz$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d_o \times d_o}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_o \times d_z}$ are learnable matrices.
4. **Training Objective:** The linear LAM is trained using a standard reconstruction loss: $L = \min_{A,B,C,D} \mathbb{E} \| \hat{o}' - o' \|_2^2$.
5. **Evaluation Metric (LLO):** To quantitatively assess the quality of the learned latents, the authors define a "Linear LAM Objective" (LLO). This objective is formulated as $I(z; a) - I(z; \epsilon) - I(z; o)$, approximated by predicting $q, \epsilon, o$ from the learned latent $z$ using separate linear layers and calculating normalized Mean Squared Errors (MSEs). The LLO is maximized when $z$ perfectly predicts $q$ while containing no information about $\epsilon$ and $o$.

**Analytical Approach:**
The paper performs algebraic manipulation of the linear LAM's reconstruction loss, leading to several key analytical insights:
- **PCA Connection:** By setting the partial derivative of the loss with respect to $A$ to zero, and assuming $E[o(q+\epsilon)^T]=0$ (i.e., observation is uncorrelated with the changes), the loss objective is shown to be equivalent to performing PCA on the combined controllable changes and exogenous noise ($q+\epsilon$). This reveals that the latent $z$ (interpreted by $B$) captures the principal components of $q+\epsilon$.
- **Noise Analysis:** Different types of noise are analyzed:
 - **No Noise ($\epsilon=0$):** Shows perfect action recovery if $d_z \ge d_a$.
 - **I.I.D. Noise:** Investigates robustness to unstructured noise.
 - **Structured Noise (Other Agents):** Analyzes scenarios where noise itself is structured and can dominate variance.
- **Data Collection Policy:** The assumption $E[o(q+\epsilon)^T]=0$ is relaxed to study the impact of correlated data-generating policies. This reveals how deterministic policies can cause the FDM to absorb changes, leaving the latent to capture noise.
- **Data Augmentation:** The linear LAM is extended to incorporate data augmentation (additive noise $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ applied to IDM and FDM inputs). Analysis shows this forces $A=I$ and $C+D=0$, effectively removing observation information from the latent ($z \propto D(q+\epsilon)$).
- **Auxiliary Action Prediction:** An auxiliary loss term ($\lambda \| \hat{a} - a \|_2^2$) is added for a small subset of labeled data. Analysis demonstrates that this term biases the encoder to be orthogonal to the noise, encouraging the latent to focus on controllable actions.

**Numerical Simulations:**
To complement the analytical work, numerical simulations are conducted using PyTorch with a standard normal distribution for observations and actions. Various noise types (i.i.d. Gaussian, structured noise from "other agents"), policy randomness levels, and hyperparameter settings ($d_o, d_a, d_z, \sigma_\epsilon$) are explored. The LLO metric is used to quantitatively evaluate performance under different conditions.

**Beyond Linear LAM Experiments:**
To bridge the gap between the simplified linear model and real-world LAMs, empirical experiments are performed on a carefully designed synthetic 4x4 grid-world dataset. This setup features:
- **Image Inputs:** Small 4x4 images instead of vectors.
- **Non-linear Components:** CNNs for IDM and FDM, a Vector Quantization (VQ) bottleneck for discrete latents.
- **Separable Dynamics:** The observation is structured such that controllable changes occur in the top 3x4 grid, while exogenous noise appears in the bottom 1x4 grid, allowing for separate measurement of "controllable loss" and "stochastic loss."
- **Augmentation and Action Prediction:** Simple image shifting for augmentation and pre-assigned codes for action prediction are used.
These experiments serve to verify whether the insights derived from the linear model hold true in more realistic, non-linear, and image-based contexts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several key findings from both the theoretical analysis of linear LAM and the empirical validation on a more realistic setup:

#### 4.1. Linear LAM's Connection to PCA and Noise Sensitivity

- **PCA Equivalence:** Under the assumption that observations are uncorrelated with the changes ($E[o(q+\epsilon)^T]=0$), training linear LAM is analytically shown to be equivalent to performing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the combined signal of controllable changes ($q$) and exogenous noise ($\epsilon$). The latent $z$ (interpreted by the FDM parameter $B$) primarily captures the $d_z$ principal components with the largest variances within $q+\epsilon$.
- **Noiseless Scenario ($\epsilon=0$):** In the absence of noise, if the latent dimension $d_z$ is greater than or equal to the action dimension $d_a$, linear LAM perfectly captures all information about the true controllable action $a$. This results in an optimal Linear LAM Objective (LLO) score.
- **I.I.D. Noise Robustness:** The model demonstrates robustness to i.i.d. (isotropic Gaussian) noise up to a certain magnitude (e.g., noise intensity half that of the signal). The FDM parameters remain largely unaffected, and the latent continues to encode controllable changes effectively.
- **Structured Noise Challenge:** When the exogenous noise is structured (e.g., from other agents) and its variance is comparable to or greater than that of the controllable signal, LAM tends to prioritize learning the larger variance components. If noise variance dominates, LAM's latent will primarily capture noise rather than actions, leading to degraded performance. This highlights the need for noise reduction preprocessing in practice.
- **Over-parametrization:** The analysis reveals that linear LAM is inherently over-parameterized; the latent $z$ can inadvertently encode information about the observation $o$, in addition to $q$ and $\epsilon$. This can be detrimental to the semantic purity of the latent.

#### 4.2. Impact of Data Collection Policy

- When observations are correlated with actions (e.g., data from a deterministic expert policy, breaking the $E[o(q+\epsilon)^T]=0$ assumption), the FDM parameter $A$ can learn to directly map $o$ to $o'$ for deterministic transitions. This reduces the need for the latent $z$ to encode action information, leaving its capacity to absorb exogenous noise instead.
- **Finding:** More deterministic data collection policies (higher correlation between $o$ and $a$) lead to a significant decrease in the action information captured by $z$, resulting in poorer LLO scores. This suggests that data-generating policies with higher randomness are preferred for training high-quality LAMs.

#### 4.3. Benefits of Data Augmentation

- **Over-parametrization Mitigation:** Implementing data augmentation by applying distinct random transformations ($\kappa_1$ for IDM inputs, $\kappa_2$ for FDM input and reconstruction target) analytically forces the FDM parameter $A=I$ and the IDM parameters $C=-D$. This crucial condition ensures that the latent $z$ becomes independent of the observation $o$ (i.e., $z = D(q+\epsilon)$), effectively addressing the over-parametrization issue and improving the semantic meaning of the latent.
- **Improved Latent Semantics:** Simulations confirm that data augmentation, even with modest variance, significantly improves the LLO, bringing it closer to the ideal. This provides theoretical justification for the common practice of designing augmentations that encode desired invariances (e.g., action invariance to position).

#### 4.4. Efficacy of Auxiliary Action Prediction

- **Noise Suppression:** By adding an auxiliary loss head that predicts real action labels from the latent $z$ (even with a small fraction of labeled data), the encoder parameter $D$ is biased to be perpendicular to the noise. In an artificial extreme case (infinite weight for auxiliary loss and $d_z \ge d_a$), the latent can be perfectly denoised, with $z$ solely representing $q$.
- **Enhanced Action Focus:** Simulations demonstrate that even a small proportion (e.g., 1%) of action labels in an auxiliary prediction task dramatically improves LAM's ability to learn controllable actions and suppress noise, especially in scenarios where noise previously caused failure. This validates the use of semi-supervised learning in LAMs.

#### 4.5. Beyond Linear LAM Experimental Validation

- Experiments on a non-linear, image-based LAM with a VQ bottleneck and a synthetic grid-world environment consistently corroborate the theoretical findings:
 - **Noise Impact:** Increased noise leads to higher "controllable loss" (less action info) and lower "stochastic loss" (more noise info), mirroring the linear model's noise sensitivity.
 - **Policy Correlation:** A correlated (deterministic) policy significantly degrades performance, aligning with the negative impact of deterministic policies.
 - **Data Augmentation:** Applying data augmentation demonstrably improves action information capture and noise suppression.
 - **Action Prediction:** Incorporating a small amount of action supervision drastically enhances the latent's focus on controllable actions, confirming its denoising effect.

In summary, the research establishes that LAMs, when ideally configured, act as PCA on state changes, but are vulnerable to noise and deterministic data policies. Critically, it provides strong theoretical and empirical evidence that practical strategies like data augmentation and auxiliary action prediction are effective mechanisms to make LAMs learn meaningful, controllable actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes a highly significant contribution to the field of embodied AI and unsupervised representation learning by providing the first rigorous theoretical analysis of Latent Action Models (LAMs). Moving beyond intuitive and empirical motivations, the authors offer a deep, mechanistic understanding of what LAMs learn, under what conditions they succeed, and how their performance can be improved. This theoretical foundation has profound implications for both future research and practical applications.

**1. Bridging the Theory-Practice Gap:**
The most immediate impact is the intellectual leap from empirical observation to analytical understanding. By demonstrating that linear LAMs reduce to PCA under certain assumptions, the paper provides a foundational insight into the intrinsic learning mechanism of LAMs. This clarifies why LAMs might succeed in low-noise, action-dominant environments but struggle in highly stochastic ones. This analytical lens provides a framework for diagnosing and predicting LAM behavior, a capability largely absent until now.

**2. Guiding Data Collection Strategies:**
The finding that deterministic data collection policies can hinder LAM's ability to learn controllable actions is particularly impactful. Many robotic datasets are collected via expert teleoperation, which tends to be deterministic. This work suggests that for optimal LAM pre-training, data collection should prioritize randomness and diversity in actions, allowing the model to disentangle action-induced changes from environment-specific correlations. This will inform how large-scale datasets for embodied AI (e.g., robotics demonstrations, human egocentric videos) are curated and designed.

**3. Justifying and Improving Model Design:**
The analytical justification for data augmentation and auxiliary action prediction is invaluable. These techniques have been adopted empirically in recent LAM literature, but their precise mechanisms and benefits were not fully understood. This paper shows that data augmentation effectively mitigates over-parametrization, ensuring the latent specifically captures *changes* rather than information about the observation itself. Similarly, auxiliary action prediction is shown to act as a powerful "denoising" mechanism, biasing the latent to prioritize controllable actions over exogenous noise, even with minimal labeled data. These findings provide clear theoretical guidance for designing more robust and semantically meaningful LAM architectures and training protocols.

**4. Advancing Robustness in Real-World Embodied AI:**
Embodied AI systems operate in inherently noisy and complex real-world environments. This research highlights the challenges posed by structured exogenous noise and provides strategies to mitigate them. Understanding that LAMs prioritize larger variance components means that preprocessing steps to reduce significant noise (e.g., camera shake stabilization) are not merely practical heuristics but theoretically grounded necessities. The work fosters the development of more robust LAMs that can effectively operate in uncontrolled settings by offering concrete methods to ensure latents encode desired action semantics.

**5. Future Research Directions:**
This work opens several avenues for future research. It provides a blueprint for analyzing other aspects of LAMs, such as the impact of different bottleneck types (e.g., continuous vs. discrete/VQ), and extending the linear model to non-additive dynamics or more complex interaction patterns. Furthermore, the insights can inform the development of novel LAM objectives that explicitly encode preferences for controllable, high-variance changes over noise, potentially leading to more targeted and efficient unsupervised action learning algorithms.

In conclusion, "What Do Latent Action Models Actually Learn?" is a foundational paper that moves the understanding of LAMs from empirical intuition to rigorous scientific analysis. Its findings will significantly influence the design, training, and application of LAMs, accelerating progress towards generalist embodied AI agents capable of learning complex skills from abundant, unlabeled video data.
