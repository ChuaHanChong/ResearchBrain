# Action Advising with Advice Imitation in Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Action advising lets a student reinforcement learning agent query a competent teacher for actions under a strict budget. This work adds a separate behavioural cloning network, regularised with dropout for epistemic uncertainty, that memorises collected state-advice pairs and re-executes imitated advice during exploration when uncertainty falls below a threshold. Across three Atari games it raises learning speed and final scores over early advising alone.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning needs millions of environment interactions, which is impractical where real-world interaction carries cost, safety risk, or requires continual post-deployment learning.
- Existing deep action-advising methods only decide when to ask, discarding advice after collection, so a tiny budget cannot be stretched and near-identical states get queried redundantly.
- Reuse was only solved for tabular settings via look-up tables; deep agents rarely revisit an exact observation, and the one deep exception alters the student's learning loss.

## Method

- A ==behavioural cloning== module trains a separate state-conditional generative network on collected state-advice pairs with ==negative log-likelihood loss==, generalising teacher advice to unseen but similar states.
- ==Dropout regularisation== inside that network supplies ==epistemic uncertainty== from output variance over many forward passes; advice is reused only when uncertainty falls below a ==reuse threshold==.
- An ==episodic reuse probability== plus a restriction to exploratory steps keeps imitated advice out of the student's greedy policy, preserving replay-buffer diversity without touching the ==DQN== learning rule.

## Results

- With a **10k**-step budget over **3M** steps, the reuse variant beat early advising on final score in all three games, most sharply on Pong at **13.35** versus **6.66** and **-2.78** unadvised.
- Learning speed measured by area under the curve improved markedly: Enduro **782.98** versus **616.29** for early advising, Pong **-1.36** versus **-8.83**, Freeway **21.52** versus **20.31**.
- Imitation accuracy reached **84.74%** on Freeway, **68.32%** on Pong, **54.44%** on Enduro, with reuse covering **20-32%** of exploration steps and at most **2x** slowdown during exploration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A small set of expert actions generalises across visually similar states with high accuracy, so budgeted advice can be amortised far beyond its nominal query count.
> - Dropout variance is a cheap, practical membership test for whether a state resembles the training set, and its threshold trades reuse frequency against imitation fidelity predictably.
> - High advice utilisation did not hurt learning here, but heavy reuse risks collapsing replay diversity; confining imitation to exploratory steps is what keeps the policy correctable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated significant capabilities in complex sequential decision-making tasks, enabling the development of agents for challenging domains such as StarCraft II and DotA II without extensive feature engineering. However, a primary challenge in deep RL is its substantial sample inefficiency, often requiring millions of environment interactions to achieve high performance. This issue poses practical limitations in real-world applications where interaction costs, safety concerns, or the availability of parallel simulations are restricted.

To address sample inefficiency, various research avenues are being explored. One prominent area involves leveraging knowledge acquired from other entities, which can broadly be categorized into peer-to-peer knowledge transfer techniques. Within this category, two notable approaches are Learning from Demonstrations (LfD) and Action Advising.

Learning from Demonstrations (LfD) typically involves incorporating a pre-recorded dataset of expert behaviors into the learning process. This approach focuses on utilizing off-policy learning and additional loss terms to extract maximum information from demonstrated state-action pairs.

Action Advising, a less extensively studied but promising technique, involves interactive knowledge exchange with a competent peer (teacher) during the learning process, particularly when pre-recorded data is unavailable or costly to obtain. In this paradigm, a learning agent (student) receives advice in the form of actions from a teacher for a limited number of times, constrained by a budget. This budget reflects practical limitations in communication and attention. Existing action advising methods in deep RL primarily focus on determining *when* to request advice to maximize the student's learning performance, often referred to as the "when to ask for advice?" problem. However, these methods often overlook the further utilization of collected advice beyond its immediate execution. The collected advice is typically not systematically stored, replayed, or reused, leading to potential redundancy in advice requests for similar states and inefficient use of the limited advising budget.

This work specifically positions itself within the domain of student-initiated action advising in deep RL. It addresses a gap in current deep RL action advising methodologies: the lack of mechanisms for students to reuse previously acquired advice by imitating it. While advice reuse has been explored in tabular RL settings, its application in deep RL, where direct state memorization is not feasible due to continuous state spaces and generalization requirements, presents distinct challenges. The paper aims to bridge this gap by enabling students to generalize and reuse teacher advice, thereby enhancing sample efficiency and making more effective use of the advising budget. The closest related work in deep RL that considers advice utilization, such as, often interferes with the student's core learning mechanism (e.g., through custom loss functions), which this paper seeks to avoid by introducing a separate, algorithm-agnostic module.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a deep reinforcement learning agent (student) to imitate and reuse previously acquired advice from a competent peer (teacher), thereby improving sample efficiency and maximizing the utility of a limited advising budget.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in deep reinforcement learning and existing action advising paradigms:

- **Sample Inefficiency in Deep RL:** Deep RL algorithms often require an extensive number of interactions with the environment to learn effective policies. This is a significant bottleneck for real-world applications where environment interactions can be costly, time-consuming, or safety-critical. Leveraging external knowledge, such as advice from an expert, is a recognized strategy to mitigate this.
- **Budget Constraints in Action Advising:** Action advising operates under a finite budget, limiting the number of times a student can request advice from a teacher. Current deep RL advising methods typically consume this budget without recording or generalizing the advice, leading to redundant advice requests for similar states. This results in an underutilization of the valuable knowledge provided by the teacher and an inefficient allocation of the advising budget.
- **Lack of Advice Reuse Mechanisms in Deep RL:** While advice reuse has been successfully demonstrated in tabular RL, where state-advice pairs can be precisely memorized in lookup tables, it is not straightforward in deep RL due to the high-dimensional, continuous state spaces. Deep RL agents require mechanisms to generalize advice across similar, yet not identical, states. Existing deep RL action advising methods largely lack this capability.
- **Avoiding Interference with Student's Core Learning:** Some prior approaches that incorporate demonstrations or advice integrate them directly into the student's learning mechanism (e.g., via modified loss functions). A motivation for this work is to provide an external, algorithm-agnostic module for advice reuse that does not alter the student's primary deep RL algorithm, allowing for broader applicability.
- **Practical Relevance:** In practical settings, real-time communication and attention from an expert teacher are limited resources. Enabling a student to "remember" and "replicate" expert actions reduces the need for repeated queries, making the action advising framework more scalable and applicable to real-world scenarios.

To address these motivations, the study articulates specific contributions:
1. To demonstrate the feasibility and accuracy of generalizing teacher advice across similar states in deep RL.
2. To present an RL algorithm-agnostic approach for memorizing and imitating collected advice, suitable for deep RL settings.
3. To show that advice reuse through imitation can significantly enhance learning performance in deep RL, even when combined with a simple baseline advising heuristic like early advising.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, termed "Action Advising with Advice Imitation" (AR), integrates a behavioural cloning module with an existing student-initiated action advising strategy to enable the student to reuse previously acquired advice. The overall system operates within the standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework, where a student agent learns in an episodic single-agent environment to maximize cumulative discounted rewards. A competent teacher agent provides action advice for a limited budget.

The core components and mechanisms of the proposed methodology are as follows:

**A. Behavioural Cloning Module for Advice Imitation:**
To enable generalization of advice across similar states in deep RL, the student employs a separate neural network acting as a state-conditional generative model, denoted as $G_{\omega}: S \rightarrow A$. This network is designed to perform behavioral cloning.
1. **Data Collection:** As the student receives advice from the teacher (state $s_t$, advised action $a_t$), these state-action pairs are stored in a dataset buffer $D$.
2. **Supervised Learning:** Once the dataset $D$ reaches a predefined size ($n_D$), the behavioural cloning network $G_{\omega}$ is trained in a supervised fashion using mini-batch gradient descent. The objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood loss, $L(\omega) = \sum_{(s,a) \in D} -\log G_{\omega}(a | s)$, which encourages $G_{\omega}$ to output the advised action given a state. This process allows the network to learn a mapping from observed states to appropriate teacher actions.

**B. Epistemic Uncertainty Estimation with Dropout:**
A challenge with behavioral cloning is that the network might generate "advice" for states it has never encountered or was not trained on, leading to potentially incorrect or misleading actions. To address this, the proposed method incorporates Dropout regularization within the $G_{\omega}$ network.
1. **Dropout for Uncertainty:** By performing multiple forward passes (e.g., 100 in experiments) with Dropout enabled at inference time, the network yields different outputs due to the inherent stochasticity. The variance in these outputs is used as an approximation of the model's epistemic uncertainty, denoted as $G^{\mu}_{\omega}(s)$.
2. **State Familiarity Assessment:** States that $G_{\omega}$ was trained on (or are very similar to them) are expected to exhibit lower variance, indicating higher certainty. Conversely, unfamiliar states will likely produce higher variance, reflecting higher uncertainty. This mechanism allows the student to distinguish between states for which it has "recorded" advice and those it has not.

**C. Advice Reuse Mechanism:**
The student's action selection process (Algorithm 1) incorporates the advice imitation module with specific conditions for reusing advice:
1. **Initial Advice Collection:** The student first follows an early advising heuristic, greedily requesting advice from the teacher until the budget ($b$) runs out. These state-advice pairs populate the dataset $D$.
2. **Training $G_{\omega}$:** Once $D$ reaches size $n_D$, $G_{\omega}$ is trained for $k_{bc}$ iterations.
3. **Episodic Reuse Probability:** At the beginning of each episode, a random decision is made with probability $\epsilon_{reuse}$ to determine if advice reuse is permitted for that episode. This ensures a balance between following imitated advice and allowing the student to explore independently to maintain replay memory diversity.
4. **Conditions for Imitated Advice Execution:** If advice reuse is allowed for the current episode and the student has not already received advice from the teacher for the current timestep, the student checks further conditions:
 - $G_{\omega}$ must have been trained.
 - The student's own Q-network's action for the current state $s_t$ must be an "explorative" action (e.g., not the greedy action from its current policy). This prevents overriding potentially learned optimal actions with imitated advice.
 - The estimated uncertainty $G^{\mu}_{\omega}(s_t)$ must be below a predefined reuse threshold $\tau_{reuse}$. This ensures that only advice for states considered "familiar" or "certain" by $G_{\omega}$ is reused.
5. **Action Selection:** If all conditions for advice reuse are met, the student executes the action $\arg \max_a G_{\omega}(a | s_t)$ generated by the behavioural cloning module. If not, or if advice reuse is not allowed, the student defaults to its own exploration policy (e.g., $\epsilon$-greedy policy).

**D. Experimental Setup:**
- **Evaluation Domain:** Three Atari 2600 games from the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) were used: Enduro, Freeway, and Pong. These games were preprocessed (greyscale, resized, frame stacking, reward clipping) and augmented with stochasticity (no-op actions at start, sticky actions) to increase task complexity.
- **Student Variants:**
 - **No Advising (None):** A baseline DQN agent without any advising.
 - **Early Advising (EA):** A DQN agent that receives advice greedily at the start of learning until the budget is exhausted.
 - **Early Advising with Advice Reuse via Imitation (AR):** The proposed method, combining early advising with the behavioural cloning and uncertainty-based reuse mechanism.
- **RL Algorithm:** All student variants used a DQN agent with Double Q-learning and Dueling Networks enhancements, employing an $\epsilon$-greedy exploration policy.
- **Network Architectures:** The DQN agent used a convolutional neural network structure followed by a fully-connected layer and dueling streams. The behavioral cloning module had an identical convolutional structure but without the dueling stream. All layer activations were ReLU.
- **Hyperparameters:** Key hyperparameters were specified for both DQN (replay memory, target network updates, learning rate, discount factor, $\epsilon$-decay) and the Behavioural Cloning Network (minibatch size, learning rate, dropout rate (0.2), number of forward passes for uncertainty (100)). The advising budget was set to 10,000 steps (approximately 0.3% of total interactions).
 - Specific AR hyperparameters: $n_D$ = 10,000, $k_{bc}$ = 50,000, $\epsilon_{reuse}$ = 0.5, $\tau_{reuse}$ = 0.01.
- **Teacher Agent:** Competent DQN agents, pre-trained for 10 million steps (40 million actual game frames) for each game, served as teachers.
- **Experiments:** Each student variant was trained for 3 million steps (12 million actual game frames) over 3 random seeds. Performance was evaluated every 25,000 steps for 10 episodes without exploration or teaching. Advice reuse statistics (total reuses and correctly imitated reuses) were also recorded.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across three Atari games: Enduro, Freeway, and Pong, comparing three student agent variants: No Advising (None), Early Advising (EA), and Early Advising with Advice Reuse (AR). The key findings related to evaluation scores, advice reuse statistics, and computational burden are presented.

**A. Evaluation Scores (Learning Performance):**
- **Enduro:** The AR variant demonstrated a significant initial performance boost, leading to superior learning speed (higher Area Under the Curve - AUC) compared to EA and None. While all variants eventually reached similar final performance levels, AR achieved this performance more rapidly.
- **Freeway:** Both EA and AR significantly outperformed None in terms of both learning speed (AUC) and final performance. AR showed a slight advantage over EA, suggesting that initial advice is highly beneficial in this game, with advice reuse offering a modest additional improvement.
- **Pong:** AR exhibited the most substantial performance gains in Pong, significantly surpassing both EA and None in both learning speed (AUC) and final evaluation scores. This indicates that in environments like Pong, a combination of initial advice and its subsequent reuse can have a profound impact on learning.
- **Overall:** The AR method consistently achieved the best or near-best performance across all games, demonstrating its ability to improve learning speed and/or final scores. Importantly, the study observed no performance degradation due to high advice utilization (even up to 104k reuses in Freeway), which previous studies had indicated as a potential risk of over-advising. The performance boost over the "None" baseline was not always substantial, but the paper notes this occurred when paired with a simple "Early Advising" baseline, suggesting potential for greater impact with more sophisticated advising strategies.

**B. Advice Reuse Statistics:**
The analysis of advice reuse rates and imitation accuracy provides insight into the behavioural cloning module's effectiveness:
- **Reuse Trends:** In all games, the rate of advice reuse decreased over time, which is attributed to the $\epsilon$-greedy exploration annealing schedule, as the agent relies less on exploration (and thus advice) as it becomes more confident in its own policy. The plots showed both cumulative reuse and reuse within 100-step windows, distinguishing between all reuses and correctly imitated reuses (matching ground-truth teacher advice).
- **Enduro:** This game exhibited the lowest advice reuse rate (20.55% of exploration steps) and the lowest imitation accuracy (54.44% of reused advice was correct). The authors suggest this might be due to long game episodes reducing the proportion of "familiar" states for the behavioural cloner. Despite lower accuracy, AR still achieved better learning speed than EA.
- **Freeway:** Freeway showed a high advice reuse rate (32.05% of exploration steps) and very high imitation accuracy (84.74% correct). However, this high reuse and accuracy did not translate into a dramatically superior performance over EA, suggesting that while the advice was good, its repeated application might have diminishing returns in this specific environment, or the critical learning phase was largely covered by the initial advice from EA.
- **Pong:** In Pong, where AR yielded the most significant performance improvement, the advice reuse ratio (22.20% of exploration steps) was similar to Enduro, but the imitation accuracy was considerably higher (68.32% correct). This combination of moderate reuse and higher accuracy appears to be effective in this game.
- **Conclusion on Reuse:** The results collectively indicate that generalizing teacher advice over unseen but similar states through imitation is feasible with high accuracy. The choice of uncertainty threshold ($\tau_{reuse}$) directly influences the trade-off between reuse quantity and accuracy. The authors state that an accuracy around the reported levels is generally safe and makes threshold tuning straightforward, which is valuable for real-world applications without prior task access.

**C. Computational Burden:**
The computational overhead introduced by the proposed AR approach primarily involves two extra operations:
- **Behavioural Cloning Network Training:** This occurs only once at the beginning of the learning process (after $n_D$ samples are collected) and is considered negligible in terms of overall training time.
- **Uncertainty Estimations:** Requiring multiple forward passes (100 in experiments) for each exploration step, this operation was found to cause a maximum of 2x slowdown during exploration. Given that exploration steps typically constitute approximately 10% of a total learning session, the overall increase in training time is estimated to be at most 10%. This is presented as a minor setback considering the sample efficiency benefits gained.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel and practical approach to enhance action advising in deep reinforcement learning (RL) by enabling student agents to imitate and reuse previously collected advice. The work addresses a significant gap in current deep RL advising methods, which predominantly focus on *when* to advise rather than *how* to effectively utilize the advice received. The significance and potential impact of this study are multifaceted:

**A. Enhanced Sample Efficiency and Budget Utilization:**
The primary impact is the improvement in sample efficiency. By allowing students to reuse imitated advice, the method effectively extends the utility of a limited advising budget. Instead of repeatedly requesting advice for similar states, the student can generate its own "self-advice," reducing reliance on the teacher and allowing the budget to be spent on genuinely novel or critical states. This is particularly valuable in real-world applications where teacher interaction is costly or attention is limited.

**B. Feasibility of Generalizing Advice in Deep RL:**
The study demonstrates that it is feasible to accurately generalize a small set of teacher advice over unseen, yet similar, states in deep RL environments. This finding is crucial because direct memorization of state-action pairs, common in tabular RL, is impractical in high-dimensional deep RL state spaces. The proposed behavioral cloning module, combined with uncertainty estimation, provides a concrete mechanism for this generalization.

**C. Algorithm-Agnostic and Modular Design:**
The proposed approach integrates advice imitation as a separate module, distinct from the student's core deep RL algorithm (DQN in this case). This modularity means the method is RL algorithm-agnostic, potentially making it compatible with various deep RL algorithms without requiring modifications to their underlying learning mechanisms. This broadens its applicability and simplifies integration.

**D. Robustness to Over-Advising:**
Unlike some previous works where excessive advising or demonstration could lead to performance degradation, this study indicates that its method can achieve high advice utilization rates (e.g., over 100,000 reused advice instances in Freeway) without detrimental effects on learning performance. This suggests a robust mechanism for integrating external knowledge.

**E. Practical Implementability and Tunability:**
The method is described as straightforward to implement and tune, with the uncertainty threshold being a key parameter that balances reuse quantity and accuracy. The relatively small computational overhead (approximately 10% longer training time for similar settings) further enhances its practical viability, especially when considering the significant sample efficiency benefits.

**F. Foundation for Future Research:**
This work opens several avenues for future research at the intersection of action advising and exploration in RL:
- **Impact of Advice Quality and Reuse Rates:** Further investigation into how different qualities of imitated advice and varying reuse rates affect learning performance could lead to more nuanced advising strategies.
- **Evaluating Advice Contribution:** Developing methods to evaluate the contribution of each piece of advice (whether from the teacher or self-imitated) to the learning progress could inform more intelligent advice selection and reuse policies.
- **Integration with Advanced Advising Heuristics:** Combining advice imitation with more sophisticated advice timing heuristics (beyond early advising) could yield even greater performance improvements by ensuring the behavioral cloner is trained on a more diverse and impactful set of teacher experiences.
- **Dynamic Uncertainty Thresholds:** Exploring adaptive or learned uncertainty thresholds for advice reuse could provide more dynamic and context-aware advice utilization.

In conclusion, this research presents a significant step towards making action advising more efficient and practical in deep RL by empowering students to learn from and reuse expert advice. Its modular nature, demonstrated effectiveness, and modest computational cost contribute to its potential for broader adoption and further innovation in the field of intelligent agent learning.
