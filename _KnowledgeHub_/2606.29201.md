# Behavior Uncloning: Distilling Mode Redirection into Policy Weights without Inference-Time Steering

> [!summary] Summary
> The MoRE (Mode Redirection) framework introduces a behavior uncloning method to suppress undesirable actions in pre-trained robot policies by distilling mode-redirection signals directly into policy weights. Researchers from Texas A&M University and collaborators developed this approach, achieving an average 44 percentage point increase in deployment success rate on simulated tasks while preserving task completion and incurring no inference-time overhead.

## Problem

- Behavior-cloned robot policies, especially when trained on diverse datasets, often learn multiple successful strategies for a task, some of which may be unsafe or undesirable for specific deployment contexts.
- Current methods to control these behavior modes are either computationally expensive, requiring full retraining from filtered datasets, or introduce significant inference-time overhead through external steering modules.
- There is a need for an efficient post-hoc policy editing framework that can reliably redirect policies towards desired behavior modes without degrading overall task competence or adding latency during real-time deployment.

## Method

- The **MoRE** (Mode Redirection) framework proposes an ==iterative weight-editing process== for pre-trained mixed-mode policies to suppress undesired behaviors.
- It utilizes a ==differentiable mode-redirection editor== guided by a lightweight, frozen ==K-way behavior-mode classifier== trained on cached features from the original policy.
- A composite loss function combines a ==retain loss==, applied to desired-mode samples to preserve task competence, and a ==gated redirect loss==, applied to undesired-mode samples to push probability mass towards the desired mode set, updating only the policy weights.

## Results

- MoRE achieved an average **44 percentage point** improvement in Deployment Success Rate (SR) across various simulated Diffusion Policy tasks, successfully redirecting rollouts toward desired behavior modes while preserving the Task Completion Rate (TCR).
- The framework consistently outperformed post-hoc preference learning baselines (CPL) and inference-time steering methods (DynaGuide) in both effectiveness and efficiency.
- MoRE generalized across policy backbones (Diffusion Policy, π0.5 VLA), mode counts (up to 4-way), and embodiments (manipulation, quadruped navigation), with real-robot deployments (e.g. Knife-Handover, Place-Bottle) confirming successful mode redirection while preserving task competence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy weights can be directly edited post-training to suppress specific behavior modes, enabling the deployment of standalone edited policies with zero inference-time overhead.
> - Applying the redirect loss only to undesired-mode samples where the source-mode probability (as predicted by the classifier) is below a threshold improves editing stability and prevents degradation of mode-specific actions.
> - A frozen behavior-mode classifier, initially trained on fixed features from the mixed-mode policy, can effectively establish a differentiable path for guiding policy weight updates without becoming part of the deployed policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Behavior cloning (BC) has facilitated advancements in robot learning by enabling capable visuomotor policies trained on extensive demonstration datasets. However, a tension arises during real-world deployment, particularly in human-robot interaction contexts, due to the diversity inherent in large datasets. These datasets often contain multiple successful strategies, or "behavior modes," for a single task. While some modes are acceptable, others may be unsafe, undesirable, or incompatible with deployment constraints (e.g., a knife handover policy learning to pass the knife blade-first). This necessitates a mechanism for policies to execute tasks through acceptable behavior modes and to support explicit editing for suppressing undesirable ones.

Existing approaches to address this issue fall into two main categories:
1. **Data-centric mode control:** This involves methods like filtered retraining, where undesired-mode demonstrations are removed from the dataset, and a new policy is trained from scratch. While providing direct control over modes, this approach demands access to the original dataset and entails costly retraining for each desired mode, potentially discarding valuable task-relevant information shared across modes.
2. **Inference-time steering:** These post-hoc methods modify policy behavior during deployment without retraining the base policy. Examples include re-ranking sampled actions with external verifiers, guiding diffusion denoising with auxiliary dynamics models, or intervening on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) hidden states at each control step. While avoiding full retraining, these methods introduce additional inference-time overhead, requiring the deployment system to run an external steering module or sampling loop, which can complicate low-latency robot control.

This research identifies a gap in the landscape: a need for a post-hoc editing framework that can redirect policy rollouts toward desired behavior modes by distilling redirection signals directly into policy weights, thereby incurring zero inference-time overhead. This concept is termed "behavior uncloning," akin to post-training weight edits in generative and language models for concept erasure or factual editing. This work aims to provide a more efficient and integrated solution compared to existing methods by directly modifying the policy's internal representation of behavior modes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient policy-editing framework capable of suppressing deployment-undesired behavior modes within already-trained robot policies, without introducing additional inference-time computational overhead.

The motivation for this objective stems from several limitations observed in current robot learning paradigms:

1. **Undesirable Behavior Modes:** Behavior-cloned policies, especially those trained on diverse, large-scale datasets, frequently learn multiple ways to complete a task. Some of these learned modes might be unsafe, inappropriate for specific deployment contexts, or simply not preferred by users (e.g., passing a knife blade-first instead of handle-first). The goal is not merely task completion but task completion through an acceptable mode.
2. **Inefficiencies of Data Curation/Filtered Retraining:** While effective, retraining a policy on a curated, filtered dataset requires full access to the original, often extensive, demonstration data. This process is computationally expensive and time-consuming, as it involves repeating the entire training pipeline for every specific mode or set of modes desired for deployment. Such an approach is not suitable for rapid adaptation or fine-tuning in response to evolving deployment requirements or user preferences.
3. **Inference-Time Overhead of Steering Methods:** Current post-hoc steering methods, which guide policy behavior during deployment, introduce additional computational cost and latency. By requiring external verifiers, auxiliary models, or continuous interventions on hidden states, these methods complicate the deployment architecture and can hinder the performance of low-latency robotic systems, making them less practical for real-time applications.
4. **Need for Standalone Edited Policies:** There is a demand for a method that results in a standalone policy that inherently suppresses undesired modes without requiring any auxiliary modules or modified inference procedures at deployment. This would simplify deployment, reduce computational footprint, and maintain the original inference speed of the base policy.

To address these motivations, the paper proposes MoRE (Mode Redirection) with the following specific objectives:
- To formulate and evaluate **behavior uncloning** as an efficient post-hoc policy-editing problem.
- To develop a methodology that **distills mode-redirection signals into policy weights**, thereby enabling the standalone edited policy to suppress undesired modes directly.
- To ensure that this editing process **preserves the policy's task-level competence** in desired modes.
- To achieve these objectives with **zero inference-time overhead**.
- To demonstrate the **generalizability** of the proposed approach across different robot policy backbones (e.g., Diffusion Policy, π₀.₅ VLA), diverse task categories (manipulation, navigation), various mode counts (binary to multi-way), and deployment environments (simulated to real-world robots).
- To investigate whether this editing can be performed using **closed-loop rollouts** from the mixed-mode policy, reducing reliance on original demonstration datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MoRE (Mode Redirection) framework addresses the problem of behavior uncloning by iteratively editing the weights of a pre-trained mixed-mode policy. The methodology involves a differentiable mode-redirection editor that combines a retain loss for desired behaviors and a gated redirect loss for undesired behaviors, all distilled into the policy weights.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The work begins with a standard behavior cloning (BC) setup where a policy $\pi_\theta$ is trained on a dataset of demonstrations $D_{BC} = \{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^N$, with each trajectory $\tau_i$ consisting of observation-action pairs. In this multi-mode context, each demonstration $\tau_i$ is additionally annotated with a mode label $m_j$, allowing the dataset to be partitioned into $D_{BC} = \bigcup_{k=1}^K D_k$, where $D_k$ represents demonstrations for mode $k$.

The objective of behavior uncloning is to start from an initial mixed-mode policy $\pi_{\theta_0}$ (trained on $D_{BC}$) and derive an edited policy $\pi_{\theta^\star}$ such that its closed-loop rollouts primarily fall into a desired deployment set of modes $S \subseteq \{1, \dots, K\}$, while preserving task competence and without incurring additional inference-time overhead. The editing process utilizes mode-labeled editing data $D_{edit}$, which can originate from original demonstrations or from closed-loop rollouts of $\pi_{\theta_0}$. This data is partitioned into $D_{des}$ (samples whose mode belongs to $S$) and $D_{undes}$ (samples whose mode does not belong to $S$).

#### 3.2. Differentiable Mode-Redirection Editor

MoRE's core mechanism is a differentiable editor that leverages a behavior-mode classifier.

1. **Mode Classifier Training:** A lightweight K-way behavior-mode classifier, $g_\phi$, is initially trained. This classifier operates on features $r_\theta(x)$ extracted from the policy for a given sample $x = (o, a, m)$. Critically, $g_\phi$ is first trained on *cached features* $r_{\theta_0}(x)$ produced by the *original mixed-mode policy* $\pi_{\theta_0}$, to predict the mode label $m(x)$. The output $q_\phi(m | r_{\theta_0}(x))$ represents the probability of mode $m$.
2. **Feature Input for Classifier:**
 - For VLA policies (e.g., π₀.₅), $r_\theta(x)$ is the mean-pooled last-layer hidden state of the PaliGemma backbone's prefix, a 2048-dimensional vector representing visual and language prompt information before the action expert.
 - For Diffusion Policy, $r_\theta(x)$ is a concatenation of the policy-independent observation condition $c_t$ and the predicted clean action chunk $\hat{a}_{0,\theta}$ reconstructed from the denoising output. This allows the classifier's gradients to flow into the denoising UNet during editing.
3. **Classifier Freezing and Gradient Flow:** Once trained, the classifier's parameters $\phi$ are frozen. During the subsequent policy editing phase, the policy-dependent feature $r_\theta(x)$ is *recomputed* under the *current policy parameters* $\theta$. This recomputation establishes a differentiable path, allowing gradients from the frozen classifier's output to flow through $r_\theta(x)$ and update the policy weights $\theta$.
4. **Classifier Architecture:** The classifier itself is a small MLP with three 256-width hidden blocks (linear, LayerNorm, SiLU) followed by a final linear layer outputting K mode logits. AdamW is used for classifier training with a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of $3 \times 10^{-4}$.

#### 3.3. Optimization for Balancing Task Competence and Behavior Uncloning

The policy editing in MoRE is driven by a composite loss function, $L_{MoRE}(\theta)$, which combines a retain loss and a redirect loss:

$L_{MoRE}(\theta) = E_{x \sim D_{des}} [L_{BC}(x; \theta)]_{\text{retain}} + \gamma E_{x \sim D^M_{\theta, undes}} [L_{red}(x; \theta, \phi, S)]_{\text{redirect}}$

1. **Retain Loss:** The first term, $E_{x \sim D_{des}} [L_{BC}(x; \theta)]$, is applied to samples from $D_{des}$ (desired-mode samples). This is the policy's original behavior cloning loss (e.g., noise prediction for Diffusion Policy, velocity matching for flow-based VLAs). Its purpose is to preserve the policy's competence and behavior quality for the desired modes, preventing degradation during the editing process.
2. **Redirect Loss:** The second term, $\gamma E_{x \sim D^M_{\theta, undes}} [L_{red}(x; \theta, \phi, S)]$, targets undesired-mode samples. $\gamma$ is a weighting coefficient.
 - The redirect loss $L_{red}$ is a unified subset-probability loss: $L_{red}(x; \theta, \phi, S) = - \log \frac{\sum_{i \in S} \exp z_i}{\sum_{j=1}^K \exp z_j}$, where $z = g_\phi(r_\theta(x))$ are the frozen classifier's logits. This loss pushes the probability mass of the classifier's output towards the desired mode set $S$. If $S$ contains only one target mode $m^\star$, it simplifies to a standard cross-entropy loss for that mode.
 - Crucially, the original imitation loss ($L_{BC}$) is *not* applied to undesired-mode samples during this phase, as it would anchor the policy to the undesired source mode, conflicting with the redirection objective.
3. **Optimization:** During editing, only the policy parameters $\theta$ (e.g., trainable VLA parameters downstream of a frozen visual encoder, or the denoising UNet for Diffusion Policy) are updated. The classifier parameters $\phi$ remain fixed. After editing, the classifier $g_\phi$ is discarded, and the edited policy $\pi_{\theta^\star}$ operates through its original inference path without any additional modules or overhead.

#### 3.4. Masking Redirection by Source-Mode Probability

To enhance stability and effectiveness, MoRE introduces a mask, $M_\theta$, that selectively applies the redirect loss only to undesired-mode samples where redirection is likely to be impactful.

1. **Gated Redirection:** The redirect loss is applied only to samples $x_t \in D_{undes}$ whose source-mode probability $q_\phi(m(x_t) | r_\theta(x_t))$ (as predicted by the frozen classifier for the current policy features) is below a threshold $\tau$.
2. **Rationale:** The motivation is to focus redirection efforts on states where the policy has not yet fully committed to an undesired mode. Applying redirection once a rollout has entered a mode-specific branch is less effective for changing the chosen mode and more prone to degrading the quality of mode-specific actions.
3. **Threshold:** In all experiments, $\tau$ is set to 0.5, meaning an undesired-mode sample is only subjected to redirection if its source mode receives less than 50% of the classifier's probability mass. The mask is re-evaluated dynamically during training using current policy-produced features $r_\theta(x_t)$.

The editing process typically involves fewer than 500 gradient steps per target setting. Checkpoints are selected based on held-out editing loss.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated MoRE across various tasks, policy backbones, and deployment scenarios, addressing four key research questions (Q1-Q4).

#### 4.1. Q1: Can MoRE improve deployment success by redirecting closed-loop rollouts toward desired behavior modes while preserving task completion?

- **Deployment Success Rate (SR) Improvement:** Across four simulated Diffusion Policy tasks (Push-T, Push-Wall, Push-Pillars, Quadruped), MoRE significantly improved the average Deployment Success Rate (SR) by 44 percentage points compared to the original mixed-mode policy. For instance, on Push-T, SR increased from 50.0% to 98.3%, and on Push-Wall, it rose from 37.0% to 80.7% (Table 1).
- **Preservation of Task Completion Rate (TCR):** The Task Completion Rate (TCR) remained high overall across tasks (e.g., 98.7% on Push-T, 80.7% on Push-Wall). This indicates that MoRE primarily shifts the policy's behavior mode choice rather than degrading its ability to complete the task successfully.

#### 4.2. Q2: How closely does MoRE approach the filtered-data retraining reference, and how does it compare with preference learning and inference-time steering baselines?

- **Comparison to Baselines (Table 1):**
 - **CPL (Contrastive Preference Learning):** CPL, a post-hoc preference learning baseline, provided only partial mode shifts and, in some cases, reduced TCR. Its average SR improvements were lower than MoRE (e.g., 90.3% on Push-T for CPL vs. 98.3% for MoRE).
 - **DynaGuide (Inference-time Steering):** DynaGuide, an inference-time steering method, improved mode alignment on some tasks but incurred a significant inference-time slowdown (8.47x original policy latency). Its average SR (e.g., 49.7% on Push-T) was generally lower than MoRE and sometimes even lower than the original policy, especially when its TCR was high, indicating it completed tasks in undesired modes.
- **Approach to Filtered-Data Retraining:** MoRE demonstrated SR values that approached or, in some specific instances, exceeded the performance of the "Filtered-retrain ref." This reference represents a policy trained from scratch on mode-filtered demonstrations using ground-truth labels and is not a post-hoc adaptation baseline. MoRE achieved this strong performance without requiring full retraining or adding inference-time overhead.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Loss Components:** On the Quadruped task, full MoRE achieved 84.3% SR and 84.7% TCR. A "retain-only" variant yielded 49.0% SR / 100.0% TCR, indicating that while competence was preserved, mode redirection did not occur. A "redirect-only" variant resulted in 27.0% SR / 53.0% TCR, showing that redirection without retaining desired behavior degrades task completion. These results support the necessity of both loss components.
 - **Source-Mode Gate Threshold ($\tau$):** Sweeping $\tau$ on Push-Wall showed that SR remained stable for $\tau \in \{0.2, 0.5, 0.7\}$ (around 80.3-80.7%), but disabling the gate ($\tau = 1.0$) reduced SR to 72.7%. This indicates the effectiveness of the gated redirection in preventing degradation.

#### 4.3. Q3: Does MoRE require access to the original demonstrations, or can it edit a policy using only closed-loop rollouts from the mixed-mode policy?

- **Data Source Flexibility (Table 2):** MoRE can utilize either original mixed-mode demonstrations or labeled closed-loop rollouts from the mixed policy as editing data.
- **Performance with Rollout Data:** While demonstration-based editing yielded the strongest results (98.3% SR on Push-T), rollout-only editing still achieved a substantial improvement in SR to 88.0% on Push-T. This indicates that access to original demonstrations is beneficial but not strictly required, especially when rollout modes can be reliably labeled.

#### 4.4. Q4: Does MoRE generalize across policy backbones, mode counts, embodiments, and real-robot deployments?

- **Q4.i: Transfer to VLA Backbone (Table 3 & 4):** MoRE successfully transferred to the π₀.₅ Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model. For instance, on the simulated Push-Wall task with the VLA backbone, MoRE increased SR from 41.0% to 72.0%. On the real-world Knife-Handover task (Table 4), MoRE shifted the policy towards the desired handover mode, achieving 70.0% SR while maintaining TCR (85.0%) comparable to the finetuned mixed policy (90.0%). This suggests MoRE is not specific to the Diffusion Policy architecture.
- **Q4.ii: More Modes and Multi-Target Edits (Table 3 & 4):**
 - **Increased Mode Counts:** MoRE effectively handled tasks with more than two behavior modes. On the four-route Push-Pillars task, MoRE increased Diffusion Policy SR from 21.5% to 72.3%.
 - **Multi-Target Editing:** The framework supports scenarios where the desired set $S$ contains multiple acceptable modes. With the VLA backbone on Push-Pillars, MoRE improved SR from 41.0% to 70.3% in a 4-to-2 mode setting, and from 61.5% to 72.0% in a 4-to-3 setting. Real-world Bottle-Placement also demonstrated this capability, successfully preserving either the {left, right} or {middle, right} subsets of placement modes.
- **Q4.iii: Transfer Beyond Tabletop Manipulation (Table 1):** MoRE generalized to a quadruped navigation task. By editing a Diffusion Policy that outputs high-level velocity commands for a frozen low-level gait controller, MoRE increased SR from 50.0% to 84.3% while maintaining a high TCR of 84.7%. This indicates applicability beyond robot arm manipulation.
- **Q4.iv: Real-Robot Deployments (Table 4):** MoRE was validated on four real-robot tasks (knife placement, bottle placement, block-obstacle, knife handover). In all cases, the edited policies successfully redirected rollouts toward the requested behavior mode, substantially increasing SR while preserving task competence. For example, on real-world Place-Bottle (3->1 mode change), SR improved from 31.7% to 96.7%.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces "behavior uncloning" as a distinct and relevant problem setting in robot learning, specifically addressing the challenge of unwanted behavior modes in pre-trained policies without compromising deployment efficiency. The proposed MoRE framework offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Efficient Post-Hoc Policy Editing:** MoRE provides a method for adapting pre-trained policies to suppress undesired behaviors without requiring a full retraining process from original, often extensive, demonstration datasets. This significantly reduces the computational cost and time associated with modifying policy behavior.
2. **Zero Inference-Time Overhead:** A primary advantage of MoRE is that the mode redirection signal is distilled directly into the policy weights. This means that after the editing phase, the mode classifier is discarded, and the edited policy can be deployed through its original inference path with no additional latency or computational burden. This makes MoRE highly suitable for real-time robotic applications where low-latency control is critical.
3. **Enhanced Deployment Reliability and Safety:** By enabling the explicit redirection of policies away from unsafe or undesirable behavior modes towards preferred ones, MoRE contributes to more reliable and safer robot deployments, particularly in human-robot interaction scenarios. This allows for fine-grained control over robot behavior beyond mere task completion.
4. **Broad Applicability:** The demonstrated effectiveness of MoRE across various policy backbones (Diffusion Policy, π₀.₅ VLA), diverse task categories (manipulation, quadruped navigation), different mode counts (binary to multi-way), and both simulated and real-world environments suggests its generalizability. This broad applicability increases its potential utility for a wide range of robotic systems and applications.
5. **Flexibility in Editing Data:** The ability of MoRE to utilize either original demonstrations or closed-loop rollouts from the mixed-mode policy as editing data offers practical flexibility. This is particularly valuable in scenarios where access to the full original demonstration dataset might be restricted or costly to obtain.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also highlight avenues for future research:
- **Labeled Editing Data Requirement:** MoRE relies on mode-labeled editing data or closed-loop rollouts whose modes can be reliably inferred. Extending the approach to settings with noisier labels or automatic discovery of hierarchical mode taxonomies would increase its robustness.
- **Classifier Interface and Mode Commitment:** The effectiveness of MoRE depends on the classifier interface exposing mode information early in the trajectory, before the policy has fully committed to a source mode. Future work could explore methods that are less sensitive to the timing of mode commitment.
- **Complex Tasks and Distribution Shifts:** The current evaluation primarily focuses on relatively short-horizon tasks. Extending behavior uncloning to longer-horizon tasks and scenarios involving stronger distribution shifts represents an important direction for further development.

Overall, MoRE represents a step towards developing more adaptable and controllable robot policies that can be efficiently fine-tuned for specific deployment requirements without sacrificing performance or incurring prohibitive computational costs.
