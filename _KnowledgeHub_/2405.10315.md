# TRANSIC: Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer by Learning from Online Correction

> [!summary] Summary
> TRANSIC transfers RL policies from simulation to real robots by letting a human watch execution and take over via teleoperation when the robot errs. Corrections train a gated residual policy added to a point-cloud base policy, itself distilled from an operational-space-controller teacher into joint-position control. It reaches 81% average success on contact-rich furniture-assembly skills.

## Problem

- Traditional sim-to-real fixes, system identification, domain randomization, simulator augmentation, need domain-specific knowledge that is rarely available a priori and is engineered case by case.
- Sim-to-real success concentrates in locomotion and in-hand or non-prehensile tasks; contact-rich whole-arm assembly stays hard because perception, embodiment, controller, and dynamics gaps coexist.
- Interactive imitation methods that fine-tune on human intervention data overwrite the simulation policy, causing catastrophic forgetting, while behavior cloning and offline RL overfit on dozens of real trajectories.

## Method

- ==Action space distillation==: a ==PPO== teacher trained under an ==operational-space controller== generates trajectories relabeled to joint positions, distilling a point-cloud student policy plus a sim-real point-cloud regularization loss.
- ==Human-in-the-loop online correction==: an operator monitors the base policy and teleoperates via SpaceMouse when it misbehaves, logging intervention flags with pre- and post-intervention joint states.
- **TRANSIC**'s ==gated residual policy==: a residual head predicts post-minus-pre intervention state deltas, and a jointly trained gating classifier decides per step whether the residual is added to base actions.

## Results

- Best average real-robot success across **four** FurnitureBench skills (**81%** in the ablation table); correction data boosted the base policy **1.24x**, beat HG-Dagger and IWR by **0.75x**, and real-data-only baselines by **3.6x**.
- Across **five** deliberately exacerbated simulation-reality pairs (perception, embodiment, dynamics, controller, asset), it averaged **77%** success versus **18%** for the strongest baseline IWR.
- Scaling correction data from **25%** to **75%** gained **42%** versus IWR's **23%**; zero-shot lamp assembly hit **75%** versus about **33%**; dropping point-cloud regularization fell from **81%** to **55%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Isolating human corrections in a separate gated residual, instead of fine-tuning the base policy, preserves simulation competence while fixing failures; separation beats intervention reweighting.
> - Sim-to-real gaps can be closed implicitly: humans supply the missing domain knowledge through corrective action, so no per-gap modeling or randomization schedule is required.
> - Pick the action space for transfer, not exploration: train with a high-level controller, then distill into low-level joint commands that execute identically on hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the critical intersection of robot learning, simulation-to-reality (sim-to-real) transfer, and human-in-the-loop systems. The broader goal in robotics is to develop "generalist robots" that can perform a wide array of tasks in unstructured environments. Learning control policies for such robots often requires vast amounts of data, which is expensive and time-consuming to collect in the real world. This challenge has driven extensive research into training robots in simulated environments, where data collection is virtually limitless and safe.

However, a fundamental hurdle for this approach is the "sim-to-real gap" – the discrepancy between simulated and real-world physics, perception, and control. While successful sim-to-real transfers have been demonstrated in specific domains like quadruped locomotion and dexterous in-hand manipulation, extending this success to complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks with robotic arms has proven significantly more challenging. This difficulty arises from various coexisting gaps:
- **Perception Gap:** Differences between synthetic and real sensor data.
- **Embodiment Mismatch:** Discrepancies between simulated and real robot models.
- **Controller Inaccuracy:** The same command yielding different results in simulation vs. reality.
- **Dynamics Realism:** Inaccurate modeling of physical interactions, especially contact and friction.

Traditionally, researchers have attempted to bridge these gaps through methods such as system identification (empirically characterizing robot parameters), domain randomization (varying simulation parameters during training to encourage robustness), real-world adaptation (fine-tuning policies on real data), and simulator augmentation (modifying the simulator to better match reality). However, these approaches often demand significant *a priori* domain-specific knowledge, labor-intensive calibration, or can incur safety risks during real-world adaptation with suboptimal policies. They tend to address individual gaps rather than providing a holistic solution.

The paper argues for a shift towards human-in-the-loop (HIL) learning and shared autonomy as a more "straightforward and feasible" paradigm. HIL learning has gained traction in AI for injecting human knowledge into autonomous systems, particularly for tasks where collecting fully labeled datasets is impractical or impossible. Within robotics, HIL is explored for continuous improvement, feedback-driven learning, and generating corrective interventions.

TRANSIC positions itself as a novel HIL method that leverages human *online correction* to address the sim-to-real gap holistically and domain-agnostically. It distinguishes itself from traditional approaches by minimizing the need for explicit domain knowledge and from purely real-world data collection methods (like imitation learning from scratch) by requiring significantly less real-robot data. By tackling challenging whole-arm manipulation tasks, TRANSIC pushes the frontier of sim-to-real transfer into a domain where it has historically struggled, highlighting the power of combining simulation-based pre-training with targeted human supervision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the TRANSIC research is to enable successful and efficient transfer of robot control policies learned in simulation to the real world, particularly for complex and contact-rich manipulation tasks. This overarching goal is driven by the vision of creating "generalist robots" that can robustly operate across diverse real-world scenarios.

The key motivations and more specific objectives are:

1. **Address the Sim-to-Real Gap Holistically:** The central challenge is the pervasive "sim-to-real gap," which arises from multiple coexisting discrepancies (perception, embodiment, controller, dynamics). Previous methods often tackle these gaps individually or require extensive, domain-specific *a priori* knowledge. TRANSIC aims to overcome these various gaps in a unified, holistic manner.
2. **Leverage Human Expertise as Domain-Agnostic Knowledge:** The authors hypothesize that humans naturally possess the implicit knowledge required to bridge sim-to-real gaps. By observing and correcting robot behavior in the real world, humans can effectively address these discrepancies without needing to formalize domain-specific parameters or dynamics. The motivation is to harness this human insight as a general-purpose solution.
3. **Develop a Data-Driven Human-in-the-Loop Framework:** The objective is to design a practical and effective data-driven methodology, named TRANSIC, that integrates human assistance into the policy transfer process. This framework allows humans to provide online correction signals that robots can learn from.
4. **Learn Residual Policies for Unmodeled Gaps:** Specifically, TRANSIC aims to learn a "residual policy" from human corrections. This residual policy would augment the base policy (trained in simulation) to compensate for unmodeled or incorrectly modeled aspects of the real world, thereby closing the sim-to-real gap implicitly.
5. **Achieve Data Efficiency in Real-World Learning:** A crucial motivation is to significantly reduce the amount of real-world robot data required for successful transfer. Traditional imitation learning or direct real-world RL often demand prohibitive amounts of physical interaction. TRANSIC seeks to be more sample-efficient by bootstrapping from robust simulation-trained policies and only collecting corrective human data when necessary.
6. **Demonstrate Efficacy in Challenging Manipulation Tasks:** The work specifically targets "complex and contact-rich manipulation tasks" (e.g., furniture assembly), which are particularly susceptible to sim-to-real discrepancies. Demonstrating success in these domains validates the approach's robustness and practical applicability.
7. **Explore Scaling and Generalization Properties:** Beyond mere success, the researchers aim to investigate how TRANSIC scales with human effort, its ability to generalize to unseen objects, and its robustness to various real-world conditions (e.g., degraded sensor inputs).

In essence, TRANSIC is motivated by the desire to make sim-to-real transfer more robust, efficient, and accessible for complex robotic tasks, by intelligently incorporating human corrective feedback to implicitly address the inherent discrepancies between simulation and reality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TRANSIC framework is a data-driven, human-in-the-loop approach designed for successful sim-to-real policy transfer. It consists of two main phases: learning base policies in simulation and learning residual policies from online human correction in the real world.

**Phase 1: Learning Base Policies in Simulation with Reinforcement Learning (RL)**

The goal here is to acquire a capable foundational policy purely in simulation.

1. **Policy Learning with 3D Representation:**
 - **Observation Modality:** Instead of RGB images, TRANSIC uses **point clouds** as the primary visual input, combined with proprioceptive observations. This choice is motivated by the fact that object geometry is crucial for contact-rich manipulation, and point clouds are less susceptible to perception gaps like varying camera poses or synthetic-real image discrepancies.
 - **Point Cloud Synthesis:** During simulation RL training, point clouds are synthesized directly from ground-truth object poses for higher throughput. These are aggregated from multiple objects in the scene and transformed into the global frame. For the robot itself, only gripper finger point clouds are used, with a semantic label to distinguish them from scene points.

2. **Action Space Distillation (Bridging the Controller Gap):**
 - This is a crucial component to address the "controller inaccuracy" sim-to-real gap.
 - **Teacher Policy Training:** A `teacher policy` ($\pi_{teacher}$) is trained using RL in simulation with an **operational space controller (OSC)**. OSC provides a high-level action space (end-effector poses), which facilitates efficient exploration and learning in RL. It also receives `privileged observations` (e.g., ground-truth object states, contact forces) for easier learning.
 - **Student Policy Distillation:** The successful trajectories generated by the `teacher policy` are then used to train a `student policy` ($\pi_{student}$) via behavior cloning. Critically, the actions in these trajectories are **re-labeled from end-effector poses to low-level joint positions**. This ensures that the `student policy` outputs actions in the robot's configuration space, which is more robust and consistent for deployment on real hardware, thus mitigating the controller gap. The `student policy` learns from synthetic point-cloud inputs and proprioception, matching real-world sensing capabilities.
 - **Regularization:** The student policy's point cloud encoder is regularized using a separate dataset of matched point clouds from simulation and reality. This helps ensure that the visual features learned in simulation are consistent with those from the real world, further reducing the perception gap.
 - The trained `student policy` becomes the **base policy ($\pi_B$)** for the real-world phase.

**Phase 2: Learning Residual Policies from Online Correction**

This phase takes the simulation-trained base policy and refines it using human feedback in the real world.

1. **Human-in-the-Loop Data Collection:**
 - The `base policy` ($\pi_B$) is deployed on the physical robot.
 - A human operator actively **monitors** the robot's execution.
 - When the robot deviates or fails due to unmodeled sim-to-real gaps, the human **intervenes** and takes control through teleoperation (e.g., using a 3Dconnexion SpaceMouse).
 - During intervention, the human corrects the robot's behavior to achieve the task.
 - Data collected includes pre-intervention states, post-intervention states, and an intervention indicator. This forms the "online correction dataset" ($D_H$).
 - The key idea is that humans implicitly address sim-to-real gaps through their corrections, without requiring explicit knowledge of the underlying physical discrepancies.

2. **Human Correction as Residual Policies:**
 - Instead of directly fine-tuning the base policy (which could lead to catastrophic forgetting), a separate **residual policy ($\pi_R$)** is learned from the collected human correction data ($D_H$).
 - The `residual policy` learns to predict the "residual action" – the *difference* between the post-intervention and pre-intervention robot states (`a_R = q_{post} ⊖ q_{pre}`). For continuous joint positions, this is a numerical difference; for binary gripper actions, it's an exclusive NOR.
 - This formulation allows the `residual policy` to capture the necessary adjustments to overcome sim-to-real gaps without altering the base policy's fundamental learned skill.

3. **Integrated Deployment Framework:**
 - During autonomous execution, the base policy ($\pi_B$) and the residual policy ($\pi_R$) are combined.
 - A **gating function ($g_ψ$)** is learned jointly with the `residual policy` (sharing its feature encoder). This gating function acts as an indicator (`1_g`) to determine *when* to apply the predicted residual actions. This is crucial because human interventions are infrequent, and applying residual actions indiscriminately could introduce errors.
 - The final deployed policy is **$\pi_{deployed} = \pi_B \oplus 1_g \pi_R$**, where `⊕` denotes combining the base and gated residual actions (e.g., by adding joint position adjustments).
 - A low-level joint position controller is used for deployment.

**Implementation Details:**
- The simulator used is NVIDIA's Isaac Gym, known for high-performance GPU-based physics simulation.
- PPO is used for RL teacher policy training.
- Student and residual policies use Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) as action heads for continuous actions, commonly employed in behavior cloning.
- Point cloud encoders include PointNet or Perceiver models, depending on the task.
- The system uses a Franka Emika 3 robot with multiple RealSense cameras for real-world point cloud reconstruction.

This meticulously designed methodology allows TRANSIC to leverage the strengths of both simulation (for data-rich pre-training) and human intuition (for targeted, real-world gap-closing), resulting in a robust and efficient sim-to-real transfer system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted with TRANSIC rigorously evaluate its performance across four complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks from FurnitureBench (Stabilize, Reach and Grasp, Insert, Screw). The findings highlight TRANSIC's effectiveness, data efficiency, robustness, and ability to generalize, answering four key research questions.

**Q1: Does TRANSIC lead to better transfer performance while requiring less real-world data?**
- **Superior Performance:** TRANSIC consistently achieves the highest average success rate (81%) across all benchmarked tasks, significantly outperforming all baseline methods. For instance, in "Stabilize" and "Reach and Grasp," it achieves 100% and 95% success rates, respectively.
- **Data Efficiency:** TRANSIC requires *significantly less real-world data* (dozens of trajectories, 62-489 corrections per task) compared to methods trained solely on real-robot data (like BC, BC-RNN, IQL), which suffer from overfitting and model collapse with limited data. TRANSIC achieves a 3.6x better performance than these methods.
- **Addressing Catastrophic Forgetting:** Unlike interactive imitation learning methods (HG-Dagger, IWR) that often lead to catastrophic forgetting of the base policy, TRANSIC's residual policy design, combined with a gating mechanism, effectively integrates human corrections without eroding the simulation-learned skill. It leverages the simulation policy for robust execution and intelligently applies residual corrections when needed.

**Q2: How effectively can TRANSIC address different types of sim-to-Real gaps?**
- **Holistic Gap Closure:** TRANSIC demonstrates remarkable ability to close various individual sim-to-real gaps when deliberately exacerbated (perception error, underactuated controller, embodiment mismatch, dynamics difference, object asset mismatch). It achieves an average success rate of 77% across these challenging scenarios, dramatically outperforming the best baseline (IWR at 18%).
- **Residual Policy's Role:** This effectiveness is attributed to the residual policy design, which implicitly compensates for unmodeled domain factors, echoing prior findings on residual learning. The ability to handle diverse gaps suggests a truly domain-agnostic approach.

**Q3: How does TRANSIC scale with human effort?**
- **Scalability:** TRANSIC scales positively with increased human correction data. Increasing the dataset size from 25% to 75% of the full collection results in a 42% average performance improvement for TRANSIC.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** In contrast, IWR shows limited scalability, with its performance often plateauing or even decreasing with more human data, further supporting the claim that it struggles with catastrophic forgetting.

**Q4: Does TRANSIC exhibit intriguing properties, such as generalization to unseen objects, policy robustness, ability to solve long-horizon tasks, and other emergent behaviors?**
- **Generalization to Unseen Objects:** TRANSIC shows strong generalization capabilities, achieving a 75% average success rate when evaluated zero-shot on assembling a lamp (an unseen object category), outperforming IWR which only succeeds one in three attempts. This indicates that it learns reusable, category-level skills.
- **Effective Gating:** Ablation studies reveal that the learned gating mechanism performs comparably to a human operator deciding when to intervene, indicating its reliability for autonomous operation.
- **Policy Robustness:** TRANSIC remains robust even when faced with reduced camera inputs (inferior point cloud quality) or noisy human correction data. The robustness to partial point clouds is partly attributed to heavy downsampling during training, while robustness to noisy correction is due to the integrated deployment where the base policy can compensate for suboptimal residual actions.
- **Consistent Visual Features:** The regularization of the point cloud encoder during distillation is shown to be critical for learning consistent visual features, preventing overfitting to synthetic data, and enabling effective sim-to-real transfer.
- **Emergent Behaviors:** Qualitatively, TRANSIC exhibits several human-like emergent behaviors including error recovery, unsticking from difficult situations, safety-aware actions, and failure prevention (e.g., adjusting an object to a stable pose to prevent dropping).
- **Long-Horizon Tasks:** The paper successfully demonstrates that individual skills transferred with TRANSIC can be effectively chained to solve complex, long-horizon tasks such as assembling a lamp or a square table, showcasing its potential for real-world application.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that TRANSIC offers a highly effective and data-efficient solution for sim-to-real transfer in challenging manipulation scenarios, overcoming multiple domain gaps through a synergistic integration of simulation-trained policies and learning from targeted human corrections.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TRANSIC framework represents a significant step forward in the field of robot learning and sim-to-real transfer, with several profound implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enabling Generalist Robots through Scalable Transfer:** TRANSIC's ability to achieve robust sim-to-real transfer for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks, which have historically been a bottleneck, directly contributes to the development of more generalist robots. By making it feasible to train policies in simulation and deploy them reliably in the real world, it paves the way for robots capable of performing diverse, sophisticated tasks beyond highly constrained industrial settings.

2. **Increased Data Efficiency and Accessibility:** The most immediate impact is the drastic reduction in the amount of real-world robot data required for successful deployment. By leveraging strong base policies from simulation and only needing "dozens of real-robot corrections," TRANSIC makes robot learning more practical, less resource-intensive, and thus more accessible for researchers and practitioners who lack vast real-world data collection infrastructure. This efficiency accelerates research and development cycles in robotics.

3. **Holistic and Domain-Agnostic Gap Closure:** TRANSIC offers a novel, holistic approach to addressing the multifaceted sim-to-real gap. Instead of tackling individual gaps with explicit, hand-crafted domain knowledge (e.g., precise system identification or exhaustive domain randomization), it implicitly resolves these issues through human correction. This domain-agnostic nature makes the approach more generalizable across different robots, tasks, and simulators, simplifying the transfer process.

4. **Enhanced Safety and Robustness in Deployment:** The human-in-the-loop framework inherently promotes safer deployment. Instead of relying on potentially unsafe trial-and-error adaptation in the real world, human operators intervene and correct only when necessary, preventing failures and guiding the robot safely. The learned residual policies further enhance robustness by providing a flexible mechanism to compensate for real-world uncertainties without overwriting learned skills.

5. **New Paradigm for Human-Robot Collaboration:** TRANSIC reframes human interaction not just as a source of demonstrations or preferences, but as a critical online correction mechanism for bridging domain discrepancies. This opens up new avenues for synergistic human-robot collaboration, where humans continuously refine and improve robot capabilities during deployment, rather than solely during the initial training phase. The concept of learning gated residual policies from sparse human intervention is particularly impactful.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:**
 - **Automated Intervention:** The paper acknowledges the limitation of manual human intervention decisions. Future work could focus on automating failure detection and triggering intervention, further increasing autonomy.
 - **Complex Robot Morphologies:** While demonstrated on a single arm, the principles could be extended to more complex robot systems (e.g., mobile manipulators, bimanual robots) with appropriate teleoperation interfaces.
 - **Integration with Advanced Simulators and Data Synthesis:** TRANSIC is compatible with ongoing advances in more realistic simulation and automated data generation techniques, promising even more robust base policies to start with.
 - **Understanding Human Correction:** Further research could delve into analyzing the characteristics of human corrections to better inform policy learning and transfer mechanisms.

In conclusion, TRANSIC offers a compelling solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics. By strategically integrating the strengths of simulation-based learning with targeted, online human expertise, it provides a robust, efficient, and generalizable framework for deploying intelligent robots into the complexities of the real world. Its impact will likely be felt across embodied AI, robot learning, and human-robot interaction research, pushing us closer to the realization of truly capable and adaptable generalist robotic systems.
