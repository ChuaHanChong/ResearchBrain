# Trained on Tokens, Calibrated on Concepts: The Emergence of Semantic Calibration in LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper defines B-calibration: an LLM's sampled answers are grouped into classes by meaning, and the paper checks whether the confidence of the most common class matches how often it is correct. It proves this calibration is equivalent to local loss optimality, so cross-entropy pretraining yields semantic calibration whenever the model can predict its own answer distribution before generating. Across 650+ experiments, base LLMs are calibrated, while DPO/RL post-training and chain-of-thought break calibration.

## Problem

- Base LLMs are known to be next-token calibrated, but that only measures confidence when a single token holds the whole answer, as in multiple-choice questions.
- For open-ended long-form answers, many phrasings share one meaning, so token probabilities give no direct confidence over what the response actually says.
- Prior semantic-confidence measures were studied only empirically, with no principled account of when or why calibration emerges, or how post-training, chain-of-thought, or scale affect it.

## Method

- **B-calibration** framework: a ==collapsing function B== maps each sampled generation to a semantic class, confidence is the top-class share of 50 temperature-1 samples, and error is scored with ==SmoothECE==.
- Theorem 7 proves that B-confidence-calibration is equivalent to ==local loss optimality== against a family of ==exponential-tilting perturbations==. Each perturbation reweights all strings in the top semantic class according to its confidence.
- Theorem 10 shows these perturbations need only a shallow circuit given the model's intermediate B-confidences. This yields a ==LoRA probe== testing whether the model can predict its own semantic distribution from the question alone.

## Results

- Across **650+** evaluations of Qwen, Gemini, Mistral and Llama models from **0.5B to 72B**, base models answering concisely or in sentences are reliably calibrated, with smECE around **0.03-0.05**.
- In the Mistral-7B lineage, the DPO-tuned Zephyr model is clearly miscalibrated while the base and SFT-only versions stay calibrated. Instruct models are typically overconfident on GSM8K, OpenMathInstruct, TriviaQA and SimpleQA.
- Chain-of-thought makes base models underconfident on math. Rank-**8** LoRA probes on Qwen2.5 **0.5B-14B** show that a lower KL gap tracks lower calibration error, and base-model calibration does not correlate with accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training on a proper loss leaves no easily learnable improvements unused, so calibration appears wherever the correcting perturbation is simple for the model to represent.
> - What makes chain-of-thought powerful, settling on the answer only after reasoning, also removes the self-knowledge that calibration needs, so reasoning and calibration trade off.
> - Calibration can be predicted without ground-truth labels by probing whether a model can forecast its own outputs. Objectives that are not proper losses, such as DPO or RL, lose this guarantee.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research makes a significant contribution to the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Models, specifically addressing the critical challenge of **uncertainty quantification and calibration**. As LLMs become more integrated into real-world applications, their ability to convey meaningful confidence in their outputs is paramount for trustworthiness and reliability.

Prior research has extensively explored the calibration of LLMs at the token level. It is well-established that *base LLMs*, trained primarily with maximum likelihood loss, often exhibit *next-token calibration* (OpenAI, 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Desai & Durrett, 2020). This means if an LLM assigns 80% probability to the next token being 'A', it is indeed correct 80% of the time for that specific token. However, this granular, token-level calibration does not directly translate to confidence in the overall *semantic meaning* of a long-form response, which is crucial for open-domain question-answering. For instance, an LLM might generate "Paris" or "The capital of France is Paris," both semantically identical but with different token probabilities, making next-token calibration insufficient.

The concept of *semantic confidence* for long-form text is an active area of research. Various approaches have been proposed, including verbalized measures and sampling-based measures like semantic entropy (Farquhar et al., 2024), or sampling-based definitions of confidence (Wang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024; Lamb et al., 2025). However, a comprehensive understanding of whether LLMs *naturally* exhibit such semantic calibration, without explicit training for it, has been lacking. Existing empirical data has shown mixed results, with calibration often depending on a multitude of factors such as the dataset, post-training procedures (e.g., RLHF, DPO), inference techniques (e.g., few-shot, Chain-of-Thought), model size, and architecture (Kadavath et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2023; Leng et al., 2025). The *reasons* behind these dependencies remained largely unexplained.

This paper distinguishes itself by moving beyond empirical observations to provide a *principled explanation* for the emergence of semantic calibration. It builds upon recent theoretical advancements linking calibration to **local loss optimality** in machine learning classifiers (Gopalan et al., 2024; Błasiok et al., 2023b, 2024). By adapting these theoretical insights to the autoregressive nature of LLMs and introducing the concept of "B-calibration" via a generic collapsing function, the authors offer a novel theoretical framework. This work redefines "emergence" not in the sense of capabilities appearing solely due to model scaling (as in Wei et al., 2022), but as a structural regularity that arises implicitly from the system's dynamics during standard training (Krakauer et al., 2025). By grounding LLM semantic calibration in a robust theoretical framework, this research significantly advances our fundamental understanding of how these complex models operate and predict their own uncertainties.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **understand the nature and extent of uncertainty within Large Language Models**, particularly their ability to assess confidence in the *semantic meaning* of their outputs, beyond mere token probabilities. While LLMs excel at generating fluent and often accurate responses, they do not inherently provide meaningful confidence estimates, which is a significant barrier to their deployment in critical applications.

The motivation behind this investigation is multi-faceted:

- **Scientific Inquiry:** A core scientific motivation is to explore whether LLMs "know what they don’t know" (Kadavath et al., 2022). This question delves into the intrinsic properties of LLMs and their internal representations of knowledge and uncertainty.
- **Addressing a Gap in Understanding:** Despite numerous empirical observations regarding LLM calibration, a unifying theoretical explanation for *when* and *why* semantic calibration emerges has been absent. The authors aim to fill this gap by proposing and validating a principled mechanism.
- **Improving LLM Reliability and Trustworthiness:** The ability for LLMs to meaningfully quantify their confidence is crucial for real-world applications. If an LLM can accurately convey its uncertainty, users can make more informed decisions about when to trust its output or seek further verification. This is essential for safety-critical domains, factual QA systems, and enhancing user experience.
- **Extending Calibration Theory to LLMs:** The research seeks to apply and adapt existing, well-understood criteria for uncertainty quantification, such as calibration in classification, to the unique challenges posed by autoregressive language generation. This involves defining a notion of "semantic calibration" that is relevant for open-ended text.

In essence, the paper aims to shift the understanding of LLM confidence from a black-box phenomenon to a theoretically grounded property, enabling more predictable and controllable model behavior.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper combines novel theoretical contributions with extensive empirical validation, forming a coherent framework to explain the emergence of semantic calibration in LLMs.

#### 3.1. Definition of Semantic Calibration (B-Calibration)

The core of the approach is the introduction of **B-calibration**, a general notion of calibration parameterized by a *collapsing function B*. This function `B: V* x VN -> [K]` acts as a post-processor, mapping an LLM's raw text output for a given prompt into one of `K` predefined semantic equivalence classes.

To measure semantic confidence calibration:
1. For a given question (`x`), the LLM generates multiple responses (`M=50`) at a sampling temperature `T=1`.
2. Each generated response is then passed through the collapsing function `B` to extract its canonical semantic class.
3. This process yields an empirical distribution over semantic classes for that question.
4. **Semantic confidence** is defined as the probability of the most-likely semantic class (i.e., the plurality vote among sampled responses).
5. **Semantic accuracy** is a binary indicator: 1 if the most-likely semantic class matches the ground truth, 0 otherwise.
6. An LLM is **semantically confidence-calibrated** if, across a dataset, the predicted semantic confidences statistically align with the empirical semantic accuracies (e.g., if 80% confident, it's 80% accurate). This definition closely relates to concepts like semantic entropy (Farquhar et al., 2024) and Empirical Semantic Confidence (Lamb et al., 2025).

The implementation of `B` varies: for math problems with "concise" or "CoT" prompts, a regex is used. For other settings, a powerful auxiliary LLM (Qwen3-14B-Instruct) is prompted to canonicalize responses into short answers and then cluster them semantically, enabling comparison despite varied phrasing.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Mechanism

The paper's main theoretical contribution lies in establishing a mechanism for why semantic calibration *emerges* from next-token prediction, leveraging a recent connection between calibration and local loss optimality (Błasiok et al., 2023b, 2024; Gopalan et al., 2024). The conjectured mechanism is presented in three steps:

1. **Calibration ↔ Local Loss Optimality (Rigorous Proof - Theorem 7):** The authors formally prove that B-confidence-calibration is *equivalent* to a model being "W_B-locally-loss-optimal" with respect to a specific family of semantic perturbations (W_B). These perturbations represent "easy wins" on the test loss; if an LLM is miscalibrated (e.g., 70% confident but only 60% accurate), a simple perturbation could improve its loss by down-weighting the probability of its top semantic class. An LLM that has been optimized (e.g., during pre-training) should not leave such easy wins on the table. This relationship holds for any proper loss function.
2. **Local Loss Optimality ↔ "Easy to Learn" Perturbations (Informal Assumption/Partial Justification):** This step posits that base LLMs are nearly locally-loss-optimal on their pretraining distribution with respect to *any perturbation that is "easy for the LLM to learn"*. This is a widely plausible assumption in deep learning, implying that training optimizes for representable improvements.
3. **"Easy to Learn" Perturbations ↔ Knowledge of Intermediate B-Confidences (Representational Proof - Theorem 10):** The crucial link for LLMs is how sequence-level perturbations (W_B) can be implemented token-by-token. The authors show that if an LLM "knows" its intermediate B-confidences (i.e., can easily predict its own distribution over B-classes at any point during generation, including before generating the first token), then the corresponding perturbations are "simple to implement." This means the perturbed next-token probabilities can be computed by a shallow arithmetic circuit that combines the original next-token probabilities with these intermediate B-confidences.

**Heuristic Claim 11:** Combining these steps, the core prediction is that a base LLM will be B-confidence-calibrated if the function `G: x -> B_x # p_x` (mapping a question to the model's own distribution over semantic answers) is "easy to learn" for the LLM (e.g., with a LoRA adapter). This simplifies the theoretical predictions into empirically testable hypotheses.

#### 3.3. Experimental Validation

The experimental setup is designed to test three concrete predictions derived from the theory:

- **Prediction 1: Semantic calibration emerges from standard pretraining.** Base LLMs (non-CoT) should be calibrated because `G` is expected to be easy to learn for straightforward questions.
- **Prediction 2: RL post-training can break calibration.** Models fine-tuned with RLHF/DPO optimize for rewards, not necessarily for cross-entropy loss, potentially breaking the local-loss-optimality condition.
- **Prediction 3: Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning can break calibration.** In CoT settings (especially complex math), the model may not "know" its final semantic answer's distribution at the beginning of generation, thus making `G` hard to learn and violating the theoretical conditions.

**Experimental Details:**
- **Datasets:** Four open-ended QA datasets: GSM8K (math word problems), OpenMathInstruct-2 (competition-level math), TriviaQA (trivia), and SimpleQA (factual questions).
- **Models:** A wide range of Qwen, Gemini, Mistral, and Llama models, from 0.5B to 72B parameters, including both base (pre-trained only) and instruction-tuned variants.
- **Prompts:** Three styles: "concise" (single word/phrase), "sentence" (complete sentence), and "chain-of-thought (CoT)" (for math). 5-shot in-context examples are used.
- **Calibration Metric:** SmoothECE (smECE) (Błasiok & Nakkiran, 2024), a kernel-smoothed version of Expected Calibration Error, visualized with reliability diagrams.
- **Quantitative Learnability Probe:** To test Claim 11, small LoRA adapters (rank-8) are trained on base LLMs to predict `G(x)`. The KL divergence between the LoRA's output and the true semantic distribution is measured and correlated with the base model's calibration error.

This rigorous combination of theoretical derivation and extensive empirical testing across diverse models, tasks, and prompting strategies provides strong support for the proposed mechanism.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results robustly validate the theoretical predictions, providing a unified explanation for semantic calibration behavior in LLMs.

1. **Semantic Calibration Emerges from Standard Pretraining:**
 - The experiments unequivocally show that **base LLMs are remarkably semantically calibrated** when operating in non-CoT settings (using "concise" or "sentence" response styles). This holds across various model families (Qwen, Gemini, Mistral, Llama) and sizes (from 0.5B to 72B) on diverse datasets (GSM8K, OpenMathInstruct, TriviaQA, SimpleQA).
 - The calibration quality does not significantly depend on model size for base models in these settings, suggesting it is a fundamental property rather than an emergent ability tied to scaling.
 - Calibration is consistent regardless of whether the model produces single-word or complete-sentence answers, supporting the theory that it pertains to semantic meaning rather than specific syntactic phrasing. The reliability diagrams for these configurations closely follow the diagonal, indicating near-perfect calibration.

2. **RL Post-Training Systematically Breaks Calibration:**
 - Instruction-tuned models (those post-trained with methods like RLHF or DPO) consistently exhibit **miscalibration**, typically demonstrating *overconfidence*. Their reliability diagrams often fall below the diagonal.
 - A direct comparison of a Mistral-7B-v0.1 base model, an SFT-only variant, and a DPO-finetuned variant clearly shows that DPO significantly exacerbates miscalibration, while SFT-only models (still trained with a proper loss) maintain better, though not perfect, calibration. This confirms the prediction that optimization objectives other than cross-entropy loss can disrupt this emergent property.

3. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning Breaks Calibration:**
 - When base models are prompted to use Chain-of-Thought reasoning, particularly in challenging math tasks like GSM8K and OpenMathInstruct, they become **miscalibrated**.
 - Interestingly, base-CoT models tend to be *underconfident* (reliability curve above the diagonal). This is attributed to a "weak plurality" effect: while the CoT process often leads to the correct answer as the plurality choice among multiple samples, the confidence (max probability) in that choice is low because other (incorrect) CoT paths might still accumulate significant probability mass.
 - This finding supports the theory that for CoT, the model does not "know" its final semantic answer distribution from the outset, thus making the underlying conditions for emergent calibration fail.

4. **Quantitative Learnability Probe Validates the Mechanism (Claim 11):**
 - Experiments using LoRA adapters to predict a base model's own semantic class distribution demonstrate a strong correlation: models for which this prediction task is "easy to learn" (i.e., achieve a low KL divergence gap between the LoRA's output and the true semantic distribution) are also the ones that are well-calibrated.
 - Conversely, CoT configurations and smaller models struggling with sentence generation showed higher KL gaps and poor calibration, directly supporting the heuristic claim that the model's ability to immediately assess its output's semantic distribution is key to its calibration.

In summary, the paper provides compelling empirical evidence that semantic calibration is an intrinsic property of standardly pre-trained LLMs in straightforward generation contexts, but this property is fragile and easily disrupted by common alignment techniques and complex reasoning paradigms like CoT.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a substantial step forward in our scientific understanding of Large Language Models and their reliability, with significant implications for both fundamental AI research and practical applications.

#### 5.1. Scientific Significance

- **Principled Explanation for Emergence:** The most profound contribution is providing the *first principled theoretical explanation* for the emergence of semantic calibration in LLMs. By connecting calibration to local loss optimality and the model's ability to implicitly "know" its intermediate semantic confidences, the authors move beyond mere empirical observation to a deeper mechanistic understanding. This theoretical grounding offers a unified lens to comprehend the complex and often counter-intuitive calibration behaviors observed in practice.
- **Demystifying LLM Internal States:** The theory suggests that base LLMs, when appropriately prompted, effectively "know" the semantic distribution of their potential answers even before generating them. This insight offers a glimpse into the internal representations and predictive capabilities of LLMs at a conceptual, rather than merely token-level, granularity.
- **Advancing Calibration Theory:** The introduction of B-calibration as a flexible framework for semantic classification and the quantitative bounds relating calibration to post-processing gaps generalize existing calibration theories to complex generative models like LLMs, opening new avenues for theoretical research.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Foundation for Trustworthy AI:** Meaningful confidence estimates are paramount for building trustworthy and reliable AI systems. By elucidating when and why LLMs are semantically calibrated, this work provides crucial guidance for designing models that can accurately convey their uncertainty. This is vital for high-stakes applications in domains like healthcare, legal tech, or scientific research, where users need to understand the reliability of AI-generated content.
- **Informing Model Design and Fine-Tuning:** The finding that RL post-training (RLHF, DPO) systematically breaks inherent semantic calibration highlights a critical challenge for aligning LLMs. Developers must become aware of this trade-off between maximizing desired behaviors (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness) and maintaining calibration. This could spur research into "calibration-aware" alignment techniques that preserve or restore confidence estimation, perhaps by incorporating calibration metrics directly into reward functions or developing novel post-training methods.
- **Guiding Prompt Engineering:** The observation that Chain-of-Thought reasoning can lead to miscalibration (specifically underconfidence in base models) suggests that how users prompt LLMs influences their uncertainty characteristics. Future prompt engineering strategies might need to account for these effects, or integrate methods to re-calibrate responses generated via complex reasoning paths.
- **Benchmarking and Evaluation:** The methodology for measuring semantic calibration (B-calibration with sampling and semantic collapsing functions) provides a robust and generalizable framework for evaluating LLM uncertainty. This could lead to more sophisticated benchmarks that assess an LLM's "awareness" of its own limitations, rather than just its accuracy.
- **New Research Directions:** The paper explicitly outlines several limitations and future work, including exploring other types of calibration (e.g., verbalized), improving the computational efficiency of semantic confidence measurements (currently expensive due to sampling), extending the theoretical framework to other continuous generative models, and further formalizing the informal steps of the conjectured mechanism. These open questions provide fertile ground for continued research in LLM understanding and development.

In conclusion, "Trained on Tokens, Calibrated on Concepts" is a seminal work that not only uncovers a fascinating emergent property of LLMs but also provides a foundational theoretical framework to understand it. This research is instrumental in advancing LLM capabilities towards greater transparency, reliability, and ultimately, trustworthiness.
