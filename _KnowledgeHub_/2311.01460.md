# Implicit Chain of Thought Reasoning via Knowledge Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Harvard University, Microsoft, and Johns Hopkins University introduce implicit chain of thought reasoning, a method that leverages knowledge distillation to enable language models to perform complex reasoning within their internal hidden states without generating explicit step-by-step outputs. This approach achieves inference speeds comparable to non-reasoning models while outperforming them on tasks like multi-digit multiplication and grade school math problems.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) face challenges with complex reasoning tasks.
- Explicit Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting, while effective, introduces significant computational inefficiency and slows down inference due to generating intermediate steps.
- Current CoT methods constrain LLMs to human-like explicit reasoning, potentially limiting the development of more efficient, architecture-optimized reasoning.

## Method

- Introduction of **implicit chain of thought reasoning** where models perform computations within their internal ==hidden states== instead of generating explicit steps.
- Utilizes ==knowledge distillation==, training a student model to learn reasoning from a teacher model that performs ==explicit Chain of Thought==.
- The process involves three stages: ==Mind-Reading the Teacher== (student predicts answer from teacher's hidden states), ==Thought Emulation== (emulator predicts teacher's hidden states from input), and ==Couple and Optimize== (==end-to-end fine-tuning== of combined system).

## Results

- Implicit CoT models demonstrated improved performance over models without any Chain of Thought on multi-digit multiplication and GSM8K datasets.
- The approach achieved inference speeds comparable to models lacking Chain of Thought, significantly faster than explicit CoT which generates intermediate steps.
- Performance, while improved, still showed an accuracy gap compared to explicit Chain of Thought, suggesting ongoing refinement is needed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language models can perform complex reasoning implicitly by leveraging their internal hidden states and "vertical computation" across layers.
> - Knowledge distillation is an effective mechanism to transfer explicit Chain of Thought reasoning capabilities into an implicit form within a student model.
> - Allowing models to reason internally may enable them to discover more computationally efficient reasoning pathways optimized for their neural architecture.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in various tasks, but one area where they particularly struggle is complex reasoning. The dominant solution to this challenge has been Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting, where models are instructed to "think step by step" and explicitly write out their reasoning process. While effective, this approach has significant limitations in terms of computational efficiency and speed.

A new research paper titled "Implicit Chain of Thought Reasoning via Knowledge Distillation" proposes an innovative alternative. This collaborative effort from researchers at Harvard University, Microsoft, and Johns Hopkins University challenges the notion that language models must reason explicitly like humans do.

#### 1.2. Understanding Chain of Thought Reasoning

Chain of Thought (CoT) reasoning has emerged as a powerful technique to improve the reasoning capabilities of large language models. In explicit CoT, a model is prompted or trained to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer. For example, when solving a math problem, the model might break down the computation step by step:

```
Problem: What is 12 × 3?
Explicit CoT: I need to multiply 12 by 3.
12 × 3 = (10 + 2) × 3
= (10 × 3) + (2 × 3)
= 30 + 6
= 36
Answer: 36
```

This approach mirrors human conscious reasoning, where we deliberately work through a problem step by step. The success of CoT has been demonstrated across various reasoning tasks, from arithmetic to logical reasoning and commonsense question answering.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. The Limitations of Explicit CoT

Despite its effectiveness, explicit CoT has several drawbacks:

1. **Computational Inefficiency**: Generating lengthy intermediate reasoning steps requires significant computational resources and time.
2. **Unnatural Constraint**: Forcing LLMs to reason like humans may prevent them from developing more efficient reasoning methods that leverage their unique neural architecture.
3. **Cognitive Mismatch**: Human reasoning often becomes more implicit and automatic with practice—we don't always need to think step by step once we've mastered a skill.

The paper draws an interesting parallel to human cognition. As humans develop expertise in a domain, their reasoning often transitions from explicit, step-by-step thinking to more implicit, automatic processing. A professional chess player doesn't consciously evaluate every possible move but relies on intuition built from experience. Similarly, the authors propose that language models could benefit from "compiling" explicit reasoning into more implicit forms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Implicit CoT: A Novel Approach

The core innovation of this research is the development of an approach called "implicit chain of thought reasoning." Instead of forcing models to generate explicit intermediate steps, this method aims to have models:

1. Learn to reason within their internal hidden states ("vertical computation")
2. Directly produce answers without the overhead of generating explicit reasoning steps
3. Potentially develop novel reasoning pathways optimized for their neural architecture

The approach exploits the layered structure of transformer models. Rather than reasoning "horizontally" through generated tokens, the model can reason "vertically" through its many layers of hidden states. This leverages the full computational capacity of the transformer architecture.

To achieve this, the researchers use knowledge distillation, a technique where a "student" model learns from a "teacher" model. In this case, the teacher is trained with explicit CoT, while the student learns to implicitly reason and directly generate answers.

#### 3.2. Three-Step Methodology

The implementation of implicit CoT follows a three-step methodology:

#### 3.3. Mind-Reading the Teacher

In this first step, a student model is trained to predict the final answer using the internal hidden states of a teacher model that was trained on explicit CoT reasoning. The teacher solves problems using explicit reasoning, generating intermediate steps. The student doesn't see these steps but instead accesses the teacher's internal hidden state representations.

The student essentially learns to "read the mind" of the teacher, extracting the reasoning information embedded in the teacher's internal states without seeing the explicit reasoning text.

#### 3.4. Thought Emulation

The second step involves training an "emulator" model to predict the teacher's hidden states directly from the input. Instead of accessing the teacher's hidden states, the emulator learns to generate similar hidden state representations from the problem statement alone.

This is achieved through knowledge distillation, where the emulator is trained to minimize the difference between its predicted hidden states and the teacher's actual hidden states. To improve performance, particularly on complex datasets like GSM8K (a collection of grade school math problems), the emulator predicts a mixture of components for the teacher's hidden states using a Gaussian mixture model.

This approach helps address the challenge of multiple valid reasoning pathways for a single problem.

#### 3.5. Couple and Optimize

In the final step, the emulator and the mind-reading student are combined into a single system and fine-tuned end-to-end. This allows the student to develop its own reasoning methods, potentially deviating from the teacher's original approach to find more efficient pathways that better suit its neural architecture.

This coupled system learns to perform the necessary reasoning entirely within its hidden states, without generating any explicit intermediate steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Experimental Results

The researchers evaluated their approach on two challenging reasoning tasks:

1. **Multi-digit multiplication**: A synthetic dataset of multi-digit multiplication problems.
2. **GSM8K**: A collection of grade school math word problems.

The results demonstrate both the promise and current limitations of implicit CoT:

- **Improved Performance**: Models trained with implicit CoT significantly outperformed models without any CoT on both tasks, demonstrating that implicit reasoning is indeed occurring.
- **Efficiency Advantage**: Implicit CoT achieves inference speeds comparable to models without CoT, while explicit CoT models are substantially slower due to the generation of intermediate steps.
- **Model Capacity Requirement**: The effectiveness of implicit CoT depends on having a sufficient number of layers in the model to represent the required intermediate calculations.
- **Performance Gap**: While implicit CoT improves performance compared to no CoT, it still lags behind explicit CoT in terms of accuracy, suggesting that there is room for improvement in the implicit reasoning approach.

The researchers also found that augmenting training datasets with synthetically generated examples improved the performance of implicit CoT, highlighting the importance of data quality and quantity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Implications for AI Research

This research opens up several exciting directions for AI reasoning:

1. **Efficiency in Complex Reasoning**: By eliminating the need to generate explicit reasoning steps, implicit CoT could make complex reasoning tasks more computationally efficient.

2. **Novel Reasoning Architectures**: The approach encourages exploration of alternative reasoning architectures that might better leverage the unique capabilities of neural networks.

3. **Beyond Human-Like Reasoning**: By allowing models to develop their own reasoning methods, we might discover more efficient approaches that differ from human reasoning patterns.

4. **Applications in Resource-Constrained Environments**: The efficiency gains from implicit CoT could make advanced reasoning capabilities available in contexts where computational resources are limited.

5. **Cognitive Science Connections**: The parallel to human cognition, where explicit reasoning often becomes implicit with practice, provides interesting connections to cognitive science and may inspire further research at this intersection.

#### 5.2. Conclusion

"Implicit Chain of Thought Reasoning via Knowledge Distillation" represents a significant step toward more efficient and potentially more powerful reasoning in language models. By moving away from the constraint of explicit, human-like reasoning, this approach opens up new possibilities for how AI systems might solve complex problems.

The research is still in its early stages, with implicit CoT currently underperforming compared to explicit CoT in terms of accuracy. However, the efficiency gains and the novel conceptual approach make this a promising direction for future research. As the authors continue to refine their methodology and as larger, more capable models become available, implicit reasoning might eventually match or even surpass the performance of explicit reasoning approaches.

This work challenges us to rethink the assumption that the best way for AI to reason is to mimic human explicit reasoning. Just as human experts develop intuition and implicit processing skills, AI systems might benefit from developing their own forms of implicit reasoning that leverage their unique computational architecture. This paper takes an important first step in that direction, potentially changing how we think about AI reasoning in the years to come.
