# Manipulator-Independent Representations for Visual Imitation

> [!summary] Summary
> Manipulator-Independent Representations (MIR) is a pretrained visual embedding for third-person imitation of manipulation trajectories across embodiments. It combines a temporally-smooth contrastive loss with cross-domain goal-conditioned policy learning, trained on paired domain-randomized, arm-randomized and invisible-arm simulations. A frozen MIR space supplies goal-reaching rewards to an MPO agent, which tracks stacking trajectories demonstrated by unseen robots, a pick-up stick, and a real human hand.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning and inverse RL need first-person action-state trajectories, unlike humans who imitate by watching another body and replicating its effect on the environment.
- Prior cross-embodiment imitation assumes the demonstrator domain in advance, collecting perceptual training data there or presupposing a human hand, so unseen manipulators break it.
- Goal-conditioned visual imitation works only for short-horizon, near-planar tasks with end-effector control, failing on multi-object contact-rich stacking driven by joint-velocity actions.

## Method

- **Temporally-Smooth Contrastive Networks (TSCN)** replace the hard softmax target of time-contrastive networks with an exponentially decaying distribution over time-steps, penalizing temporally distant confusions more than neighboring ones.
- **Cross-Domain Goal-Conditional Policies (CD-GCP)** regress one-step actions from a current observation plus a future goal drawn from the paired other domain, sharing the encoder with TSCN.
- Three paired simulation environments, domain-randomized, arm-randomized, and an invisible-arm variant, supply temporally aligned trajectories so the embedding tracks environment change while keeping coarse manipulator information.

## Results

- MIR reached **100%** lifting success on invisible-arm, Jaco hand, and real robot demonstrations, versus **27-85%** for time-contrastive networks and **80-100%** for cross-modal distance classification.
- On full stacking, MIR scored **38%**, **81%**, **29%**, **17%**, **11%** across invisible-arm, Jaco, real robot, stick, and human hand, while TCN and goal-conditioned policies scored **0%** everywhere.
- Ablation: TSCN alone reached **67%** Jaco stacking and CD-GCP plus TCN reached **38%**, both below combined MIR's **81%**; CD-GCP alone reached only **8%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cross-embodiment representations should encode the change effected on the environment while still retaining coarse manipulator position; discarding the arm entirely degrades reaching behavior.
> - Softening a contrastive loss target over time makes embedding distance correlate with reachability, which matters whenever the embedding doubles as an RL reward signal.
> - Performance collapses as embodiment and physics gaps widen: 100% lifting can coexist with 11% multi-stage stacking, so report contact-rich long-horizon metrics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic imitation learning is a critical area of artificial intelligence research, addressing the challenge of enabling robots to acquire new skills by observing demonstrations. This work primarily situates itself within the domain of visual imitation, specifically focusing on scenarios where the robot observes a behavior from a "third-person" perspective, without direct access to the demonstrator's internal states or actions. This contrasts with "first-person" imitation, where the robot observes demonstrations from its own viewpoint and often with explicit action data.

Traditional imitation learning approaches, such as behavioral cloning and inverse reinforcement learning, typically assume ample demonstration data with known actions and observations that are similar to the imitating agent's own. However, a significant gap exists between these methods and human imitation capabilities. Humans can observe behavior from diverse sources, including different species, infer the environmental effect, and map it to their own embodiment to achieve similar outcomes. Replicating this "third-person, cross-embodiment" imitation in robots is a major challenge.

Existing third-person imitation methods often encounter limitations related to domain gaps (visual differences between demonstrator and imitator, or simulation and reality), embodiment differences (e.g., human hand vs. robotic gripper), and the complexity of tasks (e.g., simple planar movements vs. multi-object, non-planar manipulation with rich dynamics). Some prior work has focused on one-shot imitation, where an agent adapts to a novel task variation, often requiring the demonstrator's domain to be known during perceptual training or relying on carefully cropped observations to exclude manipulators. Other methods address large domain gaps but are often model-dependent, operate in state space, or are primarily for locomotion tasks. While efforts have been made to generalize to human hand demonstrations using domain randomization or pixel-level translation, these often assume *a priori* knowledge of the demonstrator's morphology (e.g., human hand).

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a method that enables visual imitation of complex manipulation trajectories across significant embodiment and domain gaps, solely from pixel observations, and without *a priori* knowledge of the demonstrator's specific morphology or domain during perceptual training. It aims to develop a representation that is genuinely "manipulator-independent" and focuses on environmental changes rather than specific manipulator movements, which is a critical step toward more general and robust robotic imitation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robotic manipulators to imitate any visually-demonstrated behavior of arbitrary complexity, perceived solely from vision and without access to the demonstrator's proprioceptive information or actions. This encompasses third-person visual imitation, particularly in "cross-embodiment" scenarios where the demonstrator has a different physical form or operates in a different domain (e.g., simulation vs. reality).

The specific motivations and objectives can be detailed as follows:

- **Replicating Human-like Imitation:** The authors are motivated by the human capacity to observe diverse behaviors, understand their environmental impact, and translate this understanding into actions executable by their own bodies. The goal is to imbue robots with a similar perceptual ability to "mirror" demonstrated behavior accurately, even when the demonstrator's embodiment or domain differs significantly from the robot's.
- **Addressing the Perceptual Challenge in Cross-Embodiment Imitation:** The primary goal is to explore how robots can be provided with the perceptual ability to mirror behaviors as accurately as possible, specifically for cross-embodiment scenarios. This involves learning representations that capture the change effected in the world and related details, while abstracting away manipulator-specific information (e.g., joint angles of a specific robotic arm).
- **Developing Manipulator-Independent Representations (MIR):** The core technical objective is to identify and learn an appropriate representation method that allows a reinforcement learning (RL) agent to visually track complex, non-planar manipulation trajectories involving multiple-object interactions. This representation must be effective even when demonstrations come from experts with different embodiments and large visual and dynamics differences (e.g., simulation-to-reality gap).
- **Identifying Key Characteristics for Robust Representations:** The authors hypothesize that successful cross-embodiment visual imitation requires representations with three crucial characteristics:
 1. **Cross-domain alignment:** The representation space should be capable of aligning trajectories despite large visual and dynamic differences between domains.
 2. **Temporal smoothness:** Observations that are close in time should have similar representations, even across different domains, facilitating sequential goal tracking.
 3. **Actionability:** The representations must be suitable for training an RL agent, meaning they encode information relevant for policy learning and action prediction.
- **Enabling High-Fidelity Trajectory Tracking:** The research focuses on imitating single trajectories as precisely as possible, viewing the problem through the lens of trajectory tracking with RL. This distinguishes it from cloning policies or one-shot imitation that might prioritize generalization across task variations rather than exact replication of a specific sequence of goals.
- **Generalizing to Unseen Manipulators and Domains:** A specific objective is for the learned representations to be effective even for unseen manipulators (e.g., human hands) and domains (e.g., real-world scenarios not explicitly included in training data), going beyond approaches that assume prior knowledge of demonstrator characteristics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this paper consists of two primary phases: (a) learning a Manipulator-Independent Representation (MIR) space, and (b) utilizing this pre-trained MIR space for cross-embodiment visual imitation through reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Learning Manipulator-Independent Representations (MIR)

The core of the method involves learning a common embedding space designed to align trajectories from different domains and embodiments. The MIR learning process aims to embed three key properties into the representations: cross-domain alignment, temporal smoothness, and actionability.

##### 3.1.1. Cross-Domain Alignment

To achieve domain invariance and focus on environmental changes, the authors utilize domain randomization techniques during representation training. This involves training on data from two pairs of simulated environments:
- **'Domain-randomized' and 'invisible arm' environments:** These are designed to help the representation prioritize changes in the environment, largely ignoring the manipulator itself. In the 'invisible arm' environment, the robot arm and gripper are visually invisible, forcing the representation to learn features based on object configurations and movements.
- **'Domain-randomized' and 'arm-randomized' environments:** These environments ensure that the representation still encodes some information about the rough motion or presence of a manipulator, but without focusing on its specific visual characteristics. The 'arm-randomized' environment applies randomization only to the arm and gripper, while the 'domain-randomized' environment applies randomization to all entities (colors, textures, lighting, camera poses, and physics properties) to create visual diversity and robustness.

##### 3.1.2. Temporal Smoothness (Temporally-Smooth Contrastive Networks - TSCN)

To ensure temporal smoothness—that temporally neighboring observations have similar representations even across domains—the method builds upon Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN).
- **TCN Foundation:** TCN enforces similarity between temporally aligned observation pairs from two different camera views (or domains in this context) while distinguishing them from other non-matching pairs.
- **TSCN Enhancement:** The authors introduce Temporally-Smooth Contrastive Networks (TSCN) by modifying the TCN objective. TSCN incorporates a temporally-smooth distribution `p_ik` in its loss function (Equation 1):
 ```
    min_φ (- Σ_i Σ_k p_ik log (exp(x_i^T * x̄_k) / Σ_j exp(x_i^T * x̄_j)))
    where p_ik = exp(-|i - k|) / Σ_u exp(-|i - u|)
    ```
 This modification penalizes misclassification of temporally distant negative pairs more heavily than nearby ones, promoting smoother embedding space transitions. Negative pairs from other sequences in the mini-batch are also included to improve alignment quality.
- **Reachability Correlation:** The effectiveness of TSCN is evaluated by its ability to correlate embedding space distance with reachability distance. Human stacking trajectories are used as a proxy for optimal actions, assuming a linear increase in reachability distance over time. TSCN is shown to produce a smoother increase in cross-domain embedding distance, better reflecting reachability, compared to standard TCN.

##### 3.1.3. Actionable Representations (Cross-Domain Goal-Conditional Policies - CD-GCP)

To make the representations actionable and suitable for RL, the method incorporates an offline goal-conditioned policy training objective.
- **Goal-Conditioned Policy Training:** A standard goal-conditioned policy `π(o, g)` predicts an action `â` to move from current observation `o` to goal observation `g`. This is typically trained by minimizing `||π(o_i, o_{i+j}) - a_i||^2` over collected episodes.
- **Cross-Domain Extension (CD-GCP):** The method extends this to Cross-Domain Goal-Conditional Policies (CD-GCP). Given paired sequences from two domains, `{(o_{i+j}, a_{i+j})}` and `{(x̄_{i+j}, x̄_{i+j})}`, `π_×` is trained to minimize `||π_×(o_i, x̄_{i+j}) - a_i||^2`. Critically, the goals (`x̄_{i+j}`) are drawn from the *other* domain.
- **Combined MIR:** The final MIR space is learned by training CD-GCPs together with TSCN, where the embedding network `φ` is shared. This combined objective ensures the representation is both temporally smooth across domains and encodes action-relevant information, resulting in improved correlation with reachability distance.

##### 3.1.4. MIR Training Data Collection

A dataset of 7,194 canonical trajectories (reaching, grasping, lifting, stacking) with three geometric objects was generated in a simulated environment. These canonical trajectories were then replicated in the 'invisible arm', 'domain-randomized', and 'arm-randomized' environments. The trajectories from the 'domain-randomized' environment were paired with equivalent trajectories from the other two domains, resulting in 14,388 paired episodes. A total of 10,792 paired trajectories were used for MIR training, with the rest reserved for validation and hyperparameter tuning. The policies used for data collection were trained in the canonical simulation environment using Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization (MPO) from fully-observed states.

#### 3.2. Cross-Embodiment Imitation via Reinforcement Learning

After training the MIR encoder, it is frozen and used by an RL agent to perform trajectory tracking.
- **Goal Definition:** A target cross-embodiment trajectory `{x̄_i}` is given as a sequence of observations. Intermediate goals `{x̄_i}` are uniformly sampled from this trajectory.
- **Goal Tracking:** Only one goal is active at a time. The agent receives a reward for reaching the current active goal, after which the next goal in the sequence becomes active.
- **Reward Function:** The reward `r(o, x̄_g)` is defined as 1 if `exp(-w||φ(o) - φ(x̄_g)||²)` is above a threshold `ε`, and 0 otherwise. `φ(o)` and `φ(x̄_g)` are the current observation and goal embeddings, respectively. `w` is a normalization factor based on the mean Euclidean distance between neighboring encoded observations in the demonstration, and `ε` is a randomly sampled threshold.
- **RL Algorithm:** Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization (MPO) is used to train the RL agent to follow these goals, with actions corresponding to velocities of robot joints and gripper.

#### 3.3. Environments for Evaluation

The study utilizes eight distinct domains (Figure 4). The imitating agent consistently operates in a *canonical MuJoCo simulated environment* with a Sawyer arm and Robotiq 2-finger gripper.
- **Training Environments (for MIR data generation):** 'Invisible arm', 'arm-randomized', and 'domain-randomized' (all simulated).
- **Demonstration Environments (for evaluation):**
 - **Simulated:** 'Jaco hand' (robot arm with a 3-finger Jaco hand).
 - **Real-world:** 'Real robot' (Sawyer arm with Robotiq 2-finger gripper), 'pick-up stick' (human manipulating objects with a stick), and 'human hand' (human manipulating objects directly). These represent increasing levels of domain gap and embodiment difference compared to the imitating agent's environment.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Trajectories

The evaluation focuses on the complex manipulation task of *stacking one geometric object on top of another in the presence of a third distractor object*. This task was chosen because it involves multi-object interaction, rich contact dynamics, and represents a non-trivial, non-planar behavior. Ten stacking sequences were collected from each of the five demonstration domains. Each agent was evaluated 100 times per trajectory, totaling 1,000 trajectories per domain-method combination, and success rates for "lifting the top object" and "stacking the top object" were reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation demonstrated the efficacy of the proposed Manipulator-Independent Representations (MIR) method for cross-embodiment visual imitation, particularly in challenging scenarios involving significant domain gaps and complex manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Comparison of Imitation Performance with Baselines

The MIR method was quantitatively compared against several baselines: naive Goal-Conditioned Policies (GCP), Temporal Distance Classification (TDC), Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN), and Cross-Modal Distance Classification (CMC). Evaluation was performed on 100 attempts for each of 10 demonstrated stacking trajectories across five domains: 'invisible arm', 'Jaco hand', 'real robot', 'pick-up stick', and 'human hand'. Success rates for "lifting" and "stacking" behaviors were reported (Table I).

- **Overall Superiority of MIR:** MIR consistently achieved the best performance across all test domains for both lifting and stacking stages. For instance, in the 'Jaco hand' (simulated, unseen manipulator), MIR achieved 100% lifting success and 81% stacking success, significantly outperforming TCN (85% lifting, 0% stacking) and CMC (100% lifting, 66% stacking).
- **Performance in Simulated Domains:** MIR successfully imitated 'invisible arm' (100% lifting, 38% stacking) and 'Jaco hand' (100% lifting, 81% stacking) sequences. The 'invisible arm' domain, despite being used in training, still posed challenges for stacking due to the lack of manipulator visual cues in the early stages of a trajectory.
- **Performance in Real-World Domains:** As the domain gap increased with real-world sequences, the performance of all methods generally decreased. However, MIR maintained a notable advantage. For the 'real robot' domain, MIR achieved 100% lifting success and 29% stacking success. In the 'pick-up stick' domain, MIR achieved 44% lifting success and 17% stacking success. For the most challenging 'human hand' domain, MIR achieved 50% lifting success and 11% stacking success. Importantly, MIR was the only method that demonstrated successful full stacking trajectories in all three real-world settings.
- **Difficulty of Stacking:** Imitating the full stacking behavior, which involves long-horizon episodes with multi-object contact-rich dynamics, proved significantly harder than just lifting. Only CMC and MIR achieved considerable performance in stacking, highlighting the complexity of this task for visual imitation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study (Table II) investigated the contribution of each component within the MIR framework (TSCN and CD-GCP).

- **TSCN vs. TCN:** Replacing the proposed Temporally-Smooth Contrastive Networks (TSCN) with standard Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN) consistently led to a decrease in performance. For example, 'TSCN' alone achieved 100% lifting success for 'invisible arm', 'Jaco hand', and 'real robot', whereas 'TCN' achieved 79%, 85%, and 27% respectively. This indicates that the temporal smoothness aspect introduced in TSCN is beneficial.
- **Individual Components vs. Combination:** When comparing the individual components (TSCN, CD-GCP) with their combination in MIR (CD-GCP + TSCN), the combined MIR method consistently yielded the best results. For instance, for 'human hand' stacking, CD-GCP achieved 0% success, TSCN achieved 7% success, while MIR achieved 11% success. Similarly, for 'real robot' stacking, CD-GCP achieved 8% success, TSCN achieved 67% success (a notable outlier, possibly due to task-specific alignment properties), and MIR achieved 29% success, generally demonstrating the combined benefit.
- **CD-GCP's Contribution:** While CD-GCP alone did not perform as strongly as TSCN, its inclusion in MIR generally improved overall robustness and performance, particularly in scenarios with larger domain gaps. For instance, for 'pick-up stick' lifting, TSCN alone achieved 20%, but MIR achieved 44%.

These results indicate that both the temporal smoothness aspect of TSCN and the actionability conferred by CD-GCP are crucial for the robust performance of MIR in cross-embodiment visual imitation. Qualitative examples and t-SNE visualizations (Figure 5) further support the learned embedding spaces' ability to align trajectories across domains, particularly at points of environmental change.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic imitation learning, particularly in addressing the long-standing challenges of third-person, cross-embodiment visual imitation. The primary contribution of this work lies in the development of Manipulator-Independent Representations (MIR), which allow robots to learn complex manipulation skills by observing diverse demonstrations without *a priori* knowledge of the demonstrator's morphology or domain.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities for Robots:** By enabling robots to imitate behaviors demonstrated by previously unseen manipulators (including human hands) and across substantial visual and physical domain gaps (simulation-to-reality), this research moves robotic learning closer to human-like observational learning. This greatly expands the types of demonstrations a robot can leverage, making learning more flexible and robust.
- **Reduced Cost and Effort in Robot Skill Acquisition:** If robots can learn complex skills by simply observing humans or other robots, the need for laborious manual programming, extensive reward engineering in RL, or task-specific data collection is significantly reduced. This could dramatically decrease the cost and time associated with deploying robots in new tasks or environments.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** A robot capable of understanding and replicating human-demonstrated actions without needing to share the same physical form would lead to more intuitive and effective human-robot collaboration. Humans could teach robots new skills naturally, simply by performing them.
- **Robustness to Domain Gap and Embodiment Differences:** The demonstrated ability of MIR to handle large visual and dynamic domain gaps, including generalizing from simulation to the real world and across diverse manipulator morphologies (e.g., 2-finger gripper to 3-finger Jaco hand, or robotic arm to human hand/stick), addresses a critical bottleneck in robotic transfer learning.
- **Actionable and Temporally Smooth Representations:** The development of representations that are both temporally smooth (allowing for precise trajectory tracking) and actionable (suitable for RL policy learning) provides a foundation for high-fidelity imitation of multi-stage, contact-rich manipulation tasks, which have historically been challenging for visual imitation methods.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a method for learning representations that primarily focus on environmental change rather than manipulator-specific details. This principle could inspire future research in perception for generalizable robotics, potentially leading to more versatile and intelligent autonomous systems capable of understanding and interacting with the world more abstractly.
