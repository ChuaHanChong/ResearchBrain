# Current as Touch: Proprioceptive Contact Feedback for Compliant Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Current as Touch turns motor current and joint states into tactile-like contact feedback for dexterous hands lacking tactile sensors. An ACT-style model predicts a compliance reference position, a joint target whose PD tracking error yields appropriate grasp force, supervised by human teleoperation commands. Across four contact-rich tasks it eliminates cup deformation and lifts card-picking success from 55.8% to 76.9%.

## Problem

- Tactile and force-torque sensors give direct contact measurement but add cost, fragility, calibration burden, and integration complexity, blocking deployment on low-cost position-controlled dexterous hands.
- Impedance and admittance compliance methods assume torque-level control, accurate dynamics, or wrench sensing, none of which match the joint-position command interface of mainstream hands.
- Prior current-based approaches still recover explicit external wrenches through motor models and Jacobians, or lean on dense tactile observations and task-specific reinforcement learning.

## Method

- ==Compliance reference position (CRP)==: **Current as Touch** outputs a joint-position target whose PD tracking error against measured position generates grasp force, bypassing wrench estimation and torque commands.
- ==Human-in-the-loop supervision==: recorded teleoperation commands act as noisy but task-valid CRP labels, while user intent enters only as command velocity, preventing target leakage and shortcut learning.
- ==ACT-style encoder== over a 10-frame proprioceptive history with raw zero-delay current, plus a training-only ==auxiliary head== regressing median-filtered smoothed current; execution averages three recent chunk predictions.

## Results

- A current-plus-position regressor tracks measured normal force with **10.09g** RMSE on Dex3 and **17.75g** on LEAP Hand, R-squared **0.99** and **0.95**.
- Teleoperated cup stacking shows **0%** deformation versus **43.3%** for direct retargeting, with completion time cut from **21.9s** to **16.8s**; novice board-wiping success rose **40%** to **100%**.
- Bottle-holding stability at 250g load jumped from **16.7%** to **100%** with zero drops at out-of-distribution 350g; card picking strict success rose **55.8%** to **76.9%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actuator current is a free proprioceptive contact channel: any motor-driven hand already senses resistance, so tactile hardware is not a prerequisite for compliance.
> - Predicting a position reference instead of a force keeps compliance inside the PD interface that teleoperation and behavior-cloning stacks already speak.
> - Feed raw noisy signals but regularize with smoothed auxiliary targets: filtering at inference buys cleanliness at the cost of phase delay when contact changes fastest.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation, particularly in real-world scenarios involving fragile, deformable, or dynamically changing objects, necessitates robotic systems capable of continuous force regulation, commonly referred to as compliance. The broader research landscape for achieving such compliance typically involves equipping robot hands with external tactile sensors or force/torque (F/T) sensors. These sensors provide direct measurements of contact forces, slip, and local geometry, enabling force-aware control strategies such as hybrid position/force control, impedance control, and variable impedance control. Numerous developments in this area include high-resolution tactile fingertips like GelSight and DIGIT, scalable tactile skins such as ReSkin and AnySkin, and compact F/T sensors like CoinFT and UMI-FT. Many recent learning systems integrate these tactile or visuo-tactile observations into manipulation policies.

However, this reliance on external sensing introduces several challenges. These sensors are often costly, fragile, require intricate calibration, and can be complex to integrate into robotic systems, particularly low-cost dexterous hands. Many widely used robot hands are primarily designed for joint-level position control, utilizing low-level proportional-derivative (PD) controllers. This creates a disparity between the need for compliant interaction and the hardware interfaces commonly available, limiting the widespread deployment of advanced compliant manipulation capabilities.

Sensorless force estimation and current-based interaction control represent a related area that attempts to mitigate the need for external F/T sensors by inferring external forces or joint torques from intrinsic motor signals, joint states, disturbance observers, or actuator models. Prior work has explored calibrating motor current into joint torque for force-based grasping when combined with dense tactile feedback. Additionally, methods like Minimalist Compliance Control have demonstrated the potential of actuator signals to substitute for external F/T sensors in compliance control by estimating external wrenches and updating position references via a task-space admittance controller.

This work addresses the identified gap by proposing a method that leverages readily available intrinsic signals—motor current and joint states—to infer contact properties without external tactile or F/T sensors. Crucially, it departs from explicit force/wrench estimation or direct torque commands. Instead, it formulates compliance as the prediction of a "compliance reference position" (CRP), which is a joint-position target that, when tracked by a standard PD controller, induces appropriate compliant torques. This position-based formulation is designed to be directly compatible with existing position-controlled robot hands and mainstream teleoperation and policy-learning pipelines, thereby offering a more accessible and integrated solution for compliant dexterous manipulation within the current robotic hardware ecosystem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable compliant dexterous manipulation on low-cost robot hands without requiring external tactile or force sensors. This objective is motivated by several key observations and challenges:

1. **Demand for Compliance in Dexterous Manipulation:** Real-world dexterous manipulation tasks frequently involve interaction with objects that are fragile, deformable, or subject to dynamic changes. Examples include stacking paper cups, wiping surfaces with sustained pressure, retrieving thin objects, or adapting to changing loads while pouring liquids. These tasks necessitate continuous regulation of contact forces to maintain stable grasping, prevent damage, and execute tasks effectively. Purely rigid position control is often insufficient in these scenarios, as minor inaccuracies in target positions can lead to excessive forces, object deformation, or slip.

2. **Limitations of External Sensors:** Existing solutions for achieving compliance often rely on external tactile or force/torque sensors. While these sensors provide direct and detailed contact information, they introduce considerable drawbacks:
 - **Cost:** Implementing and maintaining these sensors adds significant expense to robotic systems.
 - **Fragility:** External sensors can be delicate and susceptible to damage during contact-rich tasks.
 - **Calibration and Integration Complexity:** They require extensive calibration and complex integration efforts, increasing development and deployment overhead.
 - **Hardware Compatibility:** Many low-cost dexterous robot hands are designed with simple position-control interfaces and low-level PD controllers, making the integration of external force/torque feedback or direct torque control challenging.

3. **Untapped Potential of Intrinsic Signals:** Actuator torque, which directly generates contact forces, is closely related to motor current. This relationship suggests that motor current, when combined with joint states (position and velocity), can provide an intrinsic, built-in proprioceptive signal reflecting useful contact cues such as contact resistance, force, grasp loading, and changes in object interaction. Empirical observations presented in the paper demonstrate that motor current and joint states vary consistently with measured contact force across different dexterous hands (e.g., Dex3 and LEAP Hand), indicating their potential as a source of contact information.

4. **Compatibility with Existing Control Paradigms:** The goal of compliant manipulation is not solely to estimate contact force but to regulate robot motion based on physical interaction. Most teleoperation systems and learned policies for dexterous hands operate by outputting target joint positions, which are then tracked by low-level PD controllers. A direct estimation of contact force or a method requiring torque-level commands would create a mismatch with these common position-based interfaces. Therefore, a key motivation is to convert tactile-like proprioceptive feedback into a position reference that can induce appropriate compliant torques through standard PD control, thus ensuring compatibility with existing robot learning and control pipelines.

In summary, the core motivation is to overcome the practical limitations of external sensing for compliant manipulation by leveraging readily available intrinsic motor signals. The key objective is to develop a proprioception-driven framework that predicts a "compliance reference position" (CRP) from motor current and joint states, thereby enabling contact-aware compliance that is compatible with widely used position-control interfaces for dexterous hands.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on a proprioception-driven compliance framework that learns contact-aware compliance cues from motor current and joint states. The core idea is to predict a Compliance Reference Position (CRP) rather than explicitly estimating external wrenches or commanding torque.

#### 3.1. Compliance Reference Position (CRP) Formulation

The method formulates compliance as a reference-position prediction problem. Given the current measured joint position (`q_t`) and velocity (`q̇_t`), the model predicts a compliance reference position (`q_c_ref,t`). This `q_c_ref,t` serves as the ideal joint-position target for the robot's low-level PD controller, which generates torque (`τ_t`) according to:
`τ_t = K_p (q_c_ref,t - q_t) - K_d q̇_t`
where `K_p` and `K_d` are fixed PD gains. The predicted `q_c_ref,t` is intended to induce appropriate contact force—preventing excessive force that could crush an object, and ensuring sufficient normal force to prevent slipping. This approach maintains compatibility with the position-control interfaces common in low-cost dexterous hands.

#### 3.2. Learning CRPs from Human-in-the-Loop Demonstrations

The supervision for `q_c_ref` is derived from human teleoperation demonstrations. During data collection, operators adjust the target joint position (`q_cmd`) based on visual feedback of task outcomes (e.g., object deformation, slip, stable grasp). This `q_cmd` trajectory is treated as the supervisory signal for the CRP: `q_c,∗_ref,t = q_cmd,t`. These labels are not analytically optimal but represent human-corrected, task-valid CRP targets.

To prevent the model from merely copying the user command during inference, user intent is represented by the command velocity: `v_intent,t = q_cmd,t - q_cmd,t-1`. This intent velocity captures whether the user is trying to grasp, release, or maintain contact, and with what aggression. The model is trained to combine this intent with current hand state and motor-current feedback. The demonstration data manifold reveals distinct patterns: in free space, position changes result in small current variations; upon contact, small position changes induce large current variations, providing contact stiffness information (`ΔI/Δq`).

#### 3.3. Current-Conditioned Teleoperation and Policy Learning

The framework supports two operational modes:

- **Teleoperation Mode:** The model assists a human operator by translating their intent into a contact-aware CRP.
 - **Observation Input (`o_teleop_t`):** History of joint positions (`q`), raw motor currents (`I`), and user intent velocities (`v_intent`) over a time horizon `T` (e.g., `T=10` frames).
 - **Prediction:** The model `f_θ` predicts the next compliance reference position: `ˆq_c_ref,t = f_θ(o_teleop_t)`.
 - The predicted CRP then drives the hand via the standard PD controller.

- **Policy-Learning Mode:** In autonomous settings, the user intent is replaced by task-level perception (e.g., object pose `g_t`).
 - **Observation Input (`o_policy_t`):** History of joint positions (`q`), raw motor currents (`I`), and task-level object/goal pose (`g_t`) over `T` frames.
 - **Prediction:** The model `f_θ` predicts `ˆq_c_ref,t`.
 - Motor current provides contact state information, allowing the policy to adapt actions based on contact formation, slip, or load, even for similar visual states.

Both modes are trained using a reference prediction loss: `L_ref = ||ˆq_c_ref,t - q_c,∗_ref,t||^2_2`. The implementation broadly follows the sequence modeling approach of ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), where the model predicts a chunk of future actions. During real-robot execution, a short exponential moving average is applied to the most recent predictions to reduce hardware jitter while maintaining responsiveness.

#### 3.4. Raw-Current Encoding with Smoothed-Current Auxiliary Supervision

Motor current is informative but can be noisy due to high-frequency PWM, communication spikes, and actuator disturbances. To address this:
- **Raw Current Input:** The model receives raw, zero-delay current to ensure responsiveness to sudden contact changes.
- **Auxiliary Supervision:** An auxiliary decoder `h_ϕ` is trained to predict an *offline-smoothed* version of the current (`¯I_t`) from the latent representation (`z_t`) generated by the observation encoder. The smoothing uses a median filter followed by a uniform moving average to preserve slow-varying load-dependent trends.
- **Auxiliary Loss (`L_cur`):** `L_cur = ||ˆ¯I_t - ¯I_t||^2_2`.
- **Total Training Objective:** `L = L_ref + λ_cur L_cur`, where `λ_cur` balances the auxiliary loss.
This auxiliary loss regularizes the latent representation to capture relevant current patterns without introducing filtering delays into the online control loop, as the auxiliary branch is used only during training.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details (from Appendix)

- **Hardware:** LEAP Hand on Franka Research 3 and Dex3 on Unitree G1.
- **Data:** Datasets include 550 trajectories for teleoperated grasping (10 diverse objects + free space), 100 for whiteboard wiping, 100 for dynamic bottle holding (with water pouring), and 150 for single-card picking. Trajectories are 8-15 seconds long at ~30 Hz.
- **Preprocessing:** Raw joint positions, target joint positions, and raw motor currents are recorded. User intent is derived as command velocity. Current smoothing for auxiliary labels uses a median filter (k_m=5) and moving average (k_u=5).
- **Chunking:** Observation history length `H=10` frames, action chunk length `K=10` frames. Small-motion filtering is applied to policy-learning data to remove ambiguous static segments.
- **Normalization:** Min-max normalization is applied to all tensors based on training split statistics.
- **Model Architecture:** ACT-style sequence model with a temporal convolutional observation encoder, an action-style encoder (for Gaussian latent style during training), and a four-layer Transformer decoder for the future action chunk. An MLP serves as the auxiliary current head.
- **Training:** AdamW optimizer, learning rate 1e-4, batch size 32, 300 epochs. KL regularization weight (`λ_KL`) is annealed.
- **Execution Aggregation:** During real-robot execution, the most recent `M` predicted CRPs are aggregated using an exponential average. `M=3` was found to balance smoothness and responsiveness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proprioception-driven compliance framework across a range of contact-rich tasks on multiple dexterous hands. Comparisons are made against direct teleoperation (Retargeting) and a current-free model (`w/o Current`) for teleoperation tasks, and an ACT-style behavior cloning baseline for policy-learning tasks.

#### 4.1. Teleoperation Tasks

- **Foam-cup stacking (LEAP Hand):**
 - **Results:** The `w/ Current` method achieved 100% success for both novice and skilled operators. For novices, it reduced completion time from 21.9s (Retargeting) and 31.1s (`w/o Current`) to 16.8s, eliminating cup deformation and grasp failures. Skilled operators also saw reduced time and 0% deformation/failure.
 - **Analysis:** The `w/o Current` model, relying only on kinematics and user intent, struggled with sustained compliant grasping, exhibiting high grasp failure rates (76.7% for novice, 71.7% for skilled). This indicates that motor current provides crucial feedback for fragile object handling.

- **Board-wiping (Dex3 on Unitree G1):**
 - **Results:** `w/ Current` achieved 100% success for both novice and skilled operators, reducing average completion time (e.g., from 9.3s to 6.9s for novice).
 - **Analysis:** This task requires sustained contact force. The `w/o Current` model did not consistently improve over direct teleoperation, suggesting that a simple motion prior is insufficient. Current feedback enabled the model to detect and maintain appropriate pressure against the board, leading to improved reliability and efficiency.

#### 4.2. Policy Learning Tasks

- **Dynamic bottle holding (LEAP Hand, water pouring):**
 - **Results:**
 - At 0g load, both methods achieved 100% stability.
 - As water was poured (increasing load), `w/ Current` consistently outperformed `w/o Current`.
 - At 250g (within training distribution), `w/ Current` achieved 100% stability compared to `w/o Current`'s 16.7% stability (with significant slips and drops).
 - At 350g (out-of-distribution load), `w/ Current` prevented all bottle drops (41.7% stable, 58.3% slipped), whereas `w/o Current` failed catastrophically in all trials (100% fell).
 - **Analysis:** This task highlights the ability of motor current to provide load-sensitive signals (e.g., from finger abduction/adduction motors), enabling the policy to adapt grip force dynamically. This allowed for partial generalization to unseen loads and prevented catastrophic failures under high stress.

- **Single-card picking (Dex3 on Unitree G1):**
 - **Results:** `w/ Current` significantly improved strict success (picking exactly one card) from 55.8% to 76.9%. Tolerant success (picking one or two cards) increased from 65.4% to 90.4%. Failures due to drawing more than two cards dropped from 7.7% to 0.0%.
 - **Analysis:** Current feedback helped the policy distinguish between sufficient contact for gripping a single card and excessive normal force that might pick multiple cards. Without current, the policy relied solely on position and object pose, leading to less precise force regulation.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Appendix)

- **Execution Aggregation Window (Dynamic Bottle Holding):** Using an exponential average over `M=3` recent CRP predictions (`q_exec_t`) provided the best balance between stable initial grasp acquisition and responsiveness to dynamic load changes. `M=1` led to jitter, while `M=4` introduced too much delay for dynamic adaptation.

- **KL Weight for ACT-style Latent (Teleoperation Fist-Closing & Bottle Grasping):**
 - **Teleoperation:** A higher KL weight (stronger regularization) made the predicted CRPs more conservative and less responsive to online user intent, reducing teleoperation success rates. Lower KL weights allowed the model to follow user intent more closely.
 - **Bottle Grasping:** The policy's success was less sensitive to the KL weight, suggesting that for this task, proprioceptive and task-state inputs had a more dominant influence than fine-grained action style variations.

Overall, the results consistently demonstrate that incorporating motor current as proprioceptive contact feedback significantly enhances compliant manipulation across diverse tasks and robot hands. It improves teleoperation safety and efficiency, and boosts the robustness and success rate of learned policies in contact-rich scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research "Current as Touch: Proprioceptive Contact Feedback for Compliant Dexterous Manipulation" presents a significant advancement in the field of dexterous manipulation, with several potential impacts:

1. **Increased Accessibility of Compliant Manipulation:** By obviating the need for expensive, fragile, and complex external tactile or force sensors, this work democratizes compliant manipulation. It makes advanced contact-aware control accessible to a wider range of robot platforms, particularly those utilizing low-cost dexterous hands that are typically constrained to position-control interfaces. This can accelerate research and development in robotics by lowering hardware barriers.

2. **Leveraging Intrinsic Hardware Capabilities:** The method innovatively utilizes intrinsic signals—motor current and joint states—that are inherent to virtually all motor-driven robot hands. This approach avoids additional hardware integration, calibration, and maintenance, streamlining system design and deployment. It demonstrates that valuable contact information can be extracted from existing robot components, making robots more autonomous and adaptable without increasing their physical complexity.

3. **Compatibility with Existing AI and Control Paradigms:** The formulation of compliance as a "compliance reference position" (CRP) is a key contribution to system compatibility. By predicting joint-position targets that induce appropriate compliant torques through standard PD controllers, the method seamlessly integrates with mainstream position-based teleoperation systems and prevalent robot learning pipelines (e.g., behavior cloning, reinforcement learning architectures that output position commands). This eliminates the need for substantial re-engineering of existing control architectures or learning algorithms to accommodate direct force or torque commands.

4. **Enhanced Robot Performance and Robustness:** The experimental results demonstrate consistent improvements in several critical aspects of robot performance:
 - **Safety:** Reduced object deformation and damage when handling fragile items (e.g., foam cups).
 - **Efficiency:** Faster task completion times in teleoperation scenarios.
 - **Robustness:** Improved stability in tasks involving dynamic loads (e.g., pouring water into a bottle) and better precision for delicate interactions (e.g., single-card picking). The ability to generalize to out-of-distribution loads without catastrophic failure further highlights the robustness gained.

5. **Broadened Application Scope for Dexterous Manipulation:** The demonstrated capabilities across diverse contact-rich tasks—from delicate handling to sustained contact and dynamic load adaptation—suggest that this framework can enable dexterous robots to tackle a wider array of real-world problems. This could include applications in manufacturing, logistics, domestic assistance, and healthcare, where sensitive object interaction and adaptive manipulation are crucial.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages further exploration of intrinsic sensing modalities, potentially integrating other proprioceptive signals beyond current. It also opens avenues for investigating how best to combine learned compliance with perception and localization systems for fully autonomous, robust, and generalizable dexterous manipulation in unstructured environments. The explicit recognition of motor current's informative yet noisy nature and the proposed auxiliary supervision technique offer a practical approach for handling such signals in learning-based control.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances compliant dexterous manipulation by offering a practical, accessible, and high-performing solution that leverages existing robot hardware. Its impact lies in enabling more robust and versatile robotic interactions, potentially accelerating the adoption of dexterous robots in real-world applications where intelligent contact regulation is paramount.
