# Diffusion Models Are Real-Time Game Engines

> [!summary] Summary
> Google Research and Google DeepMind developed GameNGen, a neural model that simulates the classic first-person shooter DOOM in real-time. This system achieves 20 frames per second on a single TPU-v5 and maintains high visual fidelity and stability over multi-minute interactive play, with human evaluators often unable to distinguish the simulation from the original game over extended periods.

## Problem

- Traditional video game engines are manually programmed with explicit rules for game state updates and rendering, requiring extensive human development.
- Previous attempts at neural game simulation struggled with limitations in game complexity, real-time performance, long-term stability, or visual quality.
- Auto-regressive generation in interactive systems often suffers from accumulating prediction errors, leading to rapid degradation and instability (auto-regressive drift).

## Method

- A two-phase training approach is used: first, a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent plays DOOM to generate a large, diverse dataset of action-observation trajectories.
- A pre-trained Stable Diffusion v1.4 model is repurposed and fine-tuned, with action embeddings replacing text conditioning and previous frames concatenated into the U-Net's latent space.
- Key innovations include 'noise augmentation' to contextual frames during training to mitigate auto-regressive drift, and separate fine-tuning of the latent auto-encoder's decoder to enhance visual fidelity.

## Results

- GameNGen simulates DOOM at 20 frames per second (FPS) on a single TPU-v5, remaining stable over multi-minute interactive play sessions.
- Human evaluators distinguish the real game from the GameNGen simulation only **58%** to **60%** of the time in short clips (**1.6s** to **3.2s**), and at chance level (**50%**) after 5-10 minutes of continuous auto-regressive generation.
- Noise augmentation during training is crucial for preventing auto-regressive drift, maintaining consistent simulation quality over long trajectories, while latent decoder fine-tuning significantly improves visual fidelity of details like the HUD.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A neural model can entirely learn and execute the complex game loop, encompassing both state updates and rendering, for a challenging game like DOOM.
> - Diffusion models can be adapted for real-time interactive simulation by incorporating action and observation conditioning, along with techniques like noise augmentation to mitigate auto-regressive drift over long trajectories.
> - Training a generative model on diverse, skill-representative gameplay data collected from a trained reinforcement learning agent is crucial for capturing complex game dynamics and achieving high fidelity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in "DIFFUSION MODELS ARE REAL-TIME GAME ENGINES" by Valevski et al. marks a significant advancement at the intersection of generative AI and interactive simulation, challenging the traditional paradigm of video game development.

**Traditional Game Engines vs. Neural Simulation:**
Historically, video games are built upon a manually crafted game loop: (1) update game state based on user input, and (2) render the game state to pixels. This loop, programmed by hand with explicit rules, runs at high frame rates to create the illusion of an interactive world. While impressive feats have been achieved in running classic games like DOOM on unconventional hardware, these instances still involve emulating the original, pre-programmed software. GameNGen fundamentally departs from this by proposing a neural model that entirely *learns* and *generates* the game logic and rendering, operating as a "game engine powered entirely by a neural model."

**Evolution of Generative Models:**
In recent years, generative models, particularly diffusion models, have revolutionized content creation, moving from static image generation (e.g., DALL-E, Stable Diffusion) to high-quality video generation (e.g., Sora, Imagen Video). GameNGen builds on this wave by adapting diffusion models for an even more complex task: interactive world simulation. Unlike standard video generation, which produces a fixed sequence of frames, interactive simulation requires real-time conditioning on dynamic user actions and maintaining consistency over long, user-controlled trajectories. This introduces unique challenges, notably the instability and sampling divergence inherent in auto-regressive generation.

**Prior Work in Neural Game Simulation and World Models:**
The concept of using neural networks to simulate interactive environments is not new. Seminal works like "World Models" (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018) demonstrated learning a compressed representation of a game environment and predicting future states, which could then be used to train an RL agent. GameGAN (Kim et al., 2020) used an adversarial objective to learn game dynamics and generate playable environments. More recent efforts include learning predictive models for RL agents (Hafner et al., 2020) or applying transformer architectures to action-conditioned video generation in environments like ViZDoom (Yan et al., 2021) and autonomous driving (Hu et al., 2023). Concurrent work, such as Alonso et al. (2024), also explores diffusion models for world modeling in Atari and high-resolution games like Counter-Strike.

**Distinguishing GameNGen:**
While these prior works laid crucial groundwork, they often faced limitations in terms of the complexity of games simulated, real-time performance, stability over extended periods, or visual fidelity. GameNGen directly addresses these shortcomings by demonstrating real-time, high-quality, and stable interaction with a complex, iconic game like DOOM. Its key distinction lies in achieving **real-time interactivity with high visual quality over long trajectories** using a diffusion model, specifically by tackling auto-regressive drift and enhancing visual fidelity through targeted fine-tuning. This work pushes the boundaries from mere video prediction to genuinely interactive and dynamic simulation, laying a foundation for a future where games might be "automatically generated, much like how images and videos have been generated by neural models in recent years."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to definitively answer the question: "Can a neural model running in real-time simulate a complex game at high quality?" The authors aim to demonstrate that this is indeed possible, using the classic first-person shooter DOOM as a challenging testbed.

The key motivations driving this research are multi-faceted:

- **Overcoming Limitations of Prior Neural Game Simulations:** Previous attempts at simulating interactive video games with neural models often struggled with complexity, simulation speed, stability over long time periods, or visual quality. The authors were motivated to develop a system that addresses these limitations, showcasing a robust and practical neural game engine.
- **Proof-of-Concept for a New Game Engine Paradigm:** Beyond simply improving existing methods, a core motivation is to demonstrate the feasibility of a fundamentally new paradigm for game development. Instead of games being manually crafted software systems with hand-coded rules, the authors envision a future where games are "weights of a neural model." GameNGen serves as a significant proof-of-concept for this vision, showing that a complex game loop (state update and rendering) can be entirely learned and executed by a neural network.
- **Real-time Interactive Experience:** A critical objective was to achieve real-time performance, allowing for fluid and immediate player interaction. The target of 20 frames per second (FPS) on a single TPU-v5, stable over multi-minute play sessions, was a concrete performance goal that distinguishes this work from non-interactive video generation.
- **High Visual Fidelity:** Another key objective was to ensure that the simulated experience closely matches the visual quality of the original game, avoiding artifacts or degradation commonly seen in earlier generative approaches. This motivated specific architectural choices like decoder fine-tuning.
- **Scalable Data Collection:** To train such a complex generative model, a vast amount of diverse gameplay data is required. The authors aimed to develop an efficient and scalable method for collecting this data from an existing game, which led to the strategy of using a trained RL agent.
- **Addressing Auto-Regressive Instability:** A significant technical challenge in auto-regressive generation is the accumulation of errors over time, leading to divergence from realistic outputs. A key objective was to develop methods (specifically, noise augmentation) to mitigate this auto-regressive drift and ensure stability over long interactive trajectories.
- **Leveraging Foundational Models:** The work is motivated by the success of large pre-trained generative models like Stable Diffusion. The objective was to adapt and fine-tune such a model for the specific demands of interactive game simulation, thereby leveraging existing powerful architectures rather than building from scratch.
- **The Choice of DOOM:** The classic game DOOM was chosen for several reasons: its complexity provides a challenging test case; its open-source nature facilitates data collection via ViZDoom; its native resolution is manageable for neural models while still being visually rich; and, as the authors humorously note, their personal familiarity with the game.

In essence, the research is driven by the ambition to bridge the gap between impressive generative media models and the demanding requirements of real-time, high-quality interactive virtual environments, paving the way for a transformative shift in how games are created and experienced.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GameNGen's methodology involves a novel two-phase training approach leveraging reinforcement learning for data collection and a meticulously adapted diffusion model for interactive generation.

**Phase 1: Data Collection via Agent Play**
The core idea for collecting scalable, diverse, and realistic gameplay data is to train an automated agent to play the game, then record its interactions.
- **Agent Training:** A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent (Schulman et al., 2017) is trained using the ViZDoom environment (Wydmuch et al., 2019). The agent's feature network is a simple CNN, similar to Mnih et al. (2015b), processing downscaled frame images and in-game map data (160x120 resolution). It also has access to the last 32 actions.
- **Reward Function:** Unlike typical RL setups focused solely on maximizing score, the goal here is to generate diverse human-like play data. A custom reward function, the only environment-specific part of the method, incentivizes various gameplay elements: player hit/death, enemy hit/kill, item/weapon pickup, secret finding, new area exploration (weighted by L1 distance), and changes in health, armor, and ammo. To further approximate human play, actions are applied for 4 frames, and repeating the previous action is encouraged.
- **Dataset Generation:** The agent's training trajectories, encompassing different skill levels (from random policies initially to more skilled play), are recorded to form the `T_agent` dataset, which contains sequences of actions and observations (frames). This dataset provides the "teacher forcing" data for the generative model. The final dataset comprises 70 million examples.

**Phase 2: Training the Generative Diffusion Model (GameNGen)**
The generative model is trained on the `T_agent` dataset to predict the next frame conditioned on past frames and actions.
- **Base Architecture:** The model repurposes a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model, Stable Diffusion v1.4 (Rombach et al., 2022). This leverages the robust image generation capabilities of an existing high-quality model.
- **Conditioning Adaptation:**
 - **Action Conditioning:** Text conditioning is removed. Instead, each action (key press) is mapped to a learned embedding (`A_emb`), which replaces the cross-attention from text in the original Stable Diffusion U-Net.
 - **Observation Conditioning:** Previous frames (`o_<n`) are encoded into the latent space using Stable Diffusion's auto-encoder (`ϕ`) and then concatenated to the noised latents along the channels dimension. This provides the model with visual history. The model uses a context length of 64 frames (approximately 3.2 seconds of history).
- **Loss Function:** The model is trained to minimize the standard diffusion loss with velocity parameterization (Salimans & Ho, 2022).
- **Mitigating Auto-Regressive Drift (Key Innovation): Noise Augmentation:**
 - A critical challenge in auto-regressive generation is that prediction errors accumulate, leading to rapid quality degradation (auto-regressive drift).
 - To counteract this, the training process corrupts context frames by adding a varying amount of Gaussian noise to their encoded latent representations.
 - The noise level is also provided as an input to the model via an embedding. This forces the network to learn to "correct" or handle noisy inputs, making it robust to slight deviations during auto-regressive inference where its own past predictions serve as context.
- **Latent Decoder Fine-Tuning (Key Innovation):**
 - The pre-trained auto-encoder of Stable Diffusion v1.4, while good, produced artifacts, particularly in small details and text (like the DOOM HUD).
 - To address this, only the decoder part of the latent auto-encoder is fine-tuned separately using an MSE loss against the target pixel frames. This improves visual fidelity without affecting the auto-regressive generation, as the U-Net still operates on the latents.
- **Training Details:** The U-Net parameters are unfrozen. Training uses 128 TPU-v5e devices with data parallelization, a batch size of 128, constant learning rate of 2e-5, Adafactor optimizer, and gradient clipping. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) is enabled for past observations by dropping the context frames condition with probability 0.1 during training.

**Inference:**
- **Sampling:** DDIM sampling is used for inference. CFG is applied to the past observations condition with a small weight (1.5) to avoid artifacts.
- **Speed Optimization:** Remarkably, GameNGen achieves robust simulation quality with only 4 DDIM sampling steps per frame. This is crucial for real-time performance (50ms total inference per frame, or 20 FPS on a single TPU-v5). The authors hypothesize this is due to the constrained image space and strong conditioning. While distillation could enable 1-step sampling (50 FPS), it incurred a slight quality penalty, so the 4-step version was preferred for main results.

This multi-faceted approach, combining strategic data generation with advanced diffusion model techniques, allows GameNGen to achieve unprecedented real-time interactive simulation capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

GameNGen demonstrates remarkable capabilities in simulating DOOM in real-time, achieving high visual fidelity and stability over long interactive sessions.

- **Real-Time Performance:**
 - GameNGen runs at **20 frames per second (FPS)** on a single TPU-v5.
 - It remains **stable over extended multi-minute play sessions**, a critical achievement for interactive simulation.
 - The low number of denoising steps (4 DDIM steps) required for high quality is a key factor enabling real-time operation, with each frame taking only 50ms (40ms for U-Net, 10ms for auto-encoder).

- **Image Quality (Teacher-Forcing Evaluation):**
 - When predicting a single frame conditioned on ground-truth past observations, the model achieves a **PSNR of 29.43** and an **LPIPS of 0.249**.
 - The PSNR value is comparable to lossy JPEG compression with quality settings of 20-30, indicating strong visual fidelity relative to the original game.

- **Video Quality (Auto-Regressive Evaluation):**
 - While per-frame metrics like PSNR and LPIPS gradually degrade in auto-regressive mode due to accumulating divergence from the ground-truth trajectory, the visual content and overall image quality remain consistent for a user.
 - The model achieves an **FVD of 114.02 for 16-frame sequences** (0.8 seconds) and **186.23 for 32-frame sequences** (1.6 seconds), which are metrics for distribution similarity between generated and real videos.

- **Human Evaluation:**
 - Human raters were presented with short clips (1.6s and 3.2s) of GameNGen simulation alongside clips of the actual game. They could distinguish the real game from the simulation only **58% to 60% of the time**, which is only slightly better than random chance (50%).
 - Even more impressively, after **5 to 10 minutes of continuous auto-regressive generation**, human raters still performed at **chance level (50%)** when asked to identify the real game in 3-second clips. This strongly highlights the model's long-term consistency and realism from a perceptual standpoint, despite the authors themselves being able to identify differences due to familiarity.

- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Context Length:** Increasing the context length from 1 to 64 frames (3.2 seconds) significantly improves generation quality (PSNR and LPIPS). However, the improvement asymptotes, suggesting that the current architecture might struggle to effectively leverage much longer histories, despite the game state persisting for much longer in DOOM.
 - **Noise Augmentation:** This technique is crucial for auto-regressive stability. Without noise augmentation during training, simulation quality (measured by LPIPS and PSNR) degrades rapidly after 10-20 frames, demonstrating severe auto-regressive drift. Its inclusion prevents this degradation, maintaining quality over long trajectories.
 - **Agent Play vs. Random Policy for Data Collection:** Training the generative model on data from a purpose-trained RL agent performs significantly better than training on data from a random policy, especially for "medium difficulty" gameplay scenarios. This validates the importance of collecting diverse, skill-representative data for training the generative model to capture complex game dynamics.

- **Latent Decoder Fine-Tuning:** Fine-tuning the latent auto-encoder's decoder significantly improves the fidelity of small visual details and text (like the HUD), reducing artifacts present with the default Stable Diffusion decoder.

The findings collectively demonstrate that GameNGen achieves its key objectives: real-time, high-quality, and stable neural simulation of a complex game, with human perception of the simulation being remarkably close to the original game even after extended play.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GameNGen represents a significant step forward in the field of generative AI and interactive systems, with profound implications for the future of game development and beyond.

**Technical Breakthrough and Proof-of-Concept:**
The most immediate significance is the empirical demonstration that a complex video game like DOOM can be simulated entirely by a neural network in real-time (20 FPS on a single TPU-v5) with high visual fidelity and stability over long interactive sessions. This addresses a fundamental question in the field and provides a concrete proof-of-concept for a "neural game engine." The innovations in mitigating auto-regressive drift through noise augmentation and improving visual details via decoder fine-tuning are critical technical contributions that enable this performance.

**Towards a New Paradigm for Game Development:**
GameNGen challenges the decades-old paradigm of manually programmed game engines. It proposes a future where games are not lines of code, but "weights of a neural model." This shift could lead to:
- **Reduced Development Costs and Increased Accessibility:** Imagine creating games or game content (new levels, characters, behaviors) not by writing code, but by providing textual descriptions, example images, or even just sketching ideas. This could democratize game creation, making it accessible to a much broader range of creators without specialized programming knowledge. The preliminary experiments in modifying game elements by editing frames highlight this potential.
- **Novel Creative Workflows:** Developers could iterate rapidly on game ideas, generate variations automatically, and focus on high-level artistic direction rather than low-level implementation details.
- **Performance Guarantees:** Neural models, once trained, can offer more predictable frame rates and memory footprints, simplifying optimization and deployment.

**Broader Impact on Interactive Software Systems:**
The principles demonstrated by GameNGen are not limited to video games. Any interactive software system with a clear input-output loop could potentially be learned and simulated by a neural model. This could extend to user interfaces, design tools, or even complex simulations in scientific or engineering domains, enabling new forms of interaction and automation.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors are transparent about current limitations, which also inform crucial future research directions:
- **Limited Memory/Context Length:** GameNGen's context length (3.2 seconds) means it relies on learned heuristics to infer long-term game state. While surprisingly effective, it can fail in situations requiring memory beyond this window (e.g., specific puzzles or enemy states not immediately visible). Future work needs to explore more sophisticated architectures for long-term memory.
- **Out-of-Distribution Behavior:** The model's behavior is bound by the training data. While agent play generates diverse data, areas or interactions not sufficiently explored lead to erroneous or unfaithful simulation. Closing the gap between agent and human play, and enabling generalization to entirely novel scenarios, is key.
- **New Game Creation:** While GameNGen can simulate an existing game, the ability to easily "create entirely new games" from human input (beyond modifying existing ones) remains a significant challenge. This involves moving from simulating `p(o_n | o_<n, a_<=n)` to `p(s_n | s_<n, a_<=n)` where `s` is a high-level game design specification.
- **Performance Optimization:** Further optimization for higher frame rates and deployment on consumer hardware is a practical next step for widespread adoption.
- **Testing on Other Games:** Validating the methodology across different game genres and complexities would broaden the applicability of GameNGen.

**Societal and Ethical Considerations:**
As with other powerful generative AI technologies, GameNGen's potential raises questions about responsible development. Empowering users and developers to create new experiences responsibly, avoiding misuse or unintended consequences (e.g., deepfakes in games, or potentially harmful content generation), will be important. The authors' commitment to reproducibility, using open-source models and environments, is a positive step in this regard.

In conclusion, GameNGen is more than just an impressive technical feat; it is a foundational piece in the puzzle of neural interactive systems. By demonstrating the viability of learning complex game logic and rendering, it opens a vast landscape of possibilities for reimagining how interactive experiences are created, consumed, and even evolved.
