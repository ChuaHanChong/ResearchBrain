# Grounding Hierarchical Vision-Language-Action Models Through Explicit Language-Action Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called Grounded Preference-based Language-Action Alignment (GPLA) enhances the transparency of hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by explicitly aligning intermediate natural language instructions with robot actions and visual observations. This approach, leveraging a contrastive grounding model and offline preference learning, reduces the need for costly human annotations while maintaining robust action execution and stable semantic coherence in generated language.

## Problem

- Existing hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often lack explicit alignment mechanisms between generated natural language sub-task descriptions and the robot's executed actions.
- Evaluation of hierarchical VLAs primarily focuses on task success rates, often neglecting the groundedness and consistency of intermediate language outputs with robot perceptions and actions.
- Developing hierarchical VLAs relies heavily on extensive and expensive human annotations for intermediate sub-task descriptions, limiting scalability.

## Method

- A **GPLA** (Grounded Preference-based Language-Action Alignment) framework is introduced, employing an ==iterative preference learning scheme (SimPO)== to refine a hierarchical VLA model.
- The framework uses a high-level VLM to decompose instructions and a low-level VLA to generate action trajectories, which are then evaluated by a ==grounding model==.
- A novel ==action-conditioned grounding model== is developed, which learns a ==shared embedding space== across vision, action, and language by modulating visual features with embedded actions via ==FiLM layers==.

## Results

- **GPLA** achieved trajectory generation performance comparable to supervised baselines (e.g., MSE **0.045** for GPLA Action-Conditioned vs. **0.043** for supervised SmolVLA).
- Generated low-level instructions, while having lower token-overlap metrics (BLEU), maintained stable semantic coherence as indicated by BERTScore, which remained around **0.98** across methods.
- The action-conditioned grounding model uniquely demonstrated multimodal fusion in its embedding space, showing integrated vision and language representations, unlike general-purpose contrastive models like CLIP or SigLIP 2.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Preference-based learning, using a grounding model, can effectively align language with actions in hierarchical VLAs, reducing reliance on expensive human annotations while maintaining performance.
> - Explicit grounding mechanisms improve robot transparency by ensuring generated natural language explanations are consistent with the robot's visual perceptions and physical actions.
> - An action-conditioned grounding model fosters a more unified, multi-modal embedding space where vision, language, and action are integrated, offering deeper insights into multimodal representations for embodied agents.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a critical area within embodied artificial intelligence (AI) and human-robot collaboration: achieving robot transparency through consistent and grounded natural language communication. The broader landscape involves the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) into robotics, leading to the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These VLAs are increasingly used for tasks such as plan generation and control policies, often leveraging the generalizability of VLMs across diverse language and image inputs to produce actions or diverse textual outputs.

A recent trend in VLAs is the adoption of hierarchical architectures, drawing inspiration from "chain-of-thought" prompting in LLMs. These hierarchical VLAs decompose high-level instructions into intermediate, executable sub-tasks, expressed in natural language. This approach aims to enhance model transparency by providing semantic grounding and verbal explanations for robot behavior.

However, the existing literature on hierarchical VLAs presents several limitations that this research seeks to address. Firstly, current models often rely on separately trained modules for language generation and action execution, lacking an explicit mechanism to align these respective outputs during training. This decoupling can lead to "generation noise" where intermediate sub-task descriptions may not be perfectly consistent with the subsequent actions. Secondly, the evaluation of these models predominantly focuses on task success rates, often overlooking the quality, correctness, or *groundedness* of the intermediate language outputs with respect to the robot's visual perception and physical actions. The problem of "symbol grounding"—relating textual descriptions and visual features to spatial object relations and actions in the real world—remains a core challenge in this domain. This research aims to bridge these gaps by introducing a framework that explicitly grounds the intermediate language outputs of hierarchical VLAs with their corresponding actions and visual observations, thereby improving transparency and reducing the reliance on costly data annotations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized need for transparent and effectively grounded robot communication in human-robot collaboration scenarios. For a robot to be a reliable and understandable collaborator, its natural language output must accurately reflect its internal reasoning and intended physical actions, and this communication must be explicitly grounded in the task and environment. The authors identify several specific motivations and objectives:

1. **Addressing the Symbol Grounding Problem in Hierarchical VLAs**: While hierarchical VLAs offer a pathway to transparency by generating natural language sub-task descriptions, their intermediate language outputs are often decoupled from the actual action space. This creates a risk that the robot's verbal explanations may not faithfully represent its actions or its understanding of the environment. The research aims to solve this by creating an explicit alignment mechanism between language, vision, and action modalities during training.

2. **Improving Model Transparency and Consistency**: Current hierarchical models generate intermediate sub-tasks that, while intended to provide transparency, carry no inherent certainty of being error-free or perfectly aligned with the subsequent actions. This introduces an additional layer of potential inconsistency. A key objective is to ensure that the robot's natural language communication is consistent with its physical actions and explicitly grounded in its visual perception.

3. **Enhancing Evaluation Beyond Task Success Rates**: Existing VLA learning objectives and evaluation metrics primarily focus on task success rates or generalization capabilities. These metrics, however, do not reveal the *groundedness* of the intermediate language outputs. The authors are motivated to develop and apply a method for explicitly evaluating the alignment of language with actions and visual observations, thereby disclosing potential misunderstandings or misalignments.

4. **Minimizing Reliance on Expensive Human Annotations**: Training hierarchical VLAs often requires extensive and costly annotations for intermediate outputs (e.g., ground-truth sub-task descriptions). A significant objective of this work is to develop a framework that can achieve strong performance and improved grounding without requiring such expensive, explicit annotations, thereby making VLA development more efficient and scalable.

5. **Establishing a Strong Baseline and Providing Insights**: The research aims to establish a robust baseline for grounded hierarchical VLA performance, comparable to fully supervised fine-tuning, while demonstrating applicability in low-data regimes. Concurrently, it seeks to provide critical insights into the nature and limitations of multimodal grounding representations within hierarchical VLA architectures.

To achieve these objectives, the authors propose the Grounded Preference-based Language-Action Alignment (GPLA) framework, which uses a contrastive grounding model and offline preference learning to refine the alignment between generated language and action trajectories.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the Grounded Preference-based Language-Action Alignment (GPLA) framework, which operates by iteratively improving a hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model using preference learning guided by a separately trained grounding model. The overall approach can be broken down into three main components: dataset preparation, the hierarchical VLA architecture, and the grounded preference alignment mechanism, which includes the action-conditioned grounding model.

#### 3.1. Dataset

The LanguageTable benchmark suite is utilized. This dataset features human language-annotated trajectories, structured into hierarchical steps that describe how to achieve a high-level task. Each episode details a robot arm pushing blocks to reach an abstract goal (e.g., "put all the blocks in a vertical line"). Segments of the episode are associated with natural language descriptions, serving as target low-level instructions (e.g., "move the green circle near to the green star").
Visual observations consist of single frames from an angled top-down perspective. Actions are represented as a series of 2D coordinates corresponding to the robot’s end-effector. Preprocessing involves removing idle actions based on a joint coordinate transposition threshold of 0.1 and applying data augmentation techniques such as stochastic brightness/contrast adjustments, cropping, scaling on images, and Gaussian noise on actions, while preserving task semantics.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Model

The hierarchical VLA (denoted as πθ) is composed of two fine-tuned components:
- **High-level VLM**: A pre-trained Gemma3 (Gemma-3-4B-IT) VLM is fine-tuned to decompose high-level instructions into low-level instructions, using the LanguageTable dataset’s captions as targets. The vision encoder weights of Gemma3 are frozen during this fine-tuning process.
- **Low-level VLA**: SmolVLA is independently fine-tuned to generate 8-step action trajectories. It is conditioned on image observations, end-effector states, high-level instructions, and ground-truth low-level instructions during training. During inference, the ground-truth low-level instruction is replaced by the output generated by the high-level VLM.
This architecture aligns with approaches that break down complex tasks into simpler subgoals, but critically, GPLA introduces an explicit mechanism to align the language and action outputs, which are typically learned separately.

#### 3.3. Grounded Preference-based Language-Action Alignment

After the initial fine-tuning of the hierarchical VLA, a preference-learning-based training scheme is applied, inspired by GRAPE and utilizing SimPO for efficiency. The process unfolds as follows:
1. **Candidate Generation**: Given an observation and high-level instruction, the hierarchical VLA generates N distinct candidate language-action pairs (a low-level instruction and its corresponding trajectory).
2. **Grounding Score Calculation**: Each candidate pair is evaluated by a separately trained action-conditioned grounding model (πg) to compute an explicit grounding score (gj). This score quantifies the alignment between the generated natural language sub-goal, the visual observation, and the subsequent actions.
3. **Preference Pair Selection**: From the N candidates, the pair with the highest grounding score (yc) is selected as the preferred option, and the pair with the lowest score (yr) is selected as the rejected option.
4. **Model Update**: The high-level VLM of the hierarchical VLA is then updated using the SimPO loss function, which directly optimizes the model to generate outputs that are more aligned with the chosen (higher-scoring) preferences. The SimPO loss is defined as:
 LSimPO(x, yc, yl) = − log σ[r(x, yc) − r(x, yr) − γSimPO]
 where r(x, y) = (βSimPO / |y|) log πg(y|x) represents the reward score, σ is the sigmoid function, and βSimPO and γSimPO are tunable hyperparameters.

#### 3.4. Action-Conditioned Grounding Model

A core component of GPLA is the action-conditioned grounding model (πg), designed to overcome the limitation of existing contrastive models (like CLIP or SigLIP) that do not incorporate an action modality. This model learns a shared embedding space across vision, action, and language.
- **Architecture**:
 - It uses two separate encoder pipelines. The vision and text encoders are initialized from a pre-trained SigLIP 2 model (specifically, SigLIP 2 ViT B/16) and their weights are frozen during the grounding model's training. These encoders are followed by projection layers.
 - An additional small transformer network serves as the action encoder.
 - Crucially, the projected vision features from the SigLIP 2 vision encoder are dynamically modulated by the embedded actions through a series of FiLM layers. This action-conditioning allows the visual features to be influenced by the action context.
- **Objective Function**: The model is trained using a symmetric InfoNCE loss, similar to CLIP, to align the vision-action embedding (EVA) with the text embedding (ET). The loss encourages correctly aligned vision-action and text inputs to have closely matched embeddings.
 - LVA→T = − Σ log [exp(sim(VAi, Ti)/τ) / Σk exp(sim(VAi, Tk)/τ)]
 - LT→VA = − Σ log [exp(sim(VAj, Tj)/τ) / Σk exp(sim(VAk, Tj)/τ)]
 - LC = 0.5 * (LVA→T + LT→VA)
 Here, τ is a learned temperature parameter, and `sim` denotes cosine similarity.
 Additionally, a diversity regularization term (Ldiv) is introduced to prevent embedding collapse and encourage learning separable embeddings:
 Ldiv = (1 / N(N − 1)) Σi≠j [max(0, SVAij) + max(0, STij)], where S = EET is the cosine similarity matrix.
 The total loss function is L = LC + γdivLdiv, where γdiv controls the regularization influence.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the GPLA framework encompassed both the quality of low-level instruction generation and the accuracy of trajectory generation, using standard text-based and trajectory-based metrics, respectively. Furthermore, an analysis of the contrastive embedding space provided insights into multimodal grounding representations.

#### 4.1. Low-level Instruction Generation

The impact of GPLA on the high-level VLM's ability to generate low-level instructions was assessed using BLEU, ROUGE-1 F1, METEOR (token-overlap metrics), and BERTScore (semantic similarity).
- **Quantitative Results**: Compared to purely supervised training, GPLA variants generally showed a decrease in token-overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR). For example, the BLEU score for "Supervised" was 0.111, while for "GPLA (Action-Conditioned)" it was 0.063. This reduction occurred despite not requiring additional low-level ground-truth data. Crucially, the semantic score (BERTScore) remained stable across supervised (0.984) and GPLA variants (e.980 for Action-Conditioned), suggesting that the model maintained its ability to generate semantically coherent outputs, which token-based measures may not fully capture.
- **Integration as Regularization**: When the GPLA objective was incorporated as a weighted regularization term (weight 0.1) into the standard language modeling loss during supervised training, the results were mixed. While BLEU further decreased (0.051), ROUGE, BERTScore, and METEOR scores displayed slight increases compared to pure preference-based grounding. Using SigLIP 2 as a grounding model in GPLA showed comparable or slightly better performance on token-overlap metrics than the action-conditioned grounding model, although the action-conditioned model performed competitively when used as a regularization term.
- **Qualitative Analysis**: Qualitative examples revealed that all three models (Supervised, GPLA Action-Conditioned, Supervised + GPLA Action-Conditioned) consistently produced semantically plausible instructions, despite low token overlap with ground-truth captions. Object relations were identified as a strong pathway for generating commands, with models successfully targeting objects by shape and color. Challenges included generating physically incompatible actions (e.g., "[...] into your hand") from supervised training and occasional linguistic noise (e.g., "[...] towards towards [...]") from pure preference grounding, highlighting areas for refinement in spatial and embodied grounding.

#### 4.2. Trajectory Generation

The accuracy of the generated trajectories was evaluated using Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Cosine Similarity (CosSim) to measure deviation from ground-truth trajectories.
- **Performance Comparison**: The supervised SmolVLA model, which receives ground-truth low-level instructions, achieved the best performance (MSE 0.043, MAE 0.158, CosSim -0.029). However, most GPLA variants exhibited performance levels similar to the supervised approach (e.g., GPLA (Action-Conditioned) achieved MSE 0.045, MAE 0.163, CosSim -0.035). This suggests that the high-level VLM, even when refined by GPLA, provides semantically useful instructions to the low-level VLA, enabling robust action execution. Among the pure GPLA variations, cosine similarity demonstrated the most favorable performance.

#### 4.3. Contrastive Embedding Space

t-SNE visualizations were used to analyze the embedding spaces of different contrastive models (CLIP, SigLIP 2, and the action-conditioned grounding model) on visual inputs and low-level instructions from the LanguageTable dataset.
- **Distinct Modality Separation**: CLIP and SigLIP 2 models showed clear and distinct separation between visual and language embeddings. CLIP displayed strong visual discrimination, while language embeddings formed mostly cohesive clusters. SigLIP 2 similarly maintained distinct visual and language clusters.
- **Multimodal Fusion in Action-Conditioned Model**: In contrast, the action-conditioned grounding model was the only one that exhibited signs of mixing vision and language embeddings. This was evidenced by a small language substructure appearing within the main vision cluster (Figure 5c, lower right). This blending indicates that the action-conditioning mechanism encourages the model to learn a more unified, multi-modal representation where linguistic and visual elements of the current robotic step are more integrated, rather than distinctly separated as observed in the other models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, GPLA, that offers several significant contributions and potential impacts within the field of embodied AI and human-robot interaction.

Firstly, a primary significance lies in its **addressing of data scarcity and annotation costs**. The GPLA framework demonstrates that a preference-based approach can maintain performance levels comparable to fully supervised fine-tuning for hierarchical VLAs, critically reducing the need for expensive and extensive human annotation of intermediate language outputs. This is particularly impactful for robotics, where collecting high-quality, dense multimodal data is challenging and resource-intensive. By validating this preference-based grounding method, GPLA opens pathways for more efficient and scalable training of complex robotic systems.

Secondly, the framework directly enhances **robot transparency and the groundedness of communication**. By explicitly aligning intermediate natural language sub-task descriptions with the robot's visual observations and action trajectories during training, GPLA helps ensure that the robot's verbal explanations are consistent with its actual behavior. This improved grounding can foster more effective and trustworthy human-robot collaboration, as human operators can better understand the robot's intent and reasoning.

Thirdly, GPLA offers a method to handle **semantic ambiguities** that supervised learning often struggles with. Standard supervised loss functions can be rigid and may not adequately capture the correctness of diverse yet acceptable outputs. Preference-based methods, by leveraging comparative feedback on chosen versus rejected options, are inherently better suited to resolve these subtle semantic differences, leading to more robust and contextually appropriate language generation.

Fourthly, the research provides **critical insights into multimodal grounding representations**. The analysis of the contrastive embedding space, particularly with the action-conditioned grounding model, highlights the potential for fusing linguistic, visual, and action modalities into a more unified representation. This suggests that incorporating action-conditioning is a relevant direction for learning more integrated multimodal understandings, which is essential for embodied agents operating in dynamic environments. This understanding contributes to the ongoing development of foundation models for robotics, suggesting that future architectures could benefit from more robust grounding mechanisms within their reasoning and planning capabilities.

Finally, while the current performance of the grounding model is acknowledged to be constrained by the relatively small corpus of language-annotated robotic episodes and available computational resources, the authors hypothesize that the GPLA method is **well-positioned to leverage future large-scale multimodal robotic datasets**. As data collections grow, the framework's ability to learn from preferences without explicit ground-truth annotations for every intermediate step could lead to substantial performance gains and more sophisticated robot intelligence. The work also indirectly points to future research directions, such as improving the understanding of object relations and embodiment in VLAs, as current models still face difficulties in inferring nuanced spatial, color, and relational information beyond basic shape recognition.
