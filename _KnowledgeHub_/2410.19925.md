# Improving Multimodal Large Language Models Using Continual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from the University of Rochester and Rochester Institute of Technology systematically quantified linguistic forgetting in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) during their adaptation and sequential acquisition of vision-language tasks. The work demonstrated that integrating continual learning techniques, such as Soft Targets and mSGM with Rehearsal, effectively preserves unimodal language abilities while sustaining or improving multimodal performance.

## Problem

- Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) often experience catastrophic forgetting of their core natural language understanding (NLU) and generation (NLG) capabilities when adapted to multimodal contexts.
- The degradation of linguistic abilities is particularly pronounced in smaller MLLMs, with NLG tasks being more susceptible to forgetting than NLU tasks.
- Current MLLM training paradigms frequently force a trade-off between acquiring multimodal skills and retaining fundamental linguistic knowledge, hindering their versatility.

## Method

- The researchers systematically analyzed ==linguistic forgetting== across **9** diverse LLMs (including Pythia, Phi2, LLaMA 2 variants) adapted into the ==LLaVA 1.5== multimodal architecture.
- Five established continual learning (CL) methods (LoRA, ==Soft Targets==, Rehearsal, ==mSGM==, mSGM + Rehearsal) were evaluated against naive fine-tuning to mitigate linguistic forgetting.
- Experiments were conducted in two settings: reducing forgetting during initial LLaVA 1.5 adaptation and enabling ==sequential learning== of multiple vision-language tasks without sacrificing prior knowledge.

## Results

- Soft Targets emerged as the most effective method for smaller models (e.g., Pythia 160M), achieving high vision-language accuracy with minimal linguistic forgetting and occasionally positive backward transfer.
- In sequential multimodal learning scenarios, mSGM + Rehearsal consistently achieved the least linguistic forgetting, preserving NLU/NLG performance while maintaining or exceeding naive fine-tuning's multimodal performance across model scales.
- While naive fine-tuning led to a consistent and severe drop in NLU/NLG performance when scaled, mSGM + Rehearsal showed little to no NLU/NLG forgetting, even achieving positive backward transfer for the Pythia **1B** model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Linguistic forgetting is significantly more severe for Natural Language Generation (NLG) tasks compared to Natural Language Understanding (NLU) tasks in MLLMs, especially for smaller models.
> - Instruction-tuned LLMs demonstrate increased resilience to linguistic forgetting during multimodal adaptation, potentially due to the distributional alignment between text-only and visual-instruction tuning.
> - Continual learning methods, notably Soft Targets and mSGM combined with Rehearsal, effectively prevent linguistic forgetting and can even induce positive backward transfer, enhancing language skills during multimodal training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents a significant frontier in AI, combining the impressive linguistic capabilities of LLMs with visual understanding. The current "recipe" for creating MLLMs typically involves aligning LLM token embeddings with visual embeddings, followed by instruction-tuning on vision-language (VL) tasks like visual question answering (VQA). However, a critical challenge, widely acknowledged but less systematically addressed, is the **catastrophic forgetting** of the original LLM's natural language understanding (NLU) and generation (NLG) capabilities during this multimodal adaptation.

Previous works, such as PaLM-E (Driess et al., 2023), have reported substantial performance degradation in unimodal language tasks (up to 87% relative drop), particularly in smaller MLLMs. Even prominent models like LLaVA, the focus of this study, suffer from this linguistic forgetting. Approaches like InstructBLIP and FROMAGe attempt to mitigate this by freezing the LLM and training secondary modules, but this often leads to underperformance compared to fully adapted MLLMs and precludes emergent capabilities like in-context learning. While VILA (Lin et al., 2024) included interleaved text-image data (MMC4) in its pre-training to reduce forgetting, this necessitated a large increase in computational resources and only studied a single NLU dataset.

This paper positions itself directly in this gap by pioneering a systematic investigation of linguistic forgetting in MLLMs through the lens of **continual learning (CL)**. It argues that the adaptation of an LLM to a multimodal context constitutes a significant distributional shift, making it a natural fit for CL methodologies designed to combat catastrophic forgetting in sequential learning scenarios. Unlike previous efforts, this work is the first to rigorously study and apply established CL methods to mitigate linguistic forgetting during the creation and sequential learning of MLLMs, particularly for open-source models with modest parameter counts (e.g., < 7B). By doing so, it bridges the gap between the rapidly advancing field of MLLMs and the specialized domain of continual learning, offering a novel and efficient pathway to developing more robust and versatile multimodal AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from a crucial requirement for practical, real-world MLLMs: they must exhibit strong performance in *both* purely text-based and vision-language tasks. Users frequently switch between these modalities in interactions, and robust linguistic understanding remains essential for generating performative and coherent responses, even within multimodal prompts. The observed degradation of linguistic abilities during MLLM adaptation poses a significant barrier to this vision.

The **key objectives** of this study are multifaceted:

1. **Analyze Linguistic Forgetting in MLLMs:** To systematically quantify the extent of linguistic forgetting when unimodal LLMs are adapted into MLLMs using the standard LLaVA 1.5 training recipe. This includes studying the role of model scale and instruction tuning on this phenomenon across a diverse set of 9 LLMs (including Pythia, Phi2, and LLaMA 2 variants).
2. **Evaluate Continual Learning Mitigation Techniques:** To investigate the effectiveness of various continual learning (CL) methods in reducing linguistic forgetting during the LLaVA 1.5 training protocol, while simultaneously maintaining or enhancing vision-language (VL) performance.
3. **Pioneer Continual Learning for Sequential VL Task Acquisition:** To extend the CL framework to MLLMs by simulating a scenario where the model sequentially acquires new multimodal capabilities across different VL datasets (VQA, OCR, Referential Grounding), assessing the ability of CL techniques to preserve both linguistic skills and previously learned multimodal knowledge. This addresses the broader goal of building general multimodal learning systems that can continuously adapt without sacrificing existing skills.

In essence, the authors are motivated to develop MLLMs that are not only capable across modalities but also **resilient** to knowledge degradation, ensuring their utility in dynamic, lifelong learning environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers adopt a comprehensive empirical approach, centering their investigation around the widely used **LLaVA 1.5 MLLM architecture** as their base model.

#### 3.1. LLaVA 1.5 Architecture

The LLaVA 1.5 model comprises three main components:
- **Visual Encoder:** A frozen pre-trained ViT-L/14 from CLIP, designed for stable image feature extraction.
- **LLM:** The core language model, which is varied across experiments.
- **Alignment Network:** A two-layer MLP projector that maps visual embeddings from the vision encoder into the LLM's text embedding space.

The standard LLaVA 1.5 training recipe involves two stages: an "alignment stage" where only the alignment network is trained with the LLM frozen, followed by a "fine-tuning stage" where both the LLM and alignment network are jointly fine-tuned on a mixture of VL instruction-tuning data.

#### 3.2. LLMs Studied

To understand the impact of scale and pre-training, the study evaluates 9 different LLMs as the base for LLaVA:
- Six **Pythia models** (160M, 410M, 1B, 1.4B, 2.8B parameters), including an instruction-tuned 1.4B version.
- **Phi2 (3B)**.
- Two **LLaMA 2 (7B)** models, including the instruction-tuned Vicuna-1.5 7B.

#### 3.3. Continual Learning Methods

The paper evaluates five distinct CL methods, alongside the standard "Naive Fine-Tuning" (original LLaVA recipe) as a baseline:
- **Naive Fine-Tuning:** The original LLaVA 1.5 protocol, where the LLM and alignment network are fine-tuned jointly without specific forgetting mitigation.
- **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation):** A Parameter Efficient Tuning (PET) method where the original LLM weights are frozen, and only low-rank updates (adapters) are learned. LoRA weights are merged after each task.
- **Soft Targets:** An adaptation of a CL technique from image classification, dynamically re-parameterizing the conditional token distribution by smoothing hard target vectors to reduce abrupt changes.
- **Rehearsal (Experience Replay):** Involves storing a small percentage (1%) of samples from previous tasks (excluding the initial LLM pre-training) and mixing them with new data during training.
- **mSGM:** A modified version of a CL method that combines elements of soft targets, weight initialization, and LoRA. The authors adapt it by omitting weight initialization and output layer freezing.
- **mSGM + Rehearsal:** A combination of the mSGM method with experience replay.

#### 3.4. Experimental Paradigms

The study follows two main experimental paradigms:
1. **Mitigating Linguistic Forgetting in LLaVA 1.5 Adaptation:** This involves a two-task setup:
 - Task 1: The pre-trained unimodal LLM (assumed to be already learned).
 - Task 2: Adapting the LLM to LLaVA 1.5's mixture of VL datasets (a single, combined multimodal task). The goal is to minimize linguistic forgetting from Task 1 while achieving high VL performance.
2. **Continually Learning Sequential VL Tasks:** This is a five-task setup:
 - Task 1: Pre-trained unimodal LLM.
 - Tasks 2-5: Sequentially learning distinct groups of VL datasets (Instruction Tuning, VQA, OCR, Referential Grounding). The aim here is to assess the methods' ability to acquire new multimodal skills incrementally without forgetting pre-existing linguistic or *previously acquired multimodal* capabilities.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Metrics and Datasets

- **Natural Language Evaluation:**
 - **NLG:** Lambada (accuracy). Other NLG datasets (WebQuestions, TriviaQA) are used with a '% Change' metric in the Appendix.
 - **NLU:** ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Winogrande, WSC (all using accuracy).
- **Vision-Language Evaluation:** VQAv2, GQA, TextVQA OCR, TextVQA Pure, RefCOCO (all using accuracy on slim test sets).
- **Forgetting Metric (∆):** Defined as `ωt(1) - ωt(k)`, where ωt(k) is the harmonic mean of accuracy values for task *t* after training on task *k*. A positive ∆ indicates forgetting, while a negative ∆ signifies positive backward transfer.

#### 3.6. Training Details

The study meticulously follows the LLaVA 1.5 training protocol, including optimizer settings (Adam), distributed training (FSDP, gradient checkpointing), mixed precision, learning rate schedules (linear warmup, cosine decay), and batch sizes. The ViT encoder is kept frozen throughout, while the LLM and alignment network are trained.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings across its two experimental paradigms:

#### 4.1. Analysis of Linguistic Forgetting in LLaVA 1.5 Adaptation

- **Prevalence of Forgetting:** Five of the nine MLLMs studied (using the naive LLaVA recipe) exhibited linguistic forgetting, while four surprisingly showed *positive transfer* (increased NLU/NLG accuracy).
- **NLG vs. NLU Forgetting:** Forgetting was consistently and significantly more severe for NLG tasks (Lambada) than for NLU tasks. Smaller models generally experienced higher NLG forgetting, aligning with observations from PaLM-E.
- **Positive Backward Transfer:** Cases of positive backward transfer (negative ∆) predominantly originated from NLU datasets, suggesting that visual-language training might enhance common-sense reasoning and world knowledge relevant to NLU.
- **Impact of Instruction Fine-Tuning:** Instruction-tuned LLMs (like Vicuna 1.5 and an instruction-tuned Pythia 1.4B) exhibited minimal or even negative linguistic forgetting, indicating greater robustness to multimodal adaptation. This is attributed to the distributional similarity between text-only instruction tuning and visual-instruction tuning tasks.

#### 4.2. Mitigating Linguistic Forgetting in LLaVA 1.5 Adaptation

- **Soft Targets Efficacy:** For the Pythia 160M model, **Soft Targets** emerged as the most effective mitigation method. It achieved the highest VL accuracy while exhibiting the least linguistic forgetting.
- **Trade-offs with Other Methods:** LoRA and mSGM preserved NLU/NLG performance better than naive fine-tuning but initially at the cost of some decrease in VL accuracy.
- **Role of Parameter Count (with Soft Targets):** When applying Soft Targets across the Pythia family, it consistently yielded zero or negative linguistic forgetting, even achieving positive backward transfer for smaller models (0.16-0.41B). In contrast, naive fine-tuning showed severe forgetting in the smaller models and only reduced forgetting for models larger than 1B parameters. This indicates Soft Targets' robustness across model scales.

#### 4.3. Continually Learning Sequential VL Tasks

- **Pythia 410M Results:** In the challenging sequential learning scenario with Pythia 410M, all continual learning methods showed efficacy in reducing linguistic forgetting compared to naive fine-tuning. **mSGM (with and without rehearsal)** achieved the least linguistic forgetting while maintaining competitive, and sometimes superior, VL performance (e.g., on RefCOCO). Naive fine-tuning, while sometimes achieving better initial VL performance, suffered from higher linguistic forgetting.
- **Model Scaling Results (Continual Learning):** Scaling up the best method (**mSGM + Rehearsal**) across Pythia models (160M to 1.4B) demonstrated its superior performance. Naive fine-tuning led to a sharp and consistent drop in task-averaged NLU/NLG performance, whereas mSGM + Rehearsal showed little to no NLU/NLG forgetting, even achieving positive backward transfer for the Pythia 1B model. Importantly, mSGM + Rehearsal generally rivaled or even exceeded naive fine-tuning's task-averaged VL performance across scales.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the development of robust and versatile multimodal large language models, offering one of the first systematic studies into the critical issue of linguistic catastrophic forgetting within this domain, especially for widely accessible open-source models.

The **significance** of the findings is multi-fold:

1. **Illuminating a Critical Problem:** By rigorously quantifying linguistic forgetting in various MLLMs, the study brings much-needed attention to a pervasive but under-explored problem. The finding that NLG tasks are far more susceptible to forgetting than NLU tasks, and that smaller models are particularly vulnerable, provides crucial insights for future MLLM design.
2. **Validating Continual Learning as a Solution:** The core impact lies in demonstrating that established continual learning techniques, particularly Soft Targets and mSGM with Rehearsal, can effectively mitigate linguistic forgetting during MLLM adaptation. These methods not only preserve pre-existing linguistic abilities but can, in some cases, even induce positive backward transfer, enhancing language skills during multimodal training.
3. **Enabling Practical MLLMs:** The ability to create MLLMs that maintain strong performance on *both* unimodal text and multimodal vision-language tasks is paramount for real-world applications. This work provides a pathway to developing MLLMs that can seamlessly handle multi-turn, multi-modal interactions without compromising their fundamental linguistic competence.
4. **Advancing Lifelong Learning:** By pioneering the application of CL to MLLM adaptation and sequential multimodal skill acquisition, the research contributes to the broader field of lifelong learning agents. It establishes strong baselines and motivates further exploration into how foundation models can continually acquire new knowledge without suffering from catastrophic forgetting.

The **potential impact** is substantial:

- **More Reliable and Trustworthy MLLMs:** Users can have greater confidence that an MLLM will not "forget" how to answer text-only questions after learning multimodal tasks, leading to more consistent and reliable AI experiences.
- **Efficient Model Development:** For developers, employing CL techniques could reduce the need for expensive and complex re-training strategies to address forgetting, potentially making MLLM development more computationally efficient and accessible.
- **Facilitating Incremental MLLM Capabilities:** The demonstrated ability to sequentially learn new VL tasks without significant forgetting paves the way for MLLMs that can continuously expand their multimodal knowledge and skills in a modular fashion, akin to human learning.
- **Inspiration for Future Research:** This study opens numerous avenues for future work, including applying these methods to even larger MLLMs, exploring other parameter-efficient tuning techniques, investigating multi-modal output scenarios, and optimizing training protocols beyond single-pass epochs.

In conclusion, Srivastava et al.'s work not only dissects a fundamental challenge in MLLM development but also offers robust, empirically validated continual learning solutions, thereby contributing significantly to the creation of more resilient, versatile, and ultimately, more intelligent multimodal AI systems.
