# Near-sensor Computing for Rapid Visuotactile Perception

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ShanghaiTech University developed a near-sensor computing architecture for visuotactile perception, integrating a spectral Poisson solver directly into a sensor node to perform 3D depth map reconstruction. This system achieves a deterministic 0.211 ms latency for 128x128 reconstructions and reduces robotic protective reflex latency by 6.0-fold to 28.3 ms, with an estimated power consumption of 324 mW.

## Problem

- Visuotactile sensors' dense 3D contact geometry reconstruction is computationally intensive, often taking tens of milliseconds on general-purpose CPUs.
- Host-based processing introduces high, variable latency and data transfer delays, severely limiting control bandwidth for high-speed robotic tasks.
- Existing acceleration methods either simplify the tactile representation, compromising 3D fidelity, or use sparse data from event-based sensors, unsuitable for dense reconstruction.

## Method

- A **near-sensor computing architecture** was developed on a Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ FPGA, integrating a custom visuotactile sensor with an on-chip processing unit.
- A fully pipelined, direct ==spectral Poisson solver== was implemented in hardware, operating on a fixed-schedule ==streaming datapath== to reconstruct dense 3D depth maps.
- A ==contact-triggered protective reflex loop== was integrated directly into the FPGA's programmable logic for immediate decision-making and motor command generation without host intervention.

## Results

- Achieved a fixed, deterministic latency of **0.211 ms** for 128x128 depth reconstruction and a throughput exceeding **10,000 frames per second**, outperforming host-based systems significantly.
- Demonstrated a **6.0-fold** reduction in robot protective reflex contact-to-motion latency, from **169.9 ms** (host-based) to **28.3 ms** (near-sensor), which is faster than reported human withdrawal reflexes.
- Exhibited substantially lower power consumption (**347 mW** on FPGA, **324 mW** for ASIC core) compared to host-based CPU/GPU solutions that require tens to hundreds of Watts for equivalent tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implementing a direct spectral Poisson solver in a fully pipelined hardware architecture enables deterministic, ultra-low latency 3D visuotactile reconstruction by avoiding iterative methods and host-based overheads.
> - Near-sensor integration of sensing, reconstruction, and decision-making dramatically reduces overall latency and variability, facilitating rapid robotic responses comparable to biological reflexes.
> - Utilizing 24-bit fixed-point arithmetic in the datapath provides an optimal balance between reconstruction accuracy and hardware resource efficiency for real-time operation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Visuotactile sensors represent a class of tactile perception systems that offer rich spatial information by imaging the deformation of a soft elastomer under structured illumination. These sensors typically reconstruct dense three-dimensional (3D) contact geometry, enabling the estimation of quantities such as contact-force distributions, surface curvature, incipient slip, and object pose. These capabilities are relevant for dexterous robotic manipulation and advanced tactile perception.

However, a significant challenge in the field is the computational cost associated with 3D reconstruction. This process commonly involves solving a Poisson partial differential equation over dense gradient fields, which is a computationally intensive task. On general-purpose CPUs, this reconstruction can take tens of milliseconds, exacerbated by data-transfer delays and variable operating-system scheduling latency. This latency and lack of determinism reduce control bandwidth, thereby limiting the utility of visuotactile sensing in high-speed robotic applications such as rapid slip reaction or dynamic manipulation.

Existing efforts to address this bottleneck have primarily followed two trajectories:
1. **Simplifying representation:** Some approaches improve throughput by estimating simpler properties like surface normals or directly predicting task-specific variables from raw tactile images, rather than reconstructing full 3D geometry. While these can achieve update rates of 100-200 Hz, they do not provide a unified 3D geometric description suitable for diverse tasks.
2. **Hardware-level acceleration via event-based sensing:** This involves replacing conventional frame-based imaging with event-based tactile sensors, reaching kilohertz-level temporal resolution. However, these systems generate sparse signals representing temporal changes, making them suitable for coarse perceptual tasks like contact detection but not for dense geometric reconstruction.

More recently, near-sensor and in-sensor computing paradigms have emerged in tactile sensing. Prior work has demonstrated edge-side processing for low-level operations such as filtering or feature extraction in various tactile modalities (e.g., capacitive, piezoresistive), highlighting the benefits of localized signal processing. Applying this strategy to visuotactile sensing, particularly for real-time dense 3D surface reconstruction via Poisson equation solving, is complex. Concurrently, hardware-accelerated PDE solvers have been developed for other domains like scientific computing and medical imaging. Many of these utilize iterative numerical methods, such as multigrid or conjugate-gradient solvers, which exhibit input-dependent convergence times and thus variable latency. Robotic visuotactile perception, by contrast, requires deterministic, low-latency dense reconstruction within tight power constraints.

This research addresses the aforementioned limitations by developing a near-sensor computing architecture specifically designed for real-time, deterministic, and power-efficient 3D visuotactile reconstruction. It aims to bridge the gap between the rich spatial information offered by visuotactile sensors and the high-speed, predictable response required for advanced robotic tasks, without compromising geometric fidelity or relying on iterative, variable-latency solvers.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a near-sensor visuotactile computing architecture that integrates sensing, dense 3D reconstruction, and contact-triggered decision-making directly at the sensor node. This integration is designed to overcome the performance limitations of conventional host-based processing for visuotactile data.

The motivation for this research stems from several key challenges and requirements in robotic tactile perception:

1. **Addressing the computational bottleneck of 3D reconstruction:** Visuotactile sensors generate gradient fields that require solving a Poisson partial differential equation to reconstruct dense 3D contact geometry. This computation is significantly more demanding than processing for many other tactile modalities, leading to processing times often in the tens of milliseconds on general-purpose CPUs. This delay limits the speed at which robots can react to tactile stimuli.
2. **Reducing latency and variability for real-time control:** Host-based processing introduces data-transfer delays from the sensor to a host computer (CPU/GPU) and variable scheduling latency due to operating system overheads. Such non-deterministic and high latency diminishes control bandwidth when geometric feedback is critical, making it unsuitable for ultrafast closed-loop robotic behaviors like rapid slip reactions, dynamic manipulation, and protective reflexes. The objective is to achieve deterministic, consistently low latency.
3. **Improving power efficiency:** Transferring large volumes of raw video data to a host processor and performing intensive computations consumes substantial power. Moving the computation closer to the sensor, into a dedicated hardware pipeline, is intended to reduce the overall power footprint, which is crucial for power-constrained robotic systems and compact sensor designs.
4. **Providing a unified and rich geometric description:** Unlike approaches that simplify tactile representations (e.g., surface normals, task-specific features) for speed, this work aims to maintain a full 3D geometric description of contact. This dense depth map provides a versatile representation that can be reused across various downstream tasks, enhancing the generality and utility of the sensor.
5. **Enabling rapid robotic responses comparable to biological reflexes:** Biological systems demonstrate rapid protective reflexes initiated at the spinal cord, bypassing slower cortical processing. The research is motivated by the desire to enable analogous, low-latency, contact-triggered decision-making directly at the sensor node, allowing for immediate robotic responses without host intervention.
6. **Developing a practical hardware solution:** While hardware accelerators for PDEs exist, many utilize iterative methods with variable convergence times. The objective is to implement a direct spectral Poisson solver on a fixed-schedule streaming datapath, ensuring deterministic latency and efficient resource utilization within the tight constraints of a sensor node.

In summary, the core motivation is to achieve accurate, energy-efficient, and deterministic real-time visuotactile perception to unlock advanced, high-speed robotic manipulation capabilities that are currently limited by computational latency and power consumption.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents a near-sensor computing framework for visuotactile perception, integrating sensing, dense 3D reconstruction, and contact-triggered decision-making directly at the sensor node.

**Sensor and Hardware Platform:**
The system utilizes a custom-developed visuotactile sensor based on the GelSight principle. This sensor comprises a soft elastomer membrane with a reflective coating, illuminated by red, green, and blue light sources, and imaged by a CMOS camera. The CMOS image sensor is designed to be interchangeable, allowing flexibility in spatial resolution, frame rate, region of interest (ROI), and field of view (FoV).

The sensor head is connected to a Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ XCZU7EV system-on-chip via a MIPI CSI-2 interface, forming a self-contained sensing unit. All depth reconstruction operations are performed within the FPGA's programmable logic, operating at a clock frequency of 166 MHz. The on-chip ARM processing system is used solely for transmitting reconstructed depth frames to a host PC via an LwIP Ethernet stack, and it is not involved in the reconstruction pipeline or the reflex decision-making process.

**Reconstruction Pipeline (Spectral Poisson Solver):**
The core of the architecture is a spectral Poisson solver, which addresses the Poisson equation (∇²z = ∇ · g) to derive the depth map (z) from the divergence of the surface-gradient field (g). This method exploits the eigen-decomposition of the discrete Laplacian under homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The computation involves a fixed sequence of streaming operations:
1. **Gradient lookup:** A photometric stereo core converts the raw RGB pixel stream into per-pixel surface gradients using a lookup module based on the tri-colour model.
2. **Finite differencing:** The divergence of the gradient field is computed.
3. **Forward Discrete Sine Transform (DST):** The 2D DST is applied to the gradient divergence. Because the 2D DST is separable, it is computed as two successive 1D passes (row-wise, then column-wise). Each 1D DST is implemented using a pipelined Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) core with input reordering and phase correction.
4. **Point-wise spectral division:** In the spectral domain, the system decouples into N² independent scalar equations, where each transformed depth coefficient (ẑ_kl) is obtained by dividing the transformed gradient divergence coefficient (f̂_kl) by the sum of corresponding eigenvalues (λ_k + λ_l). The eigenvalues are precomputed and stored in hardware ROM.
5. **Inverse DST:** The inverse 2D DST is applied to recover the dense depth map in the spatial domain.

This methodology ensures a direct solution in a fixed number of arithmetic operations, avoiding data-dependent branching or iterative convergence, which results in deterministic latency. Pixels enter the pipeline in raster-scan order at the camera pixel rate, with no full-frame input buffering. Gradient estimation and Poisson integration proceed concurrently on successive regions of each frame. All arithmetic operations within the pipeline utilize a 24-bit signed fixed-point representation, chosen after an optimization process balancing accuracy and hardware resources.

**Reflex Loop Implementation:**
To demonstrate real-time decision-making, a threshold comparator is integrated directly into the FPGA's programmable logic. As each depth value exits the reconstruction pipeline, the comparator evaluates it against a pre-programmed threshold. If the contact depth exceeds this threshold, a General Purpose Input/Output (GPIO) signal is asserted within one clock cycle, directly triggering a motor command without involving the host processor.

**Evaluation and Baselines:**
The system's performance was characterized across several dimensions:
- **Latency and Throughput:** Measured using Vivado's Integrated Logic Analyzer (ILA) on-chip, counting clock cycles from the first pixel input to the first depth value output. Throughput was measured by streaming synthetic frames continuously.
- **Power Efficiency:** Estimated using Vivado's simulation-based power analysis for the FPGA implementation and Synopsys Design Compiler for a 45 nm ASIC synthesis of the datapath, including SRAM models.
- **Resource Utilization:** Quantified by the usage of DSP blocks, LUTs, UltraRAM, and Block RAM on the FPGA.
- **Reconstruction Accuracy:** Compared on-chip results against a double-precision, host-based software reference (PyTorch implementation of the spectral Poisson reconstruction) for 15 diverse contact geometries. Root-mean-square (RMS) differences, normalized by peak contact depth, were calculated after per-frame scale-and-offset alignment to account for calibration differences.
- **Reflex Loop Performance:** Measured contact-to-motion latency using an external high-speed camera (480 frames/s) for both the near-sensor pathway and a conventional host-based baseline using a GelSight Mini connected to a PC via USB. Both pathways drove the same servo assembly.

Software baselines for latency and power consumption were evaluated on an Intel Core i7-13700 CPU, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 D GPU, and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX, all running a PyTorch implementation of the spectral Poisson reconstruction.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research yielded several findings regarding the performance, accuracy, and practical utility of the near-sensor visuotactile computing architecture.

**Deterministic Latency and High Throughput:**
- For a 128 × 128 frame, the reconstruction latency was consistently 35,107 clock cycles at 166 MHz for every frame, equating to a fixed latency of 0.211 ms. This determinism contrasts with host-based software implementations, which exhibited variable latencies with long-tailed distributions (e.g., 99th-percentile latencies of 0.93 ms on an Intel Core i7 CPU, 0.43 ms on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 D GPU, and 5.3 ms on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX).
- The latency scaled quadratically with grid size, consistent with O(N²) for an N × N grid: 9,421 cycles (0.057 ms) for 64 × 64, 35,107 cycles (0.211 ms) for 128 × 128, and 135,448 cycles (0.816 ms) for 256 × 256.
- The pipeline achieved a throughput of 10,106 frames per second for 128 × 128 depth output when synthetic frames were streamed continuously, which is within 0.3% of the theoretical limit at 166 MHz. This throughput significantly exceeds the operational frame rate of the sensor, indicating that the system is sensor-limited rather than reconstruction-limited.

**Power Efficiency:**
- The computational core logic for the reconstruction pipeline (photometric stereo and Poisson cores) on the FPGA had an estimated power consumption of 347 mW according to Vivado simulation.
- When synthesized as a 45 nm ASIC, the reconstruction datapath, including on-chip SRAM, had an estimated power consumption of 324 mW and occupied 5.10 mm² at 166 MHz.
- In comparison, sustaining reconstruction at 400 frames per second required 102 W for the desktop CPU, 73 W for the desktop GPU, and 7.2 W for the embedded Jetson module (at approximately 180 frames per second). The near-sensor approach demonstrated substantially lower power consumption.

**Resource Utilization:**
- Arithmetic resource usage increased modestly with grid size; for example, from 64 × 64 to 256 × 256 (a sixteen-fold increase in pixel count), DSP usage increased from 82 to 108 blocks, and LUT usage from 12,280 to 21,413.
- Storage requirements, however, scaled with frame size, becoming the primary constraint for larger grids. The 128 × 128 implementation used 32 UltraRAM blocks, while the 256 × 256 implementation used 64 UltraRAM blocks and an additional 176 block-RAM tiles, indicating that the maximum supported grid size depends on available on-chip memory.

**Reconstruction Quality:**
- The 24-bit fixed-point datapath closely reproduced results from a double-precision, host-based software reference.
- Across 15 diverse contact geometries, the average root-mean-square difference between the hardware and software reconstructions was 0.17% of the peak contact depth, with individual values ranging from 0.06% to 0.26%. This difference was typically observed near sensor boundaries and within the contact region.
- A word-length sweep determined that 24-bit arithmetic provided an optimal balance between accuracy and hardware resources, as accuracy did not significantly improve with longer word lengths beyond 24 bits.

**Robotic Protective Reflex Loop:**
- The near-sensor pathway enabled a complete contact-to-motion latency of 28.3 ± 4.9 ms (mean ± s.d.) for a robot protective reflex.
- In contrast, an equivalent host-based loop, using the same actuator, exhibited a contact-to-motion latency of 169.9 ± 27.8 ms. This represents a 6.0-fold reduction in latency with the near-sensor approach.
- The contact-to-command latency for the near-sensor pathway was 10.2 ± 3.2 ms, compared to 148.6 ± 28.3 ms for the host-based loop, an approximate 14.6-fold reduction.
- Notably, 18.1 ± 3.3 ms of the 28.3 ms total latency in the near-sensor loop was attributed to the servo response, indicating that the sensing and decision-making components contributed less than 10 ms.
- The achieved 28.3 ms latency for the near-sensor loop falls below the reported range for human nociceptive withdrawal reflexes (65–137 ms), while the host-based loop (169.9 ms) falls well above it.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research represents a technical advancement in visuotactile perception by successfully mapping a spectral Poisson solver onto a fully pipelined hardware architecture for near-sensor computation. The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to provide accurate, energy-efficient, and deterministically low-latency dense 3D contact geometry reconstructions, which addresses critical limitations in current robotic systems.

The potential impacts of this work are severalfold:

1. **Enabling High-Speed Robotic Manipulation and Control:** The demonstrated deterministic latency of 0.211 ms for 128x128 depth reconstruction and the 6.0-fold reduction in protective reflex loop latency (to 28.3 ms) are crucial for robotics. This capability can facilitate ultrafast closed-loop behaviors such as rapid slip reactions, dynamic object manipulation, and precise force control that have previously been constrained by the delays and variability of host-based processing. The fixed latency is particularly valuable for stable and predictable feedback control systems.
2. **Enhancing Robot Safety and Responsiveness:** The ability to close a protective reflex loop entirely at the sensor, with a contact-to-motion latency below that of human nociceptive withdrawal reflexes, significantly enhances robot safety. This enables robots to react to unforeseen contact events with extreme rapidity, potentially mitigating damage to both the robot and its environment or human collaborators.
3. **Improving Energy Efficiency and System Integration:** The substantially lower power consumption of the near-sensor architecture (estimated 324 mW for the ASIC core) compared to host-based solutions (tens to hundreds of Watts) facilitates the integration of advanced tactile perception into power-constrained robotic platforms, miniature grippers, and mobile robots. This power efficiency could lead to more compact, self-contained, and enduring robotic systems.
4. **Providing a Unified and Versatile Tactile Representation:** By outputting calibrated depth maps in millimeters directly from the sensor, the system provides a rich and common representation for various downstream tasks. This enables the transferability of force-estimation models across different visuotactile sensors and can improve performance in applications such as tactile Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) compared to appearance-based features.
5. **Advancing Near-Sensor and In-Sensor Computing Paradigms:** This work demonstrates that complex computations, specifically PDE solving for dense 3D reconstruction, can be effectively moved to the sensor interface. This extends the scope of near-sensor computing beyond simpler operations like filtering or feature extraction, validating its potential for more sophisticated real-time processing tasks.
6. **Complementary Sensing Technology:** The architecture complements existing event-based tactile sensors. While event-based sensors excel at capturing rapid temporal changes like slip and vibration, this system provides dense 3D depth information at high temporal bandwidth, offering a more complete picture of contact geometry.
7. **Broader Applicability:** The streaming architecture of the spectral Poisson solver is not limited to tactile reconstruction. Its design principles could be extended to other scientific and engineering applications governed by Poisson equations where high-speed, deterministic, and energy-efficient solutions are required.

While the current limitation regarding grid size (primarily constrained by on-chip memory for resolutions above 256x256) exists, the core architectural principles are scalable. Future designs incorporating external memory could address this. Overall, this research represents a step towards making superhuman tactile perception and dexterous manipulation more practical and responsive for advanced robotic systems.
