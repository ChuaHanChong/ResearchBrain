# From Action Labels to Sets: Rethinking Action Supervision for Imitation Learning from Corrective Feedback

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Delft University of Technology and collaborators introduced Contrastive policy Learning from Interactive Corrections (CLIC), a framework that employs set-valued supervision for robot imitation learning. This approach demonstrated increased robustness to noisy and partial human feedback and achieved competitive success rates in complex multi-modal tasks on real robots.

## Problem

- Pointwise supervision (e.g., Behavior Cloning) is brittle to imperfect, noisy, or suboptimal human actions, leading to expressive policy models overfitting to these imperfections.
- Pairwise preference learning provides insufficient constraints in high-dimensional, continuous action spaces, allowing expressive policies to remain under-constrained and allocate probability mass to suboptimal regions.
- Existing methods that convert relative corrective feedback into pointwise targets can lead to outdated and contradictory supervision as the policy improves, destabilizing training and reducing data utility.

## Method

- CLIC replaces strict pointwise action targets with set-valued supervision, defining 'desired action sets' (e.g., polytopes or circular regions) from human corrective feedback.
- It constructs desired action sets using hyperparameters that reflect the uncertainty in human input, making the supervision tolerant to noisy, partial, and relative corrections.
- The framework trains implicit (EBM-based) or explicit policies using a KL divergence objective, with a policy-weighted Bayesian update that encourages stable, incremental learning by aligning the policy's probability mass with the defined desired action sets.

## Results

- CLIC variants exhibited significantly higher robustness to Gaussian and directional noise in demonstration data, outperforming baselines like Diffusion Policy and Implicit Behavior Cloning.
- On the real-robot Insert-T task, CLIC-Half achieved up to **80%** success on hard difficulty, compared to **30%** for Diffusion Policy and **10%** for IBC, demonstrating efficacy in complex, multi-modal, long-horizon tasks.
- CLIC-Explicit enabled successful learning from partial and relative feedback in dynamic real-robot tasks like ball-catching (**100%** success within two attempts) and water-pouring (**90%** success).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Set-valued supervision provides a robust intermediate form of feedback between strict pointwise targets and weak pairwise preferences, offering more informative constraints for policy learning, especially with expressive models and imperfect human input.
> - A policy-weighted prior, when used to construct the target distribution for policy updates, enables stable and incremental learning by leveraging the current policy's knowledge and mitigating issues with potentially suboptimal actions within the desired set.
> - The geometry of the desired action sets can be specifically designed (e.g., cone-like polytopes for general or relative corrections, circular sets for more absolute feedback) to effectively accommodate different types and uncertainties of human feedback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning often leverages human demonstrations to teach complex skills, a paradigm known as Imitation Learning. A common approach within this paradigm is Behavior Cloning (BC), which frames policy learning as a supervised learning problem where each demonstrated action at a given state serves as a pointwise training target. While effective with precise data, this pointwise supervision method has demonstrated brittleness when human-provided actions are imperfect, suboptimal, or noisy. In such scenarios, expressive models, such as those derived from energy-based models (EBMs) or score functions, can overfit to these imperfections, deviating from the underlying optimal behavior.

The domain of interactive imitation learning (IIL) extends BC by allowing humans to provide corrective feedback when a learning policy performs suboptimally. However, many existing IIL methods still rely on pointwise BC objectives, inheriting the same vulnerabilities to imperfect data.

An alternative direction in robot learning is learning from preference feedback. This approach relaxes the strict pointwise assumption by utilizing pairwise comparisons between actions or trajectories. This allows the learner to reason about relative quality and can facilitate improvement even from suboptimal demonstrations. Earlier preference-based methods often involved a two-stage process of learning a reward model, followed by policy optimization via reinforcement learning, which can introduce modeling complexity and optimization instability. Recent advancements directly update policies from pairwise comparisons. However, a limitation of pairwise ranking losses is that they only constrain the relative ordering of two actions, often leaving large portions of the continuous action space weakly constrained. This issue is particularly pronounced with expressive policy classes, which can satisfy such constraints while still allocating substantial probability mass to suboptimal regions.

In parallel, research on learning from relative corrections, where feedback indicates the direction of improvement rather than an exact action, often converts these corrections into surrogate action targets, which are then used within a pointwise BC objective. This introduces a challenge: as the policy improves, past on-policy corrections can become suboptimal or contradictory, potentially destabilizing the training process and limiting the utility of replay buffers.

This research paper positions itself by proposing a set-based formulation that aims to bridge the gap between the strictness of pointwise BC and the weak constraints of pairwise preference learning. It seeks to develop a supervision objective that is more robust than pointwise BC and more informative than pairwise preference learning, particularly for training expressive policy models in continuous, high-dimensional action spaces, and in scenarios involving various forms of imperfect human feedback. The work specifically addresses the limitations of existing approaches to handling noisy, partial, and relative corrective feedback within an interactive learning framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from practical challenges encountered in imitation learning, particularly when policies are learned from human-provided data or interactive corrections. The authors identify several specific issues that existing methods struggle with, leading to the formulation of their objectives:

1. **Brittleness of Pointwise Supervision:**
 - **Motivation:** Standard Behavior Cloning (BC) treats each human demonstration or correction as an exact, optimal action label. In real-world scenarios, human feedback can be noisy, suboptimal, or imprecise due to factors such as human motor variability, cognitive load, or limitations of teleoperation interfaces.
 - **Problem:** When such imperfect data is treated as ground truth, expressive policy models (e.g., Energy-Based Models (EBMs) or diffusion models) are prone to overfitting to these imperfections rather than learning the underlying desired behavior. This results in policies that are not robust and may perform inconsistently.
 - **Objective:** Develop a supervision mechanism that is more tolerant of imperfect human-provided actions and reduces overfitting to individual, potentially noisy, data points.

2. **Insufficient Constraints from Pairwise Preference Learning:**
 - **Motivation:** Approaches based on learning from preference feedback address the strictness of pointwise supervision by only requiring relative comparisons (e.g., action A is better than action B).
 - **Problem:** While less strict, these pairwise constraints often provide insufficient information to adequately shape policies in high-dimensional continuous action spaces. Many action distributions can satisfy a given set of pairwise preferences while still assigning substantial probability mass to suboptimal actions. This is particularly problematic for expressive policy classes, which can represent a vast number of such under-constrained solutions.
 - **Objective:** Introduce a supervision objective that provides richer constraints on the action space than pairwise comparisons, ensuring more targeted policy learning without requiring exact action targets.

3. **Instability with Relative Corrective Feedback:**
 - **Motivation:** Relative corrections, which indicate a direction of improvement rather than a specific action, can be easier for humans to provide. Existing methods often convert these into surrogate pointwise targets.
 - **Problem:** As the learning policy improves iteratively, previous on-policy corrections (which were generated relative to an earlier, less capable policy) can become outdated, suboptimal, or even contradictory. Treating these outdated corrections as exact targets can destabilize training and necessitate discarding past data, reducing learning efficiency.
 - **Objective:** Formulate a method for incorporating relative corrections that preserves their utility over time, allowing for robust aggregation of feedback without destabilizing policy updates.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

- **To propose a set-valued supervision perspective for corrective feedback.** This involves interpreting human corrections not as single points but as defining sets of acceptable actions, bridging the gap between pointwise behavior cloning and pairwise preference learning.
- **To derive a practical policy-shaping framework, Contrastive policy Learning from Interactive Corrections (CLIC).** This framework constructs desired action sets from various types of human feedback (absolute, relative, noisy, partial) and trains implicit (EBM-based) or explicit policies to align their probability mass with these sets.
- **To demonstrate through extensive evaluation** (in simulation and on real robots) that this proposed formulation improves robustness to noisy feedback, effectively captures multi-modal behaviors, and unifies learning from both absolute and relative corrections within a single framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Contrastive policy Learning from Interactive Corrections (CLIC), is an iterative interactive imitation learning (IIL) framework that replaces pointwise action targets with set-valued supervision.

#### 3.1. Policy Representation

The primary policy representation used in CLIC is an Energy-Based Model (EBM), denoted as $E_\theta(s, a)$. The EBM takes a state $s$ and an action $a$ as input and outputs a scalar energy value, from which the action distribution is derived: $\pi_\theta(a|s) = \frac{1}{Z} e^{-E_\theta(s,a)}$. This implicit policy representation is capable of modeling complex, multi-modal action distributions. For unimodal tasks, an explicit Gaussian policy, $\pi_\theta(a|s) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \Sigma)$, is also considered for efficiency.

#### 3.2. Desired Action Set Formulation and Aggregation

A core aspect of CLIC is the interpretation of corrective feedback $(a_r, a_h)$ at a state $s$ (where $a_r$ is the robot's action and $a_h$ is the human-provided action or modification) as defining a *desired action set* $\hat{A}(a_r, a_h)$. This set must satisfy two properties:
1. **Consistency:** $a_h \in \hat{A}(a_r, a_h)$ and $a_r \notin \hat{A}(a_r, a_h)$.
2. **Set-valued supervision:** $\hat{A}(a_r, a_h) \cap A^*_s \neq \emptyset$, where $A^*_s$ is the true optimal action set, but $\hat{A}(a_r, a_h)$ may contain suboptimal actions. This represents a weaker assumption than pointwise BC.

Two specific types of desired action sets are introduced:

- **Polytope Desired Action Set (CLIC-Half):** This set is constructed from corrective feedback using multiple *half-space desired sets*. A half-space $A_H(a_-, a_+)$ includes actions closer to $a_+$ than $a_-$. Through data augmentation, multiple contrastive action pairs $(a_{-i}, a_{+i})$ are generated from $(a_r, a_h)$. The intersection of these half-spaces forms a polytope, which approaches a cone. Hyperparameters $\epsilon \in [0, 1)$ (magnitude certainty) and $\alpha \in (0^\circ, 180^\circ]$ (directional uncertainty) control the geometry of this cone, allowing it to reflect the uncertainty in the human feedback. This formulation is designed to be applicable to both absolute and relative corrections.

- **Circular Desired Action Set (CLIC-Circular):** Primarily for absolute corrections, this set is defined as a ball centered at $a_h$ with a radius of $(1-\epsilon) \cdot D(a_r, a_h)$, where $D(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the Euclidean distance. As $\epsilon \to 1$, the ball shrinks to a single point $a_h$, recovering pointwise supervision as a limiting case.

During interactive learning, corrective feedback is aggregated into a data buffer $\mathcal{D}$. For a given state $s$, the feedback instances in $\mathcal{D}_s$ collectively define an *overall desired action set* $\hat{A}^{\mathcal{D}_s}_k = \bigcap_i \hat{A}(a_{r_i}, a_{h_i})$, which refines as more feedback is incorporated. The policy is iteratively shaped to implicitly approximate this accumulating overall desired region.

#### 3.3. Policy Shaping Objective

The core learning objective is to encourage the policy $\pi_\theta$ to increase the probability of selecting actions within a given desired action set $\hat{A}(a_r, a_h)$. This is achieved by minimizing the KL divergence between the current policy and a *target distribution* $\pi_{target}(a|s, \hat{A}(a_r, a_h))$.
$\ell_{KL}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(a_h, a_r, s) \sim p_{\mathcal{D}}} [KL(\pi_{target}(a|s) || \pi_\theta(a|s))]$.
This objective is approximated using a set of sampled actions $A = \{a_h, a_r\} \cup \{a_j\}_{j=1}^{N_a}$, where $a_j$ are sampled from the current EBM using Langevin MCMC. The KL objective can be reformulated as a weighted sum of InfoNCE losses, where the weights are determined by $\pi_{target}(a|s)$. This differs from standard InfoNCE by distributing the learning signal across multiple desired actions, reducing overfitting to a single (potentially noisy) action label.

#### 3.4. Target Distribution Construction

The target distribution $\pi_{target}(a|s)$ is constructed as a posterior distribution from a prior $p(a|s)$ and a soft set-membership likelihood, or *observation model*, $p(\text{a} \in \hat{A}(a_r, a_h) | a, s)$.
$\pi_{target}(a|s) = \frac{p(\text{a} \in \hat{A}(a_r, a_h) | a, s) \cdot p(a|s)}{p(\text{a} \in \hat{A}(a_r, a_h) | s)}$.

- **Observation Model:** This model uses a smoothed sigmoid function, $\sigma_T(x) = (1 + \exp(-x/T))^{-1}$, to define a soft membership likelihood. The temperature $T$ controls the sharpness of the boundary.
 - For CLIC-Half, it is the product of sigmoids for each half-space.
 - For CLIC-Circular, it is a sigmoid based on the action's distance to $a_h$ relative to the set's radius.
- **Prior Choices:**
 - A uniform prior yields a baseline target.
 - A *policy-weighted prior*, where $p(a|s) = \pi_\theta(a|s)$, is used to yield conservative, incremental updates. This leverages the current policy's learned knowledge and avoids over-emphasizing potentially suboptimal regions within the desired set.

#### 3.5. CLIC-Explicit for Gaussian Policies

For unimodal tasks, a simplified CLIC-Explicit variant is introduced, assuming a Gaussian policy with fixed covariance. The objective is to ensure that the policy assigns more probability mass to the desired action set than its complement. This is implemented using a hinge loss that encourages the mean of the Gaussian to lie within the polytope desired action set by ensuring that each $a_{+i}$ has higher likelihood than its contrastive counterpart $a_{-i}$. This formulation demonstrates connections to standard pairwise ranking losses.

#### 3.6. Interactive Learning Loop (Algorithm 1)

The overall CLIC algorithm operates within a standard IIL loop. The robot executes an action; if suboptimal, a human provides feedback. This feedback is stored, and desired action sets are generated from batches of this data. The policy is then updated using the KL divergence objective with the specified target distribution. This iterative process allows for progressive refinement of the overall desired action set and continuous policy improvement.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive evaluations in both simulation and real-robot experiments, comparing CLIC variants (CLIC-Half, CLIC-Circular, CLIC-Explicit) against various state-of-the-art baselines.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Accurate Demonstration Feedback (Table 1):**
 - CLIC-Half and CLIC-Circular demonstrated competitive performance with Diffusion Policy (DP), which is a strong baseline for accurate data, and consistently outperformed Implicit Behavior Cloning (IBC). CLIC-Circular often achieved the highest success rates, indicating that its more specific circular sets are beneficial when feedback is precise.
 - CLIC-Explicit outperformed HG-DAgger on unimodal tasks.
 - PVP (Proxy Value Propagation), which uses pairwise comparison with an EBM, showed poor performance on robosuite tasks, suggesting that pairwise supervision alone is insufficient for expressive implicit policies in continuous action spaces.

- **Noisy Demonstration Data (Table 3):**
 - Under Gaussian noise and directional noise, CLIC-Half and CLIC-Circular exhibited significantly higher robustness compared to baselines like DP and IBC, which experienced substantial performance degradation. Ambient Diffusion Policy (ADP) performed better than DP but was still outperformed by CLIC, as ADP's assumption of linearly corrupted noise was not fully met.
 - This robustness is attributed to CLIC's set-valued supervision, which can accommodate uncertainty in feedback, unlike methods relying on strict pointwise targets.

- **Partial and Relative Feedback (Table 4):**
 - **Partial Feedback:** In the TwoArm-Lift task where feedback was provided only for one robot, CLIC-Half maintained a high success rate, outperforming DP and HG-DAgger. CLIC-Half's approach of constraining only feedback dimensions while leaving others unconstrained proved effective. CLIC-Circular's performance dropped, indicating that its specific set definition may not generalize to partial feedback scenarios.
 - **Relative Corrections:** CLIC-Half and CLIC-Explicit demonstrated strong performance under relative corrections, showing only minor drops compared to accurate feedback scenarios. In contrast, all baselines (DP, IBC, ADP, D-COACH) that rely on pointwise BC objectives failed or performed poorly. CLIC's polytope desired action sets (CLIC-Half, CLIC-Explicit) were effective because they provide less specific supervision but remain compatible with optimal actions even as the policy improves, avoiding conflicts with outdated feedback. CLIC-Circular failed here, as its circular sets can exclude optimal actions when feedback is only directional.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies (Fig. 9, 10)

- **Directional Certainty ($\alpha$):** An optimal range for $\alpha$ was identified, balancing the informativeness of the desired set with its ability to cover optimal actions under feedback noise. Too small an $\alpha$ (e.g., < $2\beta$ for noise angle $\beta$) risks excluding optimal actions, while too large an $\alpha$ makes the set less informative.
- **Temperature ($T$):** Intermediate values of $T$ (e.g., $T=0.1$) were found to be optimal for the sigmoid-based observation model. Very low $T$ (hard boundary) led to training instability, while very high $T$ provided insufficient information.
- **Policy-weighted vs. Uniform Bayes Loss:** The policy-weighted Bayes loss significantly outperformed the uniform variant. This highlights the importance of incorporating the current policy as a prior for stable, incremental updates, especially since desired action sets might initially include suboptimal actions.

#### 4.3. Toy Experiments on Noisy Feedback (Fig. 11)

- In a 2D action space with noisy feedback, CLIC learned consistent EBM energy landscapes with minima close to the true optimal action and low variance across trials.
- PVP, based on pairwise comparisons, yielded under-constrained landscapes, assigning high probability mass to broad, potentially suboptimal regions.
- IBC overfit to individual noisy human actions, resulting in fragmented energy landscapes with high variance across trials.
- The study also demonstrated that CLIC-Circular can reduce to IBC-like behavior as the $\epsilon$ parameter increases, shrinking the circular desired action set to a single point, thus illustrating the generalization capabilities of CLIC.

#### 4.4. Real-robot Validations

- **Insert-T (Multi-modal, Long-horizon):** CLIC-Half significantly outperformed DP and IBC, especially on medium and hard difficulty tasks (e.g., 80% success for CLIC-Half versus 30% for DP and 10% for IBC on hard tasks). This indicates CLIC's effectiveness in complex, multi-modal tasks and its stable EBM training. CLIC also showed comparable performance in offline training, suggesting broader applicability.
- **Ball-catching (Dynamic, Partial, Relative Feedback):** CLIC-Explicit achieved a 100% success rate within two attempts and 40% for the first attempt in this high-frequency, dynamic task. This demonstrated CLIC's ability to leverage relative and partial feedback for quick coordination.
- **Water-pouring (Full Pose Control, Mixed Feedback):** CLIC-Explicit facilitated learning precise position and orientation control, with success rates increasing from 60% to 90% over episodes. The ability to switch between absolute and relative corrections was shown to be beneficial for initial learning and subsequent fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel paradigm of set-valued supervision for robot imitation learning, offering a significant contribution to the field, particularly in the context of human-in-the-loop interactive learning.

- **Bridging a Gap in Supervision Paradigms:** The core contribution of proposing set-valued supervision provides a robust alternative that lies between the strictness of pointwise Behavior Cloning (BC) and the weak constraints of pairwise preference learning. This middle-ground approach addresses limitations of both conventional methods, offering a more nuanced way to interpret and utilize human feedback.

- **Enhanced Robustness to Imperfect Feedback:** A key practical impact is the demonstrated improvement in robustness to various forms of imperfect human feedback, including noise, partial information, and relative corrections. This is critical for deploying robots in real-world scenarios where human input is inherently variable and often suboptimal. By not treating every correction as an exact target, CLIC mitigates overfitting and leads to more generalized and stable policies.

- **Unified Framework for Diverse Feedback Types:** CLIC offers a single framework capable of effectively learning from both absolute (demonstrations/interventions) and relative corrections. This flexibility simplifies the design of human-robot interaction systems, as different feedback modalities can be integrated without requiring distinct learning algorithms. The choice of desired action set (polytope vs. circular) allows adaptation to the nature of the feedback.

- **Effective Training of Expressive Policies:** The method's success in shaping implicit policies (EBMs) to represent complex, multi-modal behaviors in tasks like Insert-T is noteworthy. Many advanced policy representations struggle with stability and overfitting when trained with conventional BC losses. CLIC's set-valued approach provides a more stable training signal, enabling these powerful models to realize their potential in learning intricate skills.

- **Demonstrated Real-World Applicability:** The validation through diverse real-robot tasks (Insert-T, ball-catching, water-pouring) underscores the practical utility and scalability of CLIC. These experiments showcase its effectiveness in handling long-horizon tasks, dynamic environments, precise pose control, and situations requiring coordination.

- **Foundational for Future Research:** The concepts introduced, particularly the set-valued supervision and policy-weighted Bayesian updates, lay a foundation for several promising future research directions. These include integrating non-intervention data for more data-efficient learning, exploring evaluative feedback to complement corrective signals, and applying the CLIC loss framework to other generative models like diffusion or flow-based models, further extending its reach and capabilities in robot learning. The public availability of the implementation also facilitates further research and benchmarking.
