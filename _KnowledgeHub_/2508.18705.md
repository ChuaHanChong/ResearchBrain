# Enhancing Video-Based Robot Failure Detection Using Task Knowledge

> [!summary] Summary
> A video-based robot failure detector that exploits task knowledge the robot already has: temporal boundaries of its actions and bounding boxes of task-relevant objects. These guide frame selection, action-aligned region-of-interest cropping, and a variable frame-rate augmentation that samples one action densely. On ARMBench, F1 rises from 77.9 to 80.0 with no extra compute, and to 81.4 with test-time augmentation.

## Problem

- Prior video failure detectors sub-sample frames uniformly at fixed stride, so brief failure moments are missed and coverage varies wildly across clips spanning 2-74 seconds.
- Existing methods inject task knowledge only via hard-coded fixed crops, language task descriptions for vision-language models, or auxiliary action-prediction losses, never to guide which frames are sampled.
- Available failure datasets are small, lab-collected, with induced failures and no class imbalance; the one large exception carries roughly 1100 mislabeled samples and wrong failure timestamps.

## Method

- **Action Subset selection** restricts the classifier's 32 uniformly sampled frames to MoveDestination, Wait and Place, where robot-object interaction and failures concentrate, feeding an ==MViT-B== video transformer.
- **Action-aligned ROI cropping** crops frames to the union of ==end-effector== and relevant container boxes from a ==YOLO== detector, switching source or destination region per action segment.
- **Variable frame-rate augmentation** randomly picks one action and draws most of the 32 frames from it, sparsely elsewhere; ==test-time augmentation== averages logits across all action variants.

## Results

- On ARMBench, action-subset selection plus ROI cropping lifts MViT-B F1 from **77.9** to **79.6**; train-and-test variable frame-rate augmentation reaches **81.4**, near the **82.1** oracle upper bound.
- Augmentation also helps smaller datasets: FAILURE **87.0** to **88.6** and (Im)PerfectPour **96.9** to **97.3** F1; a ResNet-18 image-pair model hits **92.6** on FAILURE versus VGG-RGB's **90.1**.
- Classifying actions in isolation collapses ARMBench F1 to **70.8** (MViT-B) and **56.8** (ResNet-18), with open-failure false-positive rate rising to about **3.5 percent**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cheap task metadata the robot already computes, action boundaries and object locations, buys accuracy in the data pipeline rather than the model, at zero inference cost.
> - Aligning variable frame-rate augmentation to action boundaries gave no gain over random high-rate windows, suggesting the benefit is rate diversity, not semantic alignment.
> - Wide temporal context is needed to disambiguate related failure classes; per-action classification spots that something failed but confuses cascading partial and full failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Detecting an execution failure lets a robot switch to a safe mode, try recovery, or replan. From video, though, a fixed handful of frames can miss what happened. In warehouse pick-and-place, some failures are visible only briefly, and an object that first appears open may later be deconstructed. Santosh Thoduka, Sebastian Houben, Juergen Gall, and Paul G. Plöger address this in “Enhancing Video-Based Robot Failure Detection Using Task Knowledge” (ECMR 2025; [published version](https://doi.org/10.1109/ECMR65884.2025.11162998), arXiv paper). They use task knowledge, meaning the timing of robot actions and the locations of task-relevant objects, to choose and crop frames before a video classifier sees them. They also vary the sampling rate across task segments during training and, optionally, inference.

The authors’ conceptual pipeline: robot-action labels and detected-object boxes guide preprocessing and frame selection before a classification model outputs “object opened” or “object deconstructed.”

### 2. The task is in the timeline

The setting is ARMBench, a dataset of warehouse pick-and-place videos. It distinguishes nominal execution, an opened object, and deconstruction. Video clips range from 2 to 74 seconds. The 32-frame input to the video classifier must still cover the task, even though failures can be brief. Some deconstruction failures progress through an open stage before breaking into pieces. For that reason, the observed state and the task’s history both matter.

The authors revised the dataset before running their experiments. They corrected about 1,100 labels. They labeled action boundaries using an MS-TCN trained with 500 manually annotated samples. They obtained boxes for the end-effector and the source and destination containers with a trained YOLO-based detector. ARMBench has five phases: Pick, MoveDestination, Wait, Place, and MoveSource. The chosen subset is MoveDestination, Wait, and Place, where the authors say most robot-object interaction and likely failures occur.

### 3. Spending the frame budget where context helps

The classifier still consumes 32 frames. Instead of sampling uniformly over the whole clip, the method can sample from the action subset. Action-based cropping uses the action phase to choose the relevant source or destination container region together with the end-effector. Variable-frame-rate augmentation randomly gives most of the 32 frames to one action and fewer to the others.

Frame-selection patterns along a timeline of robot actions: (a) baseline, (b) action subset, (c) single action, and (d) action subset plus augmentation. Gray ticks mark selected frames.

Performance is measured with F1, which combines precision and recall, on the three-outcome classification task. The tests use the MViT-B video classifier on the corrected ARMBench data, and results are means over three training runs. The baseline uses uniform full-video sampling without action-based cropping and scores 77.9 F1. Action-subset sampling plus training-time augmentation and action-based cropping scores 80.0. That comparison changes frame selection, cropping, and training augmentation together, so it does not isolate one cause.

Adding test-time augmentation raises the result to 81.4. However, this step adds inference computation, and open-class recall is 67.9 in that row versus 69.3 in the 80.0 row.

ARMBench results by method (Baseline, Action subset, Single action, Eight frames), augmentation, and crop, with F1 and deconstruction/open recall and false-positive rate.

The authors checked whether aligning augmentation to action boundaries matters. Their action-aligned-versus-random comparison finds no significant difference on ARMBench. Classifying a single action performs much worse. The authors interpret this as insufficient context for distinguishing failure types.

### 4. What the result establishes

The contribution is a change to frame selection and preprocessing that uses available task structure, rather than a new classifier architecture. The authors say focusing on task-informed actions and regions improves performance without additional computational expense. The evidence comes from benchmark classification on three datasets, not from deployed robot recovery. False negatives remain for brief failures and subtle open defects. The results also do not establish a uniquely best sampling heuristic. The authors released a [code repository](https://github.com/sthoduka/using_task_knowledge) and [corrected annotations on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/15873769).
