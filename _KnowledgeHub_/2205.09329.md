# Dataset Pruning: Reducing Training Data by Examining Generalization Influence

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UTS and Baidu Research introduce an optimization-based dataset pruning method that leverages influence functions to identify and remove redundant training data. This approach significantly reduces training time, for instance, halving convergence time on CIFAR-10 with a 40% data reduction and a minimal 1.3% accuracy decrease, and accelerates Neural Architecture Search by 96% while maintaining performance.

## Problem

- Training large deep learning models on ever-growing datasets consumes immense computational resources and energy.
- A significant portion of training data might be redundant, contributing little to a model's generalization performance.
- Existing sample selection methods are often heuristic, lacking theoretical guarantees, and do not adequately account for the joint influence of data subsets.

## Method

- Defines ==dataset pruning== as a ==constrained discrete optimization problem== to find the largest "redundant" data subset while maintaining a generalization guarantee.
- Leverages ==Influence Functions== to efficiently estimate the combined impact of removing a subset of training data on model parameters, avoiding costly retraining.
- Employs ==simulated annealing== to solve the combinatorial optimization problem, aiming to maximize pruned samples under a specified parameter change constraint.

## Results

- The proposed method consistently outperforms baseline dataset pruning techniques, particularly at higher pruning ratios, across various datasets and network architectures.
- Achieved significant improvements in training efficiency, for example, halving convergence time on CIFAR-10 (**4**0%** pruned**) with only a **1.3%** test accuracy drop.
- Substantially accelerated Neural Architecture Search, reducing search time by **96%** (from **3029** to **113** minutes) while finding architectures with comparable performance (**85.7%** vs **85.9%** full data).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Deep learning datasets contain substantial redundant information that can be systematically removed to improve training efficiency.
> - Influence functions can be effectively utilized and extended to estimate the combined impact of removing groups of data points on model parameters.
> - Constraining the change in model parameters by a small epsilon provides a quantifiable and empirically verifiable bound on the actual generalization gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in deep learning have been largely fueled by increasingly complex models and vast datasets. However, this success comes at a significant cost: immense computational resources, energy consumption, and infrastructure requirements for training, inference, hyper-parameter tuning, and neural architecture search (NAS). This paper addresses a critical aspect of this challenge: **data redundancy**.

Traditionally, research efforts to reduce computational costs in deep learning have primarily focused on **model compression techniques**, such as network pruning (e.g., Blalock et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2018; Molchanov et al., 2019), which aim to remove redundant parameters from trained neural networks. In contrast, this work shifts attention to the **data side of the problem**, arguing that not all training data equally contribute to a model's performance, and a significant portion might be redundant.

The paper situates itself within the context of **sample selection** and **dataset condensation/distillation** methods:

- **Sample Selection:** This line of work aims to identify and select the most informative or representative samples from a larger dataset. Prior methods often rely on heuristic criteria like distance to class center (Welling, 2009; Castro et al., 2018), diversity (Sener & Savarese, 2018b), forgetting scores (Toneva et al., 2019a), or gradient norms (Paul et al., 2021). The authors critique these methods for being (a) heuristic, lacking theoretical guarantees for generalization, and (b) neglecting the "joint effect" or interaction between selected samples. This paper aims to overcome these limitations by providing theoretical guarantees and considering the collective influence of subsets.
- **Dataset Condensation/Distillation:** This more recent field focuses on synthesizing a small, highly informative "condensed" dataset that can serve as a proxy for the original large dataset (e.g., Wang et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2021). While both dataset pruning and condensation aim to reduce training data, condensation creates synthetic data, whereas pruning selects a subset of real data. The authors highlight that condensation methods typically produce extremely small datasets (e.g., 50 images per class) and often yield less satisfactory performance compared to training on a large subset of real data. Thus, their proposed dataset pruning method is positioned as distinct and not directly comparable in terms of core objective or achieved performance level.
- **Influence Functions:** A key theoretical tool leveraged in this paper is the **Influence Function (IF)**, originally from robust statistics (Cook, 1977; Hampel et al., 2011) and later adapted for deep learning by Koh & Liang (2017a). IFs provide a way to efficiently estimate how much a specific training point influences the model's parameters or predictions without requiring expensive retraining. While some prior work (Borsos et al., 2020) used IF-like concepts for coreset construction, this paper directly leverages IFs to formulate an optimization problem with a generalization guarantee, specifically focusing on the *combined influence* of a *subset* of data. This differentiates it from methods that simply rank individual samples by their influence score.

In summary, this work contributes to the broader landscape by addressing the overlooked problem of data redundancy in deep learning training. It proposes a novel, theoretically-grounded, and optimization-based approach to sample selection that accounts for the joint influence of data points, aiming to deliver stronger generalization guarantees and practical efficiency gains compared to prior heuristic methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper is primarily motivated by the immense computational and infrastructural costs associated with training increasingly large deep learning models on ever-growing datasets. This leads to several crucial questions that the authors aim to address:
1. **Do all training data contribute equally to a model's performance?** The implicit assumption is that a significant portion of training data might be redundant, contributing little to generalization while incurring substantial computational overhead.
2. **How can one quantify the influence of individual training samples or sub-training-sets on a model's generalization ability?** Answering this question is crucial for identifying redundant data.
3. **How can the smallest possible subset of the entire training data be constructed to serve as a proxy training set without significantly sacrificing the model's performance?** This is the ultimate practical goal: achieving training efficiency while maintaining generalization.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of the paper are:
- To formally define and address the "dataset pruning problem" as an optimization challenge, extending traditional sample selection from a cardinality constraint to a generalization constraint. Instead of finding a fixed-size subset with good performance, they aim to find the *smallest* subset that *satisfies a predefined generalization ability*.
- To propose an **optimization-based sample selection method** that can:
 - Efficiently estimate the influence of removing a particular set of training samples on a model's generalization ability, supported by theoretical guarantees. This bypasses the prohibitive cost of re-training the model for every possible subset.
 - Construct the largest possible "redundant" subset (or equivalently, the smallest "informative" subset) such that the model trained on the pruned data maintains a strictly constrained generalization gap compared to the model trained on the full dataset.
- To empirically demonstrate that the generalization gap observed in practice is consistent with their theoretical expectations.
- To show that their proposed method significantly outperforms existing score-based sample selection methods in terms of pruning efficiency (i.e., higher pruning ratios with minimal accuracy drop) and improves training efficiency, particularly for applications like Neural Architecture Search (NAS).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology, termed **"dataset pruning,"** is an optimization-based sample selection method that leverages influence functions to efficiently identify redundant data.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation: $\epsilon$-Redundant Subset

The authors define an $\epsilon$-redundant subset $\hat{D}$ as a subset of the full dataset $D$ such that the $L_2$ norm of the difference between the model parameters trained on the full dataset ($\hat{\theta}$) and on the pruned dataset ($D \setminus \hat{D}$, denoted $\hat{\theta}_{-\hat{D}}$) is less than or equal to a small threshold $\epsilon$: $\|\hat{\theta}_{-\hat{D}} - \hat{\theta}\|_2 \leq \epsilon$. The ultimate goal of dataset pruning is to find the *largest* such $\epsilon$-redundant subset $\hat{D}^{\max}_{\epsilon}$, which implies constructing the *smallest* informative subset $\tilde{D} = D \setminus \hat{D}^{\max}_{\epsilon}$.

#### 3.2. Parameter Influence Estimation using Influence Functions

Directly calculating $\hat{\theta}_{-\hat{D}}$ by retraining for every possible subset is computationally infeasible ($2^n$ possibilities). To overcome this, the authors utilize **Influence Functions (IFs)**, a technique from robust statistics adapted for deep learning.
- The parameter change due to omitting a single training sample $z$ (i.e., $\hat{\theta}_{-z} - \hat{\theta}$) can be linearly approximated without retraining. This approximation is given by $I_{\text{param}}(z) = -H_{\hat{\theta}}^{-1} \nabla_{\theta} L(z, \hat{\theta})$, where $H_{\hat{\theta}}$ is the Hessian matrix of the empirical loss at $\hat{\theta}$ and $\nabla_{\theta} L(z, \hat{\theta})$ is the gradient of the loss for sample $z$.
- Crucially, the authors extend this to subsets: the parameter change caused by removing a subset $\hat{D}$ can be approximated by accumulating the individual parameter changes: $\hat{\theta}_{-\hat{D}} - \hat{\theta} \approx \sum_{z_i \in \hat{D}} -\frac{1}{n} I_{\text{param}}(z_i)$. This accumulated individual influence has been shown to accurately reflect the influence of removing a group of data (Koh et al., 2019).
- **Practical Implementation:** To make Hessian inversion tractable, they use a second-order optimization trick (Agarwal et al., 2016) and calculate influence only for the last linear layer of the neural network.

#### 3.3. Dataset Pruning as Discrete Optimization

With the parameter influence estimation, the dataset pruning problem is formalized as a **constrained discrete optimization problem**:
- **(a) Generalization-guaranteed pruning:**
 - **Objective:** Maximize the number of samples to be pruned (i.e., maximize $\sum_{i=1}^n W_i$, where $W_i=1$ if sample $z_i$ is pruned, $W_i=0$ otherwise).
 - **Constraint:** The $L_2$ norm of the accumulated influence vector of the pruned samples must be less than or equal to the predefined threshold $\epsilon$: $\|\mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{S}\|_2 \leq \epsilon$, where $\mathbf{S}$ is a matrix whose columns are the individual parameter influence vectors $I_{\text{param}}(z_i)$.
- **(b) Cardinality-guaranteed pruning:** (For comparison with baselines that specify output size)
 - **Objective:** Minimize the accumulated influence $\|\mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{S}\|_2$.
 - **Constraint:** The number of pruned samples $\sum_{i=1}^n W_i = m$, where $m$ is the desired number of removed samples.

The discrete nature of $W \in \{0, 1\}^n$ makes this a combinatorial optimization problem. The authors use **simulated annealing** to solve this problem, a heuristic known for its ability to find good solutions for complex discrete optimization problems within a reasonable time budget.

#### 3.4. Generalization Analysis

The paper provides a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) that links the parameter change $\epsilon$ to the actual **generalization gap** (the difference in expected test loss) caused by pruning. It states that the generalization gap $|L(\hat{\theta}_{-\hat{D}}) - L(\hat{\theta})|$ is upper bounded by $O(\epsilon/n + m/n^2)$. This theoretical result confirms that by strictly constraining the parameter change (the $\epsilon$ in their optimization), they can effectively bound the expected test loss difference. The $m/n^2$ term is typically ignorable for large $n$.

In essence, the methodology combines the computational efficiency of influence functions with a theoretically-backed optimization framework to systematically remove redundant data while ensuring the model's generalization ability remains within a desired bound.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet datasets, using various network architectures (SqueezeNet, ResNet18, ResNet50), strongly supports the paper's claims.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Analysis Verification

- Figure 2 demonstrates a strong empirical consistency between the observed generalization gap (actual test loss difference) and the theoretical expectation $O(\epsilon/n)$ derived from Theorem 1.
- The "ours" method shows a significantly smaller generalization gap and parameter influence $\epsilon$ compared to random pruning, empirically validating the effectiveness of their optimization-based approach in controlling generalization.

#### 4.2. Dataset Pruning Performance (Comparison with Baselines)

- Figure 1 illustrates that the proposed optimization-based dataset pruning method consistently **outperforms all baseline methods** (Random, Herding, Forgetting, GraNd, EL2N, Influence-score) across different pruning ratios on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet.
- The performance gap between their method and baselines, especially score-based ones (like Influence-score, Forgetting, EL2N), *increases* as the pruning ratio gets higher. This highlights the advantage of considering the "group effect" (joint influence) of pruned examples, which their optimization method inherently captures, unlike heuristic score-based methods that treat samples independently.

#### 4.3. Unseen Architecture Generalization

- Figure 3 shows that a dataset pruned using a smaller network (SENet) can **generalize well to train significantly larger and different network architectures** (ResNet18, ResNet50).
- This finding is crucial as it implies that the pruned dataset retains its fundamental informativeness across various model capacities, making the pruning process transferable and robust. This suggests that dataset pruning is not overfit to the specific architecture used during the pruning process.

#### 4.4. Improvement in Training Efficiency

- **Direct Training Efficiency (Figure 4):** The method significantly improves training efficiency. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet50, pruning 40% of the training examples **halves the convergence time** (from 3200 epochs to 1800 epochs or 1250 epochs with 40% pruned) while incurring only a **minor test accuracy decrease (1.3%)**. This is a substantial practical benefit.
- **Neural Architecture Search (NAS) Acceleration (Table 1):** The pruned datasets are highly effective as "proxy-sets" for NAS.
 - Training 720 different ConvNets on the full CIFAR-10 dataset took 3029 minutes.
 - Using a proxy dataset of only 100 images per class (2% of the original data) selected by their method, the total search time was reduced to 113 minutes (a 96% reduction in time!).
 - Crucially, the best architecture found using their pruned dataset achieved a performance of 85.7% when retrained on the full dataset, which is very close to the 85.9% achieved by searching on the entire dataset (the upper bound).
 - Their method achieved a Spearman's rank correlation coefficient of 0.94 between validation accuracies on the proxy and full datasets, indicating excellent ability to identify the best architectures, far superior to random (0.21) or herding (0.23), and significantly better than forgetting (0.79). This confirms the high fidelity of the pruned dataset in guiding architecture search.

In summary, the main findings highlight the method's theoretical soundness, empirical superiority over baselines, robust cross-architecture generalization, and significant practical impact in reducing training time and accelerating computationally intensive tasks like NAS.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Dataset Pruning" method proposed in this paper holds significant implications for the future of deep learning research and deployment, primarily by addressing the escalating costs associated with large-scale data and models.

#### 5.1. Economic and Environmental Impact

- **Reduced Computational Costs:** By enabling the removal of a substantial portion of redundant training data (e.g., 40% in CIFAR-10), the method directly leads to shorter training times and less computational effort. This translates into tangible economic savings in terms of GPU hours, server infrastructure, and energy consumption.
- **Lower Environmental Footprint:** The reduced energy consumption due to faster training contributes to a smaller carbon footprint for deep learning activities, aligning with growing concerns about the environmental impact of AI.

#### 5.2. Accelerated Research and Development

- **Faster Experimentation:** Researchers can iterate more quickly on model architectures, hyper-parameters, and new algorithms by training on significantly smaller, yet equally effective, pruned datasets. This accelerates the pace of innovation in deep learning.
- **Empowering Smaller Labs/Teams:** Access to massive computational resources is often a barrier for smaller research groups or individual researchers. Dataset pruning can democratize deep learning by making large-scale tasks more accessible with limited resources.
- **Neural Architecture Search (NAS) Revolution:** The demonstrated ability of pruned datasets to faithfully guide NAS, achieving near-optimal architectures with orders of magnitude reduction in search time, is a game-changer. NAS is notoriously computationally expensive, and this method offers a viable path to making it more practical and widely applicable. It allows for efficient exploration of vast architectural spaces.

#### 5.3. Deeper Understanding of Data and Generalization

- **Quantifying Data Value:** The method provides a principled way to quantify the contribution of individual or groups of data points to a model's generalization. This moves beyond heuristic notions of data importance.
- **Insights into Redundancy:** By systematically identifying redundant data, the work contributes to a deeper understanding of what constitutes "informative" data for deep learning models, potentially revealing underlying structures or biases within datasets.
- **Theoretical Guarantees:** The theoretical framework (Theorem 1) providing an upper bound on the generalization gap based on parameter influence is a significant contribution, lending a strong theoretical foundation to data selection methods which have traditionally been more heuristic.

#### 5.4. Practical Applications and Broader Utility

- **Efficient Model Training:** For any scenario involving repeated model training on the same dataset (e.g., hyperparameter tuning, model fine-tuning, continuous learning cycles), dataset pruning can significantly reduce operational costs.
- **Data Curation:** The method can be used as a tool for intelligent data curation, helping to build smaller, more efficient, and perhaps cleaner datasets from larger, uncurated collections. This could be particularly useful in domains where data labeling is expensive or where data privacy is a concern (allowing only necessary data to be processed).

In conclusion, "Dataset Pruning" offers a powerful, theoretically-backed, and empirically validated solution to the crucial problem of data redundancy in deep learning. Its potential to reduce computational costs, accelerate research, and provide deeper insights into data value positions it as a highly significant contribution to the field of machine learning.
