# Learning on the Fly: Rapid Policy Adaptation via Differentiable Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Robotics and Perception Group (RPG) at the University of Zurich developed a framework for rapid, real-time policy adaptation on robotic systems, integrating online residual dynamics learning with differentiable simulation. This approach reduces hovering error by 81% compared to L1-MPC and 55% compared to DATT under large, out-of-distribution disturbances, demonstrating adaptation on real quadrotors within seconds.

## Problem

- Robot control policies trained in simulation often struggle with the sim-to-real gap, performing poorly in the real world due to unmodeled dynamics and external disturbances.
- Existing solutions like domain randomization (DR) fail to generalize to out-of-distribution conditions, while offline Real2Sim2Real pipelines are too costly and slow for rapid adaptation.
- There is a lack of efficient online methods for learned policies to adapt quickly to unknown, time-varying disturbances on physical robots.

## Method

- An online adaptive learning framework is proposed that combines a pre-trained base policy with concurrent real-time components for data collection, residual dynamics learning, and policy adaptation.
- A hybrid dynamics model is used in differentiable simulation, combining a low-fidelity analytical model with a learned residual network that captures unmodeled forces and disturbances.
- Policy gradients are efficiently computed via Back-Propagation Through Time (BPTT) through the differentiable hybrid dynamics, allowing rapid, first-order policy updates on the fly.

## Results

- The method achieved superior performance in simulation, reducing hovering error by 81% compared to L1-MPC and 55% compared to DATT under large, out-of-distribution disturbances.
- Significant performance improvements were observed after only 3 policy adaptation steps, corresponding to 4.5 seconds of policy training in wall time, demonstrating rapid and compute-efficient adaptation.
- Real-world experiments on quadrotors with added mass and wind confirmed the framework's ability to rapidly adapt state-based and vision-based hovering and trajectory tracking policies to complex, unknown disturbances.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Differentiable simulation, when coupled with online residual dynamics learning, enables efficient, low-sample-count policy updates crucial for real-time adaptation in complex robotic tasks.
> - By continuously learning residual dynamics from real-world data, the internal simulation model can rapidly 'overfit' to the current environment, allowing the policy to adapt to unforeseen disturbances.
> - Selective backpropagation through only the analytical dynamics (not the learned residual network) during policy adaptation maintains computational efficiency without hindering performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing, with machine learning playing an increasingly critical role in enabling complex robotic capabilities. A central challenge in deploying learning-based control policies, particularly those trained in simulation, is the **sim-to-real gap**. This gap arises from unmodeled dynamics, environmental disturbances, and imperfect parameter identification in the real world, causing policies trained in simulation to perform poorly when transferred to physical systems.

Current dominant strategies to address the sim-to-real gap include:
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** This involves varying simulation parameters (dynamics, sensor noise, visual appearance) during training to expose the policy to a wide range of conditions, making it more robust. However, DR struggles with out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions not encountered during training, leading to performance degradation.
- **Real2Sim2Real Pipelines,:** These methods refine the simulation model using real-world data offline before retraining policies for deployment. While effective, they require extensive data collection and costly, time-consuming offline retraining, making them unsuitable for rapid, online adaptation to changing conditions.

This paper approaches the sim-to-real problem from a **"different perspective."** Instead of relying on diverse pre-training conditions or costly offline refinement, the authors propose an **online adaptive learning framework** that focuses on rapidly adapting the learned policy in the real world, in an online fashion, to unknown external disturbances. This contrasts significantly with methods like DATT and RMA, which train robust policies offline using domain randomization and then deploy them fixed, relying on disturbance estimation rather than policy adaptation.

The core enabler for this rapid adaptation is **differentiable simulation**. Traditionally, reinforcement learning (RL) methods like PPO suffer from sample inefficiency due to high variance in zeroth-order policy gradient estimates. Differentiable simulation, by providing smooth, differentiable dynamics and rewards, allows for first-order gradient computation via Back-Propagation Through Time (BPTT), leading to substantial gains in sample efficiency and training time over classical RL. While prior work has explored differentiable simulation for policy learning, this paper specifically integrates it with **online residual dynamics learning** to create a closed-loop, real-time adaptation system. The combination of a lightweight analytical model with learned residuals helps to capture complex, unmodeled dynamics and disturbances rapidly, making the simulation a more accurate representation of the current real-world state.

Therefore, this work significantly advances the state-of-the-art by demonstrating, for the first time, a practical framework that couples differentiable simulation with real-time residual learning for rapid, closed-loop policy adaptation on real robotic systems. This shifts the paradigm from pre-training for generalization to continuous, "on-the-fly" adaptation, offering a promising path toward more robust and flexible robotic control systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable rapid, real-time policy adaptation on real robotic systems** to unknown external disturbances and unmodeled dynamics.

The motivation stems from several critical limitations of existing approaches to the sim-to-real gap:
1. **Susceptibility to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Conditions:** While domain randomization (DR) improves policy robustness, it cannot exhaustively anticipate all possible real-world conditions. Policies trained with DR often degrade significantly when confronted with disturbances outside their randomized training distribution.
2. **Cost and Time of Offline Retraining:** Real2Sim2Real pipelines can effectively bridge the gap, but they necessitate extensive real-world data collection and costly, time-consuming offline retraining. This renders them impractical for scenarios requiring rapid adjustments to dynamic or unpredicted environmental changes.
3. **Difficulty with Complex, Non-Linear Dynamics:** Quadrotors, with their highly nonlinear and unstable dynamics, are particularly challenging to control adaptively, especially under unmodeled aerodynamic effects, payload changes, or wind disturbances.
4. **Limitations of Classical RL Methods:** Traditional model-free RL algorithms are often sample-inefficient, requiring prohibitively long training times, making them unsuitable for online adaptation.
5. **Lack of Vision-Based Adaptive Control:** Many adaptive control methods rely on full state information (e.g., MPC-based approaches), limiting their applicability to vision-based control tasks where explicit state estimation might be noisy, partial, or unavailable.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose to "rapidly adapt the policy to unknown external disturbances in the real world, in an online fashion." Their core insight is to integrate online residual dynamics learning with rapid policy adaptation via differentiable simulation. The ultimate goal is to allow policies to "continuously 'overfit' rapidly to the current environment scenario," paradoxically making them "more 'generalizable' across diverse conditions" by adapting to them as they arise.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework operates in two distinct but interconnected phases: **policy pretraining** and **online adaptation**.

**A. Policy Pretraining:**
Initially, a base policy (an MLP) is trained using a low-fidelity analytical dynamics model in simulation, without any residual dynamics. This phase establishes a foundational control capability before real-world deployment. For vision-based hovering, a pre-trained neural network is used to initialize parts of the policy network, drawing from prior work on state-representation learning.

**B. Online Adaptation (Interleaved Components):**
The core innovation lies in the online adaptation phase, where three components operate concurrently across multiple threads via ROS:
1. **Real-World Deployment and Data Collection:**
 - The latest policy network (initially the pre-trained base policy) is deployed on the real quadrotor, outputting control commands at 50 Hz.
 - Quadrotor states and actions are continuously recorded into a rolling history buffer (e.g., 2-5 seconds of trajectory history at 50 Hz). This data is crucial for learning residual dynamics.

2. **Residual Dynamics Learning:**
 - An MLP network, `f_res`, parameterized by `θ`, is trained to predict **residual acceleration (`a_res`)**. `a_res` is the difference between the ground-truth acceleration (`a_gt`) measured on the real system and the theoretical acceleration (`a_hat`) from a simple analytical dynamics model (`f_a`).
 - The network `f_res` takes the concatenated state and action vector `[x^T, u^T]` as input.
 - Training minimizes a loss function (`L_res`) comprising a standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) term and a spectral norm regularization term. The latter improves generalization to OOD conditions by regulating the network's Lipschitz constant.
 - An ensemble of three residual networks is trained in parallel, using the empirical mean prediction for `a_res` to reduce prediction variance due to limited buffer samples.
 - Residual dynamics learning occurs every 3 seconds for 100 iterations.

3. **Policy Adaptation via Differentiable Simulation:**
 - A **differentiable hybrid dynamics model (`f_hybrid`)** is formed by additively combining the analytical dynamics (`f_a`) and the learned residual dynamics (`f_res`). `x_{t+1} = f_hybrid(x_t, u_t)`.
 - The policy optimization objective is to maximize the cumulative task reward `R(Φ)` over an N-step rollout in this `f_hybrid` simulation.
 - **Analytical policy gradients** are obtained via Back-Propagation Through Time (BPTT) through the fully differentiable hybrid dynamics and reward function. This enables efficient first-order gradient updates.
 - Policy adaptation is performed every 5 seconds, training the state-based policy for 30 epochs (or vision-based for 50 epochs) across parallel simulated environments.

**C. Differentiable Simulation Model Details:**
- The quadrotor is modeled as a discrete-time dynamical system.
- The low-fidelity analytical dynamics `f_a` (Eq. 1) models the quadrotor as a point-mass, considering position, rotation, linear velocity, collective thrust, and body rates.
- An observation model `h` generates observations `o_t = h(x_t)`.
- A deterministic and differentiable policy network `π_Φ` outputs actions `u_t = π_Φ(o_t)`.
- A deterministic, smooth, and differentiable reward function `r` provides `r_t = r(x_t, u_t)`.
- All components are differentiable, allowing gradient backpropagation.

**D. Design Choices for Maximum Runtime Efficiency:**
- **Hybrid Dynamics:** The `f_hybrid` model combines a simple, low-fidelity analytical model with a learned residual component. The forward simulation uses `a_hybrid = a_hat + a_res`.
- **Selective Gradient Backpropagation:** Crucially, policy gradients are backpropagated **only through the analytical dynamics model** (`f_a`), not through the frozen residual network (`f_res`). This design choice, inspired by prior work, significantly speeds up training without compromising policy performance by using a "surrogate gradient" that approximates the true gradient direction.
- **Asynchronous Implementation:** The entire system is designed for concurrency, with components running in parallel threads (ROS nodes) and parameters exchanged efficiently, enabling rapid, real-time updates.

**E. Policy Adaptation Methods:**
- **Full Adaptation:** Updates all parameters of the policy network.
- **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA):** Freezes most pre-trained parameters and adapts an additive low-rank network module, offering more memory and parameter-efficient adaptation. This paper explores LoRA as an alternative for policy adaptation within the differentiable simulation framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling findings from both simulation and real-world experiments, demonstrating the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed framework.

**A. Performance Comparison to Baseline Approaches (Simulated):**
- **State-based Stabilizing Hover (Table I):**
 - Our method consistently achieved superior or comparable performance across all disturbance levels.
 - Under "Large Disturbance" (out-of-distribution, m/s²), our method reduced hovering error by **81%** compared to L1-MPC (0.552 m to 0.105 m) and **55%** compared to DATT (0.231 m to 0.105 m).
 - DATT performed well within its training distribution ("No Dist." and "Small Dist.") but struggled significantly with OOD disturbances.
 - Our policy rapidly adapted, showing significant improvement within 2-3 adaptation steps (Figure 5a).
- **Visual Feature-based Hovering (Table III):**
 - While showing larger errors than the state-based approach, our vision-based method achieved good performance (e.g., 0.207 m error for large disturbance), a capability often unattainable with classical control without explicit state estimation.
 - Adaptation was less stable than state-based, likely due to partial observability and sample inefficiency.
- **Trajectory Tracking (Table II):**
 - Our approach achieved comparable performance to baselines across various trajectories (Circle, Figure-8, 5-Point Star) and disturbance conditions.
 - It showed strong responsiveness to both smooth and non-smooth trajectories, matching DATT's accuracy on the challenging 5-Point Star.
 - DATT consistently failed (crashed) under large, OOD disturbances for all tracking tasks, further highlighting its generalization limitations.
- **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA):** Using LoRA yielded comparable performance to full adaptation across all tasks and conditions, suggesting that it can effectively combine with differentiable simulation for parameter-efficient adaptation.

**B. Computational and Sample Efficiency Analysis:**
- **Pretraining:** Our policy pretraining required 4.5 million simulation steps (15 seconds), significantly less than DATT's 20 million steps (2 hours). The use of low-variance first-order gradients from differentiable simulation allows for faster initial policy acquisition without extensive domain randomization.
- **Online Adaptation:** Each residual dynamics learning step took ~2 seconds, and each policy adaptation step took ~1.5 seconds. Crucially, significant performance improvements were observed after only 3 adaptation steps, equivalent to 4.5 seconds of policy training in wall time.
- This demonstrates that the framework enables **sample- and compute-efficient online adaptation** to OOD scenarios where offline methods and domain randomization typically fail.

**C. Continuous Adaptation to Time-Varying Disturbances:**
- The method successfully adapted a state-based hovering policy to uniform, time-varying acceleration disturbances (reversing direction every 15 seconds) within 2 adaptation steps for each change (Figure 5b). This showcases its ability to maintain stable flight under dynamic environmental shifts.

**D. Optimizing Runtime Efficiency and Performance (Design Justification):**
- **Low-Fidelity Analytical Dynamics:** Using a low-fidelity dynamics model for simulation resulted in approximately **2x faster training** compared to a high-fidelity model, with very comparable policy performance (Figure 6a, 6b).
- **Selective Backpropagation:** Performing gradient backpropagation only through the analytical dynamics model (and not the learned residual network) increased training time by only ~30% compared to a no-backprop baseline, while still achieving significant performance gains. This validates the "surrogate gradient" approach, demonstrating that it accelerates training without negatively impacting policy performance.

**E. Real-World Validation (Figure 7):**
- Experiments on modified quadrotors (added mass, different inertias) under complex wind disturbances in a motion-capture environment confirmed the framework's efficacy.
- **State-based hovering** on a small quadrotor with added mass and diagonal wind adapted rapidly (2-3 policy updates) despite complex, unstable real-world forces (Figure 7a).
- **Visual feature-based hovering** also adapted effectively in the real world, albeit with less stability than state-based, consistent with simulation findings.
- **Trajectory tracking** for the 5-Point Star with wind and the Figure-8 on a larger quadrotor demonstrated accurate tracking even with significant initial sim-to-real gaps and poor initial exploration (Figure 7b, 7c). The policies quickly adapted to achieve much improved tracking within a few updates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a highly significant contribution to the field of robotics and autonomous systems by introducing a novel, practical framework for **rapid, real-time policy adaptation** in the real world. Its potential impact is substantial:

1. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap Online and Efficiently:** The framework fundamentally shifts the paradigm from purely offline sim-to-real transfer to continuous online adaptation. By coupling differentiable simulation with real-time residual dynamics learning, it offers an unprecedented ability for robotic systems to adapt to unforeseen disturbances and unmodeled effects *within seconds* of deployment. This drastically reduces the reliance on exhaustive domain randomization or costly offline data collection and retraining, making robotic deployments more robust and flexible in dynamic environments.

2. **Enhanced Robustness to Out-of-Distribution Conditions:** Unlike existing methods that struggle when faced with conditions outside their training distribution, this approach allows policies to "overfit" to the current reality, enabling generalization across diverse and changing environments in an adaptive manner. This is crucial for real-world applications where environmental conditions are rarely static or perfectly predictable.

3. **Feasibility of Vision-Based Adaptive Control:** The demonstration of adaptive control using only visual features, without explicit state estimation, is a significant step forward. This opens doors for deploying robust learned policies in environments where high-fidelity state sensors (e.g., motion capture) are unavailable or unreliable, pushing towards truly autonomous robots.

4. **Leveraging Differentiable Simulation for Practical Robotics:** The paper showcases a powerful application of differentiable simulation, moving it beyond theoretical explorations or simplified contact scenarios to complex, real-world robotic control. The specific design choices, such as using a low-fidelity analytical model for backpropagation, are key to achieving runtime efficiency, making differentiable simulation a more viable tool for practical, real-time adaptation.

5. **Reduced Development Costs and Time:** The ability to train a base policy with fewer simulation steps and then rapidly adapt it online implies faster development cycles and lower computational costs for new robotic tasks or in response to system changes (e.g., payload modifications).

6. **Paving the Way for Continual Learning in Robotics:** The framework represents a form of continual learning where the robot constantly refines its understanding of its own dynamics and adapts its behavior. This lays groundwork for future research into more sophisticated active exploration strategies and uncertainty-aware adaptation, as hinted in the authors' discussion of future work.

In conclusion, "Learning on the Fly" demonstrates a powerful and practical solution to a long-standing challenge in robotics. Its contribution is not just in achieving superior performance but in fundamentally changing *how* robots can learn and adapt, making them more resilient and capable in the unpredictable real world.
