# Residual Reinforcement Learning for Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Siemens Corporation and UC Berkeley developed Residual Reinforcement Learning, a method that combines a hand-engineered feedback controller with a learned reinforcement learning policy to achieve sample-efficient and robust robot control for contact-rich manipulation tasks. This approach successfully learned complex block assembly skills on a physical robot within hours, demonstrating superior performance, robustness to environmental variations, and accelerated sim-to-real transfer compared to pure reinforcement learning.

## Problem

- Conventional feedback controllers are brittle and require extensive tuning for complex, uncertain interactions like contacts and friction in real-world manufacturing tasks.
- Pure reinforcement learning (RL) methods are sample-inefficient, can be unsafe due to extensive exploration, and struggle with learning low-level control from scratch, hindering their practical deployment in industrial settings.
- Developing robotic systems that can adapt to variations in object pose, sensor noise, and controller biases remains a significant challenge for robust automation.

## Method

- **Residual Reinforcement Learning** decomposes the robot's control action into two additive components: a ==hand-engineered feedback controller== handling base movements and a learned ==neural network policy== (residual controller) optimized by RL to handle complex, adaptive behaviors.
- The reward function is also decomposed, with the hand-engineered controller optimizing robot state-related rewards and the residual RL policy focusing on object state-related rewards.
- A variant of ==Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradients (TD3)== is used for the learning component, with exploration focused solely on the residual part of the control signal, ensuring a stable base while learning.

## Results

- Residual RL achieved significantly faster learning and better final task performance for block assembly, requiring only about **3 hours** of real-world training (**8,000 samples**) to learn critical corrective behaviors.
- The method demonstrated high robustness to environmental variations (e.g., randomized block orientations) and control system imperfections (e.g., actuator noise and bias), successfully adapting where hand-engineered controllers failed.
- Pre-training the residual policy in simulation and fine-tuning with real-world Residual RL accelerated deployment, allowing the robot to solve complex tasks in **under one thousand real-world timesteps**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging existing stable classical controllers as a base significantly improves RL sample efficiency and safety, as the learning agent only needs to master the 'residual' adaptive behaviors for complex interactions.
> - Decomposing the control signal allows the RL component to focus on learning robust behaviors for unmodeled dynamics (like contacts and friction) and object manipulation, where classical controllers are insufficient.
> - This hybrid approach effectively combines the stability and efficiency of model-based control with the adaptability and model-free learning capabilities of reinforcement learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge at the intersection of classical control theory and modern reinforcement learning for robotics. Conventional feedback control methods excel at efficiently solving structured robot control problems, such as precise trajectory following, when explicit physical models (e.g., rigid body dynamics) are available and accurate. However, they become brittle and require extensive manual tuning for tasks involving complex, uncertain interactions like contacts and friction, which are ubiquitous in real-world manufacturing.

On the other hand, reinforcement learning methods have shown remarkable promise in learning complex, adaptive behaviors from interaction with the environment, even in the presence of contacts and unknown dynamics. However, pure RL approaches typically suffer from several drawbacks that hinder their practical deployment in industrial settings:
- **Sample Inefficiency:** They often require an enormous amount of interaction data, which is time-consuming and expensive to collect on physical robots.
- **Safety Concerns:** The exploratory nature of RL can lead to unpredictable or unsafe robot movements, making "learning from scratch" risky in factories.
- **Difficulty with Low-Level Control:** Learning fundamental control behaviors (like basic stable movement) from scratch with RL is often overkill and inefficient when robust classical controllers already exist.

This paper proposes "Residual Reinforcement Learning" as a solution to bridge this gap. It falls into the broader category of **hybrid control strategies** that seek to combine the strengths of model-based or expert knowledge with model-free learning. This approach is distinct from:
- **Pure RL from scratch:** Which ignores any prior knowledge and faces the aforementioned challenges.
- **Intensive Reward Shaping:** Which can guide RL but is often as difficult to design as a full policy.
- **Learning from Demonstrations:** Which requires human teleoperation or expert data, and still might not generalize well to contact-rich tasks where subtle feedback is key.
- **Trajectory Planning:** Which focuses on following predefined paths rather than adapting to task-specific objectives and uncertainties.

The most closely related contemporary work cited by the authors is "Residual Policy Learning" by Silver et al. (2018), which explores a similar concept. However, Johannink et al. differentiate their work by focusing on **achieving practical real-world training of contact-intensive tasks**, providing a thorough evaluation on a physical robot. This emphasis on real-world applicability and tackling difficult contact dynamics positions their work as a significant step towards making deep RL a more viable tool for industrial robotics and manufacturing automation. By leveraging the stability and efficiency of existing feedback controllers while using RL to learn the "residual" adaptive behaviors, they aim to make robot learning safer, more sample-efficient, and more robust to environmental variations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, sample-efficient, and safe methodology for controlling robots in complex, real-world manufacturing tasks that involve contacts, friction, and unstable objects. The authors aim to overcome the inherent limitations of both conventional feedback control and pure reinforcement learning when applied to such problems.

The core motivations can be summarized as follows:

1. **Address the Brittleness of Classical Control:** Conventional feedback controllers, while efficient for well-modeled dynamics, struggle significantly with tasks involving contacts, friction, and environmental variability. Designing and fine-tuning these controllers for such scenarios is time-consuming, expensive, and often results in brittle solutions that lack adaptability. The authors seek a method that can automatically learn to handle these difficult, unmodeled interactions.

2. **Mitigate RL's Real-World Challenges:** While RL offers adaptability, its high data requirements, potential for unsafe exploration, and the need to learn basic control skills from scratch make it impractical for many real-world robotic deployments, especially in manufacturing where safety and efficiency are paramount. The motivation is to reduce the data burden and increase the safety and stability of RL training on physical hardware.

3. **Leverage Existing Knowledge:** Many robotic tasks have a significant "easy" component that can be efficiently handled by well-established classical control methods (e.g., moving the robot arm to a general location). The authors are motivated to utilize this existing domain expertise and conventional control infrastructure, rather than discarding it, to accelerate learning and improve performance.

4. **Enable Robustness to Variability and Noise:** Modern manufacturing demands robots that can handle variations in object pose, sensor noise, or even controller biases. Designing classical controllers robust to these factors is exceptionally difficult. A key objective is to demonstrate that their hybrid approach can learn adaptive behaviors that are resilient to such uncertainties.

5. **Achieve Practical Deployment in Manufacturing:** Ultimately, the motivation is to develop a control approach suitable for industrial applications, leading to more flexible and autonomous manufacturing systems that can handle "lot-size one" production and reduce the significant engineering effort currently required for robot deployment.

By decomposing the control problem into a well-structured part handled by a conventional controller and a residual, adaptive part learned by RL, the authors aim to achieve a "best of both worlds" solution: a robot controller that is both sample-efficient, safe, and robust enough for real-world manufacturing tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology, termed **Residual Reinforcement Learning**, lies in decomposing the robot's control action into two additive components: one from a conventional, hand-engineered controller and another from a learned reinforcement learning (RL) policy.

**A. Control Action Decomposition:**
The total control input `u` applied to the robot is defined as:
`u = π_H(s_m) + π_θ(s_m, s_o)`
- `π_H(s_m)`: This is the **hand-engineered (human-designed) feedback controller**. It primarily targets the robot's own states (`s_m`), such as its end-effector position or joint angles, to achieve geometric objectives or trajectory following. The paper notes this controller is assumed to provide a stable base, maximizing reward terms related to `s_m`. In their experiments, this is either a Cartesian-space position controller (simulation) or a compliant joint-space impedance controller (real-world).
- `π_θ(s_m, s_o)`: This is the **learned neural network policy**, parameterized by `θ`, which is optimized by an RL algorithm. This "residual" controller is responsible for learning the fine-grained, adaptive behaviors required to handle difficult-to-model interactions (contacts, friction) and manipulate objects (`s_o`), maximizing reward terms related to object states.

**B. Problem Formulation and Reward Structure:**
The authors frame the control problem as manipulating underactuated objects (`s_o`) using a fully actuated robot (`s_m`). The key difficulty is the unknown and complex coupling dynamics between the robot and objects, especially due to physical interactions like contacts and friction.
The reward function `r_t` is also decomposed: `r_t = f(s_m) + g(s_o)`.
- `f(s_m)`: Rewards related to the robot's own state (e.g., reaching a desired position), which the hand-engineered controller is generally effective at optimizing.
- `g(s_o)`: Rewards related to the object's state (e.g., keeping blocks upright), which the residual RL policy focuses on optimizing.

**C. Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:**
For the learning component (`π_θ`), the authors employ a variant of **Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradients (TD3)**. TD3 is chosen for its stability, sample efficiency, and reduced need for manual tuning compared to earlier algorithms like DDPG. The `rlkit` implementation of TD3 is specifically mentioned. The algorithm works off-policy, which is crucial for maximizing sample efficiency in real-world settings. While TD3 is used, the authors emphasize that their residual RL framework is generally independent of the choice of the underlying RL algorithm.

**D. Exploration Strategy:**
During training, exploration noise (represented as a random process `N_t`) is added to the output of the learned policy `π_θ(s_t)` before it is combined with the hand-engineered controller. This means the exploration is focused on the residual part, rather than perturbing the entire, potentially stable, base control signal.

**E. Experimental Setup:**
- **Task:** The primary task is a **block assembly task**, where a robot must insert a block between two standing blocks without tipping them over or moving them from their initial positions. This task is inherently contact-rich and involves manipulating unstable objects.
- **Simulated Environment:** MuJoCo physics simulator with a 7-DOF Sawyer robot and parallel gripper. Observations include end-effector position, forces/torques, block positions, and goal position.
- **Real-World Environment:** A physical Sawyer robot arm. Observations are derived from a camera-based tracking system for block positions and end-effector force in the z-direction (due to limitations in measuring contact forces in x-y).
- **Reward Functions:** Specific reward functions (Equations 6 and 7) are defined for simulation and real-world, penalizing distance to goal, block angles (tipping), and block displacement.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success rate (manual judgment for real-world, automated for simulation), average return, and learning curves showing performance over timesteps.

**F. Experimental Questions:**
The authors designed experiments to answer several key questions:
1. Does the hand-designed controller improve RL performance and sample efficiency, while allowing recovery from an imperfect base?
2. Does the method increase robustness to environment variation?
3. Can the method cope with control noise and bias?
4. Can the method facilitate sim-to-real transfer?

This rigorous methodology, combining theoretical decomposition with practical implementation and comprehensive evaluation in both simulation and the real world, forms the backbone of their claims about the effectiveness of Residual Reinforcement Learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Johannink et al. demonstrate several key advantages of Residual Reinforcement Learning over pure RL or hand-engineered control for complex, contact-rich robot manipulation tasks.

1. **Superior Sample Efficiency and Performance:**
 - In both simulation and real-world experiments, Residual RL achieved significantly faster learning (higher sample efficiency) and ultimately better final task performance compared to training a pure RL agent from scratch ("Only RL").
 - The "Only RL" approach struggled, as it had to learn basic position control and complex interaction dynamics simultaneously, leading to broad spatial variance during exploration which can be unsafe. Residual RL, by leveraging the hand-engineered base controller for basic motion, could focus its learning on the subtle, adaptive behaviors required for contact.
 - Real-world training for residual RL to learn critical corrective behaviors took only about **3 hours** (8,000 samples), highlighting its practicality.

2. **Robustness to Environment Variation:**
 - The hand-engineered controller, designed for a specific block alignment, largely failed (2/20 trials successful) when the initial orientation of the standing blocks was randomized. This demonstrated the brittleness of classical methods to unmodeled variability.
 - Residual RL, however, successfully adapted to these variations, achieving a much higher success rate (15/20 trials). The learned policy exhibited "consistent small corrective feedback behaviors" to nudge the blocks without tipping them, a behavior that is exceedingly difficult to specify manually.
 - Simulation results quantitatively confirmed this, showing that Residual RL maintained a consistent average performance even as the magnitude of block rotation noise increased, whereas the hand-engineered controller's performance deteriorated.

3. **Tolerance to Control Noise and Bias:**
 - Residual RL demonstrated a remarkable ability to cope with both zero-mean actuator noise and, more significantly, biased actuator noise. The learned policy could robustly find strategies to reduce the effect of noise and even learn to actively counteract a consistent bias in the control output.
 - In contrast, the performance of the hand-engineered controller dramatically worsened with increasing control noise or bias, as it lacked the adaptive mechanism to compensate.
 - This finding is particularly significant for real-world deployments, where issues like sensor drift or minor hardware imperfections are common. The ability of RL to learn to compensate for such biases implicitly (by adjusting its residual output) offers a powerful solution.

4. **Accelerated Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
 - The research showed that initializing the Residual RL policy with a policy pre-trained in simulation drastically accelerated real-world learning. When combined with Residual RL, this "Sim-to-Real" approach allowed the robot to successfully solve the block insertion task in **under one thousand timesteps** of real-world interaction.
 - This highlights Residual RL as a highly sample-efficient and practical pathway for deploying complex robotic skills from simulation to physical hardware.

In summary, the main findings consistently support the hypothesis that combining conventional feedback control with a residual RL policy yields a robot controller that is more sample-efficient, safer, robust to environmental variations and control imperfections, and faster to deploy in the real world than either method alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Residual Reinforcement Learning for Robot Control" paper presents a highly significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for real-world industrial applications. Its impact can be understood in several key areas:

1. **Bridging the Gap for Real-World RL Deployment:** The most profound impact is on making deep reinforcement learning a more practical and deployable solution for physical robots. By addressing the critical limitations of pure RL – namely, high sample complexity, safety concerns during exploration, and difficulty with low-level control – this work offers a viable path for transferring cutting-edge RL research into industrial settings. The demonstrated ability to learn complex, contact-rich tasks in hours rather than days or weeks is a game-changer for manufacturing.

2. **Enabling Automation of Challenging Tasks:** Many manufacturing tasks involving flexible parts, contacts, friction, or unstable objects are currently difficult and expensive to automate with traditional robots due to the engineering effort required to design and tune brittle feedback controllers. Residual RL provides a method for robots to learn the necessary adaptive behaviors automatically, expanding the range of tasks that can be reliably automated and paving the way for more flexible "lot-size one" manufacturing.

3. **A Robust Hybrid Control Paradigm:** The paper establishes a powerful hybrid control paradigm that effectively leverages the strengths of both classical control (stability, efficiency for known dynamics) and modern machine learning (adaptability, model-free learning for unknown dynamics). This additive structure is intuitive and allows domain experts to inject their knowledge through the hand-engineered controller, while RL focuses on refining and adapting the behavior where models are insufficient or unavailable. This integration is key to developing truly intelligent and capable robotic systems.

4. **Increased Robustness and Resilience:** The ability of Residual RL to maintain performance despite significant environmental variations (e.g., misaligned parts) and control system imperfections (e.g., control noise or bias) is highly impactful. In industrial environments, perfect conditions are rare. This inherent robustness, learned through interaction, can lead to more reliable and fault-tolerant robotic systems, reducing downtime and maintenance.

5. **Accelerated Research and Development:** The demonstrated effectiveness of combining simulation-based pre-training with real-world Residual RL provides a powerful "sim-to-real" pathway. Researchers and engineers can rapidly iterate and test complex skills in simulation, then quickly fine-tune them on physical hardware with minimal real-world data. This could significantly accelerate the development and deployment cycle for new robotic capabilities.

6. **Foundation for Future Work:** The paper points to exciting future directions, such as incorporating end-to-end vision input into the learning process. If the learned controller can compensate for biases in perception, it could reduce reliance on carefully crafted vision systems and further enhance adaptability to novel scenes, pushing towards even more autonomous and general-purpose robotic agents.

In essence, this research moves the field closer to realizing the promise of autonomous, adaptable robots in real-world manufacturing and beyond. By strategically combining human engineering with data-driven learning, Residual Reinforcement Learning offers a practical and powerful framework for tackling some of the most persistent challenges in robotics.
