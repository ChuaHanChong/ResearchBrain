# Learning Dexterous Grasping from Sparse Taxonomy Guidance

> [!summary] Summary
> The GRIT framework enables dexterous robot manipulation by learning control policies guided by sparse human grasp taxonomies and a target wrist orientation. This approach achieved an 87.9% overall success rate on the Objaverse dataset, outperforming baseline methods by up to 6.0%, and demonstrated adaptive, context-driven grasping on a real-world bimanual robot.

## Problem

- Generating suitable and diverse grasp configurations for novel objects and task contexts without relying on costly, extensive demonstrations.
- Translating high-level, sparse grasp taxonomies into precise, continuous multi-finger control actions.
- Providing users with the ability to adjust or intervene in grasp strategies, a feature often absent in fully autonomous reinforcement learning systems.

## Method

- A two-stage GRIT framework that decouples grasp generation and taxonomy-conditioned control.
- A planner selects a sparse grasp taxonomy from 30 predefined templates and a target wrist orientation based on object geometry and task.
- A taxonomy-conditioned control policy is trained using a multiplicative composite reward function to enforce taxonomy adherence while achieving stable object interaction.

## Results

- Achieved an 87.9% overall success rate on the Objaverse dataset, a 6.0% improvement over the RDG baseline and 1.9% over GraspXL.
- Demonstrated that the multiplicative reward design provides stable taxonomy adherence (e.g., 28.57% higher Contact Precision for 2-3 finger grasps) without compromising task success, unlike additive methods.
- Successfully deployed on a real-world bimanual humanoid robot, exhibiting geometry-dependent and task-specific grasp adaptations (e.g., selecting a "Power" grasp to "squeeze" a sponge).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit grasp taxonomy conditioning provides a robust structural inductive bias, leading to improved generalization for dexterous manipulation of novel objects.
> - A multiplicative composite reward function ensures consistent adherence to specified grasp taxonomies while maintaining high task success, proving more stable than additive reward formulations.
> - Integrating Vision-Language Models with 3D scene context allows for effective zero-shot grasp selection based on high-level task intent and object geometry.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation, which involves coordinated multi-finger control to interact with objects, is a central challenge in robotics. The broader research landscape typically approaches this problem through two main components: grasp generation and grasp execution (control).

One established line of research focuses on learning manipulation policies from human demonstrations or large-scale object-grasp datasets. Approaches like Herzog et al. and Chen ets al. retrieve or retarget grasp configurations from demonstrations. While effective for known scenarios, these methods often struggle with generalization to novel objects or task contexts due to the combinatorial explosion of data required to cover variations. Recent studies, such as Zhang et al. and Chen et al., have explored learning from large datasets or synthesizing grasp configurations using hand templates. However, the cost of collecting exhaustive demonstrations or annotations for diverse object-task combinations remains a significant barrier.

Another perspective categorizes dexterous manipulation systems by their level of autonomy. Fully autonomous end-to-end reinforcement learning methods learn direct mappings from observations to actions, optimizing for task performance. While capable, these systems typically offer limited user intervention or controllability once trained. Conversely, teleoperation-based systems provide fine-grained control through continuous human input, but require constant human involvement.

Intermediate, semi-autonomous frameworks aim to balance automation and controllability by separating high-level grasp specification from low-level control. Prior work in this area, such as Christen et al. and Huang et al., often relies on dense grasp representations, such as continuous hand poses or object-specific contact targets. This paper identifies a gap in this approach, noting that dense representations can still be complex to specify and manage across diverse tasks.

This work, GRIT (Grasp Reinforcement with Intended Taxonomies), positions itself within the semi-autonomous paradigm by adopting a two-stage decomposition. It distinguishes itself by leveraging sparse, discrete human grasp taxonomies as the high-level guidance for low-level control, rather than dense representations. This approach aims to provide a more practical and controllable interface for users while enabling effective policy learning from less specific supervision. By focusing on taxonomy-conditioned control, the paper seeks to improve generalization to novel objects and enhance system controllability, addressing limitations of both purely data-driven and dense-representation-based methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a dexterous manipulation system that can effectively select and execute hand movements based on object geometry and task context, while offering a degree of user controllability. The authors identify three key challenges motivating their work:

1. **Generation of Appropriate Grasp Configurations:** The system must be capable of inferring a suitable grasp configuration, including a discrete grasp taxonomy and a wrist orientation, that aligns with the object's geometry and the specific task at hand. Current methods often struggle to generate diverse and context-appropriate grasp configurations without extensive, costly demonstrations or annotations.
2. **Translation of Sparse Guidance into Consistent Finger-Level Control:** A policy must be able to translate a high-level, sparse grasp configuration (e.g., "tripod grasp") into continuous and coordinated multi-finger movements. This is challenging because sparse commands lack explicit motion trajectories, requiring the policy to infer detailed execution plans from limited information.
3. **Controllable Adjustment of Grasp Strategies:** The system should allow users to intervene and adjust grasp strategies, particularly when a chosen grasp proves ineffective or when task requirements change. End-to-end reinforcement learning approaches often lack this controllability, making it difficult to debug or refine behaviors during deployment.

Motivated by these challenges, the authors highlight limitations of existing approaches:
- **Cost of Data Collection:** Learning from large-scale object-grasp data or demonstrations is impractical for covering the vast combinatorial space of object geometries and task contexts. This data scarcity hinders generalization to novel objects.
- **Lack of Controllability in End-to-End Systems:** Fully autonomous reinforcement learning systems, while effective for task optimization, typically offer limited means for users to intervene or adjust behaviors, especially during failures.
- **Complexity of Dense Grasp Representations:** Semi-autonomous frameworks that rely on dense grasp representations (e.g., continuous hand poses or contact targets) still face challenges in their specification and scalability across diverse tasks.

To address these issues, the authors propose GRIT, aiming to:
- Learn dexterous control from **sparse taxonomy guidance**, reducing the need for explicit motion demonstrations.
- Bridge **high-level grasp intent** (via taxonomies) and **low-level finger execution**.
- Improve **generalization** to novel objects by leveraging the relationship between grasp taxonomies and object geometries.
- Enable **controllability** in real-world scenarios through high-level taxonomy selection based on object geometry and task intent.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GRIT framework is a two-stage system designed to infer an appropriate grasp configuration and subsequently execute dexterous grasping under sparse taxonomy guidance.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The problem is formulated as a policy learning task where a robot, given a global task context $C = \{I, T\}$ (scene observation $I$ and task description $T$), learns a control policy $\pi$ to maximize cumulative reward. This complex problem is decoupled into two stages:
1. **Grasp Generation:** A planner $\pi_{plan}(C)$ selects a grasp configuration $g = (\tau, \bar{w}_w)$, consisting of a grasp taxonomy $\tau$ from a predefined set $\mathcal{T}$ and a target wrist orientation $\bar{w}_w$.
2. **Taxonomy-Conditioned Control:** A controller $\pi_{control}(s_t, g)$ generates continuous control actions $a_t$ based on the current state $s_t$ and the selected grasp configuration $g$.

**B. State and Action Representation**
At each timestep, the controller observes a state $s_t = \{s_h^t, s_o^t\}$.
- **Hand State ($s_h^t$):** Includes current joint configuration $q$, joint tracking error ($\bar{q}^- - q$), wrist linear and angular velocities ($v_w, \omega_w$), object linear and angular velocities ($v_o, \omega_o$), and contact indicators/forces ($c, f$).
- **Object State ($s_o^t$):** Represented by a partial point cloud $O \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 3}$ from an RGB-D camera, expressed in the wrist frame.
The policy predicts desired joint and wrist configurations ($\bar{q}, \bar{p}_w, \bar{w}_w$). The action $a_t = [\Delta w_t, \Delta q_t]$ consists of delta pose for the wrist and joint displacements for the hand, which are accumulated with the current state to form target configurations.

**C. Taxonomy-Based Grasp Templates**
GRIT constructs a library of 30 canonical grasp templates based on the human grasp taxonomy of Feix et al., excluding highly object-specific patterns. Each template $\tau_i = \{\tilde{q}, \tilde{b}, \tilde{p}, \tilde{n}\}$ specifies:
- A reference joint configuration $\tilde{q} \in \mathbb{R}^D$.
- A binary mask $\tilde{b} \in \{0, 1\}^L$ indicating the engagement (active/resting) of $L$ hand and palm links.
- Reference contact positions $\tilde{p} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 3}$ and corresponding surface normals $\tilde{n} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 3}$ in the local hand frame, offering object-agnostic geometric guidance.

**D. Grasp Selection**
- **During Training:** Grasp taxonomies and target wrist orientations are uniformly sampled to ensure policy learning across diverse behaviors.
- **During Inference:** A Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Gemini 3, is employed for zero-shot grasp selection. To assist the VLM in 3D spatial reasoning from 2D images, candidate wrist orientations are sampled, and a 3D coordinate axis is overlaid onto the scene image, representing potential target directions toward the object. The VLM then processes this augmented image and a task description (e.g., "Pick up the sponge to squeeze it") to select the appropriate taxonomy $\tau$.

**E. Feature Extraction**
To condition the control policy, features are extracted from the grasp command $g$ and the partial object point cloud $O$:
- **Grasp Command Feature ($o_g$):** Comprises the reference joint configuration error ($\tilde{q} - q$), the link engagement mask ($\tilde{b}$), and the target wrist orientation ($\bar{w}_w$).
- **Geometric Features from Point Cloud:**
 - Wrist-object displacement ($x_{rel}$) based on the mean of the partial point cloud.
 - Distance-based features: displacement from each hand link to the nearest object surface point ($d_{hand}$) and distance to the table ($d_{table}$).
 - Local object geometry encoded by a wrist-centered Basis Point Set (BPS).

**F. Taxonomy-Conditioned Control Policy: Reward Design**
To learn dexterous grasping under sparse taxonomy guidance, the reward function is designed as a multiplicative composite reward to balance approach, interaction, and stability objectives, while enforcing taxonomy adherence.
$r = r_h \cdot \alpha_h + r_o \cdot \alpha_o - r_{pen}$

1. **Approach Phase Rewards and Constraints:**
 - **Hand-centric Reward ($r_h$):** Encourages hand movement towards the object surface, comprising exponential terms for hand-center proximity ($x_{rel}$) and link-wise object distance ($d_{link,l}$) for active links.
 - **Multiplicative Constraint Coefficient ($\alpha_h$):** $\alpha_h = \alpha_{dir}\alpha_{vel}\alpha_{act}$ suppresses unstable behaviors:
 - $\alpha_{dir}$: Encourages wrist alignment with a target direction.
 - $\alpha_{vel}$: Penalizes excessive linear and angular wrist motion.
 - $\alpha_{act}$: Regularizes abrupt action changes for smooth control.

2. **Grasp Execution and Taxonomy Adherence:**
 - **Object-Interaction Reward ($r_o$):** Encourages stable object interaction, weighted by contact normal alignment, contact status, and impulse magnitude for active links.
 - **Multiplicative Constraint ($\alpha_o$):** $\alpha_o = \alpha_{obj}\alpha_{mimic}$ enforces stable object motion and taxonomy-consistent hand synergies:
 - $\alpha_{obj}$: Penalizes excessive object linear and angular motion ($\alpha_{vel}$) and discourages lateral displacement in the grasp frame ($\alpha_{xy}$).
 - $\alpha_{mimic}$: Measures deviation from the reference taxonomy configuration ($L_{mimic}$), applying penalties for active and resting links exceeding defined tolerance margins ($\tau_{act}, \tau_{rest}$). These margins allow for flexibility while maintaining the intended grasp structure.

3. **Unintended Interaction Penalties ($r_{pen}$):** Explicitly penalizes physically invalid or taxonomy-inconsistent contacts, including unintended contacts and contacts on links designated as inactive by the taxonomy.

**G. Policy Distillation**
For real-world deployment, a teacher policy trained with privileged simulation information is distilled into a student policy using only accessible observations.
- **Teacher Policy:** Observes the full object point cloud ($\bar{O}_t$) and ground-truth contact states ($\bar{c}_t, \bar{f}_t$).
- **Student Policy:** Uses a single-view partial point cloud ($O_t$). Contact signals are reconstructed from an LSTM encoder processing the past 10 steps of joint states and actions.
Training involves a curriculum: DAgger-style behavior cloning with contact reconstruction and action imitation losses, followed by PPO with an asymmetric critic. The critic retains privileged inputs, while the actor relies solely on student observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GRIT involved extensive experiments, including generalization to novel objects, analysis of object-taxonomy alignment, assessment of taxonomy adherence, ablation studies, and real-world deployment.

**A. Generalization to Novel Objects (Section V-D)**
GRIT was evaluated on the Objaverse dataset, specifically the RoboCasa subset, categorized into "fruit & vegetable," "household utensil & tool," and "packed goods & drink & bread food" objects (373 objects total). Policy robustness was assessed over 240 trials per object (30 randomized trials for each of 8 approach directions).

- **Overall Success Rate:** GRIT achieved an overall success rate of 87.9%, outperforming baseline learned policies.
- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - **RDG:** GRIT improved the overall success rate by +6.0% compared to RDG (81.9%), which does not use explicit taxonomy conditioning.
 - **GraspXL:** GRIT also outperformed GraspXL by +1.9%, which relies on geometric reward shaping without predefined grasp types.
- **Taxonomy Guidance Impact:** These results suggest that explicit grasp taxonomy conditioning provides more robust structural guidance for grasp generation and improves generalization.
- **Oracle Performance:** The gap between GRIT's performance (87.9%) and the oracle selection (92.3%) was small, indicating that the predicted taxonomies were often near-optimal.
- **Category-Specific Performance:** Performance was observed to be lower for "household utensil & tool" objects due to challenging geometries (thin, flat) but higher for "packed goods & drink & bread food" objects due to regular shapes.

**B. Object-Taxonomy Alignment and Sensitivity Analysis (Section V-E)**
An exhaustive evaluation of all 30 taxonomies across the three object categories revealed significant variations in success rates based on the taxonomy-object pair.

- **Structured Preferences:** Distinct preferences for certain taxonomies emerged. For instance, "Packed Goods" showed robust success (94.31%) with "medium/Large Diameter" grasps.
- **Sensitivity to Selection:** "Fruit & Vegetable" objects exhibited the highest sensitivity, with a 25.07% performance gap between the most effective "Power Sphere" grasp (88.78%) and the least effective "Tip Pinch" grasp (63.71%). This was attributed to the instability of minimal-contact points on highly curved surfaces.
- **Conclusion:** The findings confirm that object geometry and required contact area necessitate precise taxonomy selection, with power-oriented grasps generally outperforming precision-based ones for many objects.

**C. Evaluating Taxonomy Adherence (Section V-F)**
The policy's adherence to prescribed taxonomies and the trade-off between task success and adherence were analyzed under different reward formulations.

- **Multiplicative Reward Advantage:** Unlike baseline methods that use an additive reward formulation requiring careful tuning of a `w_mimic` weighting coefficient, GRIT employs a multiplicative formulation where taxonomy adherence acts as a gating coefficient for the task reward.
- **Stable Performance:** When baselines were retrained with varying `w_mimic`, their performance was sensitive to this hyperparameter, leading to unstable trade-offs across joint error, contact precision, and success rate. In contrast, GRIT maintained consistently strong performance across all metrics without requiring hyperparameter tuning for taxonomy adherence, demonstrating the stability of its multiplicative reward design.

**D. Ablation Study (Section V-G)**
An ablation study investigated the contributions of visual features (Basis Point Set - BPS) and the multiplicative reward formulation, categorizing taxonomies into 2-3 fingers and 4-5 fingers.

- **Multiplicative Reward Impact:** For 2-3 finger grasps, a 'naive sum' reward achieved comparable success rates to GRIT but failed to maintain precise finger configurations, resulting in lower Contact Precision (0.49 vs 0.63) and higher Mean Joint Error (0.23 vs 0.097). GRIT's multiplicative reward significantly improved adherence (28.57% higher Contact Precision, 57.83% lower joint error), balancing task success with taxonomy adherence. Similar trends were observed for 4-5 finger grasps.
- **BPS Feature Importance:** Removing BPS features consistently led to a performance drop across all metrics, confirming the importance of dense geometric features for spatial awareness in high-precision grasping.

**E. Real-World Deployment (Section V-H)**
GRIT was deployed on a real-world bimanual humanoid robot, using policy outputs as Inverse Kinematics (IK) targets for the robot wrist.

- **Successful Deployment:** The framework demonstrated successful real-world deployment across a diverse set of grasp taxonomies and objects (Figure 5).
- **Geometry-Dependent Adaptation:** The success of each taxonomy was shown to be strongly dependent on object geometry (Figure 6). For rod-like objects (e.g., knife), diameter-based grasps often failed due to inability to establish stable contact, while precision 4-finger grasps succeeded by pinching the thin geometry. For spool-like objects, cylindrical grasps (e.g., medium-diameter) provided stable enclosure, whereas fingertip-based grasps tended to slip.
- **Task-Specific Selection:** GRIT also enabled intent-driven control via text prompts. For a sponge, a "Precision" grasp was selected for stable transport, but when prompted to "squeeze," it switched to a "Power" grasp to maximize force generation (Figure 1). This demonstrated adaptation to both object geometry and high-level task context.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents GRIT, a two-stage framework for dexterous manipulation that contributes to the field by proposing a method for learning control policies guided by sparse, high-level grasp taxonomies. The work demonstrates several key advancements and has implications for the development of more capable and user-controllable robotic systems.

**A. Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** By leveraging grasp taxonomies, GRIT shows improved generalization to novel objects compared to baselines that rely solely on geometric reward shaping or lack explicit grasp-level conditioning. This suggests that incorporating structured knowledge about human grasp types provides a more effective inductive bias for learning, enabling robots to handle a wider variety of unfamiliar objects without extensive retraining or object-specific data. This could lead to more versatile robotic manipulators in unpredictable environments.

**B. Improved Controllability and User Intervention:** The use of sparse taxonomy guidance allows for a higher level of user intervention than purely end-to-end learning systems. Users can influence the robot's grasp strategy by selecting an appropriate taxonomy based on task intent or object geometry, as demonstrated in real-world experiments with geometry-dependent adaptation and task-specific selection (e.g., the sponge example). This balance between automation and human control is significant for applications where task requirements may change or where human oversight is critical for safety and success.

**C. Novel Reward Design for Taxonomy Adherence:** The proposed multiplicative composite reward structure effectively enforces adherence to the specified grasp taxonomy while maintaining task success. This design is shown to be more stable and less sensitive to hyperparameter tuning than additive reward formulations, offering a robust mechanism for integrating high-level structural constraints into reinforcement learning policies. This contributes to the methodology of reward function design for complex robotic tasks.

**D. Effective Integration of Vision-Language Models:** The framework integrates a Vision-Language Model (VLM) for zero-shot grasp selection during inference, bridging semantic task descriptions with specific grasp taxonomies. This demonstrates a practical way to incorporate large perception models into robotic planning, moving towards more intelligent and context-aware manipulation.

**E. Real-World Validation:** The successful deployment of GRIT on a real-world humanoid robot, showcasing both geometry-dependent and task-specific grasp adaptations, validates the practical applicability and robustness of the proposed framework beyond simulation. This is a crucial step for transferring research findings to tangible robotic systems.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge limitations. The current policy relies primarily on the initial visual observation and lacks reactive feedback to adapt to dynamic scene changes or object displacements. Additionally, while taxonomy control provides effective high-level guidance, specifying fine-grained targets (e.g., grasping a specific functional part like a drill handle) remains challenging. Future work is projected to focus on integrating reactive visual feedback and more precise control strategies to enable robust functional manipulation in dynamic environments, further enhancing the system's capabilities.
