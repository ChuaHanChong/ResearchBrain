# Continual Domain Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> Continual Domain Randomization (CDR) trains a PPO policy first in an idealized simulator, then sequentially on one randomization parameter at a time (torque, latency, noise), using Elastic Weight Consolidation (and its online variant) to retain the effects of earlier randomizations. On real-robot reaching and grasping, CDR matches or beats jointly-randomized and finetuning baselines while being far less sensitive to randomization order.

## Problem

- Building a simulator accurate enough for zero-shot transfer is rarely possible, while real-robot finetuning is unsafe, time-consuming, and yields system-specific policies.
- Standard randomization assumes a fixed parameter set fixed at training start, so relevant parameters must be identified upfront and cannot be added later.
- Randomizing many parameters simultaneously inflates task difficulty and agent uncertainty, slowing convergence and yielding sub-optimal policies in simulation.

## Method

- **CDR** frames each randomization parameter as a separate ==continual-learning== task: start from a policy trained with all randomization disabled, then train sequentially per parameter subset.
- **CDR-λ** combines PPO with ==Elastic Weight Consolidation==, regularizing only the actor via per-task diagonal ==Fisher matrices== computed from squared log-likelihood gradients over a replay buffer.
- **CDR-Oλ** uses ==online-EWC== to keep one network snapshot and one running Fisher matrix decayed by γ, removing the linear memory growth of per-task snapshots; Fisher entries normalized to [0,1].

## Results

- On real-robot reaching, CDR-Oλ reached the best real reward (−143.88 for NLT) versus −146.52 fully randomized, −196.75 finetuning, and −208.46 ideal-only.
- Reaching transfer showed ideal and finetuning policies jitter badly: real distance-to-target 0.18–0.20 and continuity cost up to 0.30, while CDR/randomized held 0.02–0.07 and ~0.00.
- On real grasping, CDR-Oλ (NLT) achieved 0.60 grasp success; finetuning real reward swung ~12% with ordering (−14.99 vs −27.24) while CDR stayed near −23 for both orders.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Curriculum-style factorized randomization plus regularization gives robustness comparable to joint randomization without the exploration burden of randomizing everything at once.
> - Regularization strength is the pivotal knob: too low degenerates to finetuning and order-sensitivity, too high blocks adaptation; mid-range values transferred best.
> - Simulation reward is a misleading transfer proxy - the best in-sim policies overfit and produced the jittery, unstable real-robot behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses one of the most significant challenges in modern robotics: the **sim2real (simulation-to-reality) transfer problem**. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) policies are predominantly trained in simulated environments due to their sample inefficiency and the safety concerns associated with training on physical robots. However, the inherent discrepancies between simulation and reality – known as the "reality gap" – severely degrade the performance of policies transferred directly from simulation to real-world robots.

Traditionally, solutions to bridge this gap include:
1. **High-fidelity simulation:** Attempting to perfectly mimic real-world physics and dynamics, which is often infeasible and resource-intensive, even for simple systems.
2. **Domain adaptation/fine-tuning:** Training in simulation and then fine-tuning the policy on the real robot. While effective, this still requires real-world interaction, which can be time-consuming and unsafe.
3. **Domain Randomization (DR):** This widely adopted technique, which the paper builds upon, involves randomizing simulation parameters (e.g., physics properties, textures, lighting) during training. The goal is to force the DRL agent to learn a robust policy that generalizes to a wide range of conditions, including those found in the real world, without requiring real-world data during primary training.

The current work identifies and tackles key limitations of conventional DR:
- **Fixed Parameter Sets:** Most DR approaches require a fixed set of tunable parameters to be identified and randomized from the outset, which can be challenging and incomplete.
- **Increased Task Difficulty:** Randomizing too many parameters simultaneously, especially across wide ranges, significantly increases the complexity of the learning task for the DRL agent, potentially leading to sub-optimal policies and longer training times. This is analogous to teaching a student too many complex concepts at once.

This paper proposes **Continual Domain Randomization (CDR)**, a novel integration of DR with **Continual Learning (CL)**. CL, also known as lifelong learning, focuses on enabling artificial intelligence systems to learn sequentially from a stream of tasks without "catastrophically forgetting" previously acquired knowledge. In the context of robotics, CL has seen increasing application in scenarios where robots need to acquire multiple skills or adapt to changing environments over time.

CDR positions the learning of different randomization parameters as a sequence of CL tasks. This perspective allows the DRL agent to first master a simpler, non-randomized version of the task and then incrementally adapt to individual or subsets of randomization parameters. By employing CL algorithms, CDR aims to prevent the agent from forgetting the knowledge gained from earlier randomizations while learning new ones. This approach is distinct from:
- **Automated and Active DR:** Which progressively increase randomization ranges or identify informative ranges, but still typically operate within a fixed set of parameters and may not fully address sequential learning of different *types* of randomizations.
- **Other Continual Sim2Real Approaches:** Such as Progressive Networks or Policy Distillation, which often involve maintaining multiple networks or using separate datasets, whereas CDR aims for a single, continually updated network, especially with its online variant.

By introducing CL to DR, this work pushes the boundaries of sim2real transfer towards more flexible, adaptive, and efficient training paradigms, making it more practical for real-world robotic deployment where environmental conditions or robot parameters might change incrementally or where identifying all relevant randomization parameters upfront is impossible.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind Continual Domain Randomization (CDR) stems from the inherent limitations of traditional Domain Randomization (DR) for sim2real transfer in robotics. While DR is effective, its requirement for a fixed, comprehensive set of randomization parameters from the start of training and the increased task difficulty imposed by simultaneously randomizing many parameters often lead to sub-optimal policies.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this research are:

1. **Enhance Flexibility in Sim2Real Training:** To move beyond the rigid requirement of defining all randomization parameters upfront. CDR aims to provide a more flexible training process where new randomization parameters or subsets of parameters can be introduced sequentially over time.
2. **Mitigate Excessive Randomization Challenges:** By enabling sequential training on subsets of randomization parameters, the goal is to reduce the immediate task difficulty for the reinforcement learning agent, which often arises from the combined randomization of many diverse parameters. This allows the agent to build robustness incrementally.
3. **Prevent Catastrophic Forgetting:** To ensure that the knowledge gained from training under earlier randomization settings is not lost when the agent is exposed to new randomization parameters. This is achieved by integrating continual learning (CL) principles, which are specifically designed to address this "catastrophic forgetting" phenomenon.
4. **Improve Real-World Performance and Robustness:** To develop a training methodology that results in policies capable of performing robustly on physical robots, matching or outperforming policies trained with conventional full randomization or simple sequential fine-tuning.
5. **Demonstrate Generalizability:** To show that the CDR framework is broadly applicable and can be implemented with various DRL and CL algorithms, although the paper focuses on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) as a proof of concept.

In essence, the authors seek to make the sim2real transfer process more practical and effective by borrowing concepts from continual learning, allowing for a staged, adaptable, and robust policy acquisition that better reflects real-world learning challenges.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of Continual Domain Randomization (CDR) is to treat the process of adapting to different simulation randomizations as a sequence of continual learning (CL) tasks.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The objective is to train a policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$ in a simulator $\Sigma$ to maximize expected return on a real system $\Phi$. The simulator has a set of randomization parameters $E = \{\epsilon_1, \ldots, \epsilon_n\}$. Traditional DR randomizes all parameters simultaneously. CDR proposes a sequential approach.

**B. Continual Domain Randomization (CDR):**
The approach involves a sequential training pipeline:
1. **Initial Training:** An agent's policy parameters $\theta$ are initialized and optimized in an "idealized simulation" ($\Psi_{\emptyset}$), where all randomization parameters are disabled. This makes the initial task easier to solve.
2. **Sequential Randomization and CL:** The pre-trained policy $\theta_{\emptyset}$ is then sequentially optimized using CL on a series of simulations ($\Psi_{\epsilon_1}, \ldots, \Psi_{\epsilon_n}$), where different subsets of randomization parameters are enabled at each stage. The goal is to continually adapt the policy without forgetting previously learned robust behaviors.

Two main variants of CDR are proposed, both combining Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) as the DRL algorithm with regularization-based CL methods:

- **CDR-λ (with Elastic Weight Consolidation - EWC):**
 - **Principle:** EWC is an approximate Bayesian method that identifies the "importance" of neural network parameters for remembering previous tasks. It does this by calculating the empirical Fisher Information Matrix (FIM), which quantifies how sensitive the learned parameters are to changes that might degrade performance on previous tasks.
 - **Mechanism:** When learning a new task (i.e., adapting to a new set of randomizations), the loss function for PPO is augmented with a regularization term. This term penalizes changes to parameters that were deemed important for previous tasks, thereby preventing catastrophic forgetting.
 - **Loss Function:** $L(\theta) = L_C(\theta) + \frac{\lambda_B}{2} ||\theta - \theta^*_B||^2 F_B + \frac{\lambda_A}{2} ||\theta - \theta^*_A||^2 F_A$ (for task C after A and B).
 - **Implementation Details:** Only the actor network (policy) parameters are regularized, as the critic does not directly contribute to the log-likelihood computation used for FIM. The FIM elements are normalized to [0.0, 1.0] after each task to de-couple the regularization constant $\lambda$ from arbitrary scaling.
 - **Memory Footprint:** This variant requires saving a separate snapshot of the actor network parameters and its corresponding diagonal Fisher information for *each* previous randomization task, leading to a linear increase in storage requirements.

- **CDR-Oλ (with Online EWC):**
 - **Principle:** Addresses the memory scaling issue of standard EWC.
 - **Mechanism:** Instead of storing all previous network snapshots and Fisher matrices, only a single "online" Fisher information matrix ($F^*_i$) and the network snapshot ($\theta^*_{i-1}$) from the *immediately previous* task are maintained. The online Fisher information is updated iteratively as a weighted sum of the previous online Fisher and the current task's Fisher ($F^*_i = \gamma F^*_{i-1} + F_i$).
 - **Advantages:** Avoids linear memory increase.
 - **Loss Function:** $L_i(\theta) = \tilde{L}_i(\theta) + \frac{\lambda}{2} ||\theta - \theta^*_{i-1}||^2 \gamma F^*_{i-1}$.
 - **Implementation Details:** Similar to CDR-λ, normalization of $F_i$ is applied.

**C. Baselines:**
The proposed CDR methods are compared against three standard strategies using PPO:
1. **Ideal:** Policy trained exclusively in the non-randomized, idealized simulation environment. This represents the upper bound for simulation performance but typically suffers from a large reality gap.
2. **Finetuning:** The model pre-trained in the ideal simulation is sequentially fine-tuned on each randomization parameter one at a time, similar to CDR, but *without* any continual learning mechanism (i.e., no regularization to prevent forgetting previous randomizations).
3. **Randomized:** The policy is trained from scratch by activating *all* randomization parameters simultaneously throughout the entire training process. This is the common practice for robust sim2real transfer.

**D. Experimental Setup:**
- **Robotic Tasks:**
 - **Reaching:** A 2-joint robotic arm task (KUKA LBR iiwa 14) aiming to reach a target in 3D space. State includes end-effector distances and joint positions; actions are joint velocities. Reward is negative Euclidean distance.
 - **Grasping:** A more complex 7-joint robotic arm task (KUKA LBR iiwa 14 with Robotiq 3-Finger Gripper) aiming to grasp a box. State includes end-effector/object distances, joint positions, and orientation vectors; actions are joint velocities. Reward is distance-based, with penalties for collision/joint limits and a bonus for successful grasp.
- **Simulation Environment:** Unity-based platform.
- **Randomization Parameters:**
 - **Latency (L):** Random delay in state observation.
 - **Torque (T):** Randomization of joint stiffness and damping coefficients.
 - **Noise (N):** Random noise added to simulate sensor inaccuracies.
 - Ranges for these parameters are defined in Table I.
- **Ordering of Randomizations:** To investigate sensitivity, an empirical study determined "best" (TLN - Torque, Latency, Noise) and "worst" (NLT - Noise, Latency, Torque) orderings for sequential training strategies based on single-parameter sim2real transfer performance.
- **Training Details:** PPO implementation based on StableBaselines3. Uses a 2-layer MLP with 64 units/layer. Specific hyperparameters are listed in Table I. Total training duration is $4 \times 10^6$ timesteps for Reacher and $10 \times 10^6$ for Grasper.
- **Evaluation:** Policies are evaluated in both ideal simulation and on the real robot under identical conditions to isolate the sim2real gap effects.
- **Metrics (Table II):**
 - **Cumulative Episodic Reward ($r_{ep}$):** Sum of rewards over an episode. Higher is better.
 - **Continuity Cost (C):** Measures smoothness of robot motion. Lower is better (0 for constant actions, 100 for maximum action difference).
 - **Distance to Target ($d_{tgt}$):** Average Euclidean distance to target in the second half of the Reacher episode. Lower is better (indicates stabilization).
 - **Grasping Success (G):** Percentage of successful grasps for Grasper task. Higher is better.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of Continual Domain Randomization (CDR) in improving sim2real transfer for robotic manipulation tasks compared to traditional baselines.

**A. Reacher Task Results:**
- **Training Progress (Fig. 4):**
 - **Ideal:** Fastest convergence and highest reward in simulation, as expected, due to the lack of randomization.
 - **Randomized:** Slower convergence than Ideal due to increased task difficulty from simultaneous randomization.
 - **Sequential Strategies (Finetuning, CDR-λ, CDR-Oλ):** Training speed and final simulation performance were influenced by the order of randomizations. The sequence starting with Torque (TLN) led to faster policy improvement than Noise (NLT), indicating that some randomizations are harder to adapt to initially.
 - **Finetuning:** Adapts very quickly in simulation and achieves performance comparable to Ideal. However, this quick adaptation suggests overfitting to the most recent randomization.
 - **CDR-λ:** Adapts more slowly in simulation and reaches lower simulation performance compared to CDR-Oλ and Finetuning. This is attributed to the stronger, task-specific regularization from EWC.
 - **CDR-Oλ:** Adapts faster than CDR-λ and converges to a simulation performance similar to the fully Randomized approach.
- **Sim2Real Evaluation (Table III):**
 - **Ideal:** Achieves the best reward in simulation but exhibits the worst sim2real transfer. It suffers from significantly lower real-world rewards, high continuity cost (jerky motions), and high distance to target (unstable jittering). This highlights severe overfitting to the idealized simulation.
 - **Randomized:** Shows robust sim2real transfer, achieving high real-world rewards, low continuity cost, and low distance to target. This confirms its effectiveness in bridging the reality gap.
 - **Finetuning:** While performing well in simulation (comparable to Ideal), its real-world performance is significantly worse than CDR or Randomized, and only marginally better than Ideal. This confirms the hypothesis of overfitting to the last randomization, leading to a strong reality gap despite sequential training. The high continuity cost further indicates unstable behavior.
 - **CDR-λ:** Achieves very good sim2real transfer, performing comparably to or slightly better than the fully Randomized baseline in terms of real-world reward, distance to target, and continuity cost. Its stronger regularization leads to slightly lower real-world rewards than CDR-Oλ. It shows robustness to randomization order.
 - **CDR-Oλ:** Demonstrates the best overall real-world performance among all strategies, achieving the highest real-world rewards. It also matches the low distance to target and continuity cost of the Randomized baseline. Crucially, CDR-Oλ is less sensitive to the order of randomizations than Finetuning, providing consistent robust transfer.

**B. Grasper Task Results:**
- **Training Progress (Fig. 5):**
 - The grasping task is considerably more challenging.
 - **Ideal:** Easier to solve in simulation.
 - **Randomized:** More difficult due to high state uncertainty.
 - **Sequential Strategies:** Encountering noise randomization (N) in either sequence (TLN or NLT) significantly increased the difficulty of policy adaptation, leading to larger deviations in training curves.
 - **Finetuning:** Shows higher deviations during training, indicating less stable learning.
 - **CDR Strategies:** Exhibit lower and more stable deviations throughout training, with CDR-λ being more consistent than CDR-Oλ due to its separate regularization per task.
- **Sim2Real Evaluation (Table IV):**
 - **Ideal:** Achieves high reward and grasping success in simulation but suffers from poor real-world reward and extremely high continuity cost, indicating unsafe jittering behavior despite being able to grasp.
 - **Randomized:** Shows moderate simulation success but good sim2real transfer in terms of continuity cost. Its grasping success in non-randomized simulation is low, suggesting that simultaneous randomization can sometimes make finding a successful policy difficult even in sim.
 - **Finetuning:** Real-world performance is highly sensitive to randomization order, with a significant 12% difference in reward between best (NLT) and worst (TLN) cases. It can achieve high grasping success, but often at the cost of higher continuity.
 - **CDR Models:** Crucially, CDR-λ and CDR-Oλ show **much less susceptibility to the order of randomizations**, achieving similar rewards regardless of the sequence. Their real-world rewards generally lie within the range of Finetuning's best/worst outcomes and are better than the Randomized strategy. CDR-Oλ (NLT) achieved the highest real-world grasping success among all strategies at 60%, matching Ideal's real-world grasp success with significantly better continuity and higher real-world reward.

**C. Effect of Continual Learning Regularization Strength (λ) (Fig. 6):**
- **Low λ:** CDR models behave similarly to Finetuning, showing good simulation performance but poor, order-sensitive real-world transfer. This confirms that insufficient regularization leads to catastrophic forgetting.
- **High λ:** Prevents the model from adapting to new randomizations, leading to poor performance in both simulation and reality. Policies become too rigid.
- **Middle Range λ (e.g., $5 \times 10^3$):** This range yields the best balance, achieving reasonable performance in simulation and optimal real-world results while being robust to the order of randomizations. This highlights the importance of tuning $\lambda$ to allow sufficient adaptation while preserving past knowledge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Continual Domain Randomization (CDR) framework proposed in this paper represents a significant advancement in the field of sim2real transfer for robotic reinforcement learning, offering several key contributions and promising impacts:

1. **Enhanced Flexibility in Sim2Real Training:** CDR breaks away from the rigid paradigm of defining all randomization parameters at the start of training. By allowing new randomizations to be introduced sequentially as new "tasks," it provides unprecedented flexibility. This is particularly valuable in real-world scenarios where system dynamics might evolve, or new environmental factors might emerge over time, making it impractical to pre-define all possible variations. It also allows for iterative refinement of the simulation environment itself.

2. **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting:** The integration of continual learning (CL) algorithms (specifically EWC and online-EWC) is central to CDR's success. It effectively addresses the critical problem of catastrophic forgetting, ensuring that knowledge gained from training under earlier randomization settings is retained as the policy adapts to new ones. This leads to more robust and generalized policies that do not "overfit" to the most recent training condition, as demonstrated by the comparison with the Finetuning baseline.

3. **Improved Real-World Performance and Robustness:** The experimental results clearly show that policies trained with CDR, especially CDR-Oλ, consistently achieve comparable or superior real-world performance (higher rewards, lower continuity cost, better grasp success) compared to the standard "fully Randomized" approach and significantly outperform simple "Finetuning" and "Ideal" (non-randomized) models. This translates directly into more reliable and deployable robotic systems.

4. **Order-Agnosticism for Sequential Randomization:** A crucial finding is CDR's reduced sensitivity to the order in which randomization parameters are introduced. Unlike plain Finetuning, whose performance dramatically varied with different sequences (e.g., TLN vs. NLT), CDR models provided consistent high performance. This is highly beneficial because, in practical applications, the "optimal" order of introducing randomizations for sim2real transfer is often unknown and difficult to determine without extensive real-world testing.

5. **Practical Applicability and Scalability:** The online variant, CDR-Oλ, addresses the memory scaling issue of traditional EWC by only retaining a single, continually updated Fisher information matrix. This makes CDR a more memory-efficient and scalable solution, particularly important for training complex DRL policies with large neural networks. The use of widely adopted PPO further enhances its practical relevance.

6. **Guidance on Hyperparameter Tuning:** The study on the regularization strength ($\lambda$) provides valuable empirical insight, demonstrating that a "middle ground" value of $\lambda$ is crucial for optimal sim2real transfer. This practical guidance can help researchers and engineers effectively apply regularization-based CL methods in similar contexts.

**Potential Impact:**
The proposed CDR framework has the potential to:
- **Accelerate Robotic Development:** By making sim2real transfer more robust and flexible, it can reduce the need for extensive, time-consuming, and potentially hazardous real-world policy tuning.
- **Enable Adaptive Robot Behavior:** It lays the groundwork for robots that can continually adapt their skills to changing environmental conditions, wear and tear in hardware, or new tasks, purely through simulation updates.
- **Broaden the Scope of DRL in Robotics:** By making DRL more applicable to complex, dynamic real-world scenarios, it can unlock new possibilities for autonomous robots in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and exploration.
- **Inform Future Research:** The paper identifies key limitations and avenues for future work, such as combining CDR with automated/active DR for optimal parameter range definition, addressing complex interactions between multiple randomizations, and extending it to vision-based sim2real transfer. This will likely spur further research at the intersection of continual learning, domain randomization, and robotic control.

In conclusion, Continual Domain Randomization offers a robust and flexible solution to the reality gap, paving the way for more efficient and adaptable real-world robotic systems by synergistically combining the strengths of domain randomization and continual learning.
