# RAFAIL: Relationship-Aware Failure Detection for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> RAFAIL detects manipulation failures by running out-of-distribution detection on task-relevant pairwise object relationships rather than whole observations. Offline, a VLM labels successful demonstrations with relationship importance and task progress, distilled into policy auxiliary heads; online, per-relationship RND detectors conditioned on progress are gated by predicted importance. Without failure data or runtime VLM inference, it reaches 73.4% balanced accuracy across three real-world tasks versus 65.2% for the strongest baseline.

## Problem

- VLM-based failure detectors such as AHA and FailSafe recognize semantic failures but require continuous online VLM inference, adding heavy runtime computation alongside the policy.
- OOD and uncertainty detectors trained on successes flag deviation rather than failure, so harmless scene changes trigger false alarms while subtle consequential interaction errors are missed.
- Many detectors depend on global observation embeddings or policy-internal features, which are not portable across policy architectures, especially token-based policies lacking a global bottleneck.

## Method

- **Relationship encoder**: ==GroundedSAM2==/==SAM3==-segmented object-pair point clouds encoded by a ==DGCNN== trained with an ==importance-weighted triplet loss==, using same-progress cross-demo positives and progress- or relationship-mismatched negatives.
- **VLM preference distillation**: ==Gemma== pairwise rankings of relationship importance and task progress train a ==ResNet18== importance labeler and a monotone ==ResNet18-BiLSTM== progress labeler, whose labels supervise auxiliary heads on the ==ManiFlow== policy.
- **Importance-gated relationship OOD detection**: per-relationship ==RND== detectors ==FiLM==-conditioned on task progress with ==conformal-prediction== thresholds; failure fires when a relationship's OOD score exceeds its threshold and its predicted importance exceeds 0.5.

## Results

- Overall, RAFAIL reaches **73.4%** balanced accuracy (**58.5%** TPR, **11.6%** FPR) over **178** real UR10e executions, beating ACE at **65.2%** and every mean- or max-pooled OOD baseline.
- Best on Pouring (**82.3%**, **81.3%** TPR) and Uprighting (**71.4%**), but third on PickPlace at **66.7%**, behind max-pooled RND (**72.8%**) and logpZO (**68.0%**).
- Removing the importance gate raises FPR from **11.6%** to **69.8%** (accuracy **58.1%**); whole-scene encoding drops accuracy to **43.6%**; triggering relationships match annotated failure relationships in **68-93%** of true-positive detections.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Scoping OOD detection to task-relevant entity relationships, rather than global observations, brings novelty signals closer to actual failure and suppresses false alarms from harmless scene variation.
> - VLM semantics can be distilled offline into cheap auxiliary heads via pairwise preference labels, keeping runtime monitoring VLM-free and independent of policy-internal latent representations.
> - Relevance gating can only suppress alarms, trading TPR for FPR, and geometric relationship monitoring misses subtle pose errors, invisible contact forces, and segmentation-tracking breakdowns.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A manipulation robot can depart from its successful demonstrations without endangering task completion. A small mistake in a crucial interaction, by contrast, can matter greatly. RAFAIL monitors selected object-to-object or gripper-to-object relationships instead of treating every change in the whole scene as equally meaningful. It flags an anomaly only when that relationship is currently important. On three real-world tasks, it outperformed the best evaluated baseline overall, though not on every task.

Schematic of the method: relationship importance gates which relationship's high OOD score contributes to a failure alert. It illustrates the method and is not a measured result.

### 2. Watch the interaction, not everything

The method follows four steps.

1. **Find the relationships.** RAFAIL identifies relevant entities from task descriptions and forms their unique pairs, such as gripper and object or object and target. A relationship is the state of a pair at a particular task stage.
1. **Label what matters offline.** A vision-language model (VLM) compares frames from successful demonstrations. From these comparisons, it labels which relationships matter more and which frames show greater task progress. Labelers trained from those comparisons provide targets for auxiliary heads on the manipulation policy.
1. **Score each relationship at runtime.** The policy predicts task progress and relationship importance. Segmentation and tracking supply the relationship observations. A separate out-of-distribution (OOD) detector scores each relationship. OOD means unlike the successful examples used to train the detector. The detector is RND conditioned on task progress, and RND is an existing detector, not a new contribution.
1. **Gate the alarm.** A relationship triggers only if its predicted importance is above 0.5 and its OOD score exceeds its calibrated relationship-specific threshold. Any such trigger flags the execution.

The VLM and the labeler networks do not run online. Segmentation and tracking, encoding, and OOD scoring are still required at runtime, and the paper does not report a direct runtime or latency comparison.

The closest contrast is FAIL-Detect ([[2503.08558|paper]]), which learns scalar signals from policy inputs and/or outputs and calibrates thresholds on successful rollouts. RAFAIL changes what is scored. It replaces global scene or policy signals with task-progress-conditioned representations of individual relationships.

### 3. Does the focus help?

The experiments used the ManiFlow policy on three real-world tasks:

- **PickPlace:** put a cylindrical object in a small bowl.
- **Pouring:** pour a ball from a cup into a bowl.
- **Uprighting:** lift and right a fallen cup.

The team collected 12 successful teleoperated demonstrations per task. The 178 autonomous test executions were excluded from training and calibration. These executions include both successes and failures, induced by object-placement changes and task-irrelevant background objects.

The metric is balanced accuracy: the mean of the fraction of failed executions flagged (the true-positive rate) and the fraction of successful executions correctly left unflagged (the true-negative rate). It is not the fraction of all executions classified correctly.

Overall, RAFAIL reached **73.4% balanced accuracy**, compared with **65.2%** for ACE, the strongest evaluated baseline. ACE is the action-uncertainty component of FIPER ([[2510.09459|paper]]). RAFAIL's true-positive rate was 58.5% and its false-positive rate was 11.6%, versus ACE's 46.7% and 16.3%. RAFAIL led on Pouring and Uprighting. PickPlace is an exception, where RAFAIL scored 66.7% versus 72.8% for max-pooled RND.

The ablation tests the mechanism more directly:

- **Whole-scene encoding** reaches 43.6% overall balanced accuracy, versus 73.4% for RAFAIL. This supports the value of relationship-wise encoding in this evaluation.
- **Removing the importance gate** raises the true-positive rate to 85.9% but also raises the false-positive rate to 69.8%. Balanced accuracy falls to 58.1%. This is evidence that the gate suppresses alarms at a cost in detections. It does not prove importance is the sole cause of the improvement.

Ablation results for PickPlace, Pouring, Uprighting, and Overall. Each task lists balanced accuracy, true-positive rate, and false-positive rate in percent. The four columns are RAFAIL gate, RAFAIL weight, θimp = 0 (importance ignored), and Scene (whole-scene encoding).

### 4. Where the result stops

RAFAIL assumes that task-critical failures can be represented by pairwise relationships among segmentable entities. Several kinds of failure may therefore remain undetected:

- unmodeled entities
- global scene properties
- contact forces invisible in geometry
- segmentation or tracking breakdown
- subtle pose deviations within the successful distribution

The authors' failure-relationship annotations are subjective, not independent ground truth.

RAFAIL's contribution is to condition an existing OOD signal on which interaction matters now. The whole-scene and no-gate ablations support that design choice in these evaluated tasks. These are task-specific trial results, not evidence of broad deployment reliability.
