# Toward Integrating Adaptive Experience Replay and Online Uncertainty Estimation in Safe Actor-Critic Optimal Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Politecnico di Milano developed an integrated architectural framework for safe actor-critic optimal control, unifying control barrier functions, adaptive experience replay, and online uncertainty estimation. This co-design approach demonstrated superior safety, achieving zero contacts and significantly lower costs in simulated robot navigation under extreme perceptual noise compared to fragmented methods.

## Problem

- Traditional actor-critic reinforcement learning struggles to guarantee safety and maintain performance under real-world uncertainties like unmodeled dynamics and disturbances.
- Existing research often addresses safety filtering, adaptive experience replay, and online uncertainty estimation in isolation, leading to limitations like distorted exploratory data or stale replay information.
- Standard experience replay mechanisms can struggle to efficiently capture and prioritize safety-critical or uncertainty-informative transitions, impacting learning stability near constraint boundaries.

## Method

- An **integrated architecture** was designed where a nominal actor-critic, a ==CBF-based safety filter==, an ==online uncertainty estimator==, and an ==adaptive experience replay== manager explicitly interact and share information.
- The safety filter leverages online uncertainty estimates to make robust action corrections, while the replay manager prioritizes transitions based on safety-criticality, uncertainty information, and novelty.
- An ==augmented replay tuple== captures nominal actor actions, executed safe actions, safety observables (e.g., barrier slack), and uncertainty observables (e.g., estimation residuals) for more effective replay prioritization.

## Results

- Under extreme perception noise, the integrated 'Full' configuration achieved **zero** contacts and **100%** goal success, significantly outperforming other configurations that recorded substantial contact counts.
- The 'Full' configuration maintained the lowest mean cost (**7.63 ± 0.44**) and the lowest obstacle-belief RMSE (**3.52 ± 0.55 cm**) under extreme stress conditions.
- Adaptive replay methods demonstrated a significantly increased retention and sampling of safety-critical 'boundary' events (**100%** retention, **96.3%** sampling for 'Full') and 'uncertainty' events (**26.4%** retained, **22.9%** sampled for 'Full') compared to baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating online uncertainty estimation into the safety filter enhances its reliability by allowing CBF constraints to adapt to true local dynamics or obstacle geometry.
> - Training the critic on executed safe actions and employing safety-aware replay aligns the learned value function with actual closed-loop behavior, improving critic consistency.
> - Adaptive replay, informed by both safety layer and uncertainty estimator, effectively retains and samples safety-critical and uncertainty-informative transitions that are often under-represented in uniform or TD-error based replay.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the field of optimal control for nonlinear systems, specifically focusing on the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL) with safety-critical applications. Traditional actor-critic RL methods often encounter two primary challenges: ensuring safety during learning and execution, and maintaining performance under various forms of uncertainty (e.g., unmodeled dynamics, disturbances, distribution shifts). Furthermore, the design of experience replay, a crucial component for data efficiency in off-policy RL, is noted as a potential source of stale or misleading information, particularly near constraint boundaries.

Existing literature has addressed these challenges through separate lines of research:
- **Safety via Control Barrier Functions (CBFs):** A significant body of work integrates CBFs into RL as safety filters or to inform Bellman updates, aiming to guarantee forward invariance of safe sets. However, the paper points out that strong safety filters can distort exploratory data, potentially leading to inconsistent critic learning if this distortion is not accounted for.
- **Advanced Experience Replay:** Researchers have explored improvements beyond uniform sampling, emphasizing the importance of quality, diversity, and temporal relevance of retained transitions for learning stability and efficiency. The paper highlights that in constrained tasks, experiences near safe-set boundaries or those associated with abrupt disturbances are disproportionately informative.
- **Online Uncertainty Estimation:** Another line of work in continuous-time optimal control focuses on adaptive critics and observer-based designs to estimate unknown dynamics or disturbances online, recognizing that robustness and optimality are intrinsically linked. Recurrent neural networks have shown promise in modeling temporal uncertainty.

The authors argue that these three research directions—safety filtering, adaptive experience replay, and online uncertainty estimation—are often developed in isolation. This fragmented approach can lead to limitations such as robust safety but poor sample efficiency, accurate adaptation with insufficient replay diversity, or good nominal learning but fragile safety. The current work proposes an integrated architecture to address this "integration gap," positing that these components should be co-designed as structural elements that mutually influence the learning process, the safety layer, and the policy's response. This view builds upon recent work in safe actor-critic RL with CBFs and experience replay, bi-level architectures for uncertain nonlinear systems using LSTMs, and self-organizing dual-buffer adaptive clustering experience replay.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate an integrated architectural viewpoint for safe actor-critic optimal control, where safety filtering, adaptive experience replay, and online uncertainty estimation are explicitly co-designed rather than treated as separate modules or auxiliary additions.

The motivation for this integrated approach stems from several detailed observations:
- **Interdependence of Modules:** Each of the three components—barrier filtering, uncertainty estimation, and experience replay—fundamentally alters the data used for learning and control. Treating them independently overlooks critical interactions. For example, the effectiveness of a safety barrier function is tied to accurate knowledge of obstacle geometry or system disturbances, which uncertainty estimation can provide. Similarly, decisions about which experiences to replay should consider both safety-critical events and transitions that are highly informative about system uncertainty.
- **Limitations of Isolated Approaches:** Standard actor-critic methods risk generating unsafe actions. Even with a nominal safe policy, performance can degrade under uncertainty. Naive experience replay can introduce stale or misleading data, especially near constraints. Current literature, while strong in individual areas, has not systematically addressed the synergistic design of these elements.
- **Improving Critic Consistency:** If the critic learns from only nominal actions, but the system's actual trajectory is determined by filtered, safe actions, there is a mismatch. Updating the critic based on *executed* safe actions and safety-aware replay aims to align the learned value function with the true closed-loop behavior, thereby improving critic consistency.
- **Enhancing Replay Effectiveness:** Not all data points are equally valuable in constrained control tasks. Transitions near safe-set boundaries, or those revealing significant uncertainty, carry disproportionately important information. An adaptive replay mechanism is motivated to prioritize and retain such safety-critical and uncertainty-informative samples, which uniform replay might under-represent.
- **Increasing Safety Filter Reliability:** Safety filters, being model-dependent, are sensitive to inaccuracies in the underlying dynamics or environmental state. Providing online uncertainty estimates to the safety filter can allow barrier constraints to adapt to true local dynamics and disturbances, thereby enhancing the practical reliability of action correction.
- **Cross-Domain Applicability:** The authors aim for a modular architecture that can inform designs across diverse applications, from robotics (where uncertainty might be slip or actuator variation) to epidemiological control (where it could be time-varying transmission or compliance).

The main contributions summarized by the authors are:
1. Synthesizing safety filtering, adaptive experience replay, and online uncertainty estimation into one framework.
2. Formulating an integrated architectural viewpoint where policy, filter, replay, and estimator are co-designed.
3. Clarifying the integration gap in current literature through structured comparisons.
4. Providing a compact design template for future methods, including an augmented replay representation, a modular learning loop, and a formal result on boundary-critical samples.
5. Highlighting the applicability of this perspective across domains like robotics, autonomous systems, and epidemiological control.
6. Making the template testable through explicit replay scores, a finite-training bound, a conditional robust-CBF inequality, and a six-configuration simulation study.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology involves designing an integrated architecture with four interacting modules: a nominal actor-critic, a control barrier function (CBF)-based safety filter, an online uncertainty estimator, and an adaptive experience replay manager. The approach emphasizes explicit information exchange among these modules.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system considered is a class of nonlinear continuous-time control systems described by $\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t)) + g(x(t))u(t) + d(t)$, where $x(t)$ is the state, $u(t)$ is the control input, $f$ and $g$ are smooth dynamics, and $d(t)$ represents a lumped uncertainty term. The control must satisfy input admissibility bounds ($u(t) \in U$), and the state must remain within a safe set ($X_{\text{safe}}$) defined by CBF inequalities ($h_j(x) \geq 0$). The objective is to minimize an infinite-horizon performance index $J(x_0, u)$, typically a discounted sum of running costs. The integration is motivated by the need to simultaneously address safety, effective replay, and online uncertainty estimation.

#### 3.2. Integrated Architecture Design Principles

The proposed design principles are:
1. The safety filter (Π$_{safe}$) should utilize the uncertainty estimate ($\hat{d}(t)$) to inform barrier feasibility and future-state prediction, as the filter itself is model-dependent.
2. The replay manager should leverage information from both the safety layer (e.g., barrier activation) and the uncertainty estimator (e.g., estimation residuals) to prioritize informative transitions.
3. The critic's learning updates should be based on the *executed safe action* ($u_t$), which truly determines the system's trajectory, rather than solely the nominal actor output ($u_{a,t}$).

#### 3.3. Nominal Actor-Critic and Safe Action Correction

A nominal actor policy $\pi_\theta(x)$ proposes actions $u_{a,t}$. The executed action $u_t$ is derived from a safety-correction stage, $u(t) = \Pi_{safe}(x(t), u_a(t), \hat{d}(t))$. This filter typically solves a quadratic program (QP) to find the closest admissible action $u$ to $u_{a,t}$ that satisfies CBF constraints. The CBF condition, $\nabla h_j(x)^\top (f(x) + g(x)u + \hat{d}(x)) + \alpha_j(h_j(x)) \geq 0$, explicitly includes the uncertainty estimate $\hat{d}(x)$. A robust inequality is presented which states that if the error $d(x) - \hat{d}(x)$ is bounded and the robust admissible-action set is non-empty, the true derivative satisfies the CBF condition.

#### 3.4. Online Uncertainty Estimation

The uncertainty estimator $E_\phi(\zeta_t)$ is updated online using recent state-control trajectories $\zeta_t$. While various implementations are possible (e.g., observer-based critics, RNNs, LSTMs), the paper's experiment instantiates a causal filter that estimates static obstacle centers and their scales from detector data. This estimate then defines the barrier geometry for the CBF. Two design rules are proposed: the estimator should be trained on transitions collected under the *executed safe policy*, and its confidence or residual magnitude should be made available to the replay manager.

#### 3.5. Adaptive Experience Replay

Instead of a standard tuple $(x_t, u_t, r_t, x_{t+1})$, an *augmented tuple* $\xi_t = (x_t, u_{a,t}, u_t, r_t, x_{t+1}, \sigma_t, \rho_t, \eta_t)$ is stored. This includes the nominal actor action ($u_{a,t}$), the executed safe action ($u_t$), a safety-related observable ($\sigma_t$, e.g., barrier slack), an uncertainty-related observable ($\rho_t$, e.g., estimation residual norm), and a recency/context descriptor ($\eta_t$).
This augmented representation allows the replay manager to preserve:
1. **Safety-critical transitions:** where the CBF filter was active or nearly active.
2. **Uncertainty-informative transitions:** where the estimator residual was large or the operating regime changed.
3. **Representative nominal transitions:** for coverage of ordinary operation.
A generic sampling score $p_t = \lambda_{TD}\bar{\delta}_t + \lambda_{safe}s_t + \lambda_{unc}e_t + \lambda_{nov}n_t$ is defined, combining normalized temporal-difference error ($\bar{\delta}_t$), safety-importance score ($s_t$), uncertainty score ($e_t$), and novelty/diversity score ($n_t$). Specific functional forms for $s_t, e_t, n_t$ and weights $(\lambda_{TD}, \lambda_{safe}, \lambda_{unc}, \lambda_{nov}) = (0.18, 0.39, 0.30, 0.13)$ are provided for the experiment, along with a sampling probability using a temperature parameter $\beta$. A proposition on finite-training exposure of retained critical transitions is presented, highlighting the importance of the retained-transition condition.

#### 3.6. Integrated Learning Loop (Algorithm 1 Summary)

The learning process operates as follows:
1. Observe state $x_t$.
2. Compute nominal action $u_{a,t}$ from the actor.
3. Estimate uncertainty $\hat{d}_t$ using the estimator.
4. Compute safe executed action $u_t$ using the safety filter, incorporating $\hat{d}_t$.
5. Apply $u_t$, observe reward $r_t$ and next state $x_{t+1}$.
6. Compute safety, uncertainty, and novelty scores.
7. Store the augmented transition $\xi_t$ in replay memory.
8. Sample a mini-batch using the mixed priority score.
9. Update the critic using executed safe transitions.
10. Update the actor using the current critic and safety-aware targets.
11. Update the estimator using recent trajectories or uncertainty-informative samples.

#### 3.7. Experimental Validation Setup

- **Benchmark:** A 3.20 m $\times$ 3.20 m cluttered workspace with known rectangular and two hidden-center circular obstacles. A 0.062m radius robot navigates at up to 0.72 m/s. The uncertain physical obstacles are static.
- **Observation Process:** The detector reports noisy measurements of uncertain obstacle centers $z_i(t)$, which include bias, colored drift, white jitter, smooth drift, and temporary localization jumps. Intermittent holds repeat prior reports. The detector reports an overconfident 1 cm standard deviation.
- **Cost Function:** The running cost includes tracking error, control effort, action deviation from nominal, a contact penalty, and a penalty for small clearance from known obstacles.
- **Comparison Configurations:** Six progressive, component-matched configurations are compared:
 - **AC:** Waypoint actor-critic with uniform replay (baseline).
 - **AC+CBF:** Adds CBF projection using raw/held detector data.
 - **AC+CBF+PER:** Adds temporal-difference prioritized replay.
 - **AC+CBF+UE:** Adds online uncertainty estimation (robust static-center filter) with uniform replay.
 - **AC+CBF+AER:** Adds adaptive safety-aware replay.
 - **Full (present):** Combines online uncertainty estimation, replay-calibrated static anchor, robust CBF, and mixed adaptive replay.
- **Training and Evaluation:** All configurations are trained for 10 episodes (115 transitions/episode) and evaluated without further learning for 280 transitions over five paired seeds.
- **Metrics:** Safety (violations, minimum global/uncertain clearance), goal performance (success, error), cost, belief RMSE, CBF intervention rate, and runtime.
- **Stress Tests:** Evaluation includes a moderate perception noise multiplier (2.2), an eleven-level perception-noise sweep, and an exploratory extreme-stress test at multiplier 6.0, where detector errors are significantly amplified.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation, conducted on a 2D robot navigation benchmark with corrupted obstacle measurements, evaluated six configurations under common training budgets, random seeds, sensor streams, exploration, and disturbances.

#### 4.1. Extreme Perception-Stress Test (Multiplier 6.0)

Under extreme perception noise, characterized by large detector biases (6.0–11.4 cm), significant smooth drift (up to 5.4 cm), and substantial temporary jumps (30–42 cm), the results demonstrated differentiating performance:
- **Safety:** The **Full** configuration and **AC+CBF+UE** were the only methods to record **zero contacts** (violations) across all five evaluation seeds. Other configurations, including those with CBF but without integrated uncertainty estimation, recorded significant contact counts (e.g., AC: 2.79 ± 1.32, AC+CBF: 1.64 ± 0.86).
- **Goal Success:** The **Full** configuration reached the goal in **100% of the five seeds**. AC+CBF+UE reached the goal in 80% of seeds (four out of five), failing in one seed despite zero contacts. All other configurations achieved 100% goal success but with contacts.
- **Cost:** **Full** achieved the lowest mean cost (7.63 ± 0.44), followed closely by AC+CBF+UE (8.96 ± 2.08). The other configurations had substantially higher costs, ranging from 22.62 to 46.99.
- **Belief RMSE:** **Full** exhibited the lowest obstacle-belief root-mean-square error (3.52 ± 0.55 cm), significantly outperforming AC+CBF+UE (11.08 ± 1.23 cm). All other configurations had a belief RMSE of 16.71 ± 1.55 cm, indicating they were not performing online uncertainty estimation for the obstacle centers.
- **Clearance:** Full and AC+CBF+UE achieved positive minimum global and uncertain clearance, contrasting with negative clearances for all other methods, signifying contact.

#### 4.2. Moderate Perception-Stress Test (Multiplier 2.2)

At a moderate noise level:
- Every CBF configuration (AC+CBF, AC+CBF+PER, AC+CBF+UE, AC+CBF+AER, Full) achieved collision-free performance across all five seeds.
- The baseline AC configuration recorded contact in two of five seeds.
- **Full** maintained the lowest belief RMSE (3.44 ± 0.22 cm) and the largest minimum global clearance (3.73 ± 0.36 cm), indicating superior estimation and safety margins.

#### 4.3. Finite-Buffer Replay Audit (Supplementary Table S10)

The audit of event generation, retention, and sampling revealed:
- Adaptive replay methods (**AC+CBF+AER** and **Full**) significantly increased the retention and sampling fractions of safety-critical "boundary" events compared to uniform or TD-prioritized replay. Full achieved 100% retention and 96.3% sampling of generated boundary events.
- **Full** also demonstrated a substantial increase in the retention and sampling of "uncertainty" events (26.4% retained, 22.9% sampled), reflecting the integration of uncertainty information into replay prioritization. AC+CBF+UE also showed improved uncertainty event retention/sampling compared to baselines, though less than Full.

#### 4.4. Statistical Analysis

Friedman tests indicated significant differences (p $\leq$ 0.0045) across configurations for cost, violation rate, minimum clearance, and belief RMSE in the extreme-stress test. Paired Wilcoxon tests comparing ablations against Full did not consistently reach p < 0.05, which the authors attribute to the coarse resolution with only five pairs, describing them as exploratory diagnostics.

#### 4.5. Learning and Robustness Sweeps

The perception-noise sweep demonstrated that as severity increased, methods without integrated uncertainty estimation (AC, AC+CBF, AC+CBF+PER, AC+CBF+AER) experienced higher violation rates. Full and AC+CBF+UE maintained zero violations across the sweep (Figures S3a, S3b).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research paper makes a case for the co-design of safety filtering, adaptive experience replay, and online uncertainty estimation within actor-critic optimal control architectures, demonstrating this through a systematic experimental validation.

#### 5.1. Significance of Integration

The results support the central hypothesis that integrating these modules can lead to improved performance, particularly under high uncertainty. The "Full" configuration, which combines all three elements, consistently demonstrated robust safety (zero contacts in extreme tests), efficient learning (low cost), and accurate uncertainty estimation. The benefits of this integration are framed around four points:
1. **Critic Consistency:** By training the critic on *executed* safe actions and utilizing safety-aware replay, the learned value function is more congruent with the true closed-loop behavior, reducing the mismatch between proposed and executed policies.
2. **Informative Replay:** Adaptive replay, by preserving safety-critical and uncertainty-informative transitions, overcomes the limitations of standard uniform or TD-error-based sampling, which might under-represent such crucial experiences. The audit confirms that boundary and uncertainty events are preferentially retained and sampled.
3. **Reliable Safety Filtering:** The integration of online uncertainty estimates into the CBF framework allows the safety filter to adapt to actual local dynamics and obstacle geometry, making action correction more reliable under model mismatch or noisy perception.
4. **Cross-Domain Transferability:** The modular nature of the integrated architecture suggests its applicability beyond the specific robot navigation task, potentially extending to diverse fields like robotics, autonomous systems, and even epidemiological control, where various forms of uncertainty and constraints are present.

#### 5.2. Evidence from Ablation Study

The ablation study provides empirical support for the individual contributions of the integrated components:
- Nominal CBFs (AC+CBF, AC+CBF+PER, AC+CBF+AER) struggled with corrupted geometry, resulting in contacts under extreme stress, indicating that an uncertainty estimator is crucial when perception is unreliable.
- Temporal-difference priority alone (AC+CBF+PER) did not reliably preserve uncertainty events or guarantee safety under extreme conditions, highlighting the need for safety- and uncertainty-aware prioritization.
- Adaptive replay (AC+CBF+AER) alone could not correct a wrong obstacle belief without an explicit estimator, underscoring the necessity of integrated uncertainty estimation.
- The superior performance of "Full" over "AC+CBF+UE" in belief RMSE and goal success (though with limited statistical significance in paired tests) suggests that adaptive replay, when combined with uncertainty estimation, further refines the learning process by providing more relevant data.

#### 5.3. Potential Impact

This work contributes a principled architectural template for designing robust and safe autonomous systems operating in uncertain environments. The emphasis on co-design provides a framework for researchers and practitioners to consider the interplay of learning, safety, replay, and uncertainty estimation more systematically, potentially leading to more reliable and efficient RL-based controllers in safety-critical domains.

#### 5.4. Limitations and Open Challenges

The paper acknowledges several limitations and areas for future work:
- **Theoretical Complexity:** Combining adaptive replay, recurrent uncertainty estimation, and safety filtering significantly complicates closed-loop analysis, requiring advanced theoretical tools for stability and convergence guarantees.
- **Computational Burden:** Each integrated module introduces overhead. The practical trade-off between increased complexity and gains in safety, robustness, and sample efficiency needs further investigation, potentially through techniques like clustered replay and asynchronous updates.
- **Replay Prioritization Sensitivity:** The selection of weights and scales for mixed-priority replay is application-dependent. Over- or under-emphasis on certain transition types could bias learning.
- **Distribution Shift and Stale Data:** Replayed samples may reflect outdated operating regimes in nonstationary environments, necessitating robust uncertainty models and hybrid offline-online strategies.
- **Evaluation Protocols:** Comprehensive evaluation requires multi-metric assessment beyond just reward, including constraint violations, safety margins, estimator residuals, replay diversity, and computational load.
- **Experimental Scope:** The validation is based on a 2D simulation with lightweight function approximators, static physical obstacles, five seeds, no independent validation set, and a synthetic extreme detector model. The actor update is a biased off-policy surrogate. These limitations restrict the generalizability of the results to the specific benchmark and motivate further studies involving hardware, moving obstacles, calibrated sensing, and formal multi-time-scale analysis.

In conclusion, this paper advocates for a unified perspective in safe actor-critic optimal control, providing a concrete architectural blueprint and empirical evidence from a challenging simulation benchmark to support the benefits of co-designing safety filters, adaptive replay, and online uncertainty estimation.
