# CIDER: Continual Interactive Distillation for Embodied Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> CIDER is a continual reinforcement learning framework for real-world robot manipulation. Before each new task, it freezes the accumulated policy as a teacher, then interleaves human-in-the-loop online RL on the current task with reverse-KL distillation on cached teacher actions over archived past-task states, plus asymmetric adapter gradient routing. A single shared actor retains all six real-robot tasks at 100% success while learning each in 10-20 minutes.

## Problem

- Human-in-the-loop real-world RL solves single manipulation tasks in minutes, but sequential fine-tuning overwrites action distributions needed for prior tasks, causing catastrophic forgetting.
- Replay, parameter-regularization like EWC, and gradient projection constrain weights or values indirectly, never the old action distribution, and shrink update subspaces as tasks accumulate.
- Post-hoc multi-expert consolidation requires all independently trained experts simultaneously, blocking incremental deployment, and merges misaligned task-specific representations that interfere.

## Method

- ==Interactive Distillation==: at each stage the previous consolidated actor is frozen as teacher, and ==HIL-RL== updates on the current buffer alternate one-to-one with distillation updates on archived past-task states.
- Retention objective uses ==reverse KL== between student and frozen-teacher ==diagonal Gaussian action distributions==, with ==task-balanced minibatch sampling== and teacher outputs cached once per stage; critics see only current-task data.
- ==Adapter gradient routing==: current-task actor gradients are blocked from the shared visual adapter while historical distillation gradients update both adapter and action head, via separate Adam optimizers.

## Results

- Across six real tasks CIDER holds **100%** stage-average success through stage 6 (**100 trials**), while DisCoRL falls to **51.3%**, Finetune+Replay **26.2%**, EWC **16.7%**, Sequential BC **16.7%**.
- Ablations: task-balanced sampling gives **100%** versus **78.0%** dataset-proportional; MSE distillation collapses retention to **17.0%** on socket insertion while KL and NLL objectives all hold **100%**.
- Update ratio **1:1** gives **100%** retention at **19.85 min** per task versus **34.86 min** at ratio 0.5 and **97.0%** at ratio 2; task-order permutations stay at **96.2-98.4%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Constrain behavior, not parameters: distilling the teacher's action distribution on stored past states preserves skills where weight-importance penalties and replay both fail.
> - Asymmetric gradient routing matters as much as the loss: letting only the retention stream touch the shared visual representation keeps the actor-critic target stationary.
> - Incremental consolidation beats one-shot merging because each teacher is already multi-task, so representations stay aligned instead of conflicting across independently trained experts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Real-world reinforcement learning (RL) has advanced the capability of robots to acquire policies for complex manipulation tasks. However, a significant challenge in deploying such systems is the accumulation of multiple skills over time. Current high-performance single-task RL methods often do not readily extend to scenarios where a robot must learn new tasks while retaining proficiency in previously acquired ones. This challenge falls under the domain of continual reinforcement learning (CRL), where the agent must balance "plasticity" (the ability to acquire new knowledge) with "stability" (the ability to retain old knowledge).

A primary issue in CRL is catastrophic forgetting, where the process of learning a new task causes the policy to overwrite or degrade previously learned behaviors. This is particularly problematic in real-world robotics, where recovering lost skills can incur substantial costs in terms of robot interaction time, data collection, and retraining. Existing approaches to mitigate catastrophic forgetting include:

- **Replay-based methods:** Storing and re-using past experiences alongside new ones.
- **Regularization techniques (e.g., EWC, SI, MAS):** Imposing penalties on parameter updates that are deemed important for previous tasks.
- **Gradient projection methods (e.g., OGD, GPM):** Restricting gradient updates to reduce interference with past knowledge.
- **Structural isolation methods (e.g., PackNet, Progress & Compress, HAT):** Allocating or adapting network capacity for new tasks while protecting parameters related to old tasks.
- **Policy distillation:** Transferring knowledge from one or more "teacher" policies (often task-specific experts) to a "student" policy, which then learns to perform multiple tasks.

While these methods address aspects of continual learning, their application in real-world human-in-the-loop (HIL) RL for robotic manipulation presents specific difficulties. Many existing distillation methods either involve training multiple independent experts offline and then consolidating them, or decouple the online learner from a long-term offline actor, which can lead to issues such as misaligned representations or the inability to adapt incrementally.

CIDER distinguishes itself by focusing on a practical framework for real-world HIL-RL that explicitly constrains prior behaviors *during* online learning of new tasks. It builds upon policy distillation but integrates it interactively and continually within the HIL-RL training loop, rather than as a post-hoc or offline consolidation step. This approach aims to provide a mechanism that directly preserves previous-task behavior while maintaining the plasticity necessary for new-task acquisition in real-robot scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a robust and practical framework, termed Continual Interactive Distillation for Embodied Reinforcement Learning (CIDER), that enables a single robotic policy to continually acquire new manipulation skills in real-world settings without catastrophically forgetting previously learned behaviors.

The detailed motivations and objectives are as follows:

- **Addressing Catastrophic Forgetting in Real-World HIL-RL:** Real-world HIL-RL has shown effectiveness in rapidly acquiring manipulation policies for individual tasks. However, when extending this paradigm to a sequence of tasks, the learned policy often suffers from catastrophic forgetting. The policy updates for a new task overwrite the knowledge required for previous tasks, leading to a loss of proficiency in older skills. This is a critical issue for developing general-purpose, deployable robotic systems.
- **Enabling Continual Skill Accumulation with Limited Prior Interaction:** The goal is for a single policy to incrementally learn new skills as they are encountered, without requiring additional real-world interaction or retraining on previous tasks. This is crucial for efficiency and scalability in real-world deployments where robot interaction time is valuable.
- **Balancing Plasticity and Stability:** A core challenge in continual learning is maintaining a balance between the plasticity needed to learn novel tasks and the stability required to retain existing skills. Existing methods often struggle to achieve this balance effectively, especially in complex, contact-rich real-world robotic tasks. CIDER aims to achieve this balance by interleaving current-task online RL with distillation-based retention.
- **Formalizing a Practical Framework:** The paper aims to formalize CIDER as a practical and viable framework for studying continual real-world robot RL, particularly in scenarios where individual manipulation tasks can be solved reliably with HIL-RL. This involves specifying the algorithmic components, policy architecture, and training protocols necessary for real-robot deployment.
- **Empirical Validation on Diverse Real-World Tasks:** A key objective is to evaluate CIDER on a sequence of diverse real-world manipulation tasks (household and industrial) to demonstrate its efficacy in accumulating skills under realistic conditions. This includes showing that CIDER can maintain high success rates on all previously learned tasks while efficiently acquiring each new task.
- **Understanding Critical Design Choices:** The research also aims to identify and analyze the critical design choices within CIDER that contribute to its performance, particularly concerning the trade-off between stability and plasticity. This involves conducting ablation studies on components such as gradient routing, policy initialization, and distillation parameters.
- **Improving upon Existing Continual Learning Baselines:** The work seeks to demonstrate that CIDER outperforms existing continual learning baselines (e.g., finetuning, replay, regularization, offline RL, post-hoc distillation) in terms of retaining skills across a sequence of real-world tasks.

In summary, the motivation stems from the need for robotic systems that can adapt and expand their capabilities over time in real-world environments, overcoming the fundamental problem of catastrophic forgetting, and providing a practical, empirically validated solution for continual skill acquisition.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CIDER addresses continual learning in real-world visual manipulation by interleaving human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning (HIL-RL) for the current task with policy distillation for previously learned tasks. The methodology can be broken down into several key components:

#### 3.1. Continual Learning Protocol and Stagewise Training

The robot encounters a sequence of `T` physical scenarios, M$_{1:T}$. At each stage `k`, the robot interacts only with the current scenario M$_k$. Once a scenario M$_i$ is completed, no further physical interaction with M$_i$ is assumed, only its historical transition archive B$_i$ may be retained. The goal is to produce a single policy π$_θ$ that performs well on all tasks seen so far.

- **Stage 1 (First Task):** For M$_1$, the policy is trained using a standard HIL-RL procedure. This involves an off-policy actor-critic learner (RLPD, a SAC-style algorithm) with a replay buffer D$_1$ containing autonomous robot transitions, demonstrations, and human corrections. After M$_1$ reaches its success criterion, a transition archive B$_1$ is stored, and the resulting actor π$_θ^1$ is frozen as the first teacher policy π$_T^1$.
- **Subsequent Stages (k > 1):**
 - **Initialization:** Training for M$_k$ begins by initializing the current policy π$_θ$ from the previously consolidated actor (π$_θ$ ← π$_θ^{k-1}$). The previous actor is then frozen as the teacher policy (π$_T^{k-1}$ = stopgrad(π$_θ^{k-1}$)).
 - **Replay Buffer Reset:** The current replay buffer D$_k$ is reset, and new transitions are collected exclusively from the current scenario M$_k$.
 - **Interleaved Updates:** Training alternates between two types of updates:
 - **HIL-RL Updates:** `u_RL` updates applied to the current task's data D$_k$. These updates optimize the critic and actor using the SAC-style actor-critic loss (L$_Q$, L$_{RL}$), preserving plasticity and reward-driven improvement for the new task.
 - **Historical Distillation Updates:** `u_ID` updates applied using states extracted from historical transition archives B$_{<k}$. These updates constrain the current policy to preserve previous behaviors.
 - **Consolidation:** After M$_k$ is solved, the resulting policy π$_θ^k$ is frozen as the new consolidated teacher, and its transition archive B$_k$ is stored.

#### 3.2. Policy Distillation for Retention

Policy distillation is used to align the current student policy (π$_θ$) with the frozen teacher policy (π$_T$) on states from previously learned tasks.

- **Distillation Objective:** A reverse KL divergence (D$_{KL}$) is used to match the current actor's action distribution to the teacher's on states from past tasks:
 L$_{k}^{distill}$(θ) = (1/(k-1)) Σ$_{i=1}^{k-1}$ E$_{(s,a,r,s')∼B_i}$ [D$_{KL}$ (π$_θ$(·|s) || π$_T^{k-1}$(·|s))]
 This loss focuses on constraining the induced action distribution rather than direct parameter matching.
- **Teacher Caching:** Before stage `k`, the frozen teacher π$_T^{k-1}$ is evaluated once on all states from historical archives B$_{<k}$, and its Gaussian action distributions (mean μ$_T$(s), standard deviation σ$_T$(s)) are cached as C$_{<k}^{k-1}$. This avoids redundant computations.
- **Task-Balanced Sampling:** During distillation updates, minibatches are sampled in a task-balanced manner. First, a historical task `i` is uniformly sampled from `{1, ..., k-1}`, and then a transition `(s, a, r, s')` is uniformly sampled from `B_i`. Only the state component `s` is used for the distillation loss, preventing any single large archive from dominating.
- **Critic Training:** The critic is exclusively trained on the current task's data D$_k$, without using historical transitions for Bellman backups.

#### 3.3. Policy Architecture and Gradient Routing

- **Visuomotor Policy:** The policy uses two RGB images (from head and wrist cameras) as input. Each image is processed by a ResNet-10 backbone (ImageNet pre-trained), followed by a lightweight visual adapter.
- **Visual Adapter:** The adapter consists of Spatial Learned Embedding (8 learnable spatial masks) and a linear projection, yielding a 256-dimensional vector per viewpoint. These are concatenated to form a 512-dimensional joint visual representation `z`, which is shared by the actor and critic heads.
- **Actor and Critic Heads:** The critic head concatenates `z` with the action and outputs two Q-values. The actor head maps `z` through an MLP to predict the mean and log standard deviation for a diagonal Gaussian policy.
- **Gradient Routing (Critical Component):** A specific gradient routing strategy is implemented for the visual adapter:
 - Current-task actor gradients (from L$_{RL}$) are **blocked** from updating the visual adapter.
 - Historical distillation gradients (from L$_{k}^{distill}$) **update both** the adapter and the actor head.
 - The critic's gradients update the adapter.
 - Separate Adam optimizers are used for different gradient streams.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup and Baselines

- **Real-Robot Task Suite:** Six manipulation tasks (household and industrial: socket insertion, toothbrush hanging, phone-box closing, industrial connectors plugging, safety hook coupling, chip-tray placement) requiring visually guided, contact-rich manipulation. Tasks share the same robot platform and action space.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Success rate (S$_{i,k}$) on each task M$_i$ after stage `k`, measured over five independent sets of 20 real-robot trials. Average success A$_k$ over seen tasks and final average success A$_T$ are reported.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Finetune:** Simple sequential fine-tuning on new tasks, discarding old data.
 - **Finetune + Replay:** Finetune with historical transitions retained in the replay buffer.
 - **Finetune + EWC:** Finetune augmented with Elastic Weight Consolidation regularization.
 - **Sequential BC:** Offline fine-tuning using Behavior Cloning on the new task's demonstration data.
 - **Sequential Offline RL:** Offline fine-tuning using an offline RL algorithm (TD3+BC) on all new task transitions.
 - **DisCoRL:** Offline distillation of multiple independently trained per-task experts into a single student policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate CIDER's ability to accumulate skills, provide forward transfer, and understand its critical components.

#### 4.1. Continual Skill Accumulation (Q1)

- **CIDER's Performance:** CIDER demonstrated robust continual skill accumulation. It was the only evaluated method that successfully preserved all six manipulation tasks throughout the sequential training process. The policy obtained after each stage `k` achieved 100% success on all tasks seen up to that point, resulting in a final average success rate of 100% across the 100-trial evaluations for each task. This indicates that CIDER effectively prevents catastrophic forgetting.
- **Baseline Failures:**
 - **Finetune:** Catastrophically forgot previous skills after training on each new task, with the average success rate dropping to 16.7% by Stage 6.
 - **Finetune + Replay:** Showed slight improvement in retention compared to Finetune but still failed to preserve previous skills comprehensively, reaching 26.2% average success by Stage 6.
 - **Finetune + EWC:** Preserving important parameters through regularization was insufficient to prevent performance degradation, resulting in an average success rate of 16.7% by Stage 6.
 - **Sequential BC and Sequential Offline RL:** Both offline update strategies also failed to maintain previously learned tasks, with average success rates of 16.7% and 16.2% respectively by Stage 6, indicating that offline updates or weight anchoring alone were insufficient.
 - **DisCoRL:** While starting strong due to initial independent expert training, its performance dropped significantly over the task sequence, declining from 100% at Stage 3 to 51.3% by Stage 6. This suggests that the post-hoc merging of independently trained experts suffered from interference between task-specific representations.

#### 4.2. Forward Transfer (Q2)

- **New Task Acquisition Efficiency:** CIDER demonstrated efficient acquisition of new tasks. For each new task, CIDER, starting from the previously consolidated policy, required comparable or fewer real-robot training steps than learning each task independently from scratch (single-task baseline). Each new task was acquired within approximately 10 to 20 minutes of real-robot interaction.
- **No Hindrance to Learning:** The results indicate that the consolidated policy served as a useful warm start, modestly accelerating learning on some tasks and not hindering acquisition on others, despite variations in objects, contact dynamics, and success conditions.

#### 4.3. Ablations: Understanding CIDER (Q3)

Ablation studies provided insights into the critical design choices within CIDER:

- **Adapter Gradient Routing:**
 - The default asymmetric routing (current-task actor gradients blocked from adapter, historical distillation gradients updating both adapter and actor head) was found to be critical.
 - Allowing both actor and distillation gradients to update the adapter prevented convergence, likely due to making the shared actor-critic representation nonstationary.
 - Freezing the adapter for both gradient streams led to increasing distillation loss, fewer retained tasks (fewer than four), and longer current-task learning times. This highlights the importance of adapter updates from distillation for retention.
- **Continual Initialization versus From-Scratch Learning:**
 - Initializing the student policy from the previously consolidated policy ("Continue" scheme) consistently maintained lower historical distillation loss on prior tasks.
 - The "Continue" scheme achieved higher success rates on previously learned tasks compared to reinitializing from scratch ("From scratch" scheme), which showed significant performance drops on prior tasks (e.g., Phone-Box Closing dropped from 100% to 79%).
 - These findings suggest that the incrementally evolving, consolidated policy provides a more stable foundation for continuous skill accumulation.
- **Distillation Update Ratio (Appendix E):** An equal ratio of RL updates to distillation updates (n=1) provided the optimal balance. More frequent distillation (n=0.5) slowed new-task acquisition, while less frequent distillation (n=2) led to slight forgetting.
- **Historical Task Sampling (Appendix E):** Task-balanced sampling, where each previous task contributes equally to the distillation objective, consistently outperformed dataset-proportional sampling, which could lead to under-rehearsal of tasks with smaller data archives.
- **Task Order Robustness (Appendix E):** CIDER's performance showed only minor variations across different task permutations, indicating a relative insensitivity to the order in which tasks are presented.
- **Distillation Loss Type (Appendix E):** Probabilistic objectives like NLL, forward KL, and reverse KL performed similarly and effectively. In contrast, Mean Squared Error (MSE) for action matching significantly underperformed in retention, highlighting the importance of using a loss function consistent with the policy's probabilistic nature.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CIDER framework represents a notable contribution to the field of continual reinforcement learning, particularly for real-world robotic manipulation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Effective Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting in Real-World RL:** CIDER demonstrates a practical and empirically effective solution to the long-standing problem of catastrophic forgetting in continual learning. By uniquely interleaving online HIL-RL with distillation from a frozen, accumulated teacher, it enables a single policy to retain 100% proficiency in previously learned skills while acquiring new ones. This is a critical step towards robots that can learn and adapt over long periods without needing to be re-trained for their entire skill set.
- **Enabling Adaptive and General-Purpose Robotic Systems:** The ability for a robot to continually accumulate diverse manipulation skills (spanning household and industrial tasks) within a single, shared policy is essential for creating more autonomous and versatile robotic systems. Such systems could be incrementally deployed and updated with new capabilities as requirements change, reducing reliance on costly and time-consuming re-development cycles.
- **Efficient Skill Acquisition in Real-World Contexts:** CIDER facilitates rapid acquisition of new skills, typically within 10-20 minutes of real-robot training per task. This efficiency, combined with the retention of previous skills without additional interaction, represents a significant practical advantage for real-world robotics where robot uptime and human intervention are valuable resources. The positive forward transfer observed also suggests that accumulated knowledge can accelerate learning.
- **Actionable Insights into CRL Design:** The detailed ablation studies provide valuable insights into the architectural and algorithmic components crucial for balancing plasticity and stability in continual real-world RL. Specifically, the findings on asymmetric gradient routing to the visual adapter, the benefits of continual initialization, and the effectiveness of task-balanced sampling offer guidance for future research and development in this area.
- **Advancement of Policy Distillation for Continual Learning:** CIDER refines the application of policy distillation in CRL by integrating it interactively within the online learning process, rather than relying on post-hoc or purely offline consolidation. This interactive approach helps maintain a coherent policy representation that evolves incrementally, avoiding the representational misalignment issues observed in some multi-expert merging methods.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** While demonstrating strong performance on short-horizon manipulation tasks, the paper also outlines limitations, such as the focus on short-horizon tasks and the capacity of the fixed actor. These limitations open avenues for future research into scaling CIDER to longer-horizon tasks with sparse rewards, more complex environments, larger numbers of tasks, and potential integrations with structural expansion methods for policy capacity.

In conclusion, CIDER provides a robust, efficient, and well-analyzed framework that pushes the boundaries of continual learning for real-world robotic manipulation, moving closer to robots that can genuinely learn and grow their capabilities throughout their operational lifetime.
