# Self-Organizing Agent Teams Learn to Reason Together

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-Organizing Agent Teams (SAT) are fixed teams of different LLMs that learn reusable teamwork strategies. A strategy sets roles, conversation phases, who takes part and how information flows. The team's strongest member finds strategies by reflective evolutionary search on 15-25 training problems. A frozen bank of ten strategies writes checkable certificates, and one member judges them. On five math and physics benchmarks SAT averages 66.7%, beating its strongest member (48.8%) and a perfect routing oracle (59.0%).

## Problem

- Debate and Mixture of Agents combine answers that members wrote on their own. Voting over the first answers recovers most debate gains, and extra rounds can drown out a correct minority.
- Workflow search, learned routing and topology optimization assume useful subtasks can be defined in advance. That fails when no member has a full solution and the right split is unknown.
- Prior multi-agent work only compares against the best average member. That cannot show whether interaction creates correct solutions or just picks among answers members already had.

## Method

- **Teamwork-strategy language**: a shared team prompt, fixed per-agent roles, and ordered conversation phases. Each phase sets who takes part, how many rounds, local or summary-broadcast information flow, and prompts.
- **Teamwork reflection**: the strongest member runs ==evolutionary search== over per-problem archives, reading transcripts and outcomes to change roles, phases and synthesis rules. A leakage audit and five validation probes reward transfer.
- **Frozen strategy bank** plus certificate judge: greedy coverage-based selection keeps ten complementary strategies. At test time each strategy writes a checkable certificate, and one member picks the answer by naming specific local errors.

## Results

- The math-physics team (o3-mini, Claude Sonnet 4, DeepSeek-V3) averages **66.7%** on five benchmarks. Comparisons: best member **48.8%**, compute-matched linearization **58.7%**, Mixture of Agents **57.3%**, three copies of o3-mini **56.0%**.
- The AIME-trained bank transfers unchanged, reaching **71.2%** on AIME 2026, **13.4 points** above the routing oracle. Pool coverage (**75.2%** vs **59.0%**) beats the oracle on all five benchmarks, but HMMT ties linearization at **39.4%**.
- The knowledge-logic team averages **72.8%**, the best of all methods, but stays below the **79.6%** oracle despite **87.9%** pool coverage. Demonstrability tracks the gain over the best member (Spearman rho **0.90**, p **0.005**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Beating the strongest member is too weak a test. Compare against a perfect router over members' own answers to show interaction creates correct solutions, not just selects them.
> - Selection limits what collaboration can gain. Gains show up where correct reasoning is easy to recognize once written, so invest in readable certificates and verifiers, not just more generation.
> - A team of different models is an asset worth exploiting. The same learned strategies run by copies of one model score lower on average, and reflection can turn observed member strengths into roles.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

On an HMMT February 2026 problem, o3-mini, DeepSeek-V3, and Claude Sonnet 4 all initially answer incorrectly. During collaboration, o3-mini contributes the invariant and factorization but miscounts the unit-exponent primes, DeepSeek repairs the count, and Sonnet audits the corrected reasoning—yielding the team certificate 3840, an answer absent from all three initial responses. Ordinary voting or routing can only select or aggregate what agents already produced. SAT instead learns reusable rules for who should challenge, verify, preserve a minority claim, or synthesize, while the new problem determines what the agents actually work on. The authors call this **collaborative computation**: a fixed team learns how to collaborate, not merely how to answer independently.

Three agents each start wrong. o3-mini supplies the invariant and factorization; DeepSeek repairs the count; Sonnet audits the corrected reasoning; the synthesis path reaches 3840, explicitly marked absent from all three initial answers. This is a paper-selected case study, not an average-case guarantee.

### 2. What is learned

A designated team member studies earlier transcripts, answers, outcomes, and validation behavior, then rewrites the team's roles, conversational phases, participation rules, information flow, and synthesis rules. Each source problem receives six mutation rounds with up to three candidate strategies per round. A strategy that solves its source problem is tested on five other training problems as validation probes. A semantic audit excludes answer values, problem facts, and source-specific recipes; a coverage-greedy procedure freezes up to ten complementary strategies. At test time, every frozen strategy produces a candidate certificate, and a designated judge audits certificates for local defects and selects the best-supported final answer.

The math-and-physics strategy bank—o3-mini, Claude Sonnet 4, and DeepSeek-V3—is learned from 15 AIME 2024 training problems and transferred unchanged to held-out AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AIME 2026, HMMT February 2026, and TheoremQA-physics. The strategy does not assign the new problem's subtasks in advance; it creates a conversation in which the problem-specific division of reasoning can emerge, be challenged, repaired, and recombined.

Panel (b) shows three boxes—failure diagnosis, member-specific evidence, and strength → assigned role—ending in a designated final auditor who reviews all attempts and resurfaces overlooked claims. The caption reports that the resulting GPQA auditor strategy solved its source problem and all five sampled validation probes.

### 3. What the measurements establish

Two comparisons test whether SAT's gains come from something beyond selection or a larger single-agent budget.

The routing oracle is the strongest selection-only test: it can choose the correct answer whenever any member independently produced one. On the five-task math-and-physics average, SAT reaches **66.7% accuracy** versus **59.0%** for the routing oracle—a 7.7-point gap supporting the authors' interpretation that interaction sometimes produces correct solutions unavailable to routing. On AIME 2026 alone, SAT reaches **71.2%** while routing-oracle coverage is **57.8%**, a 13.4-point gap.

The compute-matched linearization is the strongest single-agent control: o3-mini serially performs the learned roles and phases at approximately the team's total inference budget. SAT's math-and-physics average is 66.7% versus **58.7%** for linearization and **48.8%** for the best independent member. Replaying the structure with one model does not reproduce the gain; however, the authors correctly note that an advantage over linearization can reflect both interaction and model heterogeneity.

The y-axis shows final-answer accuracy; the x-axis lists AIME24, AIME25, AIME26, HMMT26, TQA-phys, and Average. SAT is the tallest final-accuracy bar in every displayed benchmark. A black horizontal rule marks routing-oracle coverage. Blue annotations show SAT's absolute improvement over the best member: +20.3, +20.8, +29.0, +13.1, +6.1, and +17.9 points. TQA-physics shows a smaller gain than the other benchmarks.

The boundary matters immediately. On knowledge and logic, SAT's certificate pool contains a correct candidate on **87.9%** of problems, but final accuracy falls to **72.8%**—while the routing oracle covers **79.6%**—showing that generating a correct certificate does not guarantee the final judge recognizes it. SAT also trails Mixture of Agents on MMLU-Pro, 82.4% versus 84.3%, and trails the homogeneous team on BBEH, 56.0% versus 58.7%. A post-hoc measure called demonstrability—a ten-model panel external to both deployed rosters choosing between one correct and one incorrect certificate—tracks SAT's improvement over the strongest member across eight benchmarks with Spearman ρ = 0.90 and exact permutation p = 0.005. The authors label this relationship correlational and do not use demonstrability during strategy learning.

### 4. Organization has a condition

Learned organization changes what heterogeneous models can construct when correct reasoning can be recognized and preserved, but richer candidate generation alone does not solve selection: the knowledge-and-logic results show the gap between 87.9% coverage and 72.8% final accuracy persisting after the team generates candidates. The authors propose optimizing for demonstrability as a direct next step; they do not demonstrate that doing so improves accuracy.
