# Adapting Generalist Vehicle Models for High-Speed MPC Across Terrains

> [!summary] Summary
> OptCar introduces a methodology to adapt generalist vehicle dynamics models for specific high-speed off-road autonomous navigation tasks, achieving precise closed-loop control across diverse terrains. The specialized model, utilizing a history-conditioned architecture and targeted fine-tuning, consistently reduced trajectory tracking error, for instance, by approximately 55% compared to fine-tuned generalist models on challenging terrains at 6 m/s, and demonstrated robust adaptation to unseen payloads.

## Problem

- Achieving precise and robust high-speed autonomous control on unstructured terrain is challenging due to complex tire slip dynamics that vary with terrain and payload.
- Existing generalist learned dynamics models lack the per-vehicle accuracy needed for aggressive control, while specialist models overfit to specific conditions and lack cross-terrain transferability.
- Specializing models for new vehicles or terrains typically requires extensive, costly, and potentially unsafe real-world data collection, hindering practical deployment.

## Method

- Introduces **OptCar**, a methodology that specializes a generalist ==Forward Kinodynamic (FKD) model== for a target vehicle using a ==history-conditioned architecture==.
- A ==history-to-context module== infers latent dynamics factors from recent state-action history, which then conditions a rollout map via ==Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)==, enabling online adaptation without explicit terrain classification.
- Employs a ==targeted fine-tuning== procedure, combining limited real-world data with synthetic rollouts generated by system-identified ==dynamic bicycle models==, to effectively cover high-slip regimes.

## Results

- **OptCar FT-RS** consistently achieved the lowest trajectory tracking error (**TTE**) across diverse terrains and speeds, reducing TTE by approximately **55%** on challenging terrains like vegetation + dirt at **6 m/s** compared to fine-tuned generalist models.
- The system demonstrated robust adaptation to out-of-distribution dynamics, achieving the **lowest tracking error** when pulling an unseen trailing cart without specific training data.
- OptCar FT-RS, utilizing only **5 minutes** of real data per terrain, maintained competitive performance on known terrains (within **14%** of a specialist model trained on **30 minutes** of data) while substantially outperforming specialist models on unseen terrains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamical systems in complex environments benefit from inferring latent factors (e.g., terrain, payload) from historical data to adapt predictions online without explicit classification or mode switching.
> - Combining limited real-world data with targeted, system-identified synthetic data effectively addresses the data scarcity problem for specializing models, especially in high-slip or safety-critical regimes.
> - A generalist dynamics model can be effectively specialized for a specific vehicle while retaining robust cross-terrain generalization by conditioning its predictions on an inferred dynamics context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

High-speed autonomous navigation over unstructured terrain represents a significant challenge in robotics, with applications spanning search-and-rescue, agricultural robotics, and off-road racing. The difficulty arises from operating tires near their friction limits, where slip varies considerably with terrain and payload. This necessitates control systems that are both precise for a target vehicle and robust to changing environmental conditions.

Existing approaches to vehicle dynamics modeling and control can be broadly categorized. Analytical models, such as the dynamic bicycle model, offer computational efficiency and interpretability but typically fail to accurately represent vehicle behavior in high-slip regimes. Conversely, learned models can capture complex high-slip dynamics but often require extensive data, are computationally intensive, or overfit to specific conditions.

Within learned models, Forward Kinodynamic (FKD) models predict future states given a sequence of actions, making them suitable as rollout models for sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC), such as MPPI. Generalist FKD models, exemplified by approaches like AnyCar, aim for broad transferability across various vehicles and environments. However, this generality often comes at the cost of the per-vehicle accuracy required for aggressive, high-speed control. In contrast, specialist models are extensively trained on a single vehicle and terrain, achieving high performance in that specific context but lacking transferability to other environments. Inverse Kinodynamic (IKD) methods offer online adaptation to current terrain using an explicit history component but typically depend on a separate planner to generate desired trajectories.

The research presented here addresses a recognized gap in this landscape: the need for a method to specialize a generalist FKD model to a specific vehicle while retaining robustness and performance across diverse and changing terrains. This work seeks to combine the broad applicability of generalist models with the precision of specialist models, particularly in data-efficient manner that avoids extensive real-world data collection for every new setting. The central problem is to bridge the performance gap between generalist and specialist models without sacrificing cross-terrain generalization, a balance crucial for practical deployment in dynamic, real-world off-road scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a methodology, termed OptCar, for adapting generalist Forward Kinodynamic (FKD) models to a specific target vehicle. This adaptation aims to achieve precise closed-loop control in high-speed off-road autonomy tasks, ensuring robustness across diverse and changing terrains, particularly where tire slip significantly impacts vehicle dynamics.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several challenges inherent in high-speed off-road autonomous driving:

1. **Precision and Robustness in High-Slip Regimes:** At high speeds, vehicle tires often operate near their friction limits, leading to varied and complex slip dynamics influenced by terrain and payload. Existing analytical models often fail in these high-slip conditions. Controllers therefore require models that can accurately predict behavior in these regimes while remaining robust as conditions change.
2. **Limitations of Existing Model Types:**
 - **Generalist FKD Models (e.g., AnyCar):** While offering broad generalization across different vehicles and environments, these models often lack the specific accuracy required for aggressive, high-speed control on a particular platform.
 - **Specialist Models:** These models achieve high performance by extensively training on a single vehicle and terrain. However, they typically exhibit poor transferability when the terrain changes, necessitating re-training or significant data collection for each new environment.
 - **Inverse Kinodynamic (IKD) Methods:** While providing online adaptation, IKD approaches generally rely on a separate, potentially less robust, planner to generate reference trajectories.
3. **Data Efficiency for Specialization:** Effectively specializing a generalist model to a target vehicle often requires substantial amounts of real-world data. Acquiring this data across various terrains, especially in high-slip or challenging conditions, can be time-consuming, expensive, and potentially unsafe. There is a need for methods that can achieve specialization with limited real-world data.
4. **Real-Time Computational Constraints:** For deployment in real-time closed-loop Model Predictive Control (MPC) on embedded hardware, the dynamics model must be computationally efficient. This means accuracy must be achieved under a tight fidelity-latency budget, rather than through increasing model size indefinitely.

To address these motivations, OptCar sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

- **Develop a History-Conditioned Architecture:** Introduce a mechanism for the FKD model to infer and adapt to latent dynamics factors (like terrain friction or payload) from recent state-action history, enabling online adaptation without explicit terrain classification or mode switching.
- **Design a Data-Efficient Fine-Tuning Recipe:** Create a methodology for specializing the generalist model to a target vehicle using limited real-world data, augmented by synthetic data that specifically targets high-slip regimes. This addresses the data sparsity problem in challenging driving conditions.
- **Validate Cross-Terrain Generalization and Performance:** Demonstrate that the specialized model can maintain high accuracy and robustness across diverse terrains and under out-of-distribution dynamics (e.g., an unseen payload), outperforming both generalist and single-terrain specialist baselines.
- **Ensure Compatibility with Real-Time MPC:** Integrate the specialized FKD model into a sampling-based MPC framework (MPPI) and validate its performance in closed-loop control on actual hardware, confirming its operational feasibility.

By achieving these objectives, OptCar aims to provide a practical and efficient solution for deploying high-performance autonomous navigation systems on specific vehicles operating in complex, varying off-road environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OptCar is presented as a methodology for specializing a pretrained generalist FKD model to a specific target vehicle while maintaining adaptability across various terrains. The approach is structured around two primary contributions: a history-conditioned dynamics adaptation module and a targeted real-and-synthetic fine-tuning procedure.

#### 3.1. History-Conditioned FKD Prediction

The core idea is that latent factors, such as terrain friction, slip regime, payload, and actuator response, significantly influence vehicle motion but are not directly observed by the controller. To account for these, OptCar models the prediction process in two stages:

1. **History-to-Context Map ($g_\phi$):** This module processes a history of recent states ($s_{t-M:t-1}$) and applied actions ($a_{t-M:t-1}$) to infer a dynamics context vector ($c_t$). This vector is designed to compress relevant information about the current vehicle-terrain interaction. The context $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is learned end-to-end without direct supervision from explicit terrain or regime labels. It acts as a bottleneck, ensuring all history-dependent information passed to the rollout model is channeled through it.
2. **Context-Conditioned Rollout Map ($f_\theta$):** Given the current state ($s_t$), a candidate future action sequence ($a_{t:t+N-1}$), and the inferred context vector ($c_t$), this module predicts the resulting future state trajectory increments ($\Delta\hat{s}_{t+1:t+N}$). The conditioning of $f_\theta$ on $c_t$ is implemented using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) at every block of the rollout decoder. This ensures that the inferred dynamics context modulates all candidate future rollouts evaluated by MPC. The context $c_t$ is computed once per planning step and reused across all candidate action sequences. The decoder predicts body-frame pose increments rather than global poses, which helps in local representation and supports rollouts over non-planar terrains.

#### 3.2. Pretraining as a Generalist FKD Prior

Before specialization, the OptCar model is pretrained to establish a broad understanding of vehicle dynamics. This pretraining phase involves jointly training both the history-to-context map ($g_\phi$) and the context-conditioned rollout map ($f_\theta$) from random initialization. The training dataset consists of approximately 2 million simulated trajectories collected using the AnyCar protocol, encompassing diverse vehicles, terrains, and driving regimes from various simulators (Assetto Corsa, MuJoCo, Isaac Lab, and randomized analytical dynamic bicycle models). The objective function used for training combines position Mean Squared Error (MSE) with a geodesic quaternion loss, aiming to accurately predict body-frame future increment sequences.

#### 3.3. Targeted Real-and-Synthetic Specialization

To bridge the gap from a generalist prior to a specialist for a target vehicle, OptCar employs a fine-tuning recipe that addresses the trade-off between data relevance and coverage.

1. **Real Data Collection:** Limited real target-vehicle trajectories ($D_e^{real}$) are collected for each terrain ($e \in E$) that the vehicle is expected to operate on. The paper states approximately 5 minutes of real driving data per terrain (road, gravel, grass, mulch) were used, resulting in about 60,000 history-future windows.
2. **System Identification and Synthetic Data Generation:** For each terrain, the collected real data ($D_e^{real}$) is used to calibrate the parameters ($\psi_e$) of a dynamic bicycle model (DBM) ($F_{\psi_e}$). This is achieved by minimizing the one-step prediction error of the DBM against the real-world transitions. The calibrated DBM, $F_{\psi_e^*}$, is not used as the controller's rollout model directly. Instead, it serves as an offline data generator. It produces targeted synthetic rollouts ($D_e^{synth}$) under randomized action sequences (e.g., Bézier velocity and random-walk steering commands). This strategy allows for augmenting the scarce real data with samples from aggressive, high-slip regimes that are difficult or unsafe to collect extensively in the real world, while ensuring the synthetic data remains anchored to the identified dynamics of the target vehicle on that specific terrain.
3. **Fine-Tuning:** The pretrained OptCar model is then fine-tuned end-to-end on a combined dataset ($D_{FT} = \bigcup_{e \in E} (D_e^{real} \cup D_e^{synth})$). The same prediction loss function as pretraining is used. This process integrates the generalist knowledge with target-vehicle specific and terrain-specific dynamics.

#### 3.4. Deployment in Sampling-Based MPC

The fine-tuned OptCar model is deployed as the rollout function within an MPPI (Model Predictive Path Integral) controller. At each control step:

- The recent history window is encoded by $g_\phi$ into the context vector $c_t$.
- This $c_t$ is then passed to $f_\theta$ to condition the rollouts of many (e.g., 600) candidate action sequences.
- Each rollout's cost is evaluated using a cost function that considers trajectory tracking error (longitudinal and lateral), yaw deviation, and velocity deviation.
- The first action of the lowest-cost sequence is then applied to the vehicle.

The online adaptation occurs solely through the forward pass computation of $c_t$, requiring no terrain classifier, mode switching, or online parameter updates, which contributes to its real-time feasibility. The system is implemented in PyTorch and CuPy and runs on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate OptCar's performance in closed-loop trajectory tracking, addressing three primary research questions (RQ1, RQ2, RQ3). The evaluation used a 1/5-scale off-road vehicle across various terrains and speeds, measuring Trajectory Tracking Error (TTE) and Velocity Tracking Error (VTE).

#### 4.1. RQ1: Specialized Generalist versus Baselines

This question assessed OptCar's ability to match or exceed generalist and specialist models in cross-terrain tracking and out-of-distribution dynamics.

- **Figure-8 Trajectory Tracking (Table 1):**
 - **Low Speed (2 m/s):** All methods performed comparably, as the vehicle operates near its nominal regime with minimal slip.
 - **Medium Speed (4 m/s):** On road, where traction is high, performance remained close. However, on lower-traction terrains (wet grass + slope, vegetation + dirt), performance differences began to emerge.
 - **High Speed (6 m/s):** This regime, dominated by slip, showed the clearest differentiation. OptCar FT-RS (fine-tuned on real and synthetic data) consistently achieved the lowest TTE across all terrains. For instance, on vegetation + dirt, its TTE was approximately 55% lower than that of AnyCar FT-R (fine-tuned on real data only). The Inverse Kinodynamic (IKD) baseline, IKD FT-R, exhibited significant degradation under high slip, attributed to its reliance on a separate DBM-MPPI planner for state generation.
- **Out-of-Distribution Cart Pulling (Table 1, Figure 5):**
 - In a task involving an unseen trailing cart payload, which alters the vehicle's effective inertia and tire loading without any cart-specific training data, OptCar FT-RS demonstrated the lowest tracking error across all speeds. The most substantial performance margin was observed at 6 m/s. This result suggests that the history context encoder effectively infers unseen dynamics shifts from recent motion.
- **Comparison Against a Specialist Model (Table 1):**
 - An OptCar Specialist model, trained solely on 30 minutes of real road data, achieved the best 6 m/s tracking performance on the road. OptCar FT-RS, despite using only 5 minutes of real data per terrain, remained competitive on the road, with its 6 m/s TTE being within 14% of the specialist.
 - However, when confronted with an unseen terrain (wet grass + slope), the OptCar Specialist model's accuracy degraded substantially, performing considerably worse than OptCar FT-RS. This highlights that while single-terrain specialization can achieve high performance in-distribution, multi-terrain specialization (as in OptCar FT-RS) provides robust accuracy when the terrain varies.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Targeted Real-and-Synthetic Fine-Tuning

This research question investigated the contribution of augmenting real data with targeted synthetic rollouts.

- By comparing OptCar FT-R (fine-tuned on real data only) against OptCar FT-RS (fine-tuned on real and targeted synthetic data) in Table 1, it was observed that OptCar FT-RS consistently outperformed OptCar FT-R at 6 m/s across all terrains. This difference was most pronounced in conditions where aggressive slip significantly contributed to closed-loop error.
- In contrast, in moderate driving regimes, the performance of OptCar FT-R remained competitive. This supports the efficacy of the targeted real-and-synthetic fine-tuning recipe: real data provides grounding to the target vehicle, while the system-identified synthetic data effectively extends coverage into high-slip state-action regions that are challenging to acquire safely through real-world collection.

#### 4.3. RQ3: History Context Encoding

This question focused on isolating the effect of the history context encoder.

- Comparing AnyCar FT-R (a generalist backbone fine-tuned on real data) against OptCar FT-R (the same generalist backbone with the history context encoder and FiLM-conditioned decoder, fine-tuned on the same real data) in Table 1, OptCar FT-R demonstrated performance improvements.
- The gains were most notable at 6 m/s and on lower-traction terrains, where recent state-action transitions are most informative for inferring the active slip regime.
- The success of OptCar FT-RS in the zero-shot cart-pulling task further reinforced this finding, indicating that the history encoder facilitates adaptation to unforeseen payload changes without specific training data for that condition.
- An analysis of the learned context vectors (Appendix E), using PCA and LDA projections, indicated that these vectors organize recent history into interpretable dynamics factors such as vehicle type, terrain characteristics, speed, and turning direction, suggesting an effective encoding of relevant dynamic states.

In summary, OptCar FT-RS consistently demonstrated superior trajectory tracking accuracy, especially at high speeds and on challenging terrains, outperforming both fine-tuned generalist baselines and showcasing robust adaptation to unseen dynamics. The contributions of both history-conditioning and targeted real-and-synthetic data were individually validated as crucial to this performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents OptCar as a method for specializing a generalist Forward Kinodynamic (FKD) foundation model for a specific vehicle, yielding a model that maintains accuracy during real-time Model Predictive Control (MPC) across varying terrains. The findings contribute to the field of autonomous navigation in several ways:

1. **Bridging Generalist and Specialist Models:** OptCar demonstrates that it is feasible to develop a single dynamics model that is simultaneously specialized for a specific vehicle and robust across diverse terrains. By starting with a generalist prior and conditioning it on recent motion, the model achieves accuracy comparable to or exceeding single-terrain specialists on their home terrain, while substantially outperforming them when the terrain changes. This addresses a critical trade-off in learned dynamics models, enabling both precision and generalization.
2. **Online Adaptation through History-Conditioning:** The introduction of a history-conditioned FKD architecture provides an effective mechanism for online adaptation to latent dynamics. This adaptation occurs in a feedforward manner via a dynamics context vector, eliminating the need for explicit terrain classifiers, mode switching, or computationally intensive online parameter updates. The observed robustness in the unseen cart-pulling task highlights the ability of this mechanism to infer and adapt to unexpected changes in vehicle dynamics, which has implications for real-world scenarios involving payload variations or unforeseen environmental shifts.
3. **Data-Efficient Specialization via Targeted Sim-to-Real:** The proposed fine-tuning recipe, which combines limited real-world data with targeted synthetic rollouts generated from per-terrain system identification, addresses the challenge of data scarcity. This approach effectively resolves the coverage-relevance trade-off: real data anchors the model to the target vehicle, while synthetic data fills critical high-slip state-action regions that are difficult or unsafe to collect extensively in the real world. This methodology significantly reduces the real-world data collection burden for deploying advanced autonomy on new platforms or in new environments.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Enhanced High-Speed Off-Road Autonomy:** OptCar's ability to achieve substantial reductions in tracking error (e.g., roughly halving 6 m/s tracking error relative to a fine-tuned generalist) signifies a step towards more reliable and aggressive high-speed autonomous operation in unstructured environments. This directly benefits applications such as search-and-rescue, defense, agriculture, and off-road exploration.
- **Practical Deployment of Foundation Models:** The methodology offers a practical pathway for deploying complex foundation dynamics models on specific robotic platforms without requiring prohibitively large, per-terrain real-world datasets. This efficiency in specialization can accelerate the development and deployment cycles for autonomous systems.
- **Reduced Development Costs and Risks:** By leveraging limited real data and targeted synthetic generation, OptCar can reduce the costs and safety risks associated with extensive real-world data collection, particularly in hazardous or high-performance driving regimes.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The paper also acknowledges several limitations that suggest avenues for future research:

- **Feedforward Adaptation Scope:** OptCar's adaptation is purely feedforward, relying on its pre-trained and fine-tuned distribution. For genuinely novel dynamics beyond this distribution, combining the fast feedforward context with slower online weight updates (as in meta-learning) could enhance robustness.
- **Real-time MPC Constraints:** The tight real-time constraints of MPPI on embedded hardware limit the rollout model's complexity and horizon (e.g., N=50). Future work could explore the trade-offs between model capacity, rollout horizon, and sample budget to optimize performance under these constraints.
- **Proprioceptive vs. Exteroceptive Inputs:** Currently, OptCar adapts solely from proprioceptive history. Integrating exteroceptive inputs, such as LiDAR point clouds or RGB images, could provide richer context and enable anticipatory adaptation to terrain changes before they manifest in vehicle motion.
