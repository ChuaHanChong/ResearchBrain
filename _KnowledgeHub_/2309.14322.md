# Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google DeepMind successfully reproduce and investigate large-scale Transformer training instabilities, such as attention logit growth and output logit divergence, using much smaller models. This approach enables the study of these issues without vast computational resources and identifies new insights like the impact of AdamW's epsilon on very large models' training.

## Problem

- Training instabilities (e.g., sudden loss spikes, divergence) in large Transformer models are prohibitively expensive and resource-intensive to investigate, hindering understanding and mitigation.
- These instabilities often manifest uniquely or are more pronounced at very large scales, making it difficult to study them without vast computational resources.
- Existing research primarily focused on performance scaling, leaving a gap in systematic study of stability and failure modes at scale.

## Method

- The authors developed a methodology to reproduce and study large-scale Transformer training instabilities using small, ==decoder-only Transformer models== (2.4M to 1.2B parameters) at high learning rates.
- They introduced =='learning rate sensitivity'== as a quantitative metric to characterize training stability and systematically analyzed the impact of various architectural and optimizer interventions.
- Internal model characteristics, such as ==activation and gradient norms==, were monitored to identify scaling trends and serve as potential predictors for instability.

## Results

- Qk-layernorm successfully mitigated attention logit growth, and z-loss regularization or independent weight decay addressed output logit divergence in small models, mirroring their effectiveness at large scales.
- Longer warm-up periods and independent weight decay consistently reduced learning rate sensitivity, improving training robustness across model scales.
- Extrapolating maximum attention logits from smaller models accurately predicted instability for a **4.8 billion** parameter model at a **1e-2** learning rate, and decreasing AdamW's epsilon to **1e-15** improved training for large models at high learning rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large-scale Transformer training instabilities, such as attention logit growth and output logit divergence, can be reliably reproduced and investigated using much smaller models.
> - Monitoring internal model characteristics like attention logits and gradient norms provides early warning signals for impending instabilities, allowing for proactive adjustments.
> - A novel instability was identified where very low gradient RMS values in large models can cause AdamW updates to stall if the default epsilon (1e-8) is relatively too large.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of increasingly large Transformer-based models, from natural language processing to computer vision, has driven remarkable progress in artificial intelligence. However, as these models scale up to billions or even trillions of parameters, a critical and resource-intensive challenge emerges: training instabilities. These instabilities, such as sudden loss spikes or divergence, often manifest differently or exclusively at very large scales, making their investigation and mitigation prohibitively expensive due to the massive computational resources required.

This paper directly addresses this gap by proposing and validating methods to reproduce and study these large-scale training instabilities using much smaller, more manageable models. Prior work has documented several specific instabilities. For instance, Dehghani et al. (2023) and Zhai et al. (2023) reported the "attention logit growth" problem in large Vision Transformers, where attention logits become excessively large, leading to attention entropy collapse. Similarly, Chowdhery et al. (2022) identified "output logit divergence" in large language models like PaLM, where the model's output logits diverge from the log probabilities. These and other observations (e.g., fast loss spikes discussed by Cohen et al. (2021, 2022) and Gilmer et al. (2021) in the context of the "Edge of Stability" phenomenon) underscore the fragility of large-scale training.

Traditionally, researchers studying scaling laws (e.g., Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) primarily focused on trends related to model performance (loss) as a function of scale, compute, or data. This work distinguishes itself by shifting the focus to *stability* and *failure modes* when scaling. By demonstrating that large-scale instabilities can be observed in small models at high learning rates, the authors open a new avenue for research. This approach significantly democratizes the study of large model training dynamics, enabling researchers without access to supercomputing clusters to contribute to understanding and solving critical stability issues. The paper thus serves as a methodological blueprint, providing practical tools and demonstrating the feasibility of investigating complex, high-resource phenomena in a more accessible environment. It complements existing work by providing a predictive framework for identifying potential instabilities early in the development cycle, moving beyond reactive debugging to proactive risk assessment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the prohibitive cost associated with investigating training instabilities in very large Transformer models. As the paper highlights, "the amount of resources required to reproduce them has made investigation difficult." This resource barrier significantly hinders scientific understanding of these phenomena and the development of robust mitigation strategies, ultimately slowing down progress in large-scale AI.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Reproduce Large-Scale Instabilities at Small Scales:** The core objective is to find ways to reliably reproduce training stability and instability observed in large Transformer models using smaller, computationally less demanding models. This would allow for more rapid iteration and detailed analysis.
2. **Investigate Known Instabilities:** Specifically, the authors aim to reproduce and study two prominent sources of instability described in prior work: the growth of attention logits and the divergence of output logits. They seek to confirm if these issues manifest similarly in small models under specific conditions and if previously employed mitigations remain effective.
3. **Characterize Stability with Novel Metrics:** To systematically study stability, the paper aims to introduce quantitative tools. They propose "learning rate (LR) sensitivity" as a summary statistic to capture how susceptible a model's final loss is to variations in the learning rate, particularly at high values that often trigger instability.
4. **Evaluate the Impact of Common Interventions:** The research seeks to understand how various widely-used optimizer and model interventions (such as warm-up, weight decay, and μParam parameterization) influence the learning rate vs. loss relationship and overall training stability across different scales.
5. **Predict Instabilities from Characteristic Scaling Behavior:** A forward-looking objective is to explore whether instabilities can be predicted *before* they fully emerge or lead to divergence. This involves examining the scaling behavior of internal model characteristics, such as activation and gradient norms, as early warning signals.

In essence, the overarching goal is to transform the study of large-scale Transformer training instabilities from an inaccessible, reactive process into an accessible, proactive, and scientifically rigorous endeavor.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a comprehensive empirical approach, leveraging small Transformer models and a meticulous experimental setup to systematically investigate training instabilities.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Model Architecture:** They primarily used small, decoder-only Transformer models, similar to GPT-2, implemented in Flax. These models ranged in size from 2.4 million parameters up to 1.2 billion parameters, allowing for controlled scaling experiments. Key architectural details included pre-normalization, rotary positional embeddings, GELU activations, and standard 1/sqrt(d) scaling in self-attention. Weight initialization followed common practices (normally distributed for embeddings, truncated normal with inverse root fan-in for other weights).
- **Training Data:** The C4 dataset was used for training, with sequences packed to avoid padding.
- **Optimizer:** AdamW with standard hyperparameters (β1=0.9, β2=0.95, ϵ=1e-8) and global gradient clipping at norm 1.
- **Learning Rate Schedule:** A linear warm-up phase (default 5e3 steps) followed by a cosine decay schedule (default total 1e5 steps) was employed.
- **Regularization and Mitigations:** The default setup included independent weight decay (1e-4) and an auxiliary z-loss (coefficient 1e-4), based on prior large-scale model findings. Several interventions were explicitly tested:
 - **qk-layernorm:** Applying LayerNorm to queries and keys before computing attention logits.
 - **z-loss regularization:** An auxiliary loss term (log^2 Z) to prevent output logit divergence.
 - **Warm-up length:** Varying the duration of the initial linear learning rate ramp-up.
 - **Independent Weight Decay:** Implementing weight decay as recommended by Loshchilov and Hutter (decoupled from the learning rate schedule).
 - **μParam:** A parameterization method designed to stabilize optimal learning rates across scales.
 - **Scaling Width vs. Depth:** Investigating the impact of increasing model width (embedding size) versus depth (number of layers) on stability.
- **Hardware and Precision:** Training was conducted on Google's custom TPUs using bfloat16 precision, standard for large-scale DeepMind experiments. Flax and JAX provided the deep learning framework.

**Analytical Tools and Metrics:**
- **Learning Rate (LR) vs. Loss Curves:** A fundamental tool. Models of different sizes were trained across a wide range of learning rates, and their final evaluation loss was plotted. This visual representation allowed for identifying the critical learning rate at which instability (loss explosion) occurs and how it changes with model scale.
- **LR Sensitivity:** Introduced as a summary statistic to quantify the robustness of training to learning rate choice. It measures the expected deviation in final validation loss from the optimal loss within a defined range of learning rates (typically three orders of magnitude, 3e-4 to 3e-1). A higher LR sensitivity indicates greater instability or narrowness of the optimal LR window. The definition accounts for runs that fail completely by capping their loss at initialization loss.
- **Scaling Trends for Model Characteristics:** Beyond loss, the authors extensively monitored internal model metrics such as activation norms (e.g., maximum attention logit) and gradient norms (e.g., root mean square of gradients). By observing how these characteristics scale with model size and learning rate, they aimed to identify potential instability predictors.

This systematic methodology, combining controlled experimentation with quantitative metrics and internal state monitoring, allowed the researchers to dissect complex training dynamics at scales previously difficult to study.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several significant findings regarding Transformer training instabilities and their mitigation:

#### 4.1. Reproduction of Known Instabilities at Small Scales

- **Attention Logit Growth:** The paper successfully demonstrated that the attention logit growth instability, previously observed in large Vision Transformers, can be reproduced in small Transformer models when trained at high learning rates. Without mitigation, models diverged, and the learning rate at which divergence occurred decreased with increasing model size (Figure 1). Crucially, the mitigation technique, **qk-layernorm** (applying LayerNorm to queries and keys), proved equally effective in small models, significantly reducing LR sensitivity and enabling stable training across three orders of magnitude of learning rate variation. Analysis suggested this instability is due to the growth of query and key norms, not their alignment (Appendix E.1).
- **Output Logit Divergence:** The instability where output logits diverge from log probabilities was also reproduced in small models, particularly when no weight decay was applied (Figure 3). The **z-loss regularization** proposed by Chowdhery et al. (2022) effectively mitigated this issue, similar to its effect at large scales. Weight decay was also found to independently help mitigate this instability for larger models.

#### 4.2. Measuring the Effect of Other Known Interventions

- **Warm-up:** Longer warm-up periods were shown to consistently reduce LR sensitivity and improve final loss, especially for larger models (Figure 5). This confirms the widely acknowledged importance of warm-up for stability, now quantified across scales.
- **Independent Weight Decay:** The practice of parameterizing weight decay independently of the learning rate, as recommended by Loshchilov and Hutter (2019), was found to significantly reduce LR sensitivity compared to the common practice of scaling both terms together in AdamW implementations (Figure 6).
- **Scaling Width vs. Depth:** When independently scaling model dimensions, increasing depth (number of layers) increased LR sensitivity at a faster rate than increasing width (embedding size). However, at the largest tested scale, depth-scaled models achieved lower final validation loss (Figure 7). Joint scaling of both width and depth generally performed best and provided more reliable scaling predictions (Appendix E.3).
- **μParam:** While μParam (a parameterization method) successfully stabilized the optimal learning rate across different model scales, it did not lead to improvements in the final loss or a reduction in LR sensitivity in their experimental setup (Figure 8). Furthermore, it did not alleviate the need for qk-layernorm (Appendix E.4).

#### 4.3. Predicting Instabilities from Scaling Behavior of Model Characteristics

- **Attention Logit Growth Prediction:** By tracking the maximum attention logit values, the authors demonstrated that it's possible to predict the onset of instability. They extrapolated from smaller models to predict that a 4.8 billion parameter model would become unstable at a learning rate of 1e-2, a prediction that was experimentally confirmed. This indicates that monitoring internal characteristics can provide early warning signs of impending divergence (Figure 9). They also showed that forcing attention logits above ~1e4 inhibits learning (Figure 10).
- **New Instability (AdamW Epsilon):** A novel insight was discovered by examining gradient norms. The gradient Root Mean Square (RMS) was found to decrease with both increasing model scale and learning rate (Figure 11). At the largest scales and high learning rates, the gradient RMS approached the default AdamW epsilon hyperparameter (1e-8). When the gradient RMS falls below epsilon, the AdamW update magnitude becomes disproportionately small, effectively stalling learning (Figure 13). Decreasing the AdamW epsilon to 1e-15 significantly improved loss for a 4.8 billion parameter model at a high learning rate, while increasing it caused divergence (Figure 12, E.15). This finding suggests that the default epsilon may be too large for very large models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries profound significance for the field of large-scale AI model development, particularly for Transformer architectures. Its primary impact lies in **democratizing the study of large-scale training instabilities**. By rigorously demonstrating that critical failure modes observed in billion-parameter models can be faithfully reproduced in much smaller, resource-efficient setups (millions of parameters), the authors have effectively lowered the barrier to entry for investigating these complex phenomena. This opens "new scientific opportunities for studying training stability without access to large resource pools," enabling a broader range of researchers and institutions to contribute to solving these previously inaccessible challenges.

The ability to study instabilities at smaller scales translates directly into several practical benefits:
- **Accelerated Research and Development:** Researchers can now rapidly prototype, test hypotheses, and iterate on mitigation strategies for training instabilities without incurring the enormous computational and time costs associated with full-scale model training runs. This will significantly speed up the discovery of more robust training practices.
- **Proactive Instability Prediction:** The methodology of monitoring the scaling behavior of internal model characteristics (like activation and gradient norms) offers a powerful new tool for predicting instabilities before they lead to costly failures. This predictive capability allows practitioners to adjust hyperparameters, modify architectures, or apply specific mitigations early in the scaling process, preventing resource waste and improving training reliability.
- **Deeper Mechanistic Understanding:** The paper provides empirical validation and deeper insights into existing instabilities (attention logit growth, output logit divergence) and their mitigations. For example, linking attention logit growth to query/key norm growth, and detailing how qk-layernorm addresses this.
- **Discovery of New Failure Modes:** The identification of the "AdamW epsilon" issue—where decreasing gradient norms in large models can lead to updates being too small due to a relatively large default epsilon—is a novel and highly impactful finding. This highlights a subtle but crucial hyperparameter interaction that could be hindering optimal training of the largest models today. Adjusting epsilon based on gradient scale will be a critical consideration for future large-scale optimization.
- **Informed Hyperparameter and Architectural Design:** The systematic analysis of interventions like warm-up, independent weight decay, and the implications of scaling width versus depth provides concrete guidance for practitioners. For instance, the preference for joint scaling and the benefits of longer warm-up are reinforced, while the limitations of μParam in this context are clarified.

In conclusion, this paper provides a vital roadmap for understanding and overcoming the stability hurdles inherent in training massive AI models. Its contributions are not just theoretical; they are immediately actionable for researchers and engineers striving to build the next generation of powerful, reliably trained foundation models. By making the "unstable edge" of large-scale deep learning more accessible for scientific inquiry, this work paves the way for more robust, efficient, and ultimately more capable AI systems.
