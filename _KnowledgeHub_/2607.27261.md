# It's Not Just More Demos: Counterfactual Action Sensitivity Coverage for Data-Efficient Robust Robot Imitation

> [!summary] Summary
> Counterfactual Nuisance Behaviour Cloning (CFNBC) is an offline data-selection framework introduced by CSIRO Robotics to improve the robustness of visuomotor imitation policies. It quantifies policy sensitivity to task-preserving visual changes using "action drift" and selects compact, informative repair datasets via response-guided selection, achieving substantial robustness gains with significantly less data.

## Problem

- Visuomotor imitation learning policies exhibit brittleness to task-preserving visual nuisances like changes in lighting or background, leading to significant performance degradation in real-world deployment.
- Existing methods for enhancing robustness, such as extensive data collection or domain randomization, are often inefficient as they do not specifically target the policy's failure modes.
- Online active learning approaches, while effective for identifying failures, are costly, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe for hardware robots due to the need for online policy rollouts and expert intervention.

## Method

- The **CFNBC** framework generates task-preserving counterfactuals by applying visual nuisances to clean demonstrations, ensuring the expert action remains consistent, and measures ==action drift== as the change in the nominal policy's predicted action between paired clean and nuisance observations.
- It employs ==response-guided repair selection== to create a compact repair dataset by prioritizing candidates that expose diverse high-sensitivity policy response modes, rather than simply selecting all high-drift examples.
- The nominal policy is fine-tuned using a mixture of the original clean demonstrations and the selected counterfactual repair data, allowing robustness improvement without requiring new expert labels or online rollouts.

## Results

- Nominal ACT policies, with **90-96%** clean success, exhibited substantial drops in mean nuisance success (e.g., from **0.96** to **0.30** in cube transfer and **0.90** to **0.32** in cube stacking).
- CFNBC with response-guided repair increased mean nuisance success by **+0.66** with only **20 candidates** in the cube transfer task and by **+0.44** with **30 candidates** in the cube stacking task, outperforming random and top-drift selection baselines.
- While partial generalization was observed on held-out nuisance conditions, large random data budgets (K=500) eventually achieved superior held-out transfer (**0.90 mean**, **0.80 worst**) compared to low-budget CFNBC (**0.57 mean**, **0.43 worst**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - "Action drift" provides a reliable offline signal for policy sensitivity to task-preserving visual changes, correlating strongly (Spearman ρ of up to 0.95) with observed online failure rates.
> - Actively covering diverse fragile policy response modes with a small, selected set of counterfactual examples is more data-efficient for robustness repair than indiscriminate random data collection or merely selecting top-drift examples.
> - Robustness can be significantly improved across various nuisance conditions and for worst-case scenarios without compromising the policy's performance in clean, nominal environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visuomotor imitation learning has demonstrated capabilities in manipulation tasks, offering an alternative to costly reward-based online reinforcement learning. However, policies trained with these methods often exhibit brittleness to visual variations, termed 'nuisances,' such as changes in lighting, background, or the presence of distractors. These variations, even when task-preserving (i.e., not altering the underlying task state or desired expert action), can lead to significant performance degradation.

The broader research landscape addresses this brittleness through several avenues. One common approach involves scaling data collection efforts, leading to large datasets like ALOHA Unleashed and Open X-Embodiment, which aim to improve robustness through sheer data diversity. Another strategy focuses on learning invariant representations, utilizing techniques such as InfoNCE, Time-Contrastive Networks (TCN), R3M, and Barlow Twins. However, these methods face challenges in visuomotor manipulation, where the distinction between nuisance and task-relevant visual information can be highly task and state-dependent, potentially leading to the removal of crucial control information or the retention of nuisance correlations.

Domain randomization (DR) and synthetic augmentation methods also seek to improve robustness by expanding the training data distribution to cover a wider range of visual variations. More targeted variants, such as active or automatic DR and counterfactual data augmentation (e.g., RoCoDA), bias sampling towards more challenging or informative regions. Yet, these often select or generate data based on environment parameters or visual transformations, rather than directly assessing the policy's induced action response.

Active imitation learning methods (e.g., DAgger, HG-DAgger, ThriftyDagger) provide policy-specific targeting of failures by executing the current policy and querying an expert for corrections in uncertain or risky states. While effective, these approaches require online policy rollouts and often human-in-the-loop intervention, which can be slow, expensive, and unsafe for robotic hardware. Complementary work involves using simulation environments (e.g., SimplerEnv, REALM, GenGap, The Colosseum) to probe policy degradation under distribution shifts, but these primarily serve a diagnostic role due to the sim-to-real gap, rather than directly facilitating repair.

This current work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the data efficiency problem in robustness repair. It departs from blind data collection and online failure-driven methods by proposing an offline, policy-specific framework that identifies informative additional data. The aim is to overcome the inefficiency of random data diversity by explicitly auditing a trained policy's sensitivity to task-preserving visual changes and selecting repair data that addresses specific, identified weaknesses.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed brittleness of visuomotor imitation learning policies when deployed in real-world scenarios that deviate from their nominal training conditions. While these policies can achieve high success rates on clean, in-domain demonstrations, their performance often degrades significantly under task-preserving visual nuisances such as altered lighting, background changes, or the presence of irrelevant distractor objects. These nuisances modify the visual observation without changing the underlying task state or the required expert action. A robust policy should ideally remain invariant to such changes.

The core problem identified is the inefficiency of current approaches to enhance robustness. Simply increasing the quantity of diverse data through methods like extensive data collection or domain randomization can be inefficient. This is because a significant portion of the additional data may cover visual variations that the policy already handles adequately, while neglecting specific visual conditions that induce brittle, failure-prone responses. Online active learning methods, which identify failures during policy execution and solicit expert corrections, are effective but incur substantial costs in terms of time, resources, and potential safety risks, particularly for hardware robots.

Given these limitations, the key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Formulate task-preserving visual nuisance repair as a data-selection problem:** Instead of blindly collecting or generating more data, the research aims to identify *which* additional demonstrations or counterfactual examples are most informative for improving the robustness of a specific, already-trained visuomotor imitation policy under a limited data budget.
2. **Introduce "action drift" as an offline policy sensitivity signal:** The goal is to develop a metric that can quantify a policy's sensitivity to task-preserving visual changes without requiring expensive online rollouts or success labels. Action drift measures the change in a policy's predicted action between paired clean and nuisance observations of the same underlying task state. A large drift indicates that the policy is perturbed by task-irrelevant visual shifts.
3. **Propose "response-guided repair selection":** Building upon the action drift signal, the objective is to design a method for selecting a compact set of repair examples. This method should prioritize examples that expose diverse "fragile response modes" of the policy, rather than simply selecting all examples with the highest action drift, which may be redundant.
4. **Demonstrate data-efficient robustness improvement:** The research aims to show that fine-tuning a nominal policy on these carefully selected, counterfactual repair examples can substantially improve its robustness to nuisances, achieving comparable or superior performance to much larger, randomly selected repair datasets, particularly under low data budgets.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond mere data volume to focus on data *quality* and *informativeness* by leveraging a policy-specific offline audit to target areas of fragility efficiently.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Counterfactual Nuisance Behaviour Cloning (CFNBC), is an offline data-selection framework designed for targeted robustness repair of visuomotor imitation policies. It operates in three main stages: generating task-preserving counterfactuals and measuring action drift, selecting a compact repair set using response-guided selection, and fine-tuning the nominal policy with the selected data.

**A. Task-preserving Counterfactuals**
The process begins with a nominal policy ($\pi_{\theta_0}$) trained on a set of clean demonstrations ($D_{clean}$). For each timestep ($t$) of a demonstration, characterized by an observation ($o_t$) and robot state ($q_t$), a corresponding underlying task state ($x_t$) is assumed. Visual nuisances ($\eta$) are introduced to create a nuisance observation ($o_t = g(x_t, \eta_n)$) while preserving the original task state and robot state. This results in paired clean ($s^c_t = (g(x_t, \eta_c), q_t)$) and nuisance ($s^n_t = (g(x_t, \eta_n), q_t)$) observations. A crucial assumption is that these nuisances are *task-preserving*, meaning the desired expert action remains identical for both paired observations ($a^*(s^c_t) = a^*(s^n_t)$). This construction isolates visual sensitivity: any significant change in the policy's predicted action between such a pair indicates sensitivity to a task-irrelevant visual shift.

**B. Action Drift and Candidate Response Set**
For each sampled candidate response, which typically spans a window of timesteps ($T_i$) from a demonstration trajectory, paired clean and nuisance inputs are generated. The nominal policy is queried for its predicted actions on both the clean ($a^c_{i,t} = \pi_{\theta_0}(s^c_{i,t})$) and nuisance ($a^n_{i,t} = \pi_{\theta_0}(s^n_{i,t})$) inputs. To account for varying scales across different action dimensions, actions are normalized using statistics from the original training demonstrations.

Two key metrics are then computed:
- **Normalized action delta ($\Delta a_{i,t}$):** The difference between the normalized nuisance action and the normalized clean action ($\mathcal{N}(a^n_{i,t}) - \mathcal{N}(a^c_{i,t})$).
- **Scalar action drift ($d_i$):** The average L2 norm of the action delta over the timesteps in $T_i$ ($\frac{1}{|T_i|}\sum_{t \in T_i} \|\Delta a_{i,t}\|_2$). This scalar value serves as a policy-specific sensitivity signal, quantifying how much the policy's action changes due to a task-preserving visual nuisance, without requiring success labels.
A **response feature ($r_i$)** is also constructed for each candidate from the sequence of action deltas. This feature captures summary statistics (mean, standard deviation, mean absolute value, maximum absolute value) across timesteps and action dimensions, characterizing the *type* of policy response induced by the nuisance.
By sampling demonstrations, timesteps, and nuisance settings, a finite **candidate response set ($C$)** is assembled. Each candidate $c_i \in C$ stores its paired nuisance inputs, the inherited expert actions, its scalar action drift score $d_i$, and its response feature $r_i$.

**C. Response-Guided Repair Selection**
Given a limited repair budget ($K$), the objective is to select a compact repair set ($R_K \subseteq C$) that covers diverse policy response modes, rather than simply picking candidates with the highest action drift, which might be redundant. An affinity function $A(r_i, r_j)$ (an RBF kernel on standardized response features in practice) quantifies the similarity between the policy responses induced by two candidates.
The repair set $R_K$ is selected by maximizing a weighted response coverage objective (Equation 4 in the paper). The weights ($w_i$) can be uniform, prioritizing broad diversity, or drift-weighted ($\tilde{d_i}$), which prioritizes high-sensitivity response modes (e.g., using $\tilde{d_i}$ as a candidate quality multiplier and $\tilde{d_j}$ for weighting coverage, as detailed in Appendix A). This optimization is performed greedily by sequentially adding the candidate that yields the largest marginal improvement in the objective until $K$ candidates are selected.

**D. Counterfactual-Based Repair Fine-Tuning**
Once $R_K$ is selected, the nuisance observations from these candidates are paired with their original expert actions (inherited from the clean demonstrations, as the nuisances are task-preserving). This forms the counterfactual repair dataset ($D^{cf}_{R_K}$). The nominal policy's parameters ($\theta_0$) are then fine-tuned using a mixture of the original clean demonstrations ($D_{clean}$) and the selected counterfactual repair data ($D^{cf}_{R_K}$). A weighting factor ($\lambda_{cf}$, set to 0.5 in experiments) balances the contribution of the two datasets during behavior cloning loss minimization. The resulting policy ($\pi_{\theta_{repair}}$) is then evaluated. This process does not require new expert labels, rollout success labels, or rewards during fine-tuning.

**Experiment Setup:**
The methodology was evaluated on two simulated manipulation tasks: MuJoCo bimanual cube transfer (trained with 50 clean demonstrations) and SimplerEnv cube stacking (trained with 200 clean demonstrations), both utilizing the Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policy architecture. Both nominal policies achieved approximately 90% success in their clean domains.
The task-preserving nuisance space comprised four factors (appearance, lighting/shadow, distractor proximity, local support), each with four severity levels (L0-L3), creating 16 single-factor conditions. Additionally, six predefined multi-factor combination conditions were used, totaling 22 nuisance conditions.
Candidate response sets were generated by applying these nuisance variations to nominal demonstration episodes. Evaluation compared CFNBC's response-guided selection against baselines of random repair and top-drift selection, under matched low-budget ($K=20$ or $30$) settings. Fine-tuning used a common protocol and checkpoint. Policies were evaluated via rollout success over 100 episodes for clean, all 22 nuisance, and held-out nuisance conditions (not used in training or selection) to assess generalization. Results were averaged over three initialization seeds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of CFNBC in improving the robustness of visuomotor imitation policies.

**Nominal Policies are Brittle Under Task-Preserving Nuisances:**
Initial evaluations demonstrated that nominal ACT policies, despite achieving high success rates (around 90-96%) in clean environments, exhibited substantial performance degradation when confronted with task-preserving visual nuisances. For instance, the MuJoCo cube transfer policy’s mean success across 22 nuisance conditions dropped from 0.96 (clean) to 0.30, with a worst-case success of 0.04. Similarly, the SimplerEnv cube stacking policy dropped from 0.90 (clean) to 0.32 mean nuisance success, with a worst-case of 0.00. These significant performance gaps underscored the need for targeted robustness repair.

**Action Drift Provides an Offline Fragility Signal:**
A core finding was the strong correlation between normalized action drift and rollout failure rates under nuisance conditions. Spearman rank correlations of $\rho = 0.95$ for cube stacking and $\rho = 0.88$ for cube transfer indicated that nuisance conditions inducing higher action drift in the nominal policy also tended to result in higher failure rates during actual policy execution. This supports action drift as a reliable offline signal for identifying fragile nuisance conditions without requiring expensive online rollouts or success labels. The results also highlighted that the impact of specific nuisance types varied between tasks and policies, emphasizing the utility of a policy-specific audit.

**Response-Guided Selection Improves Data Efficiency:**
The central result showed that response-guided repair selection significantly enhanced data efficiency compared to random selection and top-drift selection, especially in low-budget scenarios.
- **Cube Transfer Task (K=20 candidates):** Response-guided repair increased the mean success rate across all nuisance conditions from the nominal 0.30 to 0.96. This represented a gain of +0.66. In contrast, random selection achieved 0.56 (+0.26 gain), and top-drift achieved 0.74 (+0.44 gain) with the same budget. Response-guided repair effectively restored near-full performance in the tested nuisance domains with just 20-30 candidates.
- **Cube Stacking Task (K=30 candidates):** Response-guided repair improved mean nuisance success from 0.32 (nominal) to 0.76 (+0.44 gain). Random selection yielded 0.60 (+0.28 gain), and top-drift yielded 0.63 (+0.31 gain).
In both tasks, response-guided selection achieved high robustness with substantially fewer repair candidates than random selection. While large random budgets (e.g., K=500) could eventually match the performance ceiling, they required significantly more data. The fine-tuned policies also maintained their clean-domain performance, indicating that robustness was gained without compromising nominal behavior.

**Broader Robustness:**
Response-guided repair demonstrated improvements not only on single-factor nuisance conditions but also on multi-factor combination conditions and worst-case scenarios, suggesting a broader enhancement of robustness across the evaluated nuisance set. This contrasted with "narrow repair" strategies (e.g., adding random samples from a single fragile nuisance family), which improved performance for the targeted nuisance but showed limited transfer and left other conditions largely unrepaired, often maintaining a worst-case success of 0.0.

**Held-Out Generalization:**
Evaluation on held-out nuisance conditions (unseen instantiations of nuisance families) showed partial generalization. For cube transfer, response-guided repair improved held-out mean success to 0.57 (from nominal 0.02) and held-out worst-case to 0.43 (from nominal 0.02) with 20 candidates, outperforming low-budget random and top-drift. However, large random budgets (K=500) ultimately achieved the best held-out transfer (0.90 mean, 0.80 worst), suggesting that the effectiveness of CFNBC is still dependent on the candidate set's coverage of potential deployment nuisances.

**Practical Implications:**
The results indicate that identifying and covering a policy's missing response modes is more critical for robust imitation learning than simply collecting more data indiscriminately. Action drift provides an effective offline audit, enabling prioritization of repair examples that address specific policy weaknesses.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Counterfactual Nuisance Behaviour Cloning (CFNBC) as a significant contribution to the field of robust robot imitation learning, particularly in addressing the challenge of data efficiency. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

**1. Data-Efficient Robustness Repair:** The primary impact is enabling substantial improvements in policy robustness with significantly fewer additional data samples compared to untargeted data collection methods. By demonstrating that 20-30 selected counterfactual candidates can outperform or approach the performance of hundreds of randomly selected examples, CFNBC offers a practical pathway to achieve robust policies under strict data budgets. This addresses a critical bottleneck in deploying imitation learning systems, where collecting vast quantities of diverse data can be prohibitive.

**2. Offline Policy Sensitivity Audit:** The introduction of "action drift" as an offline, policy-specific sensitivity signal is a key methodological advancement. This metric allows researchers and practitioners to diagnose policy fragilities to task-preserving visual nuisances without requiring costly, time-consuming, or potentially unsafe online policy rollouts or human expert labeling for new data. This shifts the paradigm from reactive, failure-driven data acquisition to proactive, diagnostic-driven data selection.

**3. Targeted Repair for Diverse Fragility:** The "response-guided repair selection" mechanism is crucial for the framework's efficiency. By actively seeking to cover diverse high-sensitivity policy response modes, rather than merely accumulating high-drift examples, CFNBC ensures that the limited repair budget is allocated to data that addresses a broader spectrum of potential failure conditions. This leads to more general and comprehensive robustness improvements compared to narrow, single-nuisance-focused repairs.

**4. Practical Workflow for Robot Learning:** CFNBC has the potential to streamline real-robot workflows. An operator could train a nominal policy, use CFNBC to identify its fragile visual modes offline, and then prioritize the collection or generation of specific, high-value repair scenarios. This could significantly reduce the cost, time, and safety risks associated with iteratively improving robot policies on hardware.

**5. Contribution to Data-Centric AI for Robotics:** This work reinforces the growing emphasis on "data-centric AI," where the quality and informativeness of data are prioritized over sheer quantity. It provides a concrete framework for how to intelligently curate and select data to address specific model weaknesses, fostering more deliberate and efficient development cycles for robotic systems.

**Potential Impact Areas:**
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By minimizing the need for extensive data collection, CFNBC can lower the financial and temporal investment required to develop robust robotic manipulation skills.
- **Safer Robot Deployment:** The ability to audit and repair policy weaknesses offline contributes to safer deployment of robots, as potential failure modes can be addressed before the robot operates in varied real-world conditions.
- **Accessibility for Smaller Datasets:** This approach can make advanced imitation learning more accessible for tasks where collecting massive, diverse datasets is not feasible.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** CFNBC opens avenues for future work, including validation on physical hardware, integration with broader policy architectures (e.g., stochastic policies), richer candidate generation techniques, and combination with other offline signals like ensemble uncertainty, further advancing the state of data-efficient robust robot learning.

In summary, CFNBC represents a practical step towards making visuomotor policies more robust and deployable by providing a data-efficient, policy-specific method for identifying and addressing fragilities, ultimately moving beyond the simple collection of "more demos" to gathering "more informative demos."
