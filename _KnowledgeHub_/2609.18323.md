# Can MiniMax-H3 Reason About the Physical World? An Evaluation of Omni-Modal Generative Model

> [!summary] Summary
> An evaluation framework probing whether the omni-modal generative model MiniMax-H3 can reason about the physical world by generating videos from implicit prompts, where key event semantics are withheld from text and distributed across images, audio, prefix videos, and audiovisual inputs. Across 517 expert-verified instances in four scenarios, MiniMax-H3 succeeds only 41.97% of the time, ranging from 56.00% on video decisions to 27.40% on audio disambiguation.

## Problem

- Video generation benchmarks like VBench and WorldModelBench use explicit prompts that fully describe the target, so models can succeed by rendering instructions without inferring anything from inputs.
- Existing evaluations condition mostly on text plus image, treating extra modalities as redundant or local signals, leaving omni-modal models' audio and video inputs untested for reasoning.
- Accepting multiple modalities is not reasoning across them: a model may ignore non-dominant evidence, miss cross-modal correspondences, or lean on textual priors, and existing benchmarks cannot detect this.

## Method

- **Implicit-prompt evaluation protocol**: text specifies only the operation (generate, continue, edit) while critical event semantics are split across modalities, so the generated video serves as a behavioral readout of ==cross-modal inference==.
- Four reasoning scenarios: **Multi-view Spatial Reasoning** over robot wrist-camera views, **Audio-based Disambiguation Reasoning** of ambiguous images, **Video-based Decision Reasoning** from prefix videos, and **Audiovisual Integrated Reasoning** for continuation or corrective editing.
- 517-instance dataset across 29 subcategories built from real robot, video, and audio sources plus synthetic clips, with ==LLM-drafted== implicit prompts refined through 10-loop expert review and three-expert binary success judging.

## Results

- MiniMax-H3 reaches **41.97%** overall success over 517 instances: Video-based Decision Reasoning **56.00%**, Audiovisual Integrated **47.89%**, Multi-view Spatial **43.50%**, and Audio-based Disambiguation lowest at **27.40%**, a **28.6-point** spread.
- Within manipulation, threading hits **75.00%** and pouring **58.30%** while cleaning, articulation, and transport sit near **33-35%**; video decisions reach **100%** on traffic but **16.67%** on puzzles and **20%** on cartoons.
- Audio grounding fails most on machinery (**17.60%**), contact (**21.90%**), and alerts (**23.10%**) versus nature sounds (**54.50%**); failures cluster into wrong evidence grounding, incomplete events, physics violations, and temporal state drift.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accepting a modality is not using it: omni-modal conditioning interfaces can pass evidence the model ignores, so input support must be tested separately from reasoning over that input.
> - Withholding target semantics from the prompt turns a generator benchmark into a reasoning probe; explicit-prompt benchmarks mostly measure rendering fidelity, not whether models infer what to generate.
> - Visual plausibility is a poor proxy for physical consistency, and generation-based scores conflate perception, cross-modal fusion, and rendering errors; modality-ablation controls are needed to localize failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the emergence of omni-modal generative models, which accept combinations of text, images, videos, and audio and can produce multimodal outputs. Earlier generative systems generally focused on text-to-video or image-to-video synthesis, where the prompt explicitly described the desired event. Recent omni-modal models, including MiniMax-H3, attempt to provide a unified interface for multimodal understanding and audio-visual generation.

This development has created a distinction between **multimodal input support** and **multimodal reasoning**. A model may technically accept several input types while relying primarily on one modality, ignoring contradictory evidence, or failing to establish correspondences between audio, images, and video. The paper focuses on this distinction by asking whether MiniMax-H3 can infer an unstated event from complementary, distributed evidence.

Existing benchmarks provide only partial coverage of this question. VBench and related video-generation evaluations primarily measure visual quality, semantic alignment, temporal consistency, or prompt adherence. Physics-oriented benchmarks such as VideoPhy and WorldModelBench assess physical plausibility and world consistency, but usually use explicit prompts and relatively limited input modalities. Other recent work examines video-based reasoning, audio-visual understanding, or interactive world models, but does not fully combine implicit task descriptions with heterogeneous observations.

The paper’s central contribution is therefore an evaluation framework in which the text prompt specifies the operation to perform but omits the crucial semantic information needed to determine the correct output. That information is distributed across images, multiple views, video prefixes, audio, or combinations of these modalities. The generated video acts as a behavioral output: success requires not only producing a plausible video, but also inferring the event, response, or correction supported by the multimodal evidence.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Primary Research Question

The main objective is to determine whether multimodal alignment improves physical-world reasoning in an omni-modal generative model. More specifically, the paper examines whether MiniMax-H3 can:

1. Associate entities and spatial relations across different views.
2. Use audio to resolve visual ambiguity.
3. Predict reasonable consequences or actions from a video prefix.
4. Integrate auditory and visual information when continuing or editing a video.

The authors emphasize that these capabilities involve more than recognizing objects or generating visually realistic content. The model must identify relevant evidence, align evidence across modalities, infer an underlying event or state, and express that inference through generation.

#### 2.2. Motivation for Implicit Prompts

In conventional generation benchmarks, prompts often describe the target video directly. For example, if the prompt explicitly states that a person should open a cabinet, a model can generate the action without determining which object or event is relevant in the input. Such evaluations measure instruction following and rendering quality, but provide limited evidence about inference from incomplete observations.

The proposed implicit-prompt setting reverses this arrangement. The prompt may request a continuation, editing operation, or manipulation sequence without stating the answer. A sound, image, or video must supply the missing information. For example, an image may show several possible objects, while an accompanying sound identifies which object was involved. In another case, two wrist-camera views may jointly specify the spatial configuration needed for coordinated manipulation. This design is intended to test whether the model uses complementary evidence rather than simply following a dominant textual or visual cue.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Evaluation Tasks

The evaluation is organized into four scenarios.

**Multi-view Spatial Reasoning (MSR)** uses multiple images of the same scene, often from different viewpoints. The model must establish cross-view correspondences, account for occlusion and viewpoint changes, and generate a video preserving the spatial relationships jointly implied by the views. Examples include coordinated manipulation using left- and right-wrist camera images.

**Audio-based Disambiguation Reasoning (ADR)** pairs an ambiguous image with an audio clip. The image permits several interpretations, while the sound identifies the relevant object or event. The model must connect the acoustic cue to the correct visual entity and generate a corresponding event. Example categories include vocalizations, machinery, contact sounds, alerts, and environmental sounds.

**Video-based Decision Reasoning (VDR)** provides a prefix video and asks the model to generate an immediate continuation. The prompt does not specify the exact next action. Instead, the model must infer a reasonable response from motion, object states, interactions, and spatial relationships. Tasks cover humans, animals, traffic, physical processes, puzzles, memory-related scenarios, and synthetic examples.

**Audiovisual Integrated Reasoning (AVIR)** combines a video with audio or spoken constraints. The output may be a continuation or an edited video. The model must interpret the audio in the context of the visual sequence, such as identifying a visual violation of a spoken instruction or generating a response consistent with both modalities.

Formally, the model generates an output video from a task prompt and observations, represented as \(\hat{Y} \sim p_\theta(Y|q,x)\). The evaluation does not require one exact reference video. Instead, an output is successful if it satisfies the semantic requirements supported by the inputs.

#### 3.2. Dataset Construction

The evaluation set contains 517 instances across four scenarios and 29 subcategories:

- MSR: 200 instances across 10 subcategories.
- ADR: 146 instances across 6 subcategories.
- VDR: 100 instances across 8 subcategories.
- AVIR: 71 instances across 5 subcategories.

The authors use both real and synthetic images, videos, and audio. Visual sources include manipulation and embodied datasets such as HiFi-UMI-2K, VISTA-UMI-5K, HuMI-Unsheathe, Hy-Embodied-0.5-VLA-Data, 10Kh-RealOmin-OpenData, and LLaVA-Video-178K. Audio is drawn from FSD50K and ESC-50, supplemented by generated audio and video sources.

For each instance, the researchers select multimodal observations, define a semantic target, and construct a prompt that describes the requested generation operation without revealing the target interpretation. ChatGPT and Qwen3 were used to generate candidate prompt formulations, which were then edited and checked by experts.

Each candidate underwent iterative expert review over ten review cycles. Reviewers assessed scene complexity, inferential richness, alignment between the conditions and target, and prompt implicitness. They also checked that the observations provided sufficient evidence while allowing multiple valid visual realizations.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Protocol

Three experts independently assessed each generated video and then cross-checked their decisions using the input conditions and task instructions. The primary metric is binary success rate, calculated as the proportion of outputs satisfying the task-specific semantic requirements.

For MSR, reviewers examined spatial relationships and coordinated actions. For ADR, they checked whether the generated event was consistent with the supplied sound. For VDR, they assessed whether the continuation followed the observed motion and represented a reasonable consequence or response. For AVIR, they evaluated whether the output correctly reflected the audio and maintained visual consistency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

Across all 517 instances, MiniMax-H3 achieved a success rate of **41.97%**. Thus, the model failed to satisfy the task requirements in more than half of the evaluated cases, despite supporting all relevant input modalities.

Performance differed substantially by scenario:

- **VDR:** 56.00%.
- **AVIR:** 47.89%.
- **MSR:** 43.50%.
- **ADR:** 27.40%.

The 28.60-percentage-point gap between VDR and ADR indicates that audio-dependent disambiguation was considerably more difficult than video continuation in this evaluation. However, the authors caution that the task types differ in prompts, data, and output requirements, so the results should not be interpreted as a controlled comparison between audio and video processing alone.

#### 4.2. Results by Task Category

Within MSR, threading achieved 75.00% and pouring 58.30%, whereas cleaning, articulation, and transport were approximately 33–35%. This pattern suggests that the model can handle some structured manipulation sequences but struggles with maintaining spatial consistency across views in more complex or less stereotyped actions.

VDR was the strongest scenario overall. The model performed relatively well on traffic, animals, humans, and physical dynamics. Traffic examples achieved 100%, although this category contained only nine instances. Performance was lower for cartoons and puzzles, at 20.00% and 16.67%, respectively. These failures included incorrect fine-grained manipulation, implausible physical transitions, and loss of temporal state.

ADR was the weakest scenario. Nature sounds produced a 54.50% success rate, while machinery reached 17.60%, contact sounds 21.90%, and alerts 23.10%. The failure cases show that the model often generated an event that was semantically plausible but associated the sound with the wrong object. It also struggled with small-object interactions and sounds that could plausibly originate from multiple sources.

AVIR achieved 47.89%. Activities and making-related tasks were relatively successful, while animation and animal cases were more difficult. The model sometimes incorporated spoken or environmental cues correctly, but it also missed visual responses, localized audio incorrectly, or produced an output that was plausible in isolation but inconsistent with the audiovisual evidence.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Findings and Failure Modes

The qualitative examples show that MiniMax-H3 can generate visually coherent continuations and often responds to dominant cues. Successful cases include cabinet opening in response to an associated sound, paper movement in response to typewriter audio, and reasonable short-term actions in video continuation.

The authors identify four recurring failure types:

1. **Incorrect evidence grounding:** The model identifies a plausible event but connects the evidence to the wrong object or action.
2. **Incomplete event realization:** Relevant objects are present, but the required interaction is not fully performed.
3. **Physical or configurational violations:** Generated actions break contact dynamics, object geometry, camera constraints, or valid state transitions.
4. **Temporal state inconsistency:** The model fails to preserve object states, motion trends, or scene content over the continuation.

These findings support the paper’s distinction between visual plausibility and physical-world consistency. Realistic appearance does not ensure that the output reflects the correct causal interpretation of the inputs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper contributes a benchmark design for evaluating reasoning through generation rather than evaluating generation only under explicit instructions. Its implicit-prompt formulation is relevant to future omni-modal systems because many real-world tasks require combining partial observations rather than following a complete textual description.

The findings also provide a more qualified assessment of MiniMax-H3’s capabilities. The model demonstrates that multimodal evidence can sometimes guide generation beyond the explicit prompt, particularly for short-term video continuation. However, the 41.97% overall success rate indicates that accepting multiple modalities does not guarantee reliable integration or physical reasoning.

Potential applications include robotics, embodied agents, audio-visual assistants, and interactive world models, where errors in object identity, spatial relations, temporal state, or physical consequences can affect decisions. The benchmark could help researchers identify these failure modes before deploying such systems in more consequential settings.

The study has several limitations. It evaluates one model without comparative baselines or modality-ablation experiments, so it cannot determine how much each modality contributes or whether MiniMax-H3 outperforms simpler systems. The generation-based metric also combines perception, cross-modal reasoning, planning, and video synthesis, making it difficult to isolate the source of an error. In addition, the reported results depend on expert judgments, and the paper does not provide inter-rater agreement statistics in the supplied text. Future work involving controlled modality removal, audio substitution, automated semantic evaluation, and broader physical scenarios would strengthen the conclusions.

Overall, the paper frames omni-modal generation as both a production capability and an observable testbed for multimodal reasoning. Its results suggest that current systems possess partial cross-modal competence but remain unreliable in grounding evidence, preserving physical constraints, and converting multimodal observations into correct actions or events.
