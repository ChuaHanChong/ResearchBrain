# Cross-Embodiment Transfer via Behavior-Aligned Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> Behavior-aligned representations (BARs) improve cross-embodiment robot skill transfer by implicitly aligning heterogeneous data from diverse robot platforms. This approach boosts real-world sim-to-real transfer by 28% in task completion, facilitating more generalist robot policies.

## Problem

- Developing general-purpose robot policies is hindered by the significant variations in observation and action spaces across diverse robot embodiments.
- Existing cross-embodiment transfer methods often require extensive manual effort or specific knowledge of target robots, limiting their scalability and effectiveness.
- Leveraging large, diverse datasets for robot learning is challenging due to platform-specific data collection and the difficulty of transferring skills between different robot morphologies.

## Method

- Introduce **behavior-aligned representations (BARs)** as an implicit alignment mechanism for heterogeneous robot data.
- Integrate BARs into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, training them to jointly predict these representations with robot actions via an ==auxiliary loss==.
- Utilize embodiment-invariant representations such as object bounding boxes, language descriptions of motion, and end-effector traces.

## Results

- Behavior-aligned representations consistently improved cross-embodiment transfer success rates, with end-effector traces showing the most significant impact.
- The approach achieved a **28%** increase in task completion progress during real-world sim-to-real cross-embodiment transfer on physical robot platforms.
- Pre-training with action-free, representation-only data led to a **14%** performance improvement over training from scratch on target robot data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Behavior-aligned representations (BARs) implicitly align heterogeneous robot data via embodiment-invariant, action-relevant information, and their effectiveness scales positively with the size and diversity of cross-embodiment datasets.
> - Much of the benefit from BARs is derived during the training process, not necessarily requiring their prediction during inference.
> - End-effector traces prove to be a highly impactful representation, particularly for bridging differences in robot end-effector geometries.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is increasingly focused on developing general-purpose policies that can operate across a wide range of tasks and environments. A key challenge in achieving this generality is the heterogeneity of robot platforms, leading to varied observation spaces (e.g., camera views, robot geometry) and action spaces (e.g., joint control, end-effector commands). While modern machine learning benefits from large, diverse datasets, robot data collection often remains specific to individual embodiments.

Prior work has explored scaling robot datasets and learning cross-embodiment policies to leverage data from various platforms and facilitate transfer to new embodiments. However, successful cross-embodiment transfer for manipulation tasks has shown mixed results, often failing to significantly outperform policies trained only on target-specific data. This limitation is primarily attributed to substantial variations in policy inputs and outputs across different robot embodiments, coupled with insufficient data coverage to overcome these discrepancies.

Existing approaches to improve cross-embodiment transfer include explicitly aligning observations (e.g., using wrist cameras, inpainting, segmentation masks) or action spaces (e.g., shared control schemes). While these methods have shown some utility, they frequently require considerable manual effort or knowledge of the deployment-time robot during training, which can impede their scalability and generality.

This paper proposes an alternative strategy to implicit alignment through "behavior-aligned representations" (BARs). This approach fits into the broader trend of using intermediate representations or auxiliary objectives in robot learning to enhance policy performance and robustness. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which fine-tune vision-language models (VLMs) to predict robot actions, have emerged as a promising class of policies for generalist robot manipulation due due to their ability to ingest large, heterogeneous datasets and leverage internet-scale vision-language knowledge. This work integrates behavior-aligned representations within the VLA framework, examining their specific role in promoting cross-embodiment transfer, a topic that has not been thoroughly investigated despite the general utility of such representations in other robot learning contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenge of achieving scalable and robust robot learning. While large, diverse datasets are crucial for general-purpose machine learning models, robot data collection is often platform-specific. To overcome this, the ability to train policies that can leverage data from multiple robot embodiments and transfer skills to entirely new, unseen embodiments is critical. Such cross-embodiment policies could significantly accelerate robot learning and adapt to evolving robot hardware.

However, existing cross-embodiment learning efforts often yield limited success in transfer. The substantial differences in robot observations (e.g., image views) and action spaces across embodiments make direct transfer challenging. Prior methods that attempt explicit alignment of observations or actions suffer from limitations such as requiring significant manual effort or prior knowledge of the target robot, which are not conducive to scalable, general solutions.

The key insight motivating this work is that instead of explicit alignment, implicit alignment can be achieved by connecting data from different embodiments through behavior-aligned representations. These representations are hypothesized to:
1. **Contain information relevant for action prediction:** They capture the essence of the desired behavior.
2. **Possess invariances across embodiments:** For a given task, the representation should be similar regardless of the specific robot executing it.
3. **Provide an additional space for policy reasoning:** This space can bridge the gaps between heterogeneous observation and action spaces.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of the research are:
- **Empirically study the role of behavior-aligned representations:** To systematically investigate how different types of behavior-aligned representations (e.g., object bounding boxes, language motions, end-effector traces) impact cross-embodiment transfer.
- **Develop a simulation-based benchmark:** To facilitate controlled assessment of cross-embodiment transfer with diverse data to new embodiments. This benchmark, named RoboCasa-X, allows for rigorous comparison of different representations and methods of their incorporation.
- **Compare different representations and incorporation methods:** To identify which representations are most beneficial for transfer and the optimal strategies for integrating them into VLA models (e.g., single representation, chained prediction, joint prediction).
- **Investigate scaling properties:** To determine if the benefits of representations are more pronounced with larger, more diverse cross-embodiment datasets compared to smaller, single-embodiment datasets.
- **Assess inference-time requirements:** To evaluate whether predicting and conditioning on representations during inference is necessary for their benefits, or if the advantages are primarily derived during training.
- **Explore action-free data utilization:** To examine if behavior-aligned representations can enable transfer from datasets that lack action information.
- **Demonstrate real-world applicability:** To validate the findings in a sim-to-real cross-embodiment transfer setting, assessing their effectiveness on physical robot platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates the utility of behavior-aligned representations (BARs) for cross-embodiment transfer within a language-conditioned imitation learning setting. The core approach involves modifying Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to jointly predict these representations alongside robot actions.

**A. Formalization:**
The policy is defined as $\pi_{\theta}(a | o, l)$, mapping observations ($o$) and language instructions ($l$) to actions ($a$). Expert data $D = \{(o_i^r, a_i^r, l_i)\}$ is assumed, where $r$ denotes the embodiment. Standard behavior cloning minimizes $L_{BC}(\theta) = E_{(o,a,l) \sim D} [\ell(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot | o, l), a)]$.
To incorporate BARs, each observation $o_i$ is annotated with a tuple of representations $z_i = (z_i^{(1)}, \ldots, z_i^{(K)})$. The policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is modified to condition on a sampled subset of representations $\tilde{z}$ and is also trained to predict these representations. The total loss combines behavior cloning with auxiliary supervision for representation prediction:
$L_{total}(\theta) = E_{(o,z,a,l) \sim D, \tilde{z} \sim p_{rep}(z)} [\ell(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot | o, l, \tilde{z}), a) + \ell_{aux}]$
where $\ell_{aux} = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \ell_{rep}^{(k)}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot | o, l), z^{(k)})$.
In practice, the VLA model autoregressively predicts a subset of BARs as text, followed by actions. Different text prompts indicate which representations to predict. The hypothesis is that BARs, being embodiment-invariant and action-predictive, implicitly align heterogeneous cross-embodiment data.

**B. Choice of Behavior-Aligned Representations:**
The study focuses on three types of BARs chosen for their ease of annotation, informativeness for action prediction, and inherent cross-embodiment invariance:
- **Bounding Boxes:** 2D bounding boxes of target objects in the current image observation. In real-world data, these are obtained using a pipeline combining VLM scene descriptions and Grounding DINO. In simulation, ground truth labels are used.
- **Language Motions:** Textual descriptions of the upcoming robot motion (e.g., "move left and down"). These are generated via a pipeline that thresholds changes in robot proprioceptive state, similar to prior work.
- **End-Effector Traces:** Sequences of future 2D positions of the end-effector in the image observation frame, representing the path the end-effector should take. A pre-trained model from prior work is used to detect these positions from real images; ground truth is used in simulation.

**C. Implementation Details:**
The policy architecture is MiniVLA, initialized from a pre-trained VLM. Observations consist of single third-person camera views. All loss weights ($\lambda_k$) for auxiliary representation tasks are set to 1.

**D. Simulation Experiments (RoboCasa-X Benchmark):**
A new simulation benchmark, RoboCasa-X, is introduced, based on RoboCasa, to evaluate cross-embodiment transfer.
- **Tasks:** Variations of three existing RoboCasa tasks (PnP Counter to Sink, PnP Sink to Counter, Turn On Sink Faucet) and one new task (Flip Mug Upright). These tasks feature diverse kitchen layouts, textures, object types, and poses.
- **Source Embodiments (Pre-training):** IIWA, Kinova3, and UR5e robots. The Kinova3 and UR5e use the Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, while the IIWA uses the Robotiq 2F-140. Camera poses are randomized to enhance diversity and sim-to-real transfer. Datasets XP-900 (300 demos/task/embodiment) and XP-3K (1000 demos/task/embodiment) are generated using MimicGen.
- **Target Embodiments (Transfer Evaluation):** Panda and Jaco with the Robotiq 2F-85 gripper, and Panda with its original Franka Hand gripper (Panda-OG). For each, 50 human demonstrations per task are collected.
- **Evaluation:** Policies are pre-trained on source data, then co-fine-tuned on both source and limited target data. Success rate over 100 rollouts with fixed scene conditions is the primary metric. Ground truth labels for BARs are available in simulation. All simulated robots share the same delta Cartesian end-effector pose control and controller.

**E. Real-World Experiments:**
To assess sim-to-real cross-embodiment transfer, two real target embodiments are used: Franka Research 3 (FR3) and ViperX 300 S.
- **Tasks:** Variations of the PnP tasks from RoboCasa-X.
- **Setup:** Models pre-trained on XP-3K simulation data are adapted using 50 real demonstrations per task. Visual observation and action space differences between simulated source robots and real target robots are significant. Camera view and environment layout are loosely aligned with simulation.
- **Action Alignment:** Real robots have substantial differences in control stacks (frequency, blocking/non-blocking control) compared to simulated robots, adding to transfer difficulty.
- **Evaluation:** Task completion progress is reported over 10-30 rollouts per embodiment/task pair. Single models are trained for both real target embodiments to allow for inter-robot transfer.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents several findings across simulation and real-world experiments regarding the effectiveness of behavior-aligned representations (BARs) for cross-embodiment transfer.

**Q1: Incorporating Representations**
- **Individual Representation Benefits:** Each representation (bounding box, language motions, end-effector traces) individually improved overall transfer success rates compared to using no representations.
- **End-Effector Traces Most Impactful:** End-effector traces generally exhibited the most significant positive impact on transfer, followed by language motions, and then bounding boxes.
- **Combined Representations:** Combining representations using either "ECoT" (sequential chain of thought) or "Joint Reps" (single model predicting one of multiple representations or actions) generally led to better performance than individual representations for target robots like Panda and Jaco (with Robotiq 2F-85 gripper).
- **End-Effector Specialization:** For Panda-OG, which has an end-effector unseen in the prior data, using only end-effector traces outperformed combined representations. This suggests that end-effector traces are particularly crucial for aligning data when end-effector geometries are diverse.

**Q2: Cross-Embodiment Scaling**
- **Scaling with Larger Datasets:** The benefits of behavior-aligned representations were generally more pronounced with larger cross-embodiment datasets (XP-900 and XP-3K) compared to smaller, single-embodiment datasets or no prior data. For most tasks, the overall improvement when training with no prior (+5%) was smaller than with XP-900 (+15%) or XP-3K (+19%).
- **Strong Transfer:** For Panda and Jaco, training on XP-3K with representations often matched or exceeded the performance of training on a same-embodiment prior (SP-900), indicating strong transfer.
- **Limitations with Larger Gaps:** Performance with Panda-OG still lagged significantly behind training on a same-embodiment prior, suggesting that representations, while helpful, may have limits when embodiment gaps are substantial.
- **Task-Specific Effects:** For the "Turn On Sink Faucet" task, representations had a larger impact when training only on target robot data (+22%) than with XP-900 (+4%) or XP-3K (+1%). This was attributed to the task's lower variation, leading to quicker performance saturation and less criticality for large prior datasets.

**Q3: Representation Inference**
- **Training-Time Benefit Dominant:** Evaluating models by directly predicting actions (without first predicting representations) showed comparable performance to predicting representations before actions. While predicting representations during inference could offer minor benefits in some settings, the overall effect was not substantial. This indicates that representations primarily enhance cross-embodiment transfer implicitly during training.

**Q4: Action-Free Transfer**
- **Enabling Action-Free Transfer:** Pre-training on action-free cross-embodiment data (only representations) using Joint Reps models resulted in improved performance compared to learning from scratch with target robot data (+14% overall) and using the full prior dataset with actions but without representations (+11%).
- **Performance Trade-off:** While beneficial, action-free pre-training with representations performed slightly worse than using representations with the full action-annotated prior dataset.
- **Embodiment Similarity:** Action-free pre-training did not provide benefits for Panda-OG, suggesting its effectiveness might depend on the similarity between the target embodiment and those in the action-free data.

**Real-World Experiments**
- **Significant Sim-to-Real Enhancement:** Behavior-aligned representations (Joint Reps) significantly improved sim-to-real cross-embodiment transfer on real Franka Research 3 (FR3) and ViperX 300 S robots. An overall increase of 28% in task completion progress was observed when using Joint Reps with cross-embodiment simulation data.
- **Increased Impact in Real World:** The benefits of BARs were more pronounced in real-world experiments than in simulation, likely due to larger domain gaps in observations and action spaces between simulated and real robots.
- **Enabling Transfer:** Without representations, the cross-embodiment simulation data provided limited benefit (+7%) over using target data alone, highlighting that BARs were instrumental in enabling this otherwise difficult transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robot learning by addressing a key challenge in scaling policies to diverse robot platforms: cross-embodiment transfer. The study provides empirical evidence for the utility of behavior-aligned representations (BARs) as a mechanism for implicitly aligning heterogeneous datasets, offering a scalable and general approach compared to explicit alignment methods.

The findings demonstrate that BARs consistently enhance cross-embodiment transfer in both simulation and real-world settings. The identification of end-effector traces as a particularly impactful representation, especially for embodiments with novel end-effectors, is a notable contribution. This suggests that carefully chosen representations can act as robust bridges across different robot morphologies and actuation schemes.

The investigation into the scaling properties of BARs indicates that their benefits become more substantial with larger, more diverse cross-embodiment datasets. This finding aligns with the broader machine learning trend where larger datasets enable more capable models, and provides a pathway for effectively leveraging future large-scale robot datasets. The observation that much of the benefit of BARs is derived during training, rather than requiring their prediction during inference, is significant for deployment, as it mitigates concerns about increased latency during real-time robot operation.

Furthermore, the demonstration that BARs can facilitate transfer from action-free datasets opens avenues for utilizing passively collected or unannotated visual data, which is abundant but traditionally difficult to integrate into imitation learning pipelines without costly action labeling. This could significantly expand the amount of data available for robot policy training.

The successful application of BARs to enhance sim-to-real cross-embodiment transfer on physical robots is a critical validation of the proposed approach. It shows that these representations can effectively bridge substantial domain gaps in visual observations and action spaces, which are common hurdles in transferring policies from simulation to reality. The pronounced impact in the real world suggests that BARs are especially valuable in complex, unconstrained environments where domain shift is significant.

In summary, this work provides a framework and empirical evidence for understanding and improving positive transfer in cross-embodiment robot learning. Its potential impact lies in enabling the development of more generalist robot policies that can learn from a broader range of data sources and adapt to new robot hardware more efficiently, thus accelerating the deployment of capable robots in diverse real-world applications. Future research, as suggested by the authors' limitations, can build upon this foundation by exploring BARs in less structured settings and investigating a wider array of representations suitable for different tasks and embodiment variations.
