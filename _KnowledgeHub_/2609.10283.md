# SwingBot: Learning Whole-Body Brachiation for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> SwingBot learns continuous whole-body brachiation on a 20-DoF humanoid with passive wrist hooks. Sparse biomimetic keyframes supply an annealed action bias so PPO can discover release-swing-capture transitions, while a privileged-information RSSM estimates segment-relative displacement and hook contact for deployment. Simulation all-8 success reaches 61.27%, and the real robot traverses bars under payload, pushes and varied spacing.

## Problem

- Existing brachiation robots use low-DoF task-specific morphologies, predefined trajectories or model-based control under fixed bar spacing and structured motion patterns.
- Pure reinforcement learning rarely discovers the sparse long-horizon release-swing-capture contact sequence in a high-dimensional whole-body action space.
- Deployed policies get only proprioception, lacking reliable onboard measurement of segment-relative body displacement and left/right hook-contact state.

## Method

- ==Residual keyframe guidance== blends sparse primate-inspired release/swing/capture postures with actor output, annealing the keyframe coefficient to zero over 2000 epochs, then low-pass filtering and joint-limit clipping.
- A privileged-information ==RSSM== reconstructs the compact target of segment-relative displacement and hook contacts, with encoder correction only at command switches and ==open-loop future-prediction loss== for self-iteration.
- ==Adaptive sampling== stores successful terminal states in command-indexed buffers to initialize the opposite leading hand, exposing the policy to feasible left-right handoff states.

## Results

- Across 20 seeds, adding the RSSM latent lifts all-8 success from **57.28%** to **61.27%** at a **1.5 s** switch and **43.12%** to **48.86%** at **1.0 s**.
- All variants lacking residual keyframe guidance fail the eight-segment protocol entirely, while the true-privileged teacher tops out at **65.78%** and **53.21%**.
- Real-robot single-swing success ranges **88.00-94.12%** across nominal, **1 kg** payload, disturbance and bar-spacing conditions; all-8 completion falls to **40-60%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sparse hand-designed keyframes annealed into a residual action bias can replace expensive expert datasets when exploration, not reward shaping, is the bottleneck.
> - Defining privileged targets relative to the current segment and resetting them at contact switches limits latent drift versus estimating an absolute map.
> - Learned latents matter more as transition time shrinks; binary contact reconstructs far better than continuous displacement, which accumulates error.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Brachiation is a locomotion strategy in which an animal moves between overhead supports by alternating hand contacts and using body momentum. In robotics, it offers a potential complement to walking, wheeled movement, and climbing, particularly in environments containing gaps, debris, hazardous ground surfaces, scaffolding, pipe networks, or other overhead structures.

Earlier robotic brachiation research generally focused on low-degree-of-freedom systems, simplified morphologies, or model-based controllers. Classical two-link brachiation robots used feedforward inputs, heuristic control, trajectory feedback, and neural adaptation to exploit pendular dynamics. More recent systems, such as AcroMonk and RicMonk, use passive hooks or grippers and are designed specifically for energy-efficient swinging. Other work has used simplified models and imitation learning to make reinforcement-learning exploration more manageable.

In parallel, reinforcement learning has become increasingly effective for dynamic control of legged and humanoid robots. Recent studies have demonstrated learned locomotion over challenging terrain, agile soccer behaviors, parkour, whole-body manipulation, and sim-to-real transfer. These results establish that high-dimensional policies can coordinate many joints under contact and dynamic constraints. However, most such skills concern ground-based locomotion or manipulation rather than repeated movement between overhead supports.

SwingBot addresses the intersection of these areas. It applies reinforcement learning to continuous brachiation on a high-degree-of-freedom humanoid robot rather than a specialized low-dimensional platform. The paper identifies two obstacles that distinguish this task from ordinary learned locomotion. First, the release-swing-capture sequence is a sparse, long-horizon behavior that random exploration is unlikely to discover. Second, important variables—such as the robot’s displacement relative to the current bar and whether either hook is in contact—are not reliably available from onboard proprioception. SwingBot therefore combines structured exploration with learned estimation of hidden transition states.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The main objective is to learn a controller capable of continuous whole-body brachiation, in which the robot alternates between left- and right-leading hand transitions across overhead bars. The intended behavior is not simply reaching toward a nearby bar. Each transition must include releasing one support, swinging the body, lifting the free hand, capturing the next support, and arriving in a posture that enables the opposite hand to begin the next transition.

This objective is challenging for several reasons. Brachiation is underactuated, so the robot must use gravity, body configuration, and momentum rather than relying only on direct arm pulling. The timing of release and capture must be coordinated with the body’s swing. Small errors in the swing trajectory can result in missed contact or an unrecoverable posture. The robot must also coordinate its legs, torso, and arms even though the hooks provide the primary external contacts.

The paper further aims to reduce dependence on privileged simulation information. In simulation, the exact body displacement, hand-to-target distance, and hook-contact state can be measured. On the physical robot, these quantities are difficult to infer without external vision or dedicated contact sensors. The authors therefore seek a policy that can use only proprioception, previous actions, and a learned recurrent latent state at deployment.

A related motivation is to develop a practical training method that does not require large expert datasets or manually optimized trajectories. Instead of providing a dense reference trajectory, the authors use sparse biomimetic keyframes as an early exploration scaffold. The scaffold is gradually removed so that the final controller is learned rather than being a fixed trajectory tracker.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Robot and Control Interface

SwingBot is deployed on a Mini Pi plus humanoid robot with 22 actuated degrees of freedom. The two head joints are mechanically locked, leaving a 20-degree-of-freedom control interface comprising 12 leg joints and 8 arm joints. Passive hook-shaped end effectors are attached to the wrists. Because the hooks do not actively close around the bars, the controller must produce suitable release and capture geometry through whole-body motion.

The policy outputs a 20-dimensional residual joint-position action. This action is converted into joint targets and tracked using low-level proportional-derivative control. The target is first blended with a keyframe command, low-pass filtered, and clipped to the robot’s measured joint limits. The same action limits and clipping procedure are used in simulation and on hardware to reduce inconsistencies during sim-to-real transfer.

#### 3.2. Residual Keyframe Guidance

The authors design separate keyframe sequences for left-leading and right-leading transitions. These keyframes represent semantic postures associated with support preparation, release, downward swing, forward swing, free-hand lifting, and target-bar capture. Their design is qualitatively inspired by observations of primate brachiation but is adapted to the robot’s morphology and passive hooks.

The keyframes do not directly determine the final motion. They provide a phase-dependent joint-position bias, while the policy learns a residual correction. If \(a_t^\pi\) is the policy output and \(a_t^{key}\) is the reference action, the applied command is formed as:

\[
u_t=\beta_e a_t^\pi +(1-\beta_e)a_t^{key}.
\]

The coefficient \(\beta_e\) increases linearly from zero to one over 2,000 training epochs. Early training is therefore dominated by the biomimetic scaffold, making release and swing postures more reachable. Later training gives the policy full control and removes the keyframe influence. A first-order low-pass filter smooths the final joint targets.

The reinforcement-learning controller is trained with proximal policy optimization (PPO). The reward is command-dependent and includes terms for moving the commanded hook toward the target bar, approaching the bar from above, leaving the previous support, aligning hook orientation, and reaching a final posture. Regularization penalizes torque, joint acceleration, action changes, excessive body motion, undesirable body contact, and falling.

#### 3.3. Recurrent Privileged-State Estimation

During training, the critic receives simulator-only variables, including base velocity, segment-relative body displacement, target-hand distance, and left/right hook-contact indicators. The actor does not receive these privileged quantities.

To provide comparable information at deployment, SwingBot trains a recurrent state-space model (RSSM). The RSSM processes short histories of proprioception and actions and learns a compact latent representation. Its target vector contains:

\[
y_t=[\Delta x_t,\Delta z_t,C_t^L,C_t^R],
\]

where \(\Delta x_t\) and \(\Delta z_t\) represent body displacement within the current bar-to-bar segment, and \(C_t^L,C_t^R\) indicate hook contact.

The model includes a recurrent deterministic state, a stochastic latent state, and decoders for displacement and contact. It is trained with reconstruction losses, latent dynamics and representation regularization, and an open-loop future-prediction loss. The future-prediction component is important because, during deployment, the RSSM cannot continually access the true privileged targets. It must propagate its estimate using its own recurrent dynamics.

At the start of a segment or after a command switch, the model receives a correction based on a repeatable boundary condition: both hooks are in contact and segment-relative displacement is treated as zero. Within the segment, the latent state is propagated at the 50 Hz control rate using proprioceptive and action history. The actor observes four frames of proprioception together with the RSSM deterministic latent.

#### 3.4. Connecting Consecutive Transitions

The command alternates between left- and right-leading transitions. To avoid training only on isolated swings, successful terminal states are stored in command-indexed buffers. These states can be used to initialize the opposite-hand command, exposing the policy to physically plausible handoff configurations. This adaptive sampling mechanism helps connect individual transitions into continuous traversal.

Training is conducted in Isaac Lab with domain randomization over friction, robot and link mass, joint friction, reset conditions, action and observation delays, actuation noise, arm motor strength, and velocity perturbations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Ablations

The simulation evaluation uses eight alternating command segments, with switches every 1.5 seconds or 1.0 seconds. A segment is considered successful when the robot achieves sufficient horizontal displacement and the commanded hand reaches an adequate height. The reported all-eight success rate requires every segment to succeed.

Variants without residual keyframe guidance fail to complete the full eight-segment protocol across the reported training seeds. This result supports the claim that ordinary PPO exploration is insufficient for discovering the complete release-swing-capture sequence in this setting.

With keyframe guidance but no RSSM latent, the student policy achieves an all-eight success rate of 57.28% at a 1.5-second switch interval and 43.12% at a 1.0-second interval. Adding the learned RSSM latent raises these values to 61.27% and 48.86%, respectively. The corresponding improvements are 3.99 and 5.74 percentage points. A teacher policy receiving the true privileged transition variables reaches 65.78% and 53.21%, providing an upper reference for the value of transition-state information.

The larger RSSM improvement under faster command switching suggests that hidden displacement and contact states become more important when the controller has less time to complete each transition. The RSSM reconstruction plots show that contact states are tracked relatively closely, while displacement estimates exhibit small errors that can accumulate during a segment.

A hip-torque ablation indicates that the legs are functionally important. When hip torque is severely restricted, the robot fails to complete the task. Increasing the available hip torque produces a marked improvement before gains become more gradual. The analysis indicates that the learned behavior uses leg motion to reshape body configuration and generate or regulate momentum rather than relying solely on the arms.

#### 4.2. Hardware Evaluation

On the physical robot, SwingBot performs alternating left- and right-hand transitions across overhead bars. The qualitative demonstrations include continuous traversal, external pushing and pulling, carrying an additional 1 kg payload, and traversal across bars with different spacings.

The quantitative evaluation uses five trials per condition, with each trial planned for up to eight swings. Under nominal conditions, 26 of 28 individual swings succeed, corresponding to 92.86%, and three of five trials complete all eight swings, corresponding to 60%. With a 1 kg payload, individual success is 22 of 25, or 88.00%, while all-eight completion falls to 40%. Under disturbance, 26 of 29 swings succeed, or 89.66%, and two of five trials complete all eight swings, or 40%. Under bar-spacing variation, 32 of 34 swings succeed, or 94.12%, and three of five trials complete all eight swings, or 60%.

Repeated-trial failures often occur after multiple transitions rather than during the first swing. The paper attributes many failures to shoulder-motor heating, which reduces available torque. In those cases, the robot can remain supported but cannot generate sufficient motion for the next swing and capture.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SwingBot contributes a learning formulation for a class of dynamic manipulation-locomotion tasks that is difficult to address with either pure model-based control or unstructured reinforcement learning. Its key methodological contribution is the combination of sparse, phase-structured guidance with recurrent estimation of deployment-time hidden variables. The keyframes address exploration, while the RSSM addresses partial observability.

The hardware results indicate that a high-degree-of-freedom humanoid can use coordinated arm and leg motion to perform repeated overhead transitions with passive end effectors. This supports the broader possibility that humanoids could use multiple locomotion modes depending on environmental structure. In environments with broken ground access but usable overhead supports, brachiation could provide an alternative route.

The approach may also generalize to other contact-rich skills in which phase transitions are sparse and important state variables are not directly measured. Examples could include dynamic climbing, hand-over-hand traversal, hanging transitions, or other whole-body maneuvers involving intermittent contacts.

The current system remains limited. It does not use external perception, so bar locations and spacings must remain within the range represented during training and testing. Passive hooks also impose structured environmental and contact requirements. The observed motor heating limits endurance during repeated traversal. Future work proposed by the authors includes visual perception for estimating arbitrary support geometry, active grippers or dexterous hands, and improved actuator endurance. These developments would be necessary before applying the method to less structured environments such as disaster sites, forests, or industrial infrastructure.
