# FlowDAgger: Human-in-the-Loop Adaptation of Generative Robot Policies in Latent Space

> [!summary] Summary
> FlowDAgger introduces a human-in-the-loop method for adapting pretrained generative robot policies by inverting human corrective actions into the policy's latent space and training a lightweight noise policy to steer the frozen base model. The approach improves task success with few interventions (e.g., 5-20 episodes on real-world tasks), preserves the base model's broad behavioral priors, and operates with low computational overhead.

## Problem

- Pretrained generative robot policies often encounter real-world failure modes in novel situations (e.g., unfamiliar objects, unmodeled scene dynamics) not covered by their training data.
- Current adaptation strategies like offline fine-tuning are computationally expensive, prone to 'catastrophic forgetting' of general skills, and require extensive data collection.
- Existing interactive imitation learning methods can be unstable or computationally intensive, while latent-space reinforcement learning requires difficult reward engineering and slow autonomous exploration.

## Method

- **FlowDAgger** converts human expert corrective actions into noise vectors in the generative policy's latent space using a novel ==action inversion== mechanism.
- A small, separate neural network (the ==noise policy==) is trained to regress these inverted latent targets, steering the ==frozen base generative policy== without modifying its weights.
- An ==on-policy DAgger-style learning loop== combines human interventions with successful autonomous rollouts to efficiently train the noise policy, learning from corrections while staying anchored to the base policy's manifold.

## Results

- FlowDAgger achieved a mean success rate improvement of **+0.25** over the frozen base policy on **12** MetaWorld tasks, outperforming competing DAgger-style and latent-space RL baselines with **50 rollouts**.
- The method successfully adapted both action-head VLA (π0.5) and World-Action Models (Cosmos-Policy), showing comparable gains (mean **+0.26** and **+0.21** success rates respectively) and versatility across generative architectures.
- On eight real-world manipulation tasks, FlowDAgger consistently improved success rates with **5 to 20** intervention episodes (e.g., Toolbox Packing from **13%** to **80%**), while effectively preserving performance on unrelated, held-out tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adapting generative robot policies by steering in their latent (noise) space provides a stable and sample-efficient mechanism for human-in-the-loop learning, as it operates within the base policy's learned manifold.
> - The action inversion mechanism enables translating human expertise (action-space corrections) into a format directly usable for latent-space adaptation, allowing efficient supervision without direct access to latent variables.
> - Freezing the large base generative policy and learning only a small noise policy prevents the degradation of broad pretrained skills (catastrophic forgetting) while still enabling rapid adaptation to specific tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements through the development of foundation models, particularly those based on generative architectures such as flow matching and diffusion processes. These policies, exemplified by visuomotor diffusion policies, vision-language-action (VLA) models (e.g., π0.5, Gr00t N1.7), and world-action models (WAMs like Cosmos-Policy), leverage large-scale demonstration datasets to encode broad behavioral priors. This approach enables impressive performance across various tasks and embodiments.

However, a recurring challenge in real-world deployment is the presence of failure modes that lie outside the distribution of the pretraining data. These failures can arise from novel objects, unmodeled scene dynamics, unique embodiment characteristics, or long-tail edge cases. Addressing these gaps efficiently and safely is a significant research problem.

Existing adaptation methods face limitations:
- **Offline fine-tuning:** Requires extensive additional data collection, is computationally expensive for large models, and can lead to "catastrophic forgetting," eroding general skills learned during pretraining.
- **Interactive imitation learning (e.g., DAgger, HG-DAgger, IWR):** While effective at addressing covariate shift with on-policy supervision, these methods typically modify the entire generative policy. This can be computationally intensive, unstable, and potentially corrupt the valuable pretrained behavioral priors.
- **Residual methods (e.g., Residual DAgger):** These approaches keep the base policy frozen and learn an additive correction in the action space. However, applying unconstrained corrections in the action space can push the policy outside the manifold of behaviors that the base model reliably produces, leading to unpredictable or undesirable outcomes.
- **Latent-space reinforcement learning (e.g., DSRL):** This approach trains a lightweight controller in the policy's noise space, offering greater sample efficiency. However, it relies on a well-defined reward signal and autonomous exploration, both of which can be bottlenecks for rapid deployment and adaptation in complex real-world robotic scenarios.

FlowDAgger contributes to this landscape by introducing a method that aims to overcome these limitations. It seeks to combine the benefits of human-in-the-loop intervention with the stability and prior preservation offered by latent-space adaptation, without requiring reward functions or extensive autonomous exploration. This work positions itself as a practical strategy for adapting robot foundation models to specific deployment contexts while retaining their broad capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a sample- and compute-efficient method for adapting pretrained generative robot policies using human interventions in the policy's latent space, without modifying the weights of the foundational base model.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges observed in the deployment of state-of-the-art generative robot policies:
- **Real-world performance gaps:** Despite impressive generalizability, pretrained policies frequently encounter novel situations (e.g., unfamiliar objects, unexpected scene dynamics, embodiment specificities) not covered by their pretraining data. These situations lead to task failures.
- **Impracticality of current adaptation strategies:**
 - **Data scarcity:** Collecting large volumes of task-specific demonstration data for offline fine-tuning is time-consuming and labor-intensive.
 - **Computational cost:** Fine-tuning large foundation models is computationally expensive, limiting rapid iteration.
 - **Skill erosion:** Modifying the base policy's weights often degrades its performance on previously mastered tasks, undermining the value of its broad behavioral priors.
 - **Stability issues:** Unconstrained adaptation in the action space can lead to unstable policy updates or behaviors that deviate significantly from the learned manifold of the base policy.
 - **Reward engineering:** Latent-space reinforcement learning requires a reward function, which can be challenging to design for complex, nuanced manipulation tasks in the real world, and relies on autonomous exploration that might be slow or unsafe.
- **Leveraging human expertise:** Human operators can provide precise corrective actions in real-time, representing a valuable source of supervision for addressing specific failure modes. The challenge is to effectively translate this action-space expertise into a format suitable for efficient policy adaptation that preserves base model integrity.

FlowDAgger aims to address these issues by proposing a practical adaptation path that:
1. Enables rapid skill acquisition by learning from a small number of human interventions.
2. Preserves the broad behavioral priors encoded in the frozen base generative policy.
3. Is computationally lightweight, allowing adaptation on consumer-grade hardware.
4. Provides a stable and reliable adaptation mechanism by operating within the policy's latent space.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FlowDAgger is a human-in-the-loop adaptation method for generative robot policies. Its core components are action inversion, which translates human corrections into latent space targets, and a lightweight noise policy that learns from these targets to steer the frozen base policy.

**Generative Action Policies Background:**
The method targets generative policies, such as those based on flow matching or diffusion, where an action `a` is produced by integrating an ordinary differential equation (ODE) driven by an initial noise sample `w` from a noise space `W`. The process maps an observation `s` and noise `w` to an action `a = π_gp(s, w)`. In practice, this ODE is solved using a K-step Euler discretization. The noise sample `w` thus deterministically controls the action for a given observation `s`, making the noise space a suitable surface for policy adaptation without altering the base model's parameters.

#### 3.1. Action Inversion

Action inversion is a mechanism to convert a human expert's corrective action `a*` into a corresponding noise vector `w*` in the policy's latent space. Given an observation `s` and `a*`, the goal is to find `w*` such that `π_gp(s, w*) ≈ a*`.
1. **Reverse-time Integration:** The process of generating an action `a` from noise `w` involves `K` forward Euler steps. To invert this, each forward step `x_{k+1} = x_k + Δt v_θ(x_k, t_k, s)` is rearranged to an implicit equation for `x_k`: `x_k = x_{k+1} - Δt v_θ(x_k, t_k, s)`.
2. **Fixed-point Iteration:** This implicit equation is solved iteratively using fixed-point iteration: `x^{(m+1)}_k = x_{k+1} - Δt v_θ(x^{(m)}_k, t_k, s)`. Starting from `x^{(0)}_k = x_{k+1}`, a few iterations (`M=5` in the paper) are sufficient for convergence because the right-hand side is a contraction under certain conditions.
3. **Backward Pass:** By running this fixed-point iteration backward from the target action `x_K = a*`, the intermediate states `x_{K-1}, ..., x_0` are recovered. The final `x_0` is taken as the inverted noise `w*`.
The fixed-point solution at each step is preferred over a single explicit reverse pass because it precisely minimizes the residual at each step, making inversion reliable for the few-step ODE schedules typical of action heads where `Δt` is not negligible. This process requires `K * M` evaluations of the velocity field `v_θ` and does not involve backpropagation through the base policy, enabling online operation.

#### 3.2. Inverting World-Action Models (WAMs)

WAMs, such as Cosmos-Policy, do not have a separate action-only generative process; they generate actions jointly with future world states as part of a single latent video sequence. FlowDAgger adapts to WAMs by inverting this joint generative process directly.
1. **Constructing the Joint Inversion Target:** The inversion target `x*₀` is created from the base policy's own predicted clean latent `x_base₀`. Only the action frame of `x_base₀` is perturbed by adding the expert's `a*` minus the base policy's action. The state and value frames remain as generated by the base policy. This strategy aims to create a minimal-delta target that remains on the base manifold.
2. **Joint Reverse Pass:** The reverse pass then recovers a joint-latent noise `w*` that would produce this full `x*₀`.
For WAMs using EDM-style diffusion schedules (which terminate at a non-zero noise level), the 1-Euler integration steps are inverted using the same fixed-point method. The terminal denoise step, a strong transform, is inverted using a short local Adam optimization solve, anchored at the base policy's pre-terminal state, backpropagating through a single denoiser call. This is the only part of the inversion process that involves backpropagation through the base model. For high-dimensional WAM noise, the noise policy can regress either the full latent or PCA coefficients of a learned basis for stability.

#### 3.3. FlowDAgger: Human-in-the-Loop Adaptation

FlowDAgger employs an on-policy, DAgger-style learning loop:
1. **Noise Policy (`π_w`):** A small, separate neural network (observation encoder followed by an MLP) with its own parameters `ϕ` is trained. At deployment, this noise policy replaces the standard noise draw, outputting `w = π_w(s)`, and the executed action becomes `a = π_gp(s, π_w(s))`.
2. **Adaptation Loop:**
 - The current policy (base policy steered by `π_w`) is deployed.
 - When the human operator observes unsatisfactory behavior, they intervene by providing a corrective action `a*` for the current observation `s`.
 - Action inversion converts this `(s, a*)` pair into a noise-space target `(s, w*)`.
 - This `(s, w*)` pair is added to an "intervention buffer."
 - In parallel, for every successful autonomous rollout of the policy, the corresponding `(s, w)` pairs (where `w` is the noise that generated the action) are added to a "successful autonomous buffer."
 - The noise policy `π_w` is trained to regress these targets by minimizing a mean squared error loss: `L(ϕ) = E_{(s,w*)~D} ||π_w(s) - w*||^2`.
 - Training batches draw in equal proportion from both buffers. This dual-buffer strategy ensures that `π_w` learns from human corrections while remaining anchored to the base policy's existing successful behaviors, preventing overfitting to sparse interventions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FlowDAgger was conducted across various manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world environments, utilizing different generative policy architectures.

#### 4.1. Setup

- **Tasks:** MetaWorld (simulated, diverse contact-rich tasks), and real-world bimanual (FR3 Duo, Dual UR5e) and single-arm manipulation tasks (Block Pick, Glassware Stacking, BusyBox: Button Push, Slider, Wire Pull; Bimanual: Jenga Stack, Toolbox Packing, Plug Insertion).
- **Base Policies:** π0.5 (action-head VLA), Cosmos-Policy (WAM). Appendices also evaluated Gr00t N1.7 (another action-head VLA) and a vanilla diffusion policy.
- **Baselines:** Frozen base policy, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) via offline behavior cloning, LoRA-DAgger (low-rank weight adaptation), Residual-DAgger (action-space residual), and DSRL (latent-space RL with reward).
- **Protocol:** Methods were compared with a matched budget of 50 additional rollouts (interventions for DAgger variants, offline demos for SFT, autonomous for DSRL). Success rates were averaged over 3 seeds and 25 rollouts per evaluation point.

#### 4.2. FlowDAgger adapts π0.5 from a handful of corrections

- **Performance:** On 12 MetaWorld tasks with π0.5 as the base policy, FlowDAgger achieved the largest mean improvement over the frozen base (+0.25 success rate) and outperformed all other non-base methods on 8 of the 12 tasks (Table 1).
- **Comparison to DAgger Baselines:** FlowDAgger demonstrated superior performance compared to LoRA-DAgger and Residual-DAgger, which consume the same human correction stream but adapt in weight-space or action-space, respectively. This suggests the advantage lies in the noise-space adaptation strategy.
- **Comparison to Latent-space RL (DSRL):** DSRL showed minimal improvement (+0.02 mean success rate) within the 50-rollout budget, indicating that direct human intervention provides a more potent and rapid learning signal than sparse reward and autonomous exploration in this context (Table 1, Figure 3).
- **Stability:** FlowDAgger exhibited smooth and consistent learning curves, whereas Residual-DAgger showed erratic behavior on some tasks, occasionally causing performance collapse below the base policy (Figure 3). This stability is attributed to staying within the base policy's learned manifold by steering in noise space.
- **Compute Efficiency:** FlowDAgger operates with a low computational footprint, fitting within approximately 8 GB of VRAM, enabling training on a single consumer GPU. This positions it favorably compared to methods that adapt the full base model (Figure 4).

#### 4.3. Adaptation transfers across base-policy families

- FlowDAgger successfully adapted both π0.5 (an action-head VLA with action-space inversion) and Cosmos-Policy (a WAM requiring joint world-action inversion) on a shared set of 7 MetaWorld tasks.
- The gains observed were comparable for both policy families (mean improvement of +0.26 for π0.5 and +0.21 for Cosmos-Policy, Table 2). This demonstrates that the core FlowDAgger methodology, including the generalized action inversion, is not specific to a single generative architecture or the existence of a separable action head.
- Additional experiments in Appendix B.3 and B.4 confirmed these findings for Gr00t N1.7 and a vanilla diffusion policy, reinforcing the versatility of the approach.

#### 4.4. Noise-space adaptation preserves the base prior

- An experiment adapting policies on "Hammer" task and evaluating on five held-out, already-saturated tasks showed FlowDAgger's ability to preserve pretrained skills.
- FlowDAgger achieved significant improvement on the "Hammer" task while maintaining held-out task performance closest to the frozen base policy (mean ∆ of -0.08, Table 3).
- In contrast, weight-space adaptation methods (SFT, LoRA-DAg) demonstrated substantial degradation of held-out skills (mean ∆ of -0.94 and -0.66 respectively) despite improving the in-distribution task. Residual-DAgger preserved the prior better but offered limited adaptation. This highlights a key benefit of keeping the base model frozen and adapting in latent space.

#### 4.5. Real-world adaptation

- On eight real-world manipulation tasks using two robot platforms, FlowDAgger consistently improved the frozen base policy with only 5 to 20 intervention episodes (Table 4).
- For example, on "Toolbox Packing," 10 corrections raised the success rate from 13% to 80%, transforming a largely unusable policy into a reliable one.
- FlowDAgger generally outperformed the SFT baseline on all real-world tasks except Jenga Stacking. This demonstrates the practical applicability and efficiency of FlowDAgger for real-robot deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FlowDAgger introduces a methodology that addresses a core challenge in deploying robot foundation models: adapting to novel scenarios while preserving their broad capabilities and computational efficiency. The significance of this work lies in several aspects:

- **Efficient and Stable Adaptation:** By operating in the latent space of frozen generative policies, FlowDAgger facilitates adaptation with a significantly smaller number of human interventions compared to data-intensive fine-tuning approaches. This approach also maintains stability, preventing the erratic learning or performance degradation observed in some action-space or weight-space adaptation methods.
- **Preservation of Pretrained Priors:** A key finding is FlowDAgger's ability to improve performance on specific tasks without compromising the general skills encoded in the base model. This addresses the "catastrophic forgetting" issue that often plagues weight-space fine-tuning, thereby maximizing the utility and longevity of expensive-to-train foundation models.
- **Broad Applicability:** The method's demonstrated effectiveness across diverse generative architectures (action-head VLAs, WAMs, diffusion policies) and various manipulation tasks (simulated and real-world, single-arm and bimanual) suggests its broad applicability in robotic systems. The development of action inversion for joint world-action models further expands its scope.
- **Computational Accessibility:** The lightweight nature of the noise policy and the design of the inversion process allow adaptation to run on consumer-grade GPUs, making advanced robot policy adaptation more accessible to a wider range of researchers and practitioners.
- **Practical Path for Robot Foundation Models:** FlowDAgger provides a concrete and practical mechanism for improving robot foundation models through interaction, fostering their real-world deployability. It redefines interaction as a means to supervise the latent decisions of a frozen generative policy, decoupling adaptation from the scale of the base model.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
1. **Bounded Adaptation Capacity:** The method's adaptation capability is constrained by the support of the pretrained model. If a desired behavior lies significantly outside the policy's action manifold, inversion may only recover the closest representable behavior, potentially necessitating more extensive data collection or weight-space adaptation.
2. **Dependence on Human Intervention Quality:** As with other DAgger-style methods, the effectiveness of FlowDAgger relies on the quality, consistency, and coverage of human interventions. Biases or inconsistencies in expert corrections can be reflected in the adapted policy.
3. **Inversion Accuracy:** The fidelity of action inversion can be compromised for highly multimodal action distributions or poorly conditioned generative dynamics, which could reduce how accurately expert corrections are represented in the latent space.

In conclusion, FlowDAgger offers a strategy for robot foundation model adaptation that balances rapid learning from human input with stability and preservation of learned priors, addressing practical deployment challenges in robotics.
