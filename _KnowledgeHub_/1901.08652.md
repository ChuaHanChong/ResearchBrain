# Learning agile and dynamic motor skills for legged robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zurich and Intel developed a methodology to train agile and dynamic motor skills for the ANYmal quadrupedal robot entirely in simulation, then directly deploy them on hardware. Their approach uses a learned actuator network to bridge the reality gap for complex Series-Elastic Actuators, enabling the robot to achieve superior performance in speed, efficiency, and dynamic fall recovery compared to prior model-based controllers.

## Problem

- Developing agile and dynamic locomotion for legged robots is challenging due to their high-dimensional, non-smooth dynamics, and complex environmental interactions.
- Traditional control methods (modular design, trajectory optimization) are laborious, require extensive tuning, or are computationally expensive and often compromise performance due to model inaccuracies.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) promises end-to-end optimization but faces a significant "reality gap" when transferring policies from simulation to real robots, especially those with complex, hard-to-model actuators like Series-Elastic Actuators (SEAs).

## Method

- A hybrid simulation approach that integrates a fast rigid-body simulator with a data-driven "actuator network" trained on a small dataset from the physical robot to accurately model complex actuator dynamics.
- Reinforcement learning (TRPO) is used within this hybrid simulation, employing dynamics randomization, observation noise injection, and curriculum learning to robustify policies against real-world discrepancies.
- Policies learned purely in simulation are directly deployed on the physical ANYmal robot, leveraging their computational efficiency for real-time control without external hardware.

## Results

- The learned controller achieved command-conditioned locomotion with **95%** lower linear velocity error and **60%** lower yaw rate error than the previous state-of-the-art model-based controller, while using 23-**36%** less torque.
- Enabled the ANYmal robot to run at a record-breaking speed of **1.5** m/s (**25%** faster than previous records), demonstrating the policy's ability to exploit hardware limits and discover a novel, efficient flying trot gait.
- Successfully implemented a dynamic fall recovery controller that restored the robot to an upright configuration from nine different challenging initial fall poses, a complex behavior previously unachieved on a real quadrupedal robot.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Accurately modeling complex, non-linear actuator dynamics via a learned neural network (Actuator Net) is crucial and highly effective for successful sim-to-real transfer of agile behaviors, outperforming hand-tuned analytical models.
> - Reinforcement learning, when combined with a robust hybrid simulation framework, can autonomously discover highly efficient, dynamic, and novel locomotion gaits and recovery behaviors that surpass human-designed controllers.
> - Extensive randomization of dynamics and observation noise during simulation training, coupled with curriculum learning, enables learned policies to be robust to real-world uncertainties and disturbances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of agile and dynamic locomotion for legged robots remains one of the grand challenges in robotics. Such robots hold immense promise for traversing rough terrains and unstructured environments where wheeled or tracked vehicles struggle. However, their complex, high-dimensional, and non-smooth dynamics, coupled with constantly changing contact points and noisy sensor data, make control extremely difficult.

Historically, control algorithms for legged robots have fallen into two main categories:
1. **Modular Controller Design:** This approach breaks down control into largely decoupled submodules based on template dynamics (e.g., Spring-Loaded Inverted Pendulum) or heuristics. While successful in achieving robust locomotion over challenging terrain (e.g., Kalakrishnan et al., Bellicoso et al.), these methods are highly laborious to design and tune, require extensive expert knowledge, and suffer from limited model accuracy, leading to performance compromises (e.g., slow acceleration, fixed body pose, limited velocity).
2. **Trajectory Optimization:** These methods use rigid-body dynamics and numerical optimization to compute optimal paths. While more automated, they still rely on approximations (e.g., smooth contact dynamics, pre-specified contact timings), are computationally expensive for real-time execution on complex models, and typically perform worse than state-of-the-art modular controllers. They often require external powerful machines or reduced precision, and the planning and tracking modules don't adapt to each other.

More recently, **data-driven methods, particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, have emerged as a compelling alternative. RL promises to learn effective controllers directly from experience, optimizing them end-to-end from sensor readings to low-level control signals, thus reducing the need for extensive human craftsmanship. RL has achieved remarkable success in simulation, enabling animated characters to exhibit complex motor skills (e.g., Schulman et al., Heess et al., Peng et al.).

However, the primary hurdle for RL in real-world robotics is the **"reality gap"** – the discrepancy between simulated and physical system dynamics and perception. Two main strategies to bridge this gap are:
1. **Improving Simulation Fidelity:** Analytically or data-driven system identification to make the simulator more accurate. This is often cumbersome and difficult for complex systems.
2. **Robustness to Imperfections:** Randomizing aspects of the simulation (dynamics, observations, disturbances) to make the learned policy robust to variations.

Previous successful sim-to-real transfers, like Tan et al. on the Minitaur robot, have often relied on very accurate analytical models for simple direct-drive actuators. This paper specifically targets the limitations of these approaches when applied to **larger, more dynamically challenging robots equipped with complex Series-Elastic Actuators (SEAs)**, like ANYmal. SEAs, while robust against impact and allowing accurate torque measurement, are notoriously difficult to model accurately analytically due to their non-linear dynamics, cascaded feedback loops, and unobservable internal states. Prior analytical modeling attempts for SEAs have proven insufficient for high-performance control.

Thus, this work introduces a novel and practical methodology to bridge the reality gap for such complex systems by learning the actuator dynamics, thereby enabling purely simulation-trained RL policies to achieve unprecedented levels of agile and dynamic motor skills on a real quadrupedal robot. It represents a significant step forward in making RL a viable and superior alternative for real-world dynamic robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a robust and practical methodology for autonomously learning and transferring agile and dynamic motor skills for complex and large legged robotic systems, specifically the ANYmal quadruped, from simulation directly to real-world deployment.

The motivation for this objective stems from several critical challenges and limitations in the field of legged robotics:

- **Difficulty of Control for Legged Robots:** Legged systems are inherently high-dimensional, non-smooth, and prone to complex interactions with their environment, making the design of effective control algorithms exceptionally challenging. Their freedom to choose contact points is an advantage but also a control burden.
- **Limitations of Traditional Control Methods:**
 - **Modular Control:** While effective, these methods require extensive manual design and tedious hand-tuning of parameters for each robot and every new maneuver. They often compromise performance due to simplified models.
 - **Trajectory Optimization:** These methods are computationally intensive, often requiring approximations that limit performance or demanding powerful external hardware. They struggle with complex, unspecified contact dynamics and generally perform worse than state-of-the-art modular controllers.
- **Promise and Problems of Reinforcement Learning (RL):** RL offers the potential for automated, end-to-end policy optimization, leading to highly agile and efficient controllers with minimal human craftsmanship. However, RL's application to real legged robots is largely hindered by:
 - **Prohibitively Long Training Times:** Real-robot training is expensive, risky, and time-consuming.
 - **Safety Concerns:** Chaotic or unexpected behavior during learning poses safety risks and logistical complications.
 - **The Reality Gap:** Policies trained in simulation often fail to transfer to the real world due to discrepancies in dynamics and perception.
- **Specific Challenge of Complex Actuators:** Existing sim-to-real methods, particularly those relying on accurate analytical models, are often inadequate for robots with complex actuators like the Series-Elastic Actuators (SEAs) used in ANYmal. SEAs have highly non-linear dynamics, internal feedback loops, and significant delays that are extremely difficult, if not impossible, to model precisely using analytical methods.
- **Desire for Superior Performance:** The authors aim to achieve locomotion skills on ANYmal that surpass the capabilities of previously existing model-based controllers in terms of speed, precision, energy efficiency, and dynamic behaviors like fall recovery, with a method that is more generalized and less labor-intensive.

In essence, the motivation is to unlock the full potential of RL for real-world dynamic legged locomotion by developing a novel approach that effectively addresses the reality gap, particularly for robots featuring complex and hard-to-model actuation systems, thereby leading to more capable, robust, and autonomously controlled robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology lies in a novel hybrid simulation approach that combines classical rigid-body dynamics with a learned model of the robot's complex actuation, followed by reinforcement learning to train control policies, and then direct deployment. The entire process is summarized in four key steps (Fig. 1):

**Step 1: Physical Parameter Identification and Uncertainty Estimation.**
The process begins with identifying the physical parameters of the robot, primarily from its Computer-Aided Design (CAD) model. These parameters, such as inertial properties (masses, center of mass, joint positions) of the links, are crucial for the rigid-body simulation. To account for inherent inaccuracies in these estimations (e.g., due to unmodeled cabling), the methodology explicitly incorporates *stochastic sampling* of these inertial properties during training. This "dynamics randomization" helps robustify the learned policies against real-world modeling errors.

**Step 2: Training an Actuator Network (Actuator Net).**
This is a *key innovation* of the paper. Recognizing the extreme difficulty of analytically modeling complex actuators like ANYmal's Series-Elastic Actuators (SEAs) due to their non-linear dynamics, delays, and unobservable internal states, the authors propose a data-driven approach.
- **Self-supervised Learning:** A deep neural network, termed the "actuator net," is trained on the physical robot to learn the mapping from commanded actions (joint position targets) to the resulting torques at the joints. This network implicitly captures all complex actuator/software dynamics within a single control loop.
- **Data Collection:** A small dataset (less than 4 minutes) of real-robot data is collected by commanding simple sine-wave foot trajectories with varying amplitudes and frequencies, and manually disturbing the robot. This ensures rich data covering a wide frequency spectrum. Data is collected at 400 Hz from the 12 identical actuators.
- **Network Architecture:** The actuator net is a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with three hidden layers (32 units each) and softsign activation functions for computational efficiency and smooth mapping. It takes as input a history of joint position errors and velocities, assuming this historical information allows it to infer internal actuator states.
- **Accuracy:** The trained actuator net significantly outperforms both an "ideal" (zero delay, infinite bandwidth) and a laboriously hand-tuned analytical actuator model in predicting real joint torques, validating its effectiveness in bridging the critical actuator-dynamics part of the reality gap.

**Step 3: Reinforcement Learning in Hybrid Simulation.**
The trained actuator network is then integrated into a high-fidelity rigid-body simulator to create a "hybrid simulator."
- **Rigid-Body Simulation:** The simulator uses a fast and accurate hard contact solver that respects Coulomb friction, capable of simulating ANYmal (41 collision bodies) at ~900,000 time steps per second. This speed is critical for generating the hundreds of millions of samples required for RL training.
- **Control Policy:** A control policy, represented by an MLP (two hidden layers with 256 and 128 units, tanh nonlinearity), is trained using the **Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)** algorithm. TRPO is chosen for its stability and minimal parameter tuning requirements.
- **Observation Space:** The policy receives observations reflecting real-world sensor data: joint angles/velocities, body linear/angular velocities, gravity vector, a history of joint states (for implicit contact detection), previous action, and high-level commands. Crucially, noise is injected into observed velocities during training to robustify the policy against noisy real-robot measurements.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs low-impedance joint position commands, which are then converted to torques by a simple PD controller with fixed gains. This "position policy" is found to be effective and robust.
- **Reward Function & Curriculum Learning:** Task-specific reward functions are designed using a logistic kernel to provide bounded costs, preventing the policy from favoring early termination (falling) during initial training. A "curriculum learning" approach is employed, gradually increasing the difficulty by modulating cost term coefficients and disturbances. This guides the policy from learning basic task objectives to respecting constraints and achieving natural motions.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The hybrid simulator runs roughly 1000 times faster than real time (500K time steps/sec), enabling training sessions to complete in a few hours on a single CPU/GPU (e.g., ~4 hours for locomotion, ~11 hours for fall recovery).

**Step 4: Direct Deployment on the Physical System.**
Once trained in simulation, the learned policy (the neural network's parameters) is directly transferred to the ANYmal robot's onboard PC.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The simple MLP policy requires minimal computational resources (less than 25 µs on a single CPU thread, or ~0.1% of available resources), allowing it to run at high control rates (100-200 Hz) without needing external powerful computers.
- **Generality:** The same codebase is used for different behaviors; only the network parameters and high-level task description are swapped.

This methodology combines the accuracy and speed of model-based rigid-body simulation with the adaptability and powerful function approximation capabilities of deep reinforcement learning, specifically addressing the critical challenge of complex actuator dynamics through a learned model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling results demonstrating the efficacy of its hybrid simulation and learning methodology for real-world quadrupedal locomotion, achieving skills previously unattainable on complex legged robots.

1. **Command-Conditioned Locomotion:**
 - **Robustness and Adaptability:** The learned policy robustly follows high-level body velocity commands (forward, lateral, yaw rate) provided via a joystick, adapting its gait as needed. Qualitative tests showed stable performance for minutes, even with external disturbances (Movie S2).
 - **Superior Performance over Prior Methods:** Quantitatively, the learned controller significantly outperforms the best previously existing model-based controller on ANYmal. It achieved 95% lower linear velocity error and 60% lower yaw rate error.
 - **Enhanced Efficiency:** The learned policy used considerably less torque (23% to 36% less) and less mechanical power (1.2 to 2.5 times more efficient than flying trot) compared to prior methods (Fig. 2C-E). This efficiency gain is attributed to a straighter nominal knee posture, which the learned policy can maintain robustly.
 - **Natural Gait Emergence:** The policy autonomously discovered trot-like gaits, transitioning from walking trot at low speeds to flying trot at higher speeds, without being explicitly programmed (Fig. 2A).
 - **Criticality of Actuator Model:** Ablation studies showed that policies trained with either an "ideal" actuator model (assuming infinite bandwidth and zero latency) or an analytical actuator model (even with weeks of tuning) failed to take a single step on the real robot, manifesting violent shaking (Movies S4, S5). This decisively validates the necessity and accuracy of the learned actuator network.

2. **High-Speed Locomotion:**
 - **Record-Breaking Speed:** The trained high-speed controller enabled ANYmal to run at 1.5 m/s on the physical system, surpassing the previous platform record of 1.2 m/s by 25%.
 - **Hardware Limit Exploitation:** The policy effectively pushed the hardware to its limits, utilizing maximum joint torques (40 Nm) and velocities (12 rad/s) (Fig. 3B, C). This highlights the ability of RL to discover highly performant behaviors that fully exploit robot capabilities while respecting constraints, a challenge for conventional planning methods.
 - **Novel Gait Pattern:** The learned high-speed gait was distinct, resembling a flying trot but with significantly longer and asymmetric flight phases, a pattern not commonly observed in nature and likely a highly optimized solution for this specific task (Fig. 3D, Movie S6).

3. **Recovery from a Fall:**
 - **Dynamic and Agile Recovery:** The learned recovery controller successfully restored ANYmal to an upright configuration from nine different, challenging initial fall poses, including nearly upside-down and complex configurations where the robot was resting on its own legs (Fig. 4, Movie S7).
 - **Unprecedented Complexity:** This dynamic roll-over behavior, involving multiple unspecified internal and external contacts and leveraging momentum, has never before been achieved on a real quadrupedal robot of comparable complexity with existing optimization schemes.
 - **Robustness:** The policy was successful on its very first attempt on hardware and achieved 100% success rate after minor adjustments to joint velocity constraints.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the proposed methodology enables purely simulation-trained RL policies to achieve robust, agile, efficient, and dynamic motor skills on a complex real-world legged robot, significantly outperforming prior state-of-the-art model-based approaches and enabling novel behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in the field of robotics, particularly for dynamic legged locomotion and reinforcement learning applications in the real world. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Breakthrough in Sim-to-Real Transfer for Complex Systems:** The paper effectively addresses one of the most persistent challenges in robotics: reliably transferring complex, dynamic policies learned in simulation to physical robots. By specifically tackling the modeling of highly complex actuators (SEAs) through a learned actuator network, it opens the door for applying RL to a broader class of advanced robotic platforms where accurate analytical models are intractable.
- **Superior Performance and Agility:** The demonstrated performance on the ANYmal robot, including higher precision, increased energy efficiency, record-breaking speeds (25% faster than previous records), and novel dynamic fall recovery capabilities, clearly shows that the learned policies surpass the capabilities of traditional model-based controllers that took years to develop. This indicates a potential paradigm shift in achieving optimal and agile behaviors.
- **Reduced Development Time and Cost:** The methodology drastically reduces the laborious and time-consuming process of manually designing and tuning controllers on physical hardware. Instead, policies are trained in simulation within hours or days on standard computing hardware, significantly accelerating the development cycle for new tasks and robots. This democratizes the development of advanced robotic skills.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The learned policies prove robust to various real-world challenges, including external disturbances, noisy sensor readings (modeled by noise injection during training), and even hardware changes/wear over an extended period (demonstrated over three months of heavy use). This intrinsic robustness, stemming from randomization and data-driven learning, is crucial for real-world deployment.
- **Computational Efficiency at Runtime:** Unlike many model-based approaches that require significant onboard computational power or external machines for real-time operation, the learned neural network policies are computationally lightweight, executing in microseconds on the robot's embedded CPU. This makes the approach practical for robots with limited onboard resources.
- **Generalizability of the Framework:** The presented simulation and learning framework is designed to be applicable to any rigid-body system. For new tasks, only the high-level task description (cost function, initial state distribution, randomization parameters) needs to be defined, rather than rebuilding a controller from scratch. This promises faster development of diverse skills for future robotic applications.
- **Enabling New Robotic Capabilities:** The successful demonstration of dynamic fall recovery, a behavior that requires fine coordination and leveraging complex, unspecified contacts, is a major step towards truly resilient and autonomous robots. Such a capability is essential for robots operating in unpredictable, unstructured, or hazardous environments (e.g., disaster response, exploration, logistics).

In conclusion, this work provides a compelling demonstration that advanced motor skills for complex legged robots can be learned almost entirely in simulation and effectively transferred to reality. By innovating on the crucial aspect of actuator modeling and integrating it into a robust RL framework, the authors have laid a strong foundation for future generations of agile, versatile, and autonomous legged robotic systems.
