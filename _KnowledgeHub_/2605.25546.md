# Safety-Critical Whole-Body Control for Humanoid Robots via Input-to-State Safe Control Barrier Functions

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical control framework utilizes Input-to-State Safe Control Barrier Functions (ISSf-CBFs) to enhance safety-critical whole-body control for humanoid robots by robustly transferring kinematic safety guarantees to full-order dynamics. The approach, validated through simulations with model mismatch and on a physical TOCABI robot, ensures collision avoidance and stability despite unknown disturbances and hardware uncertainties.

## Problem

- Operating humanoid robots safely in complex, human-centered environments requires robust control that handles joint limits, self-collision, and obstacle avoidance.
- Traditional safety-critical control methods like standard Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) often degrade safety guarantees when faced with unknown disturbances, such as model uncertainties or tracking errors.
- Kinematic safety assurances established at higher control levels do not reliably transfer to the full-order robot dynamics, leading to potential safety violations in real-world scenarios.

## Method

![[2605.25546-method.png]]
*The proposed hierarchical safety-critical whole-body control framework, integrating kinematic task prioritization, an ISSf-CBF safety filter, and a dynamic-level controller.*

- A hierarchical safety-critical whole-body control framework was developed, integrating a kinematic-level controller (KinWBC), an ISSf-CBF safety filter, and a dynamic-level controller (DynWBC).
- The ISSf-CBF safety filter minimally modifies nominal joint-velocity references from KinWBC to enforce kinematic safety, treating the discrepancy between a Reduced-Order Model (ROM) and the Full-Order Model (FOM) as a bounded disturbance.
- The DynWBC then computes joint torques based on these filtered references, ensuring full-body dynamic feasibility and contact stability through a Quadratic Program (QP) formulation.

## Results

- Simulations with a **20%** mass mismatch in the humanoid robot model demonstrated that the ISSf-CBF filter consistently maintained collision-free behavior, while standard CBF and eCBF baselines exhibited collision ratios of approximately **50%**.
- Real-robot experiments on the TOCABI humanoid confirmed robust contact stability and enforcement of kinematic workspace-boundary constraints during single-leg balancing (TAICHI motion) under inherent hardware uncertainties.
- During teleoperation, the ISSf-CBF filter successfully prevented robot-obstacle collisions in real-time by modifying hand motions, with the effectiveness being sensitive to the parameter `ε`.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - ISSf-CBFs can effectively compensate for bounded disturbances arising from ROM-FOM discrepancies, ensuring forward invariance of an enlarged safe set and thus reliably transferring kinematic safety to full dynamics.
> - Formulating safety constraints on a simplified kinematic ROM and modeling the ROM-FOM mismatch as an input disturbance allows for a practical and robust safety-transfer strategy.
> - Careful, conservative tuning of ISSf-CBF parameters (specifically `ε` and `α`) is crucial for achieving superior safety assurance without introducing excessive jitter or compromising task performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The operation of humanoid robots in complex, human-centered environments necessitates robust safety-critical control to manage interactions with the environment and the robot itself. This research contributes to the field of robotic safety by addressing limitations in existing control approaches, particularly concerning the degradation of kinematic safety guarantees in the presence of unknown disturbances.

Historically, safety enforcement in whole-body control has employed methods such as Artificial Potential Fields (APFs). However, APF-based approaches can lead to oscillatory behavior near constraint boundaries due to abrupt changes in potential gradients, and their scalability can be limited when handling multiple objectives and constraints through hierarchical projections.

Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) emerged as an alternative, offering a minimally invasive approach to safety enforcement by formulating safety as optimization-based inequality constraints. CBF-based methods modify nominal commands only when necessary, aiming to preserve task performance. These methods have been applied to various robotic systems, including manipulators, quadrupedal robots, and humanoid robots.

Within CBF-based methods, two main strategies exist: velocity-based inverse kinematics and acceleration-based inverse dynamics. Velocity-based inverse kinematics enforces safety at the joint-velocity level but does not explicitly account for full-body dynamics or contact stability, limiting its application in legged robots that require stable locomotion. Acceleration-based inverse dynamics controllers are generally preferred for dynamic locomotion, as they incorporate full-body dynamics and contact stability. Recent studies have integrated exponential CBFs (eCBFs) into such controllers for kinematic safety constraints in humanoids. Nevertheless, the safety guarantees of these approaches are often contingent on high-fidelity dynamic models and accurate tracking, which can be compromised by model uncertainties or tracking errors in real-world scenarios.

To address the challenge of unknown disturbances, Input-to-State Safe Control Barrier Functions (ISSf-CBFs) have been developed. ISSf-CBFs generalize the concept of safety by ensuring the forward invariance of a disturbance-dependent enlarged safe set, thereby bounding the deviation from the original safe set based on the disturbance magnitude. A notable trend in this area involves integrating ISSf-CBFs with Reduced-Order Models (ROMs). In this paradigm, safety constraints are formulated on simplified ROMs, with the discrepancy between the ROM and the Full-Order Model (FOM) treated as an input disturbance. ISSf-CBFs then facilitate the transfer of safety guarantees from the ROM to the FOM. While this approach has shown promise in fields such as autonomous driving and quadrupedal robot navigation, its extension to humanoid robots had not been extensively explored prior to this work. This paper aims to bridge that gap by developing an ISSf-CBF-based safety-critical whole-body control framework specifically for humanoid robots, addressing the transfer of safety guarantees from kinematic-level references to full-order dynamics under disturbances.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of humanoid robots in complex, human-centered environments. For these robots to operate effectively and reliably, ensuring physical safety is paramount. This involves satisfying a range of critical constraints, including joint limits, self-collision avoidance, obstacle avoidance, and adherence to workspace boundaries, all of which are essential for safe interaction with both the robot itself and its surroundings. Unsafe control actions can lead to hazardous situations, making safety-critical control a fundamental requirement.

Existing safety-critical control methods, particularly those employing safety filters to modify nominal motions, face a significant practical limitation: their safety guarantees often degrade in the presence of unknown disturbances. Such disturbances can arise from various real-robot operational factors, including model uncertainties (e.g., inaccuracies in mass or inertia parameters), trajectory-tracking errors by low-level controllers, and external perturbations (e.g., unexpected impacts or uneven terrain). When these disturbances are present, kinematic safety assurances established at a higher level of control may not be reliably transferred to the actual robot dynamics, potentially leading to safety violations.

To address this critical limitation, the core objective of this work is to develop a control framework that can consistently transfer kinematically safe motion references to the full-order dynamics level of humanoid robots. This transfer aims to ensure both safety assurance and robust task performance, even when unknown disturbances are present during real-robot operation.

Specifically, the key objectives articulated by the authors include:
- Developing a hierarchical safety-critical whole-body control framework that explicitly integrates kinematic safety, dynamic feasibility, and contact stability. This involves designing an architecture that coordinates these aspects effectively.
- Formulating a robust safety-transfer strategy from velocity-level kinematics to full-order dynamics. This strategy is based on an Input-to-State Safe Control Barrier Function (ISSf-CBF)-based safety filter combined with a conservative parameter tuning approach. The goal is to preserve safety guarantees under bounded disturbances, which traditional CBFs may not fully achieve.
- Validating the proposed framework through comprehensive simulations and real-robot experiments. These validations are intended to demonstrate reliable safety enforcement under conditions of model mismatch and across diverse operational scenarios, including locomotion, teleoperation, and single-leg balancing with hand control. The scope of validation ensures the practical applicability of the theoretical contributions.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to enhance the reliability and safety of humanoid robots, facilitating their broader and safer deployment in complex, dynamic, and human-centric environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology presents a hierarchical safety-critical whole-body control framework for humanoid robots. This architecture integrates three main components: a kinematic-level whole-body controller (KinWBC), an Input-to-State Safe Control Barrier Function (ISSf-CBF) safety filter, and a dynamic-level whole-body controller (DynWBC).

#### 3.1. Overall Hierarchical Control Architecture

The control flow begins with high-level task commands (e.g., locomotion, manipulation, teleoperation). These commands are processed by KinWBC, which generates nominal joint-motion references. These references are then fed into the ISSf-CBF safety filter, which minimally modifies them to ensure kinematic safety under bounded disturbances. The filtered references are subsequently passed to DynWBC, which computes joint torques to track these references while enforcing full-body dynamic feasibility and contact stability.

#### 3.2. Kinematic-level Whole-Body Controller (KinWBC)

KinWBC is responsible for generating nominal joint-position and velocity references (`q_des`, `q̇_des`). It operates by solving hierarchical differential inverse kinematics for a set of prioritized operational-space tasks. This prioritization, achieved through null-space projections, allows lower-priority tasks to be pursued without degrading the performance of higher-priority tasks. The desired joint velocity `q̇_des` is computed iteratively, and the desired joint position `q_des` is obtained by integration. Unlike previous approaches that directly map `q_des` and `q̇_des` to acceleration commands for DynWBC, this framework introduces the ISSf-CBF safety filter as an intermediate step.

#### 3.3. ISSf-CBF-based Safety Filter

This is the central component for ensuring safety under disturbances. It modifies the nominal joint-velocity references from KinWBC (`q̇_des`) into kinematically safe references (`q̇_safe`).
- **Reduced-Order Models (ROMs):** To manage the complexity of full-order dynamics while accounting for discrepancies, safety constraints are formulated on an idealized ROM. The full-order state `x` (comprising `q` and `q̇`) and input `u` (joint torques `τ`) define the Full-Order Model (FOM). A state projection map `π(x)` defines the reduced-order state `y`. For this work, the ROM is a whole-body velocity kinematic model, where the reduced-order state is the joint position (`y = q`), and the reduced-order input is the safety-filtered joint velocity (`v = q̇_safe`).
- **Discrepancy as Disturbance:** The actual reduced-order dynamics (`ẏ`) derived from the closed-loop FOM can be expressed as `f(y) + g(y)v + d`, where `d` represents the discrepancy between the projected closed-loop FOM dynamics and the idealized ROM. This `d` term, modeled as an unknown but bounded disturbance, accounts for factors like modeling errors, external perturbations, and tracking mismatches.
- **ISSf-CBF Condition:** The core of the safety filter is the ISSf-CBF condition applied to the disturbed ROM. For each safety constraint defined by a continuously differentiable function `h(q) ≥ 0`, the filter enforces:
 `∇h(q)ᵀq̇ ≥ -α(h(q)) + (1/ε) ||∇h(q)||²`
 Here, `α(·)` is a class-K function (linear in this case, `αh`) that drives the system towards the safe set, and `ε > 0` is a user-defined parameter that helps compensate for the bounded disturbance `d` by ensuring forward invariance of an *enlarged* safe set. A smaller `ε` generally leads to a tighter safe set.
- **QP Formulation:** The safety filter is implemented as a minimally invasive Quadratic Program (QP):
 `q̇_safe = arg min_q̇ ||q̇ - q̇_des||²`
 subject to a set of linear inequality constraints derived from the ISSf-CBF condition for various safety requirements.
- **Kinematic Safety Constraints:**
 - **Joint Limits:** Defined by `h_JL,min/max(q) = q_i - q_min/max_i`. The ISSf-CBF condition translates to velocity bounds on each joint `q̇_i`.
 - **Self-Collision Avoidance:** Modeled by signed distances `h_SC_AB(q)` between collision spheres/capsules of robot links. The condition involves the Jacobian of the signed distance `J_AB(q)`.
 - **Object-Collision Avoidance:** Similar to self-collision, `h_OC_AO(q)` represents the signed distance between robot links and environmental obstacles. It includes obstacle velocity `v_O` and its Jacobian.
 - **Workspace-Boundary Constraints:** Defined by maximum allowable distances `h_WS_AB(q)` between designated robot points (e.g., shoulder and hand).
- **Parameter Tuning:** The paper notes that practical tuning involves initializing `α` and `ε` with small values and gradually increasing them to achieve adequate performance and safety transfer.

#### 3.4. Dynamic-level Whole-Body Controller (DynWBC)

DynWBC takes the safety-filtered kinematic references (`q_safe = q + q̇_safe dt`, `q̈_safe`) and computes the optimal joint torques (`τ_opt`) and contact reaction wrenches (`F_c`).
- **QP Formulation:** It solves a QP that minimizes a weighted sum of tracking errors (generalized acceleration `q̈` to `q̈_safe`, contact reaction wrenches `F_c` to `F_des_c`), changes in joint torque (`τ` to `τ_prev`), and an acceleration energy regularization term (`q̈ᵀM(q)q̈`).
- **Constraints:** The QP is subject to two main constraints:
 - **Contact Stability:** Linear inequalities (`U F_c ≤ 0`) derived from the linearized 6D contact wrench cone, ensuring no lift-off, no slip, and bounded contact moments.
 - **Full-Body Dynamics:** The robot's equations of motion (`M(q)q̈ + h(q,q̇) = Sᵀτ + J_c(q)ᵀF_c`).
- **Final Torque Command:** The motor torque command `τ_cmd` applied to the actuators includes `τ_opt` and additional PD feedback terms (`K_p(q_safe - q) + K_d(q̇_safe - q̇)`) to compensate for friction and unmodeled disturbances.

By combining these layers, the framework aims to ensure that kinematic safety constraints, robustified by ISSf-CBFs against disturbances, are respected at the dynamic level, leading to safe and stable robot operation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated the proposed ISSf-CBF safety filter through both simulation and real-robot experiments, demonstrating its ability to transfer kinematic-level safety to full-order humanoid dynamics under various conditions, including model mismatch and real-hardware uncertainties.

#### 4.1. Simulations to Validate Safety Transfer from ROM to FOM

Simulations were conducted using the humanoid robot TOCABI model in MuJoCo, with a 20% mass increase in the simulated robot compared to the URDF model used by the controller, mimicking model uncertainty. The proposed ISSf-CBF method was compared against `w/o-CBF` (no safety filter), `CBF` (standard CBF without the ISSf term), and `eCBF` (exponential CBF applied at the acceleration level in DynWBC).

- **Safety Assurance under Model Mismatch:**
 - **Hand-Trajectory Tracking:** In a scenario where hands were commanded to track circular trajectories that would nominally collide with the torso, the `w/o-CBF` case resulted in the left hand penetrating the torso by over 5 cm. In contrast, the `ISSf-CBF` filter maintained a positive signed-distance barrier value, indicating successful self-collision avoidance throughout the motion.
 - **Locomotion:** During lateral steps designed to induce foot-to-foot collisions, the `w/o-CBF` case showed feet penetrating each other by over 3 cm. The `ISSf-CBF` filter consistently kept the signed-distance barrier value positive, preventing self-collisions.
 - *Conclusion:* These results indicate that the proposed ISSf-CBF filter effectively transfers kinematic safety constraints to the FOM controller, maintaining collision-free behavior despite significant model mismatch.

- **Comparison of Safety Assurance Performance with Baseline Methods:**
 - The performance of all methods was evaluated in the hand-trajectory-tracking scenario by varying the CBF parameter `α` and the ISSf parameter `ε`. The metric used was the "remaining collision ratio," defined as the percentage of collisions that still occurred (where `h_SC_AB(q) < 0`) after applying the safety filter.
 - **`w/o-CBF`:** As expected, resulted in collisions.
 - **`CBF` baseline:** Showed a collision ratio of approximately 50% across tested `α` values (1 to 50 s⁻¹), indicating that varying `α` did not significantly improve collision avoidance in this scenario. Large `α` values (`≥ 30`) also caused severe jitter in joint-velocity commands.
 - **`eCBF` baseline:** Similarly exhibited an average collision ratio of about 50% in its evaluated range (`α ≥ 20`). Lower `α` values were excluded due to substantial deviation from the reference trajectory.
 - **`ISSf-CBF` (proposed):** Demonstrated superior performance, remaining collision-free when `ε` and `α` were set to relatively low values (e.g., `ε = 10`, `α ≤ 30`). At higher `α` values (`≥ 35`), the remaining collision ratio increased due to jitter. Increasing `ε` also led to an increased collision ratio, suggesting that an excessively large `ε` rendered the disturbance compensation insufficient.
 - *Conclusion:* The ISSf-CBF filter provided superior safety assurance under bounded ROM–FOM disturbances and model mismatch compared to the baseline methods, especially with appropriate tuning of `α` and `ε`.

#### 4.2. Real-Robot Experiments to Validate Safety Transfer from ROM to FOM

Experiments were conducted on the physical TOCABI humanoid robot, which inherently presented discrepancies from the nominal model (e.g., a 6% mass discrepancy).

- **Contact Stability and Workspace-Boundary Enforcement during Single-Leg Balancing (TAICHI Motion):**
 - Scenario: The robot performed a TAICHI-like motion involving single-leg balancing and hand movements towards workspace boundaries.
 - Observation: The robot successfully maintained balance, with the measured Zero Moment Point (ZMP) remaining within the support polygon of the supporting foot. Without the optimal torque (`τ_opt`) from DynWBC (i.e., relying only on joint PD feedback), the robot fell, underscoring the importance of DynWBC for dynamic stability.
 - Observation: Both hands accurately tracked their reference trajectories until they approached the defined maximum allowable distance (`d_max = 62 cm`) from their respective shoulders. The ISSf-CBF safety filter then modified the hand commands, preventing the hands from exceeding the workspace boundaries.
 - *Conclusion:* The hierarchical framework successfully maintained contact stability and enforced kinematic workspace-boundary constraints in real time on a physical humanoid robot with inherent hardware uncertainties.

- **Obstacle-Avoidance Enforcement during Teleoperation:**
 - Scenario: A user teleoperated the robot to manipulate objects, with an external obstacle placed in the path. The objective was to evaluate obstacle avoidance while preserving the user's intended motion.
 - Observation (with `ε_OC_AO = 30.0`): When the user commanded the right hand towards the obstacle, the ISSf-CBF safety filter effectively modified the motion, keeping the robot's hand link collision geometry within the collision-free set (`h_AO ≥ 0`), thereby preventing collisions. This was demonstrated across multiple trials.
 - Observation (with `ε_OC_AO = 50.0`): With a larger `ε` value, the obstacle-avoidance barrier value fell below zero in multiple trials, resulting in up to 8 cm of penetration between the hand and obstacle collision geometries.
 - *Conclusion:* The real-robot teleoperation experiment confirmed the ISSf-CBF filter's ability to enforce obstacle avoidance in real time under real-robot uncertainties. It also highlighted the importance of appropriate `ε` parameter tuning for effective safety guarantees.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that the proposed ISSf-CBF-based hierarchical control framework provides robust safety assurance for humanoid robots operating under practical conditions, including model mismatch and real-world disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of humanoid robotics by addressing a critical challenge: ensuring reliable safety guarantees in the presence of unknown disturbances. The degradation of kinematic safety assurances due to model uncertainties, tracking errors, and external perturbations has been a notable limitation in deploying humanoid robots in complex, human-centered environments.

The development of a hierarchical safety-critical whole-body control framework based on Input-to-State Safe Control Barrier Functions (ISSf-CBFs) offers a robust solution. By integrating a kinematic-level controller, an ISSf-CBF safety filter, and a dynamic-level controller, the framework establishes a comprehensive approach to simultaneously manage task execution, kinematic safety, dynamic feasibility, and contact stability. The core innovation lies in the use of ISSf-CBFs with conservatively tuned parameters to explicitly account for and bound the effects of unknown disturbances, thereby enabling the reliable transfer of kinematic safety guarantees to the full-order dynamics level.

The validated performance in both simulation and real-robot experiments, across diverse scenarios like locomotion, teleoperation, and single-leg balancing with hand control, underscores the practical applicability of this work. The demonstrated improvements in safety margins under model mismatch and the consistent enforcement of multiple safety constraints in real time highlight the framework's robustness.

The potential impact of this research is substantial:
- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Safety:** By providing stronger, disturbance-aware safety guarantees, this work moves humanoid robots closer to safe and autonomous operation in unstructured and dynamic environments where human presence is common. This could facilitate deployment in service, assistance, and industrial applications.
- **Bridging Theory and Practice:** The explicit consideration of ROM-FOM discrepancies and their compensation via ISSf-CBFs helps bridge the gap between idealized control theories and the realities of physical robot systems, which are always subject to unmodeled dynamics and disturbances.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies future directions, particularly the integration of ISSf-CBF-based safety filters with reinforcement-learning-based locomotion and whole-body control policies. This suggests a pathway to developing highly robust and adaptive robot behaviors that also maintain explicit safety guarantees, combining the learning capabilities of AI with formal safety assurance methods. This could lead to humanoid robots capable of learning complex tasks while inherently operating within safe boundaries, even in the face of unexpected situations.
- **Generalizability:** While focused on humanoid robots, the principles of hierarchical control, ROM-FOM discrepancy modeling, and ISSf-CBF application under bounded disturbances may be transferable to other complex robotic systems operating in safety-critical contexts.

In summary, this work represents an advancement in safety-critical control for humanoid robots, providing a robust and experimentally validated framework that addresses a key vulnerability of previous approaches and paves the way for safer, more reliable human-robot interaction and collaboration.
