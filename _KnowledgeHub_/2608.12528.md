# Excitation-Supervised Closed-Loop Self-Calibration and Target Seeking for an Unknown-Pose Range-Bearing Relay

> [!summary] Summary
> A vehicle seeking a hidden target via an unknown-pose range-bearing relay must know when its own motion has calibrated the relay. This work shows the trajectory-spread margin S_v simultaneously bounds seed accuracy, decomposes target variance, and prices circular excitation, then uses it to supervise an excitation-reset controller that projects seeking away from the excitation push, holding yaw RMSE below 0.0191 rad at 100% success.

## Problem

- Prior identifiability results classify a stored trajectory window as sufficient only after the fact, giving an online controller no rule for when calibration is trustworthy.
- Classical persistency-of-excitation conditions are spectral requirements imposed offline on reference signals, not scalar certificates a supervisor can check online from stored measurement packets.
- Fixed decaying exploration schedules stop exciting at a preset rate, silently failing when the decay outruns the unknown time needed to reach adequate excitation.

## Method

- Quantitative excitation theory: the ==spread margin== bounds constructive two-view seed error by noise over root-spread, decomposes target covariance into packet averaging plus calibration propagation, and yields a ==closed-form circular excitation budget==.
- Algorithm 1 supervises a ==damped Gauss-Newton estimator==: it resets the decaying swirl excitation epoch whenever the stored window's ==spread certificate== falls below an accuracy-driven threshold set from desired yaw standard deviation.
- An ==underexcited-phase projection== clips only the seeking component opposing the excitation push direction, closing the gap between proof and implementation and enabling a finite excitation-acquisition proposition under sampled ==zero-order-hold actuation==.

## Results

- Over **100** paired trials with fast decay, the fixed schedule's yaw RMSE rose **0.010** to **0.065 rad** and success fell to **56%**, while supervision held **0.0095-0.0191 rad** at **100%**.
- Threshold ablation matched predictions at every level: measured yaw RMSE **0.0239**, **0.0187**, **0.0125**, **0.0053 rad** for thresholds **0.05-9.04**, costing **18.3** to **109.2** packets of certification time.
- ROS 2/Gazebo software-in-the-loop over ten seeds gave **3.1 mm** target and **0.7 mrad** yaw RMSE; sensing delay shifted certification from packet **3** to **6**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Calibration failure can be silent in the task metric: target error stayed near 3.5 mm while relay yaw degraded sixfold, so evaluate frame accuracy separately.
> - A measured scalar richness certificate can replace both offline exploration schedules and matrix-valued observability optimization, provided its threshold is derived from an accuracy requirement rather than tuned.
> - Ordinary closed-loop motion often supplies excitation incidentally, so supervision earns its 5% arrival-time cost only when transients are absent or the geometry stays degenerate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in autonomous systems: a vehicle attempting to locate a hidden target through an intermediate relay beacon whose position and orientation (yaw) are initially unknown. This problem necessitates the self-calibration of the relay's pose simultaneously with target seeking. Prior work by the same author established a static identifiability condition, demonstrating that two distinct vehicle-relative observations are sufficient to recover the relay's yaw, position, and the hidden target's global coordinates. However, this prior result was an *after-the-fact* classification of a stored trajectory window, not an online decision-making framework for a closed-loop control system.

This paper extends that foundational identifiability by integrating it into a dynamic, online control strategy. It draws parallels with the concept of "persistency of excitation" in adaptive control, where parameter estimates require sufficiently rich regressor signals for convergence. While classical adaptive control often imposes spectral conditions offline, this work, similar to "concurrent learning", focuses on a verifiable *online* condition—the scalar trajectory-spread margin $S_v$—monitored from recorded data.

The approach also relates to "observability-aware trajectory optimization" and "active SLAM", which aim to design motion that maximizes observability metrics for state estimation or self-calibration, often involving complex matrix-valued information objectives. This paper takes a complementary route by leveraging a relay-specific scalar certificate ($S_v$) that is less computationally intensive than online trajectory optimization and matrix-valued information objectives. The use of an exploratory envelope also resonates with "extremum-seeking control" and adaptive source localization, which inject periodic dithers or excitation to maintain information. The concept of an "excitation budget" is consistent with research on optimal sensor-target localization geometries [11–13]. By providing a quantitative, certifiable, and implementable solution for online excitation management, this work bridges the gap between static identifiability analysis and robust closed-loop autonomous operation in an uncertain environment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to enable autonomous vehicles to effectively seek hidden targets when relying on an unknown-pose range-bearing relay. The core challenge is that the vehicle must continuously decide, during online operation, whether its own motion has generated sufficient data to accurately calibrate the relay, and consequently, to trust its estimate of the target's location.

The paper identifies a critical gap in existing knowledge: while previous work established the static identifiability of the relay and target given sufficient observations, it did not provide an operational framework for a vehicle to *acquire* these sufficient observations or to *supervise* its control actions based on the quality of its self-calibration. A vehicle operating online requires a dynamic mechanism to determine if its stored trajectory data is "excited enough" to confidently pursue the target, or if it should temporarily prioritize exploratory motion to improve calibration.

The key objectives of this work are articulated through five distinct contributions:

1. **Quantitative Excitation Theory:** To develop a theoretical framework that quantifies the utility of the trajectory-spread margin ($S_v$). This includes demonstrating its role in bounding constructive-seed error, decomposing target-estimate variance into averaging and calibration-propagation terms, and providing an explicit excitation budget for circular motion. A significant objective is to derive an accuracy-driven rule for selecting the $S_v$ threshold based on a desired calibration uncertainty level, moving beyond heuristic choices.
2. **Excitation-Supervised Algorithm Design:** To formalize an excitation-supervised estimation and target-seeking algorithm (Algorithm 1) that explicitly incorporates an online supervision loop. This loop is triggered solely by the $S_v$ certificate and integrates a mechanism to project the target-seeking input away from the excitation's push direction when calibration is insufficient.
3. **Formal Guarantees of Acquisition and Recovery:** To provide theoretical proofs for the algorithm's performance. This includes a proposition demonstrating finite-time acquisition of the required excitation under explicit sampling assumptions, ensuring the supervision rule itself can accumulate any necessary spread. Additionally, a local recovery proposition aims to show that, in a noiseless model and with positive excitation decay, estimator convergence leads to target-seeking convergence after certification.
4. **Software-in-the-Loop Validation:** To validate the proposed approach through a ROS 2/Gazebo software-in-the-loop experiment. This objective is to demonstrate the supervised loop's ability to steer a physics-integrated vehicle through realistic conditions, including message transport and configurable sensing delays.
5. **Empirical Performance Analysis:** To conduct comprehensive empirical evaluations through a 100-trial paired Monte Carlo decay-rate sweep and a spread-threshold ablation study. These experiments aim to validate the accuracy-driven threshold rule against population root-mean-square error targets, quantify the excitation cost, expose the domain of validity, and analyze scenarios where incidental motion naturally provides excitation.

In essence, the research aims to transform a static identifiability result into a robust, certifiable, and practically implementable online control strategy for autonomous target seeking with an unknown-pose relay.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on leveraging the trajectory-spread margin ($S_v$) as a real-time scalar certificate for relay self-calibration within a closed-loop control system.

**Measurement Model and Identifiability Recap:**
The paper begins by recapping the single-beacon model from previous work. A vehicle at pose $q_k$ receives noisy range-bearing packets ($\tilde{\ell}_v$, $\tilde{\ell}_t$) from a relay beacon whose position ($x$) and yaw ($\psi$) are unknown. The target ($p$) is reported by this relay in its local frame. The state to be estimated is $z = (p, x, \psi)$. Estimation involves minimizing a batch objective function $J(z)$ based on native range-bearing residuals, weighted by measurement precision. Fact 1 from is reiterated: two distinct vehicle-relative observations are necessary and sufficient for unique recovery of $p$, $x$, and $\psi$.

**Quantitative Excitation Theory (Section III):**
The core of the methodology lies in the theoretical characterization of $S_v$:
- **Spread Margin ($S_v$):** Defined as the centered local vehicle-vector spread over a window of $K$ packets, $S_v = \sum_{k=1}^K \Vert \ell_v_k - \bar{\ell}_v \Vert^2$. Lemma 1 establishes its link to yaw perturbation and, in a noiseless model, to the spread of vehicle positions.
- **Constructive-Seed Accuracy (Proposition 1):** This proposition quantifies how seed error (for yaw, relay position, and target position) scales with measurement noise ($\epsilon$) and the distance between two distinct local vehicle packets ($d_v$), where $d_v^2 \propto S_v$ for two packets. This provides a direct link between $S_v$ and the initial accuracy of the estimator.
- **Local-Vector Variance Decomposition (Corollary 1):** For a linearized model with isotropic noise, the variance of the estimated yaw ($\mathrm{var}(\hat{\psi})$) is shown to be $\sigma^2/S_v$. The covariance of the target estimate ($\mathrm{cov}(\hat{p})$) is decomposed into an averaging term (related to packet count) and a calibration error propagation term, which is controlled by $S_v$. This highlights $S_v$'s role in the precision of the calibration-propagated target estimate.
- **Excitation Budget (Corollary 2):** For circular exploratory motion, $S_v$ is expressed in a closed form: $S_v = K\rho^2(1 - \Vert\bar{u}\Vert^2)$. For equally spaced positions over a full revolution, this simplifies to $S_v = K\rho^2$. This provides an explicit "budget" for acquiring a desired $S_v$ based on the number of packets ($K$) and excitation radius ($\rho$).
- **Accuracy-Driven Threshold (Remark 1):** The theory directly informs the selection of the threshold $\bar{S}$. A desired yaw standard deviation ($\epsilon_\psi$) can be translated into a required $\bar{S} \ge \sigma^2/\epsilon_\psi^2$, providing a principled, accuracy-driven approach rather than heuristic tuning. The effect of native range-bearing noise is handled by using an effective noise standard deviation ($\sigma_{\text{eff}}$).

**Excitation-Supervised Estimation and Target Seeking (Section IV):**
The paper proposes **Algorithm 1**, which integrates the $S_v$ certificate into a closed-loop control strategy:
- **Control Law:** The vehicle's velocity command $u$ is a sum of a nominal target-seeking component ($u_{\text{seek}}$) and an exploratory excitation component ($u_{\text{exp}}$).
 - $u_{\text{exp}}$ is a decaying circular motion: $A e^{-\lambda(t-t_0)} [\cos \omega t, \sin \omega t]^T$.
 - $u_{\text{seek}}$ is a proportional control law: $-k(q - \hat{p})$.
- **Supervision Mechanism:** The key innovation lies in how $u_{\text{seek}}$ is handled.
 - If $S_v(W) < \bar{S}$ (underexcited): The excitation epoch $t_0$ is reset to the current time, restarting the decaying excitation. Critically, $u_{\text{seek}}$ is *projected* away from the excitation's current "push direction" ($n(t)$) to ensure it does not counteract the exploratory motion. This is formalized as $\Pi_{H(t)}[-k(q - \hat{p})]$.
 - If $S_v(W) \ge \bar{S}$ (sufficiently excited): Unrestricted target seeking proceeds, and $u_{\text{seek}}$ is applied without projection.
- **Estimation Flow:** The estimator uses the Gauss-Newton method with warm-starting and damping. It remains inactive until two distinct views are available, then uses the constructive seed, and refines estimates with subsequent packets.
- **Finite Acquisition of Excitation (Proposition 2):** This proposition proves that, under specific conditions (bounded inter-sample times, the projected seeking law, sufficiently fast sampling relative to excitation, and cumulative window), the supervision rule guarantees that $S_v(W)$ will reach $\bar{S}$ within a finite time ($T^\star$). The projection (9) is explicitly designed to satisfy the assumptions of this proof, linking the theoretical guarantee directly to the implemented controller.
- **Closed-Loop Recovery (Proposition 3):** This proposition states that if $S_v \ge \bar{S}$ and the excitation eventually decays ($\lambda > 0$), then the rank condition holds, the constructive seed is well-defined, and local convergence of the estimator leads to convergence of the vehicle's position to the target, via input-to-state stability (ISS) analysis of the tracking error dynamics.

**Validation:**
Validation involves five main parts:
1. **Closed-loop simulation:** Demonstrates the algorithm's behavior, error reduction, and $S_v$ evolution over time in an idealized kinematic model.
2. **ROS 2/Gazebo Software-in-the-Loop:** Evaluates the algorithm in a more realistic environment, accounting for physics integration, message transport, and sensing delays.
3. **Baseline Comparison:** Compares the supervised algorithm against a fixed decaying-swirl excitation schedule and an information-gradient-based schedule, particularly in scenarios where incidental excitation is minimal.
4. **Decay-Rate Sweep:** A 100-trial paired Monte Carlo study to assess robustness across varying excitation decay rates ($\lambda$).
5. **Spread-Threshold Ablation:** Examines the impact of varying the $\bar{S}$ threshold on accuracy and excitation effort.
6. **Target Seeking with Incidental Excitation:** Investigates scenarios where the vehicle's natural target-seeking motion implicitly provides sufficient excitation.

The paper also discusses **finite-memory deployment (Section VI)** for rolling-window implementations, maintaining the accuracy-driven threshold, and a **statistical validation protocol (Section VII)** outlining the use of paired trials, bootstrap confidence intervals, and separate accuracy criteria for target and calibration errors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical and empirical findings supporting the efficacy of the excitation-supervised closed-loop control strategy.

**Theoretical Findings:**
- **Multi-faceted Role of $S_v$:** The spread margin $S_v$ is quantitatively established as a critical metric. It not only governs identifiability but also directly bounds the error of constructive initial estimates (Proposition 1), decomposes target-estimate variance into averaging and calibration-propagation components (Corollary 1), and provides an explicit excitation budget for circular motion (Corollary 2). This provides a comprehensive understanding of its impact on calibration quality.
- **Accuracy-Driven Threshold:** The paper demonstrates that the threshold $\bar{S}$ for excitation can be selected rigorously based on a desired calibration-accuracy level (e.g., yaw standard deviation $\epsilon_\psi$), specifically $\bar{S} \geq \sigma^2 / \epsilon_\psi^2$ (Remark 1). This moves threshold selection from heuristics to a design choice.
- **Guaranteed Excitation Acquisition:** Proposition 2 provides a formal proof that the proposed supervision rule, in conjunction with a projected seeking input (9), provably acquires any required $S_v$ within a finite and explicit time, given specific sampling assumptions. This ensures that the exploration phase is not merely heuristic but a guaranteed process for achieving sufficient excitation.
- **Closed-Loop Recovery Guarantee:** Proposition 3 establishes that if the excitation threshold is met, and the estimator enters its local basin of convergence, the system achieves convergence of the target estimate ($\hat{p} \to p$) and the vehicle's position to the target ($q \to p$) in the noiseless model with decaying excitation. This provides a theoretical underpinning for the overall system stability and performance.

**Empirical Findings (Validation):**
- **Closed-Loop Simulation Performance:** The algorithm effectively guides the vehicle to the target, reducing initial target error from 1.415 m to 0.0026 m and yaw error from 0.750 rad to 0.00119 rad within 120 packets. The $S_v$ trace shows an initial increase as excitation is acquired, followed by a leveling off as target seeking dominates, confirming the supervisory mechanism.
- **ROS 2/Gazebo Software-in-the-Loop Robustness:** The algorithm demonstrated comparable performance in a more realistic environment incorporating physics, message transport, and sensing delays. While delays shifted the certification time (e.g., from packet 3 to packet 6 with a two-packet delay), the final estimation accuracy remained similar (e.g., target RMSE 3.1 mm, yaw RMSE 0.7 mrad across ten seeds). This indicates robustness to real-world system complexities.
- **Superiority over Fixed Schedules:** In scenarios where initial motion provides minimal incidental excitation (e.g., vehicle starts at the true target, "no-transient"), fixed decaying excitation schedules and even information-gradient methods failed to achieve adequate calibration, resulting in high yaw errors (e.g., 0.093-0.109 rad) and poor relay position estimates. In contrast, the supervised policy maintained its excitation loop until $S_v$ cleared, achieving significantly lower errors (e.g., 0.0070 rad yaw error). This highlights the crucial role of active supervision when natural exploration is insufficient.
- **Robustness to Decay-Rate Mismatch (Decay-Rate Sweep):** A 100-trial paired Monte Carlo study demonstrated that the fixed excitation schedule's performance degraded monotonically as its decay rate ($\lambda$) increased, leading to a rise in yaw RMSE from 0.010 to 0.065 rad and a drop in success rate from 100% to 56% for fast decay. Notably, target tracking error remained insensitive, confirming Corollary 1's prediction about silent calibration failure. The supervised controller, however, consistently kept yaw RMSE between 0.0095 and 0.0191 rad with 100% success across all tested decay rates, demonstrating its ability to adapt to unknown excitation requirements. The average number of retriggered packets remained relatively constant (30.8–31.7), illustrating the consistent acquisition mechanism.
- **Trade-offs with Threshold (Spread-Threshold Ablation):** The ablation study confirmed that achieving higher calibration accuracy (lower $\epsilon_\psi$, corresponding to a larger $\bar{S}$) requires increased excitation effort and longer certification times. For instance, increasing $\bar{S}$ from 0.05 to 9.04 improved yaw RMSE from 0.0239 to 0.0053 rad but raised mean certification time from 18.3 to 109.2 packets. This validates the accuracy-driven design rule and quantifies its cost.
- **Incidental Excitation:** When the vehicle started significantly away from the true target, the target-seeking transient itself provided sufficient excitation. In such cases, both fixed and supervised policies achieved statistically indistinguishable final accuracy, with only a marginal (5%) increase in arrival time for the supervised policy due to initial input projection. This indicates that supervision is most critical when incidental exploration from the primary task is lacking.
- **Decoupling of Calibration and Task Success:** The studies consistently showed that target RMSE could remain low even when relay calibration (position and yaw) was poor under fixed schedules. This emphasizes the operational importance of separately evaluating calibration trustworthiness, as task success alone does not guarantee a trustworthy calibrated frame for future use or other applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of autonomous navigation and target seeking, particularly in environments with uncertain sensor infrastructure.

**Theoretical Advancements:**
The paper establishes a robust theoretical foundation for online excitation management in self-calibration problems. By quantifying the role of the trajectory-spread margin ($S_v$) in relation to estimation accuracy and providing a principled method for setting excitation thresholds, it moves beyond heuristic approaches. The finite-time acquisition guarantee, proven for the *implemented* controller (including the crucial projected seeking input), is a notable contribution that closes the gap between theoretical analysis and practical robotic system design. This guarantee offers strong assurances for system designers that necessary excitation will be acquired within predictable bounds.

**Practical Implementability and Robustness:**
The proposed Algorithm 1 offers a pragmatic and implementable solution for real-time robotic systems. Unlike complex online trajectory optimization schemes that may be computationally intensive, the scalar $S_v$ certificate provides an efficient and easily verifiable condition for triggering exploration. The validation through ROS 2/Gazebo software-in-the-loop experiments demonstrates its robustness to typical real-world complexities such as sensing delays and message transport, making it a viable candidate for deployment on physical robots.

**Addressing a Critical Operational Gap:**
The work effectively addresses the "probe-versus-exploit" dilemma in target seeking by providing a certifiable mechanism to switch between exploration (to improve calibration) and exploitation (to seek the target). The empirical results, especially the decay-rate sweep, highlight the vulnerability of unsupervised or fixed excitation schedules to calibration failure that is "silent" in the primary task metric (target tracking error). This distinction is operationally crucial: merely reaching a target does not certify the accuracy of the underlying sensor calibration, which might be vital for subsequent tasks, multi-robot coordination, or integration into downstream planners. The supervised approach ensures that the recovered relay frame is trustworthy.

**Broadening Applicability:**
While demonstrated for a specific range-bearing relay scenario, the underlying principles of excitation supervision, accuracy-driven thresholds, and guaranteed acquisition could potentially be extended to other self-calibration and active sensing problems in robotics, such as visual-inertial odometry calibration or sensor network deployment. The paper's explicit consideration of finite-memory deployment also paves the way for resource-constrained applications.

**Future Directions:**
The identified future work, including three-dimensional extensions of the relay geometry, greedy $S_v$-maximizing planners, and physical robot validation, indicates that this research provides a strong foundation for continued development in reliable autonomous systems operating in partially unknown environments.
