# ThinkAct: Vision-Language-Action Reasoning via Reinforced Visual Latent Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> A dual-system framework named ThinkAct enables Multimodal Large Language Models to perform long-horizon planning and guide robotic actions through reinforced visual latent planning. This approach achieves success rates up to 15.5% higher than baselines on SimplerEnv robot manipulation tasks and demonstrates emergent capabilities like few-shot adaptation and self-correction.

## Problem

- End-to-end Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often lack explicit reasoning, limiting their ability to handle complex instructions and long-horizon tasks.
- Supervised chain-of-thought (CoT) methods for VLA require expensive, high-quality reasoning annotations, hindering scalability and generalization.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) for MLLM reasoning in embodied tasks typically uses abstract, QA-style rewards that lack direct grounding in physical actions and visual outcomes.

## Method

- A ==dual-system framework== integrates a 'slow thinking' ==Multimodal Large Language Model== (MLLM) for high-level planning and a 'fast control' action model for low-level execution.
- The MLLM is fine-tuned using ==reinforced visual latent planning==, employing novel ==action-aligned rewards== that measure visual goal completion and trajectory consistency. This is the core of **ThinkAct**.
- MLLM reasoning is compressed into a compact ==visual plan latent== which explicitly conditions the downstream action model, effectively bridging high-level deliberation and low-level control.

## Results

- ThinkAct achieved improved success rates on robot manipulation benchmarks, including a **15.5%** increase over its base policy on SimplerEnv Google-VM split and an **84.4%** overall success rate on LIBERO, outperforming CoT-VLA and OpenVLA.
- It demonstrated higher accuracy on embodied reasoning tasks, achieving **48.2%** on EgoPlan-Bench2 and a BLEU score of **59.8** on RoboVQA, surpassing competitive MLLMs like GPT-4V and fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL.
- The framework exhibited emergent capabilities such as few-shot adaptation to novel skills with limited demonstrations and the ability to self-correct from execution errors by detecting failures and generating revised plans.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reinforcement learning with action-aligned visual feedback effectively grounds MLLM reasoning in embodied tasks, enabling robust long-horizon planning.
> - Compressing complex MLLM reasoning into a visual plan latent provides a crucial interface for explicitly steering and enhancing downstream action execution.
> - A dual-system architecture with asynchronous reasoning and acting can elicit emergent capabilities such as few-shot adaptation to new tasks and self-correction from execution failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI, particularly Vision-Language-Action (VLA) reasoning, is a rapidly evolving area aimed at enabling AI agents (such as robots) to understand multimodal instructions, plan complex actions, and interact adaptively with dynamic physical environments. ThinkAct positions itself at the forefront of this landscape by addressing critical limitations of existing approaches.

**Current Landscape and Limitations:**

1. **End-to-End VLA Models:** Many recent VLA models, exemplified by works like OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) and TraceVLA (Zheng et al., 2024), initialize from pre-trained multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and fine-tune them on large-scale robotic datasets. While promising for short-horizon tasks, these models typically map visual and textual inputs directly to low-level actions in an end-to-end fashion. This direct mapping often bypasses explicit reasoning or structured planning, limiting their ability to handle complex instructions, generalize to diverse visual scenes, perform long-horizon planning, or adapt to out-of-distribution scenarios.
2. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting for VLA:** Inspired by the success of CoT prompting in general LLMs (Wei et al., 2022), some works (e.g., ECoT by Zawalski et al., 2024; RAD by Clark et al., 2025; CoT-VLA by Zhao et al., 2025) have integrated explicit step-by-step reasoning into VLA models. These methods typically involve generating intermediate subgoals or decomposed plans, often through prompting off-the-shelf MLLMs, and then supervising VLA models to predict these reasoning traces. The primary limitation here is the high cost and difficulty of producing high-quality, diverse reasoning annotations, which can lead to models overfitting to specific visual scenes or reasoning patterns and hindering scalability.
3. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLM Reasoning:** More recent developments have explored RL to incentivize reasoning behaviors in LLMs by rewarding exploration of thinking traces rather than relying solely on supervised CoT annotations (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). While this paradigm has been extended to multimodal tasks (Feng et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025), these approaches often rely on abstract, QA-style reward signals (e.g., verifiable answer accuracy). Such rewards are effective for question-answering but lack the direct grounding in physical actions and long-horizon planning necessary for embodied AI, making it difficult to connect reasoning with real-world action execution.

**ThinkAct's Contribution to the Landscape:**

ThinkAct addresses these limitations by introducing a novel dual-system framework that effectively bridges high-level, structured reasoning with robust, low-level action execution. It innovates by:

- **Action-Aligned Reinforcement Learning:** Unlike prior RL-based reasoning models that use abstract QA rewards, ThinkAct designs a unique "action-aligned visual feedback" mechanism. This reward directly measures visual goal completion and trajectory consistency, grounding the MLLM's reasoning deeply in the physical environment and the anticipated actions, thus enabling more effective long-horizon planning.
- **Visual Latent Planning:** ThinkAct compresses the MLLM's complex intermediate reasoning steps into a compact "visual plan latent." This latent then explicitly conditions a downstream action model. This mechanism serves as an effective interface between deliberative thinking and reactive control, addressing the challenge of connecting abstract plans to concrete actions.
- **Asynchronous Operation:** The framework allows for asynchronous operation, where the reasoning MLLM can perform "slow thinking" (planning every N steps) while the action model executes "fast control" (acting at every step). This design is highly practical for real-world robotics, balancing computational overhead with the need for timely reactions.

By combining these elements, ThinkAct moves beyond the limitations of purely end-to-end models (lack of reasoning) and supervised CoT methods (data cost and generalization issues), while also refining RL-based reasoning by providing more relevant and grounded reward signals for embodied tasks. It represents a significant step towards creating more deliberative, adaptable, and robust embodied AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind ThinkAct is to empower Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) with the ability to reason effectively before performing actions in complex physical environments. The authors identify a critical gap in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: their struggle with interpreting nuanced multimodal instructions, performing robust long-horizon planning, and adapting intelligently to dynamic, real-world scenarios.

**Key Motivations:**

1. **Limitations of End-to-End Models:** Existing VLA models, which often directly map visual and textual inputs to actions, lack explicit intermediate reasoning. This "black-box" approach fundamentally restricts their capacity for multi-step planning and adaptation to varied task conditions.
2. **Challenges of Supervised Reasoning:** While chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting offers a path to explicit reasoning, methods relying on supervised fine-tuning of CoT traces face significant hurdles. The generation of high-quality, diverse reasoning annotations is prohibitively expensive and often leads to models that overfit to specific scenarios, hindering generalization.
3. **Abstractness of Current RL-Based Reasoning Rewards:** Recent advancements in using reinforcement learning (RL) to incentivize reasoning in LLMs show promise. However, when applied to multimodal tasks, the reward signals typically focus on abstract QA-style accuracy. Such rewards are insufficient for guiding long-horizon planning in embodied contexts, as they fail to establish a direct link between reasoning steps and physically executable actions or visual outcomes.
4. **Demand for Advanced Embodied AI:** Enabling foundation models with robust action awareness and embodied reasoning capabilities is crucial for unlocking a wide range of practical AI applications, including advanced robotics, augmented reality (AR) assistance, and intelligent automation systems. These applications demand agents that can not only perceive and interpret complex multimodal information but also deliberate, plan, and adapt their actions over extended horizons.

### 3. Key Objectives of ThinkAct

To address these motivations, ThinkAct sets out to achieve the following specific objectives:

1. **Propose a Dual-System Framework:** Develop "ThinkAct," an innovative architecture that seamlessly integrates a high-level reasoning MLLM with a low-level action execution model, ensuring mutual enhancement between thinking and acting.
2. **Develop Action-Aligned Visual Rewards:** Design and implement a novel reinforcement learning reward mechanism that leverages visual feedback from goal completion and trajectory alignment. This aims to ground the MLLM's long-horizon reasoning directly in the embodied scene, overcoming the limitations of abstract rewards.
3. **Advance Visual Latent Planning:** Introduce a method to compress the MLLM's reasoning plans into a compact "visual plan latent." This latent will serve as a crucial interface, effectively steering downstream action execution by providing reasoning-enhanced trajectory guidance across diverse environments.
4. **Demonstrate Emergent Capabilities:** Empirically validate that the learned reasoning VLA system exhibits critical capabilities such as few-shot adaptation to new tasks and environments, robust long-horizon planning, and emergent behaviors like failure detection and self-correction in complex physical AI scenarios.

By pursuing these objectives, ThinkAct aims to deliver a scalable and robust approach to embodied reasoning, pushing the boundaries of what MLLMs can achieve in interactive, dynamic environments.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

ThinkAct proposes a sophisticated dual-system framework designed to enable Vision-Language-Action (VLA) reasoning by mutually enhancing embodied reasoning and action execution. The core idea is to allow an MLLM to "think" by generating high-level plans, which then guide a separate action model to "act" in the physical environment.

#### 4.1. Dual-System Architecture

ThinkAct comprises two main components that operate in tandem:

1. **Reasoning MLLM (ℱ𝜃):** This model is responsible for interpreting visual observations ($o_t$) and textual instructions ($l$) to generate high-level, structured reasoning plans. These plans are internally represented as latent embeddings ($v_t$) and, crucially, a visual plan latent ($c_t$). The visual plan latent is further inferred into a sequence of 2D points ($\tau = [p_k]_{k=1}^K$) representing the anticipated gripper end-effector trajectory, capturing the spatial-temporal intent of the plan.
2. **Action Model (𝜋𝜑):** This is a Transformer-based policy (e.g., Diffusion Policy) that takes the current state (visual observations, language instructions) and the visual plan latent ($c_t$) as input. Its role is to predict a sequence of $N$ executable, low-level actions ($[a_t]_{t+N}^t$) tailored to the target environment.

A key design principle is **asynchronous operation**: the Reasoning MLLM can perform "slow thinking" and generate a plan every $N$ steps, while the Action Model executes "fast control" by predicting actions at every step within that $N$-step horizon. This allows for deliberative planning without sacrificing reactive control.

#### 4.2. Reinforced Visual Latent Planning for Embodied Reasoning (MLLM Training)

To incentivize the MLLM to produce effective and visually grounded long-horizon plans, ThinkAct employs reinforced fine-tuning with a novel reward shaping mechanism:

- **Action-Aligned Visual Feedback:**
 - **Goal Reward ($r_{goal}$):** This reward encourages the MLLM to anticipate the visual goal completion. It compares the predicted start ($p_1$) and end ($p_K$) positions of the gripper trajectory from $\tau$ with corresponding ground-truth points ($\hat{p}_1, \hat{p}_K$) obtained from an off-the-shelf detector on demonstration videos. The reward is higher when these points are closer in Euclidean distance.
 - **Trajectory Reward ($r_{traj}$):** This reward enforces that the predicted trajectory $\tau$ is physically plausible and aligns with demonstrated trajectories ($\hat{\tau}$). It uses Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance to measure the similarity between the predicted and ground-truth trajectories, rewarding closer matches.
 - **Overall Reward ($r$):** The final reward for the MLLM is a weighted combination: $r = 0.9 \cdot r_{visual} + 0.1 \cdot r_{format}$, where $r_{visual} = 0.5 \cdot r_{goal} + 0.5 \cdot r_{traj}$. $r_{format}$ is a score for the correctness of the generated text format, included to maintain coherence with existing reasoning works.

- **Reinforced Fine-Tuning with GRPO:**
 - The MLLM ($\mathcal{F}_\theta$) is optimized using **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** (Shao et al., 2024).
 - Given an input $(o_t, l)$, GRPO samples $M$ distinct reasoning responses ($z_i$) from the current MLLM. Each response is evaluated using the action-aligned reward function $r$.
 - The model is then updated to maximize an objective that promotes responses with higher rewards relative to other candidates in the sampled group, while a KL divergence term regularizes the model to prevent excessive deviation from its previous state ($\mathcal{F}_{\theta_{old}}$). This process incentivizes the generation of coherent and effective long-horizon visual plans.
 - The framework is also flexible enough to incorporate public QA datasets (e.g., robotic VQA, failure detection) by framing their accuracy as an additional reward signal.

#### 4.3. Reasoning-Enhanced Action Adaptation (Action Model Training)

The generated visual plan latent $c_t$ serves as the bridge between high-level reasoning and low-level action execution:

- **Conditioning the Action Model:** The action model ($\pi_\phi$) is enhanced by explicitly conditioning its predictions on the latent plan $c_t$. A latent projector (implemented as a Q-Former) transforms $c_t$ into an input space compatible with the action model, allowing the high-level intent to guide the low-level control effectively.
- **Imitation Learning:** The action model, along with its state encoder and the latent projector, is trained using imitation learning on annotated action demonstrations. During this phase, the Reasoning MLLM ($\mathcal{F}_\theta$) is kept frozen, ensuring that the action model learns to robustly translate the MLLM's plans into executable actions in the target environment.

#### 4.4. Multi-Stage Learning Strategy and Inference

ThinkAct employs a multi-stage training strategy:

1. **Cold-Start Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
 - The MLLM ($\mathcal{F}_\theta$) is initially fine-tuned on supervised data, including 2D visual trajectories, QA tasks (RoboVQA, EgoPlan-IT), and CoT data (Video-R1-CoT). This stage teaches the MLLM to interpret visual information, produce reasoning, and adhere to correct output formats.
 - The Action Model ($\pi_\phi$) is independently pre-trained on large-scale robot datasets like Open X-Embodiment (OXE) to establish a strong foundation for low-level action execution.
2. **Reinforced Fine-Tuning:** The MLLM is then fine-tuned with GRPO using the action-aligned rewards, guiding it to generate effective visual plan latents.
3. **Reasoning-Enhanced Action Adaptation:** With the MLLM frozen, the action model, state encoder, and latent projector are fine-tuned on target environment data, leveraging the pre-computed (cached) visual plan latents from the MLLM.

During inference, given an observation and instruction, the MLLM generates a visual plan latent $c_t$. This latent then conditions the action model, which predicts a sequence of actions, embodying the "think-before-acting" paradigm with its asynchronous reasoning and execution.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

ThinkAct demonstrates strong performance across diverse robot manipulation and embodied reasoning benchmarks, showcasing its capabilities in long-horizon planning, few-shot adaptation, and self-correction.

#### 5.1. Quantitative Evaluation

- **Robot Manipulation Tasks:**
 - **SimplerEnv (Li et al., 2024):** ThinkAct significantly outperforms baseline action models and state-of-the-art VLAs. For instance, on the Google-VM split, ThinkAct (71.5%) surpasses its underlying DiT-Policy (56.0%) by 15.5%. Similar improvements are observed on Google-VA (65.1% vs 48.2%) and Bridge-VM (43.8% vs 32.4%), demonstrating enhanced robustness to visual variations.
 - **LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023):** ThinkAct achieves an overall success rate of 84.4%, outperforming CoT-VLA (83.9%), OpenVLA (76.5%), and its base DiT-Policy (76.8%). It shows superior generalization across all subtasks: LIBERO-Spatial (88.3%), LIBERO-Object (91.4%), LIBERO-Goal (87.1%), and notably, LIBERO-Long (70.9%), where long-horizon planning is critical.
- **Embodied Reasoning Tasks:**
 - **EgoPlan-Bench2 (Qiu et al., 2024):** ThinkAct achieves an overall accuracy of 48.2%, outperforming competitive MLLMs like GPT-4V (32.6%) and a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL* (45.7%), demonstrating strong multi-step planning in egocentric daily-life scenarios.
 - **RoboVQA (Sermanet et al., 2024):** ThinkAct records an overall BLEU score of 59.8, surpassing Qwen2.5-VL* (55.7%) and other strong baselines. This highlights its strength in long-horizon reasoning for robotic manipulation.
 - **OpenEQA (Majumdar et al., 2024):** ThinkAct achieves the highest overall score of 56.2%, demonstrating enhanced embodied understanding and generalization across diverse environments, including object state, recognition, functional reasoning, and spatial awareness.

#### 5.2. Qualitative Results

- **Robot Manipulation Visualization (Fig. 3):** ThinkAct's reasoning process is qualitatively showcased by its ability to decompose complex instructions (e.g., "Pick up the book and place it in the back compartment") into sequential sub-tasks and generate corresponding plausible visual trajectories. This confirms the effectiveness of its long-horizon planning.
- **Embodied Reasoning Process (Fig. 4 & A7):** Comparing ThinkAct with and without RL fine-tuning reveals the impact of reinforcement. The RL-tuned model consistently provides more accurate and insightful reasoning, leading to correct answers for complex questions requiring future state inference or detailed environmental understanding, where the cold-start model often fails or misinterprets.

#### 5.3. Ablation Study

- **Importance of Action-Aligned Visual Rewards (Tab. 3 & A6):** The ablation study conclusively demonstrates the critical role of both the goal reward ($r_{goal}$) and trajectory reward ($r_{traj}$). Removing either reward leads to a performance drop across all benchmarks. When both are removed, and only QA-style rewards remain, performance degrades significantly, nearing the SFT cold-start baseline. This validates that the custom action-aligned visual feedback is crucial for effective multi-step planning and grounding reasoning in embodied tasks. The SFT cold-start model yields the lowest scores, confirming the necessity of RL fine-tuning for eliciting reasoning capabilities.

#### 5.4. Analysis of ThinkAct Capabilities

- **Reasoning-Enhanced Few-Shot Adaptation (Fig. 5 & A9):** ThinkAct consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods like OpenVLA and Magma on LIBERO with as few as 5 or 10 demonstrations per task. This highlights its superior few-shot generalization to novel skills and environments, attributed to its robust reasoning capabilities.
- **Reasoning Elicit Self-Correction (Fig. 6 & A8):** A significant emergent behavior is ThinkAct's ability to self-correct from execution errors. By providing the reasoning MLLM with temporal context (short video segments), it can detect failures (e.g., dropping an object), reflect on the unexpected situation ("Let's reconsider how to complete this task"), and generate a revised plan to recover and complete the task successfully.
- **Smaller Model Generalizability (Tab. A5):** ThinkAct's approach proves effective even with smaller MLLM backbones (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-3B), showing consistent outperformance over similarly sized models on embodied reasoning tasks.
- **Inference Speed:** While ThinkAct incurs a modest 17% increase in execution time compared to end-to-end OpenVLA due to its autoregressive reasoning, this overhead is justified by substantial performance gains (e.g., 2.8% to 15.3% success rate improvements on LIBERO categories), indicating that the reasoning overhead is a worthwhile trade-off for improved task performance.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

ThinkAct represents a significant advancement in the field of embodied AI, offering a robust and scalable framework for integrating high-level reasoning with low-level action execution. Its core contributions address fundamental challenges in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, paving the way for more intelligent and autonomous agents in physical environments.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Bridging the Reasoning-Action Gap:** ThinkAct effectively connects the deliberative "thinking" capabilities of MLLMs with the practical "acting" requirements of robots. By translating abstract reasoning into concrete visual plan latents that directly condition an action model, it overcomes the limitations of purely end-to-end approaches that lack explicit planning.
2. **Novel Grounded Reinforcement Learning:** The introduction of action-aligned visual rewards (goal completion and trajectory alignment) is a crucial innovation. Unlike abstract QA-style rewards, these rewards directly incentivize reasoning that is physically plausible and visually grounded, which is essential for effective long-horizon planning in embodied tasks. This mechanism makes RL a far more powerful tool for teaching complex robot behaviors.
3. **Emergent Deliberative Capabilities:** ThinkAct demonstrates impressive emergent behaviors that are critical for real-world deployment:
 - **Long-Horizon Planning:** The ability to break down complex instructions into sequential sub-tasks and generate coherent visual plans.
 - **Few-Shot Adaptation:** Rapidly adapting to new tasks and environments with minimal demonstrations, significantly reducing the data burden for new skills.
 - **Self-Correction from Failures:** The remarkable capacity to detect execution errors, reflect on the situation, and dynamically generate revised plans to recover and complete tasks. This moves beyond merely executing commands to understanding and adapting to unforeseen circumstances.
4. **Practical Dual-System Design:** The asynchronous "slow thinking, fast control" architecture offers a pragmatic solution for balancing the computational demands of complex reasoning with the real-time requirements of robot control. This design makes ThinkAct more amenable to real-world robotic systems where latency and responsiveness are critical.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Enhanced Robotic Autonomy:** ThinkAct could lead to a new generation of robots capable of interpreting more ambiguous or high-level human instructions, executing complex multi-step tasks robustly, and operating more autonomously in unstructured, dynamic environments without constant human intervention. This has direct applications in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare (assistive robotics), and domestic settings.
- **Broader Embodied AI Applications:** The principles and techniques developed in ThinkAct can be extended beyond traditional robotics to other embodied AI domains, such as virtual agents, augmented reality (AR) assistance, and human-robot collaboration, where agents need to reason about and interact with physical spaces.
- **Advancing Foundation Models for Control:** This work further establishes the viability and critical role of large language and multimodal models as foundational components for intelligent control and reasoning in physical systems. It highlights how these powerful models can be effectively aligned with the physical world through novel training paradigms.
- **Improved Human-AI Interaction:** Agents that can explain their reasoning, adapt to new situations, and self-correct from mistakes will naturally foster greater trust and usability in human-AI interaction.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**

- **Inherited MLLM Hallucinations:** As ThinkAct builds upon pre-trained MLLMs, it inherits their inherent limitations, particularly the tendency for hallucinations in visual or spatial reasoning. This can lead to generated plans that reference incorrect object attributes or spatial relationships, potentially affecting downstream action execution.
- **Need for Grounding-Aware Training:** While the latent planning and action grounding mitigate hallucination to some extent, future work on explicit grounding-aware training or hallucination suppression techniques within MLLMs is necessary to enhance robustness and reliability in safety-critical real-world deployments.

**Broader Impacts (as acknowledged by the authors):**

- **Positive Impacts:** Significant potential for assistive robotics, home automation, and industrial systems by making robots more intelligent and adaptable.
- **Ethical Concerns:** Increased autonomy and reasoning capabilities also raise concerns. Misinterpretation of ambiguous commands, reliance on hallucinated visual reasoning, or overconfidence in CoT outputs could lead to unintended or unsafe behaviors. The authors emphasize the need for future research on safeguards and alignment with human intent to mitigate these risks.

In conclusion, ThinkAct represents a compelling step forward in embodied AI, offering a robust framework that pushes the boundaries of autonomous agents by empowering them to think, plan, and self-correct in complex physical environments. Its focus on grounded, reinforced reasoning provides a promising direction for developing more intelligent, adaptable, and reliable robotic systems.
