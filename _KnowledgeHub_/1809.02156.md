# Object Hallucination in Image Captioning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley and Boston University introduced CHAIR (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance), a new metric to quantify object hallucination in image captions. Their analysis revealed that 7.4% to 17.7% of generated sentences contain hallucinated objects, finding that attention models generally reduce hallucination, while self-critical training can increase it despite higher standard metric scores.

## Problem

- Image captioning models frequently "hallucinate" objects, generating descriptions of entities not present in the given image.
- Existing evaluation metrics for image captioning (e.g., BLEU, CIDEr) often fail to adequately penalize visually unfaithful generations.
- Hallucinations are misleading and problematic for real-world applications, indicating a fundamental lack of true visual grounding in the models.

## Method

- Introduced **CHAIR** (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance), a new metric to quantify object hallucination by checking generated objects against ground truth segmentation and caption annotations.
- Developed ==image consistency== and ==language consistency== scores to analyze whether hallucination stems from visual misclassification or over-reliance on language priors.
- Conducted a systematic comparative study of diverse image captioning models, varying in architecture (e.g., ==attention== vs. non-attention, object proposal use) and learning objectives (==Cross-Entropy==, ==Self-Critical==, ==GAN==).

## Results

- Between **7.4%** and **17.7%** of generated sentences on the Karpathy Test set contain at least one hallucinated object, with rates increasing on more challenging test sets.
- Models optimizing for CIDEr with self-critical loss often exhibit increased hallucination, while GAN-based models show reduced hallucination.
- Integrating CHAIR with standard metrics (METEOR, CIDEr, SPICE) consistently improved their correlation with human judgments of caption quality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Stronger visual grounding, particularly through access to spatial features and explicit object proposals, is more crucial for reducing hallucination than attention mechanisms alone.
> - Hallucinations are primarily driven by an over-reliance on language priors rather than true visual misclassification, especially in models with higher hallucination rates.
> - Optimizing image captioning models solely for standard metrics like CIDEr can paradoxically increase object hallucination.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of image captioning has seen remarkable progress over the last decade, primarily driven by advancements in deep learning, particularly recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and attention mechanisms. Early models adopted an encoder-decoder framework, where a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) encodes the image into a fixed-length vector, and an RNN decodes it into a natural language sentence. Subsequent innovations, such as visual attention mechanisms (e.g., "Show, Attend and Tell"), allowed models to focus on relevant image regions when generating specific words, significantly improving performance. More recently, models like "Top-Down and Bottom-Up Attention" and "Neural Baby Talk" (NBT) have integrated explicit object detections and semantic reasoning, pushing the state-of-the-art in terms of descriptive accuracy.

Despite these continuous improvements, a critical limitation persists: the models sometimes "hallucinate" objects, meaning they generate descriptions of entities that are not actually present in the image. This phenomenon, referred to as object hallucination, is a significant concern for several reasons:
1. **Human Preference:** Human users, especially in applications like assistive technologies for the visually impaired, often prioritize correctness and faithfulness to the image over comprehensiveness or fluidity. Hallucinating objects can be misleading or even dangerous in such contexts.
2. **Model Understanding:** Hallucination reveals a fundamental lack of true visual grounding or understanding within the models. It suggests that models might be over-relying on language priors (e.g., "a person is usually sitting on a bench") rather than robustly interpreting visual content.
3. **Limitations of Metrics:** Standard evaluation metrics for image captioning (e.g., BLEU, METEOR, CIDEr, SPICE) primarily measure n-gram overlap or semantic similarity with human-written ground truth captions. While these metrics have been instrumental in driving progress, they often fail to adequately penalize visually unfaithful generations. A hallucinated object might not severely impact these scores if the rest of the caption is good, or if the hallucinated object frequently co-occurs in ground truth captions for similar scenes.

This paper directly addresses these limitations. It contributes to a growing body of research that critiques the sole reliance on traditional metrics and calls for more nuanced evaluation protocols that emphasize visual faithfulness, interpretability, and robustness. It aligns with the broader push in AI towards not just "performance" but "reliability" and "trustworthiness." By proposing a dedicated metric for object hallucination and systematically analyzing its causes across diverse models, the authors aim to provide actionable insights for developing more visually grounded and reliable image captioning systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the observed phenomenon of "object hallucination" in contemporary image captioning models, where models generate descriptions of objects that are not present in the given image. Despite impressive performance gains measured by standard metrics, the authors argue that these metrics often fail to adequately capture the captions' relevance and faithfulness to the visual content.

Based on this motivation, the paper sets forth several key objectives:

1. **To propose a new image relevance metric:** The authors aim to introduce a novel metric, CHAIR (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance), specifically designed to evaluate the rate of object hallucination in image captions. This metric would provide a direct measure of visual fidelity, complementing existing language-centric evaluation tools.
2. **To analyze object hallucination across diverse models:** The study seeks to systematically investigate which types of captioning models – characterized by different architectural designs (e.g., with or without attention mechanisms) and learning objectives (e.g., cross-entropy, self-critical, GAN-based losses) – are more prone to hallucinating objects. This comparative analysis aims to identify model characteristics that either mitigate or exacerbate hallucination.
3. **To explore the underlying causes of hallucination:** The paper endeavors to understand why hallucination occurs. Specifically, it investigates whether hallucinations are primarily due to visual misclassification (the model misidentifies an object visually) or an over-reliance on language priors (the model generates words that statistically "go together" regardless of visual evidence). To this end, the authors propose "image consistency" and "language consistency" scores.
4. **To assess the effectiveness of standard metrics in capturing hallucination:** A crucial objective is to determine how well commonly used automatic sentence metrics (like CIDEr, METEOR, SPICE) reflect the presence of object hallucination. The authors aim to highlight discrepancies between high standard metric scores and high hallucination rates, arguing for the necessity of a dedicated hallucination metric.

Ultimately, the overarching goal is to foster the development of more visually grounded and reliable image captioning models that produce faithful descriptions, thereby enhancing their utility for real-world applications and user satisfaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-faceted methodology to systematically analyze object hallucination, centered around the introduction of a new evaluation metric and a thorough comparative study of various captioning models.

#### 3.1. The CHAIR Metric (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance)

The core of their evaluation approach is the proposed CHAIR metric, designed to quantify object hallucination. It assesses how many of the generated objects are actually present in the image according to ground truth. CHAIR has two variants:
- **CHAIRi (per-instance):** Calculates the ratio of hallucinated object instances to all mentioned object instances in the caption.
- **CHAIRs (per-sentence):** Measures the fraction of sentences that contain at least one hallucinated object.
To determine ground truth objects, the authors wisely combine two sources from MSCOCO:
- **MSCOCO Object Segmentation Annotations:** Provides precise bounding box and segmentation masks for 80 common objects.
- **MSCOCO Captions (sentence annotations):** Leveraged to identify synonyms or alternative phrasings for objects (e.g., "table" vs. "dining table") that might not be explicitly segmented but are relevant contextually.
The process for identifying objects in generated captions involves tokenization, singularization, and mapping words to MSCOCO objects using a pre-defined synonym list, carefully handling multi-word compounds to avoid misattribution.

#### 3.2. Consistency Measures for Analyzing Causes

To investigate the root causes of hallucination (visual error vs. language bias), the authors introduce two consistency scores:
- **Image Consistency:** A multi-label classification model is trained to predict the presence of MSCOCO objects given an image (P(w|I)). For a hallucinated word, higher P(w|I) implies the error is more consistent with a visual misclassification. The image model shares visual features with the captioning models, ensuring a fair comparison.
- **Language Consistency:** An LSTM-based language model is trained on MSCOCO text to predict the next word given previous words. For a hallucinated word, its rank R(w_t) in the language model's predictions (1/R(w_t) is used as the score) indicates how consistent the error is with a purely linguistic prior. Higher consistency means the error is more language-model-driven.

#### 3.3. Baseline Captioning Models

The study evaluates a diverse set of state-of-the-art captioning models, categorized by:
- **Model Architecture:**
 - **Non-attention models:** FC (image features initialize LSTM hidden state), LRCN (visual features input at each time step).
 - **Attention models:** Att2In (similar to Xu et al. 2015), TopDown (Anderson et al. 2018, operating on feature maps), TopDown-BB (operating on object proposals from a detector), and NBT (Lu et al. 2018, explicitly using object detections).
- **Learning Objective:**
 - Standard Cross-Entropy (CE) loss.
 - Self-Critical (SC) loss (optimizes CIDEr using reinforcement learning).
 - GAN loss (Shetty et al. 2017, adversarial training for human-like captions).
All models, except for GAN, TopDown-BB, and NBT, are implemented within a consistent open-source framework (Luo et al., 2018) to ensure fair comparison of architectural choices.

#### 3.4. TopDown Model Deconstruction

To precisely understand the impact of individual architectural components, the TopDown model is "deconstructed" by gradually removing features:
- **NoAttention:** Attention mechanism replaced by mean pooling.
- **NoConv:** Spatial feature maps are not input (only fully connected features).
- **SingleLayer:** Only one LSTM layer is used.
- **FC equivalent:** Visual features used only for LSTM initialization, mimicking the FC model. This controlled experiment helps isolate the contribution of each component to hallucination.

#### 3.5. Dataset Splits

Experiments are conducted on two standard MSCOCO splits:
- **Karpathy Test set:** A widely used split for general image captioning benchmarks.
- **Robust Test set:** A "compositional split" (Lu et al., 2018) where object pairs in the test set do not overlap with those in the training set, posing a greater challenge for generalization and potentially revealing more language-prior-driven errors. A "Novel Object split" (Hendricks et al., 2016) is also briefly discussed.

#### 3.6. TD-Restrict Model for Metric Analysis

To demonstrate how standard metrics handle hallucination, a modified TopDown model, **TD-Restrict**, is introduced. This model actively prevents the generation of MSCOCO objects deemed absent from the image by setting their log probabilities very low. Comparing captions from TD and TD-Restrict allows for a direct assessment of how standard metrics penalize (or fail to penalize) hallucination.

#### 3.7. Human Evaluation

To validate the utility of CHAIR, a human evaluation study is conducted via Amazon Mechanical Turk. Workers rate the preference of captions from five different models for 500 randomly selected images. The average human scores are then correlated with individual standard metrics and with combinations of standard metrics and (1-CHAIRs) or (1-CHAIRi) to demonstrate CHAIR's complementarity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several insightful findings regarding object hallucination in image captioning models:

1. **Prevalence of Hallucination:** Object hallucination is a widespread problem. On the standard Karpathy Test set, between 7.4% and 17.7% of generated sentences include at least one hallucinated object (CHAIRs). This rate consistently increases on more challenging splits like the Robust Test set and Novel Object split, where models encounter novel compositions or objects unseen during training.

2. **Model Architecture and Hallucination:**
 - **Attention Mechanisms:** Models incorporating attention generally exhibit lower hallucination rates than non-attention models. Notably, models that utilize bounding box attention (e.g., TopDown-BB, NBT) tend to have even lower hallucination, suggesting that finer-grained visual grounding is beneficial.
 - **Deconstruction Insights:** The analysis of deconstructed TopDown models revealed that access to spatial feature maps (convolutional features) is more critical for reducing hallucination than the attention mechanism itself. Removing convolutional input (NoConv) dramatically increased hallucination, while removing attention (NoAttention) had a less severe impact.
 - **Feature Input Strategy:** Surprisingly, models that input visual features only at the first step (like FC) sometimes exhibited lower hallucination than those inputting features at every time step (LRCN, SingleLayer), especially without spatial features. This suggests that continuous visual input without proper spatial grounding can lead to overfitting to visual features.

3. **Learning Objective and Hallucination:**
 - **Self-Critical Loss:** Models trained with the self-critical (SC) loss, which directly optimizes for CIDEr, frequently showed *increased* hallucination despite achieving higher CIDEr scores. This indicates that CIDEr does not sufficiently penalize object hallucination, leading models to exploit this loophole.
 - **GAN Loss:** In contrast, the GAN-based model demonstrated a *decrease* in hallucination compared to its cross-entropy baseline. The authors hypothesize that hallucinated objects serve as a strong signal for the discriminator, encouraging the generator to produce more visually faithful captions to appear "human-like."

4. **Causes of Hallucination (Image vs. Language Consistency):**
 - Models with lower hallucination rates tend to make errors that are more consistent with the image model (i.e., their hallucinations are more visually plausible, even if incorrect).
 - Conversely, models with higher hallucination rates tend to make errors more consistent with the language model, indicating an over-reliance on linguistic priors or patterns ("memorizing which words go together") rather than actual visual content.
 - Training dynamics showed that models initially learn fluent language (errors more language-consistent) before effectively incorporating visual information (errors become more image-consistent).

5. **Types and Contexts of Hallucinated Objects:**
 - **Common Hallucinations:** "Furniture" (especially "dining table"), "Outdoor objects," "Sports," and "Kitchenware" are the most frequently hallucinated super-categories. "Dining table" is the most common single hallucinated object across models.
 - **Trigger Words/Context:** Hallucinated objects often appear towards the end of sentences and are triggered by preceding words (e.g., "sitting" or "top" before "dining table") or by the presence of co-occurring objects in the image (e.g., a "cat" triggering "laptop," or a "dog" triggering "chair"). While many hallucinations occur in relevant scenes (e.g., "surfboard" on a beach), some are out of context (e.g., "bed" in a bathroom).

6. **Standard Metrics' Inadequacy:**
 - Standard metrics (CIDEr, METEOR, SPICE) do not consistently penalize hallucination. Examples show that a caption with a hallucinated object can still receive a higher CIDEr score than a more visually faithful but less descriptive caption.
 - While SPICE showed a slightly higher correlation with the absence of hallucination (1-CHAIRs) compared to METEOR and CIDEr, it was still insufficient.
 - Crucially, the study found that even when standard metric scores are similar between two models, their hallucination rates can differ significantly, highlighting that traditional metrics cannot reliably predict hallucination risk.
 - **CHAIR's Complementarity:** Combining CHAIR (specifically 1-CHAIRs or 1-CHAIRi) with standard metrics (METEOR, CIDEr, SPICE) consistently increased their correlation with human preference scores. This demonstrates that CHAIR provides valuable, complementary information that aligns better with human judgment of caption quality.

7. **Cascading Impact of Hallucination:** The generation of a hallucinated word significantly impacts subsequent words in the sentence; following a hallucination, subsequent words differed 47.3% of the time, leading to further deviations from visual reality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research paper makes significant contributions to the field of image captioning and the broader domain of multimodal AI, with several important implications:

1. **Introduction of a Crucial New Metric:** The most direct impact is the proposal and validation of **CHAIR (Caption Hallucination Assessment with Image Relevance)**. This metric provides a much-needed, image-grounded evaluation tool that directly quantifies a critical failure mode of image captioning models – hallucination. By offering both instance-level (CHAIRi) and sentence-level (CHAIRs) assessments, it enables a more nuanced understanding of visual fidelity that standard text-based metrics cannot provide.

2. **Deeper Understanding of Model Limitations:** The systematic analysis reveals fundamental limitations of current state-of-the-art captioning models. It demonstrates that optimizing solely for standard metrics (like CIDEr) can inadvertently lead to increased hallucination, highlighting a potential misalignment between current evaluation practices and desired model behavior. The investigation into image versus language consistency provides valuable insights into the underlying causes of hallucination, pushing the field beyond superficial performance numbers to address issues of true visual grounding.

3. **Guidance for Future Model Design and Training:** The findings offer clear directions for developing more robust and reliable captioning models:
 - **Focus on Image Relevance:** The paper strongly advocates that model design and training should be guided by image relevance, not just fluency or similarity to reference captions. This implies the need for loss functions or regularization techniques that explicitly penalize visually unfaithful generations.
 - **Improved Visual Features:** The importance of strong visual processing, particularly access to spatial features (convolutional maps) and explicit object proposals, is underscored as a key factor in reducing hallucination. Future research should prioritize enhancing the visual encoder's ability to discriminate and localize objects accurately.
 - **Mitigating Language Prior Over-reliance:** The observation that highly hallucinating models tend to rely on language priors suggests a need for techniques that encourage models to be more visually conditioned and less prone to memorizing common co-occurrences without visual verification.

4. **Enhanced Real-World Applicability and Trustworthiness:** Object hallucination is not merely an academic curiosity; it poses significant practical challenges, especially for applications like assistive technologies for the visually impaired. If an image captioning system tells a blind user there's a "bench" when there isn't one, it can be misleading or unsafe. By providing a metric to quantify and reduce hallucination, this work contributes directly to building more trustworthy and user-friendly AI systems.

5. **Broader Implications for Multimodal Generative AI:** The methodology of dissecting errors into visually-driven versus language-driven components has broader applicability to other multimodal generative tasks, such as video captioning, visual question answering, or even text-to-image generation. Understanding the source of errors in complex AI systems is crucial for developing more reliable and interpretable models across different domains.

In conclusion, "Object Hallucination in Image Captioning" serves as a critical examination of a pervasive problem in image captioning. By introducing a novel evaluation metric and conducting a thorough empirical analysis, the authors not only quantify this issue but also provide profound insights into its causes and potential solutions. This work is a significant step towards developing more visually intelligent, faithful, and truly reliable multimodal AI systems.
