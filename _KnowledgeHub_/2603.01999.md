# Learning Vision-Based Omnidirectional Navigation: A Teacher-Student Approach Using Monocular Depth Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> NVIDIA and German Research Center for Artificial Intelligence (DFKI) developed a teacher-student reinforcement learning framework enabling omnidirectional mobile robots to navigate using monocular depth estimation (MDE) from multiple cameras, eliminating reliance on 2D LiDAR. The approach allows industrial robots to perceive and avoid complex 3D obstacles, achieving an 80% average success rate in real-world navigation, outperforming a 2D LiDAR baseline which reached 37%.

## Problem

- 2D LiDAR sensors, commonly used in industrial AMRs, fail to perceive obstacles outside a single horizontal scan plane, leading to collisions with objects like forklift tines or overhanging loads.
- Integrating dedicated 3D sensors (e.g., stereo cameras, ToF) increases cost, weight, and integration complexity for AMRs, hindering widespread adoption.
- Bridging the sim-to-real gap for deep reinforcement learning (DRL) policies, especially when relying on imperfect, learned vision-based perception, remains a challenge.

## Method

- A teacher-student DRL framework where a 'teacher' policy is trained in simulation with privileged 2D LiDAR (perceiving full 3D obstacle extents) and a 'student' policy learns to imitate the teacher's behavior using monocular depth maps from multiple cameras.
- A fine-tuned Depth Anything V2 model generates real-time depth maps from four synchronized RGB cameras, processed onboard an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX.
- A seven-stage noise augmentation pipeline is applied to simulated depth maps during student training to explicitly model real-world MDE artifacts and improve sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) are increasingly deployed in industrial environments for tasks such as material transport and logistics. These robots commonly rely on 2D Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) sensors for navigation and collision avoidance. However, 2D LiDAR provides perception limited to a single horizontal scan plane, which presents a significant challenge in environments with complex three-dimensional obstacles that extend above or below this plane. Examples include forklift tines, overhanging loads, or low-profile objects, which can lead to collisions if not perceived.

Existing approaches to address this limitation include the integration of supplementary 3D sensors such such as stereo cameras or time-of-flight (ToF) systems. While these sensors provide richer 3D information, they often incur additional costs, increase robot weight, and introduce complexities in integration compared to standard RGB cameras. This economic and engineering overhead limits their widespread adoption in cost-sensitive industrial applications.

Concurrent advancements in monocular depth estimation (MDE), particularly those driven by large-scale foundation models like Depth Anything V2, have enabled the inference of dense and relatively accurate depth maps from single RGB images. These models can effectively transform inexpensive, off-the-shelf monocular cameras into depth-sensing devices, offering a lightweight alternative to dedicated 3D sensors.

Within the field of robotic navigation, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a paradigm for learning direct mappings from sensory observations to control commands, circumventing the need for hand-crafted planners or costmaps typical of traditional navigation architectures. However, transferring DRL policies from simulated environments to real-world robots (the "sim-to-real" gap) remains a significant challenge. Domain randomization is a common technique used to bridge this gap, but its effectiveness can be limited when the deployed sensors fundamentally differ from those used in training.

The teacher-student learning paradigm offers a robust solution for sim-to-real transfer. In this approach, a "teacher" policy is trained in a simulation environment with access to privileged, often perfect or simplified, information that is unavailable in the real world. The "student" policy then learns to imitate the teacher's behavior using only the sensor modalities available during real-world deployment. This method has been successfully applied across various robotic tasks, including locomotion and vision-based indoor navigation.

This research integrates these contemporary advancements: leveraging MDE foundation models to provide 3D perception from monocular cameras, and employing a teacher-student DRL framework to train robust navigation policies that can effectively transfer from simulation to real-world deployment on embedded systems. The work addresses a critical gap by providing a vision-based alternative to 2D LiDAR for obstacle avoidance that is both comprehensive in its perception of 3D obstacles and viable for onboard, real-time deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the inherent limitations of 2D LiDAR sensors, which are widely prevalent in industrial autonomous mobile robots (AMRs). While effective for detecting obstacles within a horizontal plane, 2D LiDAR fails to perceive objects that extend above or below this single scan plane. This blind spot poses a significant safety and operational challenge in industrial settings, where complex three-dimensional obstacles like forklift tines, overhanging pallet loads, or low-profile components are common. Such unperceived obstacles can lead to collisions, operational downtime, and potential damage to equipment or goods.

Traditional solutions involving additional 3D sensors (e.g., stereo cameras, time-of-flight sensors) typically increase the cost, weight, and integration complexity of AMRs, hindering their broader adoption. Conversely, advancements in monocular depth estimation (MDE), particularly with the emergence of large-scale foundation models, offer the potential to derive rich 3D information from inexpensive, off-the-shelf RGB cameras. This presents an opportunity to overcome the limitations of 2D LiDAR without the associated drawbacks of other 3D sensing modalities.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Teacher-Student Reinforcement Learning Framework:** To establish a DRL framework for vision-based mobile robot navigation that eliminates the reliance on LiDAR sensors. This involves training a "teacher" policy with privileged 2D LiDAR observations in simulation to learn robust navigation behaviors, and subsequently distilling this learned behavior into a "student" policy that operates solely on MDE-derived depth maps from a multi-camera setup.
2. **Achieve Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To ensure that the policies learned in simulation can be successfully transferred and effectively deployed on a physical robot in real-world industrial environments. This objective necessitates the use of techniques such as domain randomization during teacher training and noise augmentation of depth maps during student training to bridge the fidelity gap between simulated and real sensor data.
3. **Enable Onboard Deployment on Embedded Platforms:** To demonstrate that the complete inference pipeline, encompassing MDE, policy execution, and motor control, can run entirely onboard an embedded computing platform (specifically, an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX) in real-time without requiring external computation for inference. This ensures the practical applicability of the system in real-world AMR deployments.
4. **Evaluate and Compare Performance:** To conduct a comparative evaluation of the MDE-based student policy against a standard 2D LiDAR-based teacher policy in both simulated and real-world environments. A specific aim is to show that the MDE-based student policy can match or exceed the performance of the 2D LiDAR teacher, especially when navigating around complex 3D obstacles that pose challenges for single-plane LiDAR perception.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a teacher-student framework designed to enable vision-based, omnidirectional mobile robot navigation, leveraging monocular depth estimation (MDE) and deep reinforcement learning (DRL). The methodology addresses the domain gap between rendered and MDE-predicted depth by separating the training process into distinct phases.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The navigation task is formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) for collision-free, goal-directed navigation in static indoor environments. The robot receives partial observations ($o_t$) and selects actions ($a_t$) according to a policy ($\pi_\theta(a_t | o_t)$). A key distinction is the observation space, which differs between the teacher (privileged 2D LiDAR) and student (monocular depth maps). Both policies share the action space and proprioceptive observations, but only the teacher uses a reward function, as the student is trained via offline behavior cloning.

**B. Monocular Depth Estimation Backbone**
The Depth Anything V2 model (Vision Transformer-Small variant) was chosen as the MDE backbone due to its affine-invariant depth predictions, zero-shot generalization capabilities, and compatibility with embedded NVIDIA Jetson platforms.

- **Fine-Tuning:** To reduce the sim-to-real domain gap, the model was fine-tuned on a domain-specific dataset. This dataset comprised 24,499 RGB and ground-truth depth map pairs recorded in a warehouse environment using an Orbbec Femto Bolt time-of-flight (ToF) camera. The ViT-Small model was fine-tuned for 80 epochs on 77.6% of this dataset. This fine-tuning resulted in a 118.4% improvement in $\delta_1$ accuracy on the test split compared to the model fine-tuned on the Hypersim dataset.
- **Deployment:** The fine-tuned model was exported to TensorRT (BF16 precision). Benchmarking on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX showed it could process a batch of four 480 × 300 pixel camera images in 50.16 ± 8.22 ms, achieving 19.9 frames per second (FPS). This performance provides sufficient headroom for the required 10 Hz control frequency of the navigation policy.

**C. Teacher Policy**
The teacher policy is trained online using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in the NVIDIA Isaac Lab simulation environment, leveraging privileged information.

- **Observation Space:**
 - **Exteroceptive:** A 2D LiDAR scan with 3000 range measurements. Crucially, these LiDAR observations are generated by raycasting against "privileged collision meshes" that extend beyond the visual boundaries of obstacles. This ensures the teacher perceives regions where the robot cannot physically fit, allowing it to learn truly traversable paths.
 - **Proprioceptive:** Includes a history of the robot's odometry (current and 5 past velocity estimates: $v_x, v_y, \omega_z$), a unit vector pointing towards the goal in the robot's frame, and the Euclidean distance to the goal. The previous action is also included.
- **Action Space:** The policy outputs normalized linear velocities ($v_x, v_y$) and angular velocity ($\omega_z$) in the range [-1, 1], which are then scaled to maximum physical velocities of 0.5 m/s for linear motion and 1.0 rad/s for angular motion. Actions are commanded at 10 Hz.
- **Reward Function:** A piecewise function combining sparse terminal rewards and continuous shaping terms. Positive rewards are given for reaching the goal, and negative penalties for collisions or proximity to obstacles. Continuous terms encourage progress toward the goal and penalize large actions or rapid action changes.
- **Network Architecture:** An actor-critic architecture. Both actor and critic process exteroceptive (LiDAR) input through min-pooling (kernel size 10) followed by two 1D IMPALA-CNN blocks. Proprioceptive input is processed by a 96-unit linear layer. These features are concatenated and passed through a shared 256-unit MLP. The actor's output head uses a tanh activation, and the critic produces a scalar value.
- **Training:** Online PPO training runs across 1024 parallel simulation environments, simulated at 360 Hz. The Adam optimizer with an adaptive learning rate (based on KL divergence) is used. Training is performed for 300 epochs.

**D. Student Policy**
The student policy is trained offline via supervised imitation learning (behavior cloning) using expert demonstrations collected from the trained teacher.

- **Data Logging:** The trained teacher policy is run in inference mode in Isaac Lab. At each timestep, the teacher's expert actions, four simulated depth maps (from cameras covering 360° FOV), and the proprioceptive state are recorded. Only successful episodes are included, resulting in a dataset of 28,838 episodes.
- **Noise Augmentation:** To simulate the real-world artifacts and noise of MDE outputs, a seven-stage augmentation pipeline is applied to the simulated depth maps during data collection. This includes Gaussian smoothing, motion blur, background smudging, elastic smearing, random scale-shift perturbations (to model MDE affine-invariance), low-frequency Gaussian noise, and quantization noise.
- **Network Architecture:** Similar to the teacher's actor, but designed for visual input. It receives four depth maps (4x300x480) stacked along the channel dimension. These are min-pooled (5x5 kernel) and then processed by a 2D IMPALA-CNN encoder (three blocks with 16, 32, 32 features), followed by ReLU and a 256-unit fully connected layer. The proprioceptive input is processed identically to the teacher. The latent representations are concatenated and passed through a shared 256-unit MLP, with the final action output bounded by tanh(·). Depth maps are clipped to a maximum range of 5 m.
- **Training:** Offline supervised learning minimizes the mean squared error (MSE) between the teacher's expert actions and the student's predicted actions. The Adam optimizer is used with a constant learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁴, a batch size of 8, and training for 100 epochs.

**E. Hardware Platform and Simulation Asset**
- **Hardware:** A DJI RoboMaster S1 platform equipped with four Mecanum wheels. An NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX handles onboard inference. Four synchronized RGB cameras (AR0234 global shutter, 480x300 pixels, Arducam LN056 wide-angle lenses) provide visual input, calibrated using the Basalt framework and AprilTag targets. An RPLIDAR S2 2D LiDAR is also mounted for baseline comparison.
- **Simulation Asset:** The robot model in Isaac Lab precisely replicates the DJI RoboMaster S1, including Mecanum wheels with 12 free-rolling rollers, each approximated by 7 sphere colliders. Physical parameters were optimized using ground-truth motion data and system identification techniques.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed teacher-student framework in both simulated and real-world environments, focusing on the performance of the MDE-based student policy against a LiDAR-based teacher.

**A. Monocular Depth Estimation Performance**
- The fine-tuned Depth Anything V2 model showed a 118.4% improvement in $\delta_1$ accuracy on the domain-specific test dataset compared to the baseline metric ViT-Small model.
- For real-time deployment, the model achieved an inference rate of 19.9 FPS on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX when processing four camera images in a batch (50.16 ± 8.22 ms latency), meeting the 10 Hz control frequency requirement.

**B. Teacher Policy Simulation Results**
- The teacher policy, trained with privileged 2D LiDAR observations in Isaac Lab, exhibited fast and stable convergence. The average episode reward increased steeply within the first 25 epochs and plateaued around 14.5 after epoch 50.
- The policy's success rate during training reached a peak of 98.5%, with episode terminations primarily shifting from timeouts and collisions to successful goal reaching. This demonstrated the teacher's ability to learn robust navigation using privileged information.

**C. Student Policy Simulation Results**
- A comparative evaluation was performed in simulation environments with varying obstacle densities (5 to 25 objects). In these evaluations, the teacher policy operated with standard (non-privileged) collision meshes for a fair comparison with the student.
- **Teacher (Standard LiDAR):** The teacher's performance with standard 2D LiDAR (without privileged collision meshes) significantly degraded as obstacle density increased, with success rates falling from 100% (5 obstacles) to 50% (25 obstacles). This degradation was attributed to the 2D LiDAR missing obstacles outside its single scan plane.
- **Teacher (Privileged LiDAR):** When re-evaluated with privileged collision meshes, the teacher maintained high success rates (89.5-100%), confirming the benefit of the privileged information during its training phase.
- **Student (MDE):** The MDE-based student policy consistently outperformed the standard 2D LiDAR teacher across all obstacle densities, achieving success rates between 82% and 96.5%. For lower obstacle counts (5-10), the student's performance approached that of the privileged LiDAR teacher, and for higher counts, it maintained significantly higher success rates than the standard LiDAR teacher. The student's depth maps effectively captured the full vertical extent of obstacles.

**D. Real-World Results**
- Both policies were deployed on the DJI RoboMaster S1 in an 8 m × 8 m arena with various obstacles. The VICON motion capture system provided ground-truth goal information to isolate the performance difference due to exteroceptive perception (2D LiDAR vs. MDE).
- Across three different endpoints, with 10 runs per endpoint, the MDE-based student consistently outperformed the LiDAR-based teacher.
 - Endpoint A: Student 80% success, Teacher 40% success.
 - Endpoint B: Student 60% success, Teacher 30% success.
 - Endpoint C: Student 100% success, Teacher 40% success.
- The average real-world success rate for the student was 80%, compared to 37% for the teacher.
- Teacher failures were predominantly attributed to collisions with low-lying obstacles (e.g., a ladder) that fell outside the 2D LiDAR's scan plane. The student, perceiving these obstacles through its full-frame depth maps, was able to navigate successfully.

**E. Sim-to-Real Analysis**
- While successful, the student's real-world trajectories occasionally exhibited oscillatory behavior. This was attributed to a combination of factors including inference latency, residual MDE depth errors, and inaccuracies in the dynamic modeling of the real robot compared to the simulation.
- Analysis of MDE-predicted depth showed a residual scale mismatch compared to ground-truth LiDAR, particularly increasing at larger distances. Close-range distances were estimated with higher accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards enabling robust, vision-based navigation for autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) in complex industrial environments, offering a viable alternative to traditional 2D LiDAR systems.

**Significance:**
- **Overcoming 2D LiDAR Limitations:** The primary significance lies in addressing a critical limitation of widely used 2D LiDAR sensors, which fail to perceive obstacles outside a single horizontal scan plane. By effectively replacing 2D LiDAR with monocular depth estimation (MDE) from multiple RGB cameras, the system gains a comprehensive 3D understanding of the environment, enabling the detection of overhanging structures, low-profile objects, and other complex obstacles that commonly lead to collisions.
- **Effective Teacher-Student Framework for Vision:** The study successfully demonstrates a teacher-student reinforcement learning framework optimized for vision-based navigation with MDE. The use of privileged information during teacher training and a meticulous noise augmentation pipeline for the student's depth maps effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap, a perennial challenge in robotics. This approach shows promise for developing complex behaviors that are difficult to learn directly with noisy, real-world sensory input.
- **Onboard Real-time Deployment:** The successful deployment of the entire inference pipeline, including MDE and policy execution, on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin AGX embedded platform at a control frequency of 10 Hz highlights the practical feasibility of the solution. This eliminates the need for external, high-performance computing resources, making it suitable for standalone, mobile robotic applications.
- **Performance Validation:** Both simulation and real-world experiments quantitatively demonstrate that the MDE-based student policy can outperform a standard 2D LiDAR-based teacher, particularly in scenarios involving 3D obstacles that challenge the LiDAR's limited perception. This empirical validation strengthens the case for vision-only navigation.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Cost Reduction in AMRs:** By relying primarily on inexpensive off-the-shelf RGB cameras and MDE, this work could substantially reduce the sensor-related costs of AMRs. This cost-effectiveness could accelerate the deployment of autonomous solutions in various industrial and commercial sectors, especially in applications where budget constraints or space limitations preclude expensive 3D LiDAR or stereo systems.
- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness:** Improved perception of complex 3D obstacles directly translates to safer and more robust robot operation. This could reduce collision rates, minimize damage to robots and infrastructure, and increase operational uptime in dynamic industrial environments, thereby improving overall efficiency and reliability.
- **Broader Application Scope:** The ability to navigate effectively using vision alone, coupled with robust obstacle avoidance, opens up new application areas for AMRs. Robots can operate in environments previously considered too challenging for 2D LiDAR, such as warehouses with varied shelf heights, construction sites, or even outdoor settings with varied terrain.
- **Advancement in Sim-to-Real Transfer for Vision:** The detailed methodology for noise augmentation in depth maps, tailored to MDE characteristics, provides a valuable contribution to the sim-to-real transfer literature. This could inform future research aiming to deploy DRL policies that rely on learned, imperfect perception systems.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a foundation for addressing more advanced challenges, such as dynamic obstacle avoidance, which is a stated area for future work. Investigating online adaptation strategies to further close the residual sim-to-real gap and address MDE scale errors could lead to even more resilient and versatile navigation systems.
