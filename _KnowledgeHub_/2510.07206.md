# EigenScore: OOD Detection using Posterior Covariance in Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Shoushtari et al. (WUSTL / UC Riverside, ICLR 2026) introduce **EigenScore**, an unsupervised OOD detector that uses the ==leading eigenvalues of the posterior covariance== of a diffusion denoiser as a distribution-shift signal. Estimated via a **Jacobian-free subspace iteration** (central-difference power method on forward denoiser evals), it reaches SOTA AUROC on CIFAR-10/100, SVHN, CelebA, TinyImageNet and remains robust in near-OOD (C10 vs C100) — up to **+5% AUROC** over the best baseline.

## Problem

- Existing diffusion-based OOD detectors are reconstruction-based (input–output similarity) or trajectory-based (path geometry); both can fail in near-OOD regimes where likelihoods are spuriously high on OOD inputs.
- Density / likelihood methods assign high likelihoods to semantically distant OOD samples (e.g., CIFAR-10-trained flow gives higher likelihood to SVHN).
- A principled signal that characterizes **local curvature of the learned distribution** at a query point is missing from the current toolbox.

## Method

- **EigenScore** = leading-k eigenvalues (or trace) of the ==posterior covariance $\Sigma(x_t) = \sigma_t^2 \cdot \partial_x D_\theta(x_t, \sigma_t)$== of the denoiser $D_\theta$ at noise level $\sigma_t$, averaged over a few timesteps.
- ==Jacobian-free subspace iteration==: uses only forward denoiser evaluations via central-difference approximation $(D(x+\epsilon v) - D(x-\epsilon v))/(2\epsilon) ≈ J v$, then QR orthogonalization — no backprop through the denoiser.
- ==Calibrated on in-distribution data only== (zero failure / OOD labels needed); threshold set by quantile over ID trace-spectrum.
- Built on EDM (Karras et al.) noise schedule; code operates on DDPM/EDM-style $\epsilon$-predictors.

## Results

- **Up to +5% AUROC** improvement over best baseline (reconstruction-based, likelihood-based, or MSMA) on CIFAR-10 / CIFAR-100 / SVHN / CelebA / TinyImageNet.
- Particularly strong in **near-OOD** settings (CIFAR-10 vs CIFAR-100) where diffusion-based methods typically fail.
- Trace and leading eigenvalues both work; leading eigenvalue is competitive with trace at a fraction of compute cost.
- Compute cost: a few forward denoiser passes per power-iteration step × a few steps × a few timesteps; competitive with reconstruction-based scoring.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - ==Posterior covariance of a diffusion denoiser is a distinct OOD signal-family from likelihood==: it measures local **curvature / uncertainty geometry** rather than where a point sits in the learned density.
> - Jacobian-free subspace iteration brings the signal into practical reach (central differences + QR on forward evals), a crucial engineering contribution for 2B-scale models.
> - ID-only calibration makes this compatible with success-only functional CP protocols — natural fit for robotics failure detection.
> - Candidate signal for **imag anchor #2** in FM-backbone WMs, conditional on (i) FM velocity ↔ score reformulation of the posterior-covariance identity and (ii) verification that the spectral signal on $\hat{O}_{t+1}$ is structurally decorrelated from `logpZO` density (H5 Spearman ρ < 0.6).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is a critical area within machine learning, particularly for the safe deployment of systems in sensitive domains such as medical imaging and autonomous driving. The primary challenge lies in the assumption that machine learning models operate on data matching their training distribution, whereas real-world applications frequently encounter distribution shifts or OOD data. Such shifts can result from various factors, including sensor noise, semantic variations, or changes in data acquisition, leading to degraded performance and unreliable predictions.

Existing OOD detection methodologies broadly fall into four categories:
- **Uncertainty-based methods:** These leverage signals like softmax confidence, ensemble variance, or Bayesian inference to identify anomalous inputs.
- **Distance-based methods:** These compare test input embeddings to in-distribution (InD) features, often using metrics like Mahalanobis distance.
- **Density-based methods:** These include flow- and energy-based models that attempt to estimate likelihoods. However, these methods have been observed to assign spuriously high likelihoods to OOD data, challenging their reliability.
- **Representation-learning methods:** These aim to improve robustness by explicitly shaping feature spaces, often employing self-supervised or contrastive learning techniques.

In recent years, diffusion models (DMs) have emerged as generative models capable of producing high-quality samples and capturing complex data distributions through iterative denoising. Their success has expanded beyond generation to applications in imaging inverse problems and medical tasks. Critically, diffusion models are relevant for OOD detection because their iterative denoising process provides access to score functions that characterize the data distribution. Initial applications in OOD detection exploited this property through likelihood- or reconstruction-based scores. More recent work has explored structural aspects of the diffusion trajectory, such as score geometry and intermediate representations, signaling a move towards more principled, less heuristic approaches.

This work positions itself within the diffusion-based OOD detection landscape, specifically as an unsupervised, feature-based framework. While other diffusion-based methods analyze input-output similarity (reconstruction) or diffusion-path geometry, EigenScore differentiates itself by leveraging the *covariance structure* of the denoising process. This approach is presented as a theoretically grounded and interpretable signal for identifying distribution shift, addressing some of the inconsistencies observed in prior diffusion-based OOD metrics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and reliable unsupervised OOD detection method that leverages the capabilities of diffusion models, particularly for challenging scenarios such as near-OOD detection.

The motivation for proposing EigenScore stems from several observed limitations and theoretical opportunities:

1. **Unreliability of Existing Diffusion-Based OOD Metrics:**
 - **Likelihood-based scores (e.g., Negative Log-Likelihood - NLL):** While diffusion models train via a variational lower bound, NLL has been shown to be an unreliable OOD indicator. Generative models can assign high likelihoods to OOD inputs, and NLL does not consistently align with semantic structure, often emphasizing low-level statistics over higher-level semantics. Empirical observations (Figure 1 in the paper) indicate that NLL frequently fails to separate InD from OOD samples, showing substantial overlap, especially in near-OOD tasks.
 - **Score function norms and their temporal derivatives:** Metrics derived from the score function $\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t, t)$ or its temporal derivative $\partial_t \epsilon_{\theta}(x_t, t)$ have been used. However, these metrics are often unstable. In near-OOD settings (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100), their distributions can overlap significantly. Furthermore, in some scenarios (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. SVHN), the ordering can invert, with OOD samples receiving lower scores than InD samples, making fixed thresholds impractical and signal direction inconsistent. These scalarized metrics collapse complex uncertainty information into a single number, potentially discarding discriminative structural cues.

2. **Theoretical Link Between Denoising Uncertainty and Distribution Shift:**
 - The authors aim to move beyond heuristic scoring rules by establishing a principled connection between the intrinsic uncertainty of the denoising process in a diffusion model and distribution mismatch.
 - They leverage established connections of KL divergence with score-based representations (Equation 4) and further extend this to denoising error (Proposition 1). This proposition posits that KL divergence, and thus distribution shift, can be quantified by the accumulation of excess denoising error when an InD-trained denoiser is applied to OOD data. This implies that OOD samples should systematically incur larger denoising error.
 - This denoising error is then linked to the total posterior variance (Equation 5), suggesting that the model's predictive uncertainty directly corresponds to distribution shift. Specifically, when an InD diffusion model processes OOD inputs, its posterior variance is hypothesized to inflate.

3. **Leveraging Covariance Structure for a Stable Signal:**
 - To operationalize the concept of inflated posterior variance, the research proposes analyzing the *eigenvalue spectrum* of the posterior covariance. The Miyasawa identity (Equation 6) directly relates the posterior covariance to the Jacobian of the MMSE denoiser. Consequently, the total posterior variance is equivalent to the trace of this covariance matrix, which is the sum of its eigenvalues (Equation 8).
 - The hypothesis is that for InD samples, the eigenvalue spectrum will be compact, reflecting structured denoising. For OOD samples, the uncertainty will spread across multiple eigen-directions, leading to an inflation of both the spectrum and its trace. This "spectral inflation" is proposed as a consistent and quantitative signal of distribution shift, addressing the ordering inconsistency of scalar metrics.

4. **Tractability and Efficiency:**
 - Direct computation of the denoiser's Jacobian for high-dimensional data is computationally prohibitive. Therefore, a practical objective is to develop an efficient, Jacobian-free method to estimate the leading eigenvalues, ensuring the proposed approach can be applied at scale.

In summary, the key motivation is to provide a theoretically grounded, stable, and empirically superior OOD detection method that overcomes the limitations of previous diffusion-based approaches by focusing on the spectral characteristics of denoising uncertainty.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EigenScore is an unsupervised, feature-based OOD detection framework that leverages the covariance structure of the denoising process within a diffusion model. The methodology comprises theoretical derivations, a specific OOD scoring function, and an efficient computational approach.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Foundation

1. **Diffusion Models and Score Functions:** The work builds on the principles of diffusion models (DMs), which learn to generate data by approximating a reverse Markov chain that iteratively denoises a perturbed input. A denoising network $D_{\theta}(x_t, t)$ is trained to predict the clean signal or noise given a noisy input $x_t$ at timestep $t$. Tweedie's formula establishes a link between Gaussian denoising and score estimation, stating that the posterior mean $E_p[x|x_t]$ (which the denoiser approximates) is related to the gradient of the log-density $\nabla \log p(x_t)$. This connection implies that DMs provide access to score functions and posterior statistics, which EigenScore aims to utilize.

2. **Connecting KL Divergence to Denoising Error:** The authors revisit the KL divergence between an in-distribution $p(x)$ and an OOD distribution $q(x)$. Equation 4 relates this divergence to the integral of the squared difference between the score functions of $p(x_t)$ and $q(x_t)$ across timesteps. Proposition 1 then re-expresses this KL divergence in terms of the Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the denoising process: $D_{KL}(p \Vert q) = \int_0^T [MSE(D_q, t) - MSE(D_p, t)] \sigma_t^{-3} dt$. This proposition indicates that distribution shift can be viewed as the accumulation of "excess" denoising error incurred when the optimal denoiser for $q$ is compared against that for $p$. Crucially, it implies that an InD denoiser $D_p$ will exhibit a larger expected denoising error on OOD inputs compared to InD inputs, providing a consistent directional signal for OOD detection.

3. **Denoising Error and Posterior Covariance:** The MSE of the MMSE denoiser $D_p(x_t)$ is equated to the expected trace of the conditional posterior covariance $E_{x_t}[\text{tr}(\text{Cov}_p[x|x_t])]$ (Equation 5). This establishes that denoising error is equivalent to the total posterior variance, meaning that the model's predictive uncertainty directly quantifies distribution shift. Larger posterior variance signifies higher uncertainty.

4. **Posterior Covariance and Jacobian Eigen-spectrum:** The Miyasawa identity (Equation 6) is applied to link the posterior covariance to the Jacobian of the MMSE denoiser: $\text{Cov}_p[x|x_t] = \sigma_t^2 \nabla D_p(x_t)$. Therefore, the total posterior variance (and MSE) can be expressed as $E_{x_t}[\sigma_t^2 \text{tr}(\nabla D_p(x_t))]$, which is the sum of the eigenvalues of $\sigma_t^2 \nabla D_p(x_t)$ (Equation 8). The core insight is that for OOD samples, this posterior covariance (and its eigenvalues) will inflate, indicating a "spectral signature" of distribution shift.

#### 3.2. EigenScore Metric Formulation

The EigenScore metric is constructed as follows:
1. **Eigenvalue Estimation:** For each input $x$ and a selected diffusion timestep $t$, the sum of the top-$K$ leading eigenvalues, $\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_t^k(x_t)$, is computed. This is done across $I$ different noise realizations to average out stochasticity, yielding $m_t(x)$ (Equation 9).
2. **Feature Vector Creation:** A feature vector $M(x) = [m_1(x), \ldots, m_T(x)]^T$ is formed by aggregating these sums across a predefined set of timesteps $T$ (Equation 10).
3. **Normalization and Final Score:** Each coordinate of $M(x)$ is normalized using Z-scores: $z_t(x) = (m_t(x) - \mu_t)/\sigma_t$, where $\mu_t$ and $\sigma_t$ are the mean and standard deviation of $m_t(x)$ computed on the in-distribution training set. The final OOD score, $S_{\theta}(x)$, is the sum of these normalized coordinates across the chosen timesteps.

#### 3.3. Efficient Computation of Eigenvalues

Directly computing the Jacobian of the denoiser is computationally expensive for high-dimensional images. To ensure tractability, EigenScore adopts a Jacobian-free subspace iteration method, inspired by previous work on uncertainty quantification in denoising. This method approximates Jacobian-vector products using finite differences of the denoiser, allowing for the estimation of the top-$K$ eigenvalues using only forward evaluations of the denoiser (Algorithm 2, Equation 11).

#### 3.4. Capturing Structure vs. Collapse of Scalar Metrics

The methodology explicitly addresses the limitations of scalar metrics like MSE or score norms by demonstrating why they might fail to distinguish OOD data effectively.
- **Lemma 1:** At high noise levels (large $\sigma_t$), the posterior covariance matrix $\Sigma_t(x_t)$ asymptotically approaches $\sigma_t^2 I$. This means all eigenvalues converge to $\sigma_t^2$, and the spectrum flattens. In such scenarios, scalar metrics aggregate mostly isotropic noise rather than discriminative structural information.
- **Proposition 2 (Ky Fan's Theorem):** This theorem states that the sum of the top-$K$ eigenvalues represents the maximal variance captured by any $K$-dimensional projection. By focusing on the top-$K$ eigenvalues, EigenScore aims to retain the most informative uncertainty directions, discarding noise-dominated components that become prominent at high noise levels, thereby providing a more stable and informative detection signal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of EigenScore was conducted across various OOD detection benchmarks, comparing its performance against a suite of generative baselines.

1. **Overall State-of-the-Art Performance:**
 - Table 1 demonstrates that EigenScore achieves the highest average AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve) across all tested InD-OOD dataset pairings.
 - It consistently ranks as either the best or second-best method across nearly all individual dataset pairs, indicating a generalizable improvement over existing approaches.

2. **Robustness in Near-OOD Settings:**
 - A significant finding is EigenScore's performance in challenging "near-OOD" scenarios, where OOD data shares substantial low-level statistics with InD data (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100, or vs. TinyImageNet).
 - In these specific settings, EigenScore showed an improvement in AUROC of up to 5% over the best diffusion-based baseline.
 - Table 2 highlights EigenScore's superior average AUROC in these near-OOD tasks, maintaining reliable separation where many likelihood-based or trajectory-based baselines (e.g., NLL, IC, WAIC) struggle and exhibit substantial overlap between InD and OOD scores. This robustness supports the theoretical claim that retaining leading eigenvalues preserves discriminative structural information.

3. **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Number of Timesteps (T):** Table 3 shows that performance improvements diminish with an increasing number of timesteps. Even a small budget (e.g., $T=5$) yields performance comparable to larger $T$, suggesting that a compact subset of timesteps captures most of the useful information. This aligns with Lemma 1, which predicts that very high noise levels reduce discriminative power.
 - **Number of Repetitions (I):** The number of noise realizations averaged per timestep has diminishing returns. Table 3 indicates that small values (e.g., $I=5$) are largely sufficient, with marginal gains beyond $I=15$, implying stability in eigenvalue estimation.
 - **Number of Eigenvalues (K):** Table 3 indicates that using only the first eigenvalue ($K=1$) often yields the best average performance, although $K=3$ can slightly improve AUROC in some cases. This suggests that the majority of discriminative information resides in the principal modes of the posterior covariance, consistent with Ky Fan's theorem.
 - **EigenScore vs. MSE:** Table 4 directly compares EigenScore against a scalar MSE-based metric. EigenScore consistently outperforms MSE across all dataset pairs, reinforcing the argument that leveraging the *spectral structure* of posterior covariance is more effective than collapsing uncertainty into a single scalar value.
 - **Informativeness Across Timesteps:** Figure 3 visually confirms Lemma 1 and Proposition 2. The AUROC for individual eigenvalues generally decreases as noise levels (timestep $t$) increase, demonstrating that higher noise reduces discriminative value. Furthermore, the first leading eigenvalue ($\lambda_1$) consistently provides the strongest OOD signal compared to $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$, supporting the premise that dominant modes capture the most informative variance.

In summary, the empirical results demonstrate that EigenScore provides a robust and quantitatively superior approach to OOD detection, particularly excelling in near-OOD scenarios where other diffusion-based methods tend to underperform. The ablation studies provide further validation for the design choices and theoretical underpinnings of the method.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of EigenScore contributes to the field of OOD detection in several ways, with potential implications for the reliability and safety of machine learning systems.

### 6. Significance

1. **Theoretically Grounded OOD Signal:** EigenScore establishes a principled connection between distribution shift and the spectral characteristics of posterior covariance in diffusion models. By linking KL divergence to excess denoising error, which is then shown to equate to total posterior variance, the method provides a robust theoretical basis for using this signal. This moves beyond empirical scoring rules towards a more fundamental understanding of how diffusion models can quantify out-of-distributionness.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Prior Methods:** The work critically identifies and addresses the shortcomings of existing diffusion-based OOD detection methods, particularly the unreliability and inconsistent ordering observed with likelihood-based scores (NLL) and score norms. EigenScore offers a more consistent and directional signal for OOD detection.
3. **Enhanced Robustness in Challenging Scenarios:** The demonstrated superior performance in near-OOD settings, such as CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100, is particularly significant. These scenarios are known to be difficult for many OOD detectors due to shared low-level features, and EigenScore's robustness in these conditions indicates a substantial advance.
4. **Emphasis on Spectral Structure:** By focusing on the eigenvalue spectrum, rather than scalar aggregates like MSE, EigenScore highlights the importance of retaining structural information about uncertainty. This shift in perspective, supported by Lemma 1 and Ky Fan's theorem, suggests that the "how" of uncertainty (its distribution across principal directions) is as important as the "how much" (total variance).
5. **Computational Tractability:** The adoption of a Jacobian-free subspace iteration method ensures that the proposed theoretically sound approach is also practical and scalable, requiring only forward passes of the denoiser.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Improved Safety and Reliability of ML Systems:** By offering a more robust method for identifying OOD inputs, EigenScore can enhance the safety and reliability of machine learning applications in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, medical diagnostics, and industrial control, where unexpected data can lead to catastrophic failures.
2. **New Research Directions:** The work opens avenues for further research into leveraging the rich structural information within generative models, particularly diffusion models, for uncertainty quantification and anomaly detection. Future studies could explore the utility of eigenvector structure, temporal dynamics of eigenvalues, or the rate of change of eigenvalues as additional discriminative signals.
3. **Foundation for Interpretable OOD Detection:** The link between posterior covariance inflation and distribution mismatch offers a more interpretable signal compared to black-box confidence scores. Understanding *how* uncertainty spreads across different feature dimensions could provide insights into the nature of the distribution shift.
4. **Broader Adoption of Diffusion Models for OOD:** By demonstrating a principled and effective use of diffusion models for OOD detection, this research may encourage their wider adoption and integration into OOD detection pipelines, moving beyond their primary role in generation.

**Limitations (as stated by the authors):**
1. **Incomplete Information Utilization:** EigenScore currently leverages only the eigenvalue magnitudes of the posterior covariance, discarding the potentially informative eigenvector structure.
2. **Limited Temporal Analysis:** Features are computed at a finite and often small subset of timesteps along the diffusion trajectory, potentially overlooking valuable temporal dynamics of uncertainty that might be present across the full trajectory.
3. **Magnitude-Focused Analysis:** The method focuses on the magnitude of eigenvalues, rather than explicitly exploiting their rate of change or inter-eigenvalue relationships, which could also serve as discriminative signals for distribution shift.
