# Using Deep Convolutional Neural Networks to Detect Rendered Glitches in Video Games

> [!summary] Summary
> A deep convolutional neural network approach was developed and evaluated for automatically identifying graphical glitches in video games. The method achieved an average validation accuracy of 86.8% in classifying normal frames or one of four specific texture-related glitch types, demonstrating a viable path to automate a component of game quality assurance.

## Problem

- Identifying visual bugs (glitches) in modern video games relies on manual testing, which is time-consuming, costly, and inefficient due to the sporadic nature of glitches.
- Manual testing lacks systematic procedures, leading to potential oversight of graphical glitches and a reduced player experience.
- The inherent complexity of game development frequently introduces numerous graphical anomalies, demanding more robust and scalable detection methods.

## Method

- Utilized ==Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs)== in a supervised learning framework to classify 800x800 RGB images into five categories: normal or one of four texture-related glitch types.
- Generated a large dataset of 34,200 labeled images synthetically from various Unity3D game environments, encompassing normal scenes and four types of texture glitches (stretched, low resolution, missing, and placeholder).
- Evaluated DCNN architectures like ==ResNet== and ==ShuffleNetV2==, leveraging ==ImageNet pre-trained weights==, and optimized hyperparameters for efficient and accurate classification.

## Results

- The best-performing ShuffleNetV2 model achieved an average validation accuracy of **86.8%** for the 5-class glitch classification problem.
- When treated as a binary classification (glitch vs. normal), the model attained a recall of **88.1%** with a **6.7%** false positive rate.
- Aggregating classification probabilities from multiple images of the same object improved accuracy, increasing from **83.2%** (single image) to **86.4%** (20 images) per object.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pre-training DCNNs on ImageNet significantly enhances performance for glitch detection, indicating transferability of features from natural images to game visuals.
> - The design of placeholder textures influences detection accuracy; patterned placeholders are more easily classified than plain ones, suggesting practical guidance for asset pipeline design.
> - ShuffleNetV2 emerges as an optimal architecture, balancing high accuracy (86.8%) with real-time computational efficiency (60 frames per second) for this specific task.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of modern video games is a complex undertaking, frequently involving hundreds of developers and artists. This complexity can lead to the introduction of numerous bugs, many of which manifest as graphical anomalies or "glitches" in the rendered output. Traditionally, identifying these visual bugs has relied on manual testing by human quality assurance personnel. This process is often time-consuming, costly, and inefficient due to the sporadic nature of glitches and the lack of systematic detection procedures, making it susceptible to human oversight.

This work situates itself within the broader field of anomaly detection, a discipline that has demonstrated utility in various domains such as finance, fault detection, surveillance, and medical research. Recent advancements in deep learning have enabled the detection of anomalous instances in complex data like images and video, often utilizing unsupervised or semi-supervised approaches due to the scarcity of labeled anomaly data in real-world scenarios.

For video games, the authors identify two distinct characteristics that influence the applicability of anomaly detection:
1. **Availability of Data:** Video game rendering engines can synthetically generate a large volume of images, unlike natural image datasets that require sampling.
2. **Generation of Negative Samples:** When a game element malfunctions and produces a glitch, the source can often be traced, allowing for the synthetic reproduction of similar artifacts.

These characteristics enable a supervised learning approach, where models are trained on large, labeled datasets of both normal and glitched images. This contrasts with many general anomaly detection scenarios where labeled anomalous data is rare. The paper leverages Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs), a class of models that have shown substantial performance improvements in image classification tasks since networks like AlexNet, ResNet, and ShuffleNet were introduced.

In comparison to previous efforts in automating video game testing, such as those by Nantes et al. (2008, 2013) which employed multi-layer perceptrons, self-organizing maps, or corner detection, this research proposes a DCNN-based methodology. This approach aims to be versatile, requiring only game captures as input, without the need for pre-processing or additional information from the game state, and adaptable to new glitch types through additional sample generation. The work therefore contributes to the ongoing effort to automate and enhance the quality assurance processes in video game development by applying state-of-the-art deep learning techniques tailored to the unique data generation capabilities of game engines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a method for detecting common graphical glitches in video games using Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs). The problem is specifically framed as an image classification task, where an 800x800 RGB input image is categorized into one of five predefined classes: "normal image" or one of four distinct types of texture-related glitches.

The core motivations for pursuing this objective are rooted in the challenges inherent in modern video game development and quality assurance:
- **Inefficiency of Manual Testing:** Manual graphical testing is identified as a time-consuming, labor-intensive, and often monotonous task. This inefficiency is exacerbated by the infrequent occurrence of glitches, making comprehensive manual coverage difficult and costly.
- **Risk of Overlooked Glitches:** The lack of a systematic procedure in manual testing means that many graphical glitches can be overlooked, potentially leading to a reduced player experience in the final product.
- **Importance of Visual Quality:** Graphics constitute a primary component of a video game's appeal. Ensuring the absence of visual malfunctions is therefore of high importance for maintaining player satisfaction and game quality.
- **Potential for Automation:** The authors recognize the critical need for automated testing of rendered images to achieve an effective development process. They hypothesize that DCNNs can fulfill this role, offering a more robust and scalable solution than traditional methods for detecting visual anomalies.
- **Leveraging Game Engine Capabilities:** A key motivation is to capitalize on the unique characteristics of video game development, specifically the ability to synthetically generate vast amounts of labeled data, including specific glitch types. This capability enables a supervised learning paradigm, which often yields higher performance compared to unsupervised methods necessitated by data scarcity in other anomaly detection domains.
- **Developing a Versatile Tool:** The research aims to produce a detection method that operates directly on game captures without requiring intrusive game state information or extensive pre-processing. This design choice is intended to create a tool that is highly adaptable and easily integrated into various stages of a game development pipeline.
- **Focus on Specific Glitch Types:** The initial focus on texture-related glitches (stretched, low resolution, missing, and placeholder textures) is motivated by their common occurrence during game development, particularly concerning asset management and rendering pipelines.

In summary, the detailed objective is to demonstrate that DCNNs can effectively identify specific graphical anomalies in video game imagery, thereby partially automating the graphical testing phase, improving efficiency, reducing costs, and ultimately contributing to higher quality game releases.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method utilizes a supervised learning approach with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs) to classify 800x800 RGB images into one of five categories: "normal" or one of four specific glitch types.

**Data Generation:**
The training data was synthetically generated using the Unity3D game engine, focusing on common texture-related glitches. Four distinct glitch types were synthesized:
- **Corrupted Textures:**
 - **Stretched:** Simulated by scaling a texture in one random direction on the object surface, leading to characteristic line patterns.
 - **Low Resolution:** Recreated by uniformly scaling the texture in every direction, mimicking scenarios where a lower Level of Detail (LoD) texture is loaded.
- **Missing Textures:**
 - **Missing:** Achieved by removing the texture, causing the rendering engine to display a default solid color, such as a pink monochromatic placeholder in Unity3D.
 - **Placeholder:** Generated by replacing original textures with default placeholder textures. Two versions were created: a plain white placeholder and a checked pattern with text, designed to assess the impact of placeholder visibility.

The core dataset comprised 12,700 samples from the "Book of the Dead" Unity3D environment, with a balanced distribution of 20% normal images and 80% glitch images. These images were captured from 127 unique objects, with 100 images per object covering various angles and distances. To evaluate the model's generalization capabilities, two additional datasets were generated from the "Unity3D FPS Sample" (10,000 samples) and "Viking Village" (11,500 samples) environments, bringing the total to 34,200 samples.

For model validation, data separation between training and validation sets was performed at the object level (80% objects for training, 20% for validation), ensuring that the model was tested on unseen objects.

**DCNN Architectures:**
The selection of DCNN architectures considered both classification performance on ImageNet and computational efficiency, the latter being crucial for potential real-time application alongside game rendering. The study focused on:
- **ResNet:** Explored variants with 18, 34, and 50 convolutional layers (ResNet18, ResNet34, ResNet50).
- **ShuffleNetV2:** Explored versions with varying filter complexities (x0.5, x1.0, x1.5, x2.0).
Other architectures like VGG, AlexNet, and MobileNetV2 were also evaluated but were found to be less optimal in this context.

**Training Details:**
Models were implemented in Pytorch, leveraging the Torchvision library.
- **Optimizer:** Adam optimizer was used.
- **Loss Function:** Cross-entropy loss was minimized.
- **Epochs:** Models were trained for 100 epochs, with performance typically stabilizing within 50 epochs.
- **Initialization:** Networks were initialized in two ways: with random weights and with weights pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, to assess the benefits of transfer learning.
- **Hyperparameter Tuning:** For the best-performing ShuffleNetV2 model, the learning rate (10^-2, 10^-3, 10^-4, 10^-5) and batch size (4, 8, 16, 32) were systematically explored.

**Metrics:**
Model performance was evaluated using several metrics:
- **Accuracy:** The primary metric, calculated as the fraction of correctly predicted samples, suitable due to the balanced dataset.
- **Binary Classification Metrics:** Precision (glitch predictions that are actual glitches), Recall (actual glitches detected), and False Positive Rate (FPR, non-glitches predicted as glitches) were calculated for the best model when considering the problem as a binary classification (normal/glitch).
- **Confusion Matrices:** Normalized confusion matrices provided detailed insights into classification behavior, indicating which classes were most frequently mistaken.

**Refined Model Modifications and Explorations:**
The study also explored several modifications:
- **Class Grouping:** Investigated grouping the five classes into three (corrupted, missing, normal) or two (glitch, normal) classes, but the 5-class setup was deemed optimal for information content.
- **Semantic Segmentation:** Explored U-Net, Fully Convolutional Networks (FCN), and Deep Lab V3 for pixel-wise glitch localization, but this approach yielded substantially worse detection rates compared to classification.
- **Image Aggregation:** Evaluated the effect of classifying an object by averaging probabilities from multiple images captured from different angles and distances, observing improvements in accuracy with more images.
- **Confidence Measures:** Investigated the Gauss-Confidence measure to potentially reduce false positives, noting its varying confidence levels across different glitch types.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy and practical considerations of using DCNNs for glitch detection:

**DCNN Architecture and Initialization:**
- **Pre-trained Models:** Utilizing ImageNet pre-trained weights for network initialization consistently led to significant performance improvements across all tested DCNN architectures compared to random initialization. This was attributed to the similarity between video game imagery and natural images.
- **Overfitting:** A notable difference between training and validation accuracy was observed, indicating that the models, despite their performance, exhibited a tendency to overfit the relatively small dataset of 12,700 samples.
- **Optimal Architecture:** ShuffleNetV2 was identified as the most suitable architecture for this task, offering a balance of high accuracy and computational efficiency, achieving an execution rate of 60 frames per second (fps). Different complexity versions of ShuffleNetV2 showed no significant performance differences. Other architectures like VGG and AlexNet performed worse, while MobileNetV2 had similar performance but slower training times.

**Hyperparameter Optimization for ShuffleNetV2:**
- **Learning Rate:** An optimal learning rate of 10^-4 resulted in the highest validation accuracy (0.801) for ShuffleNetV2.
- **Batch Size:** Increasing the batch size generally improved validation accuracy, with the highest accuracy (0.819) achieved at a batch size of 32, limited by the available GPU memory.

**Impact of Placeholder Texture Design:**
- The design of placeholder textures had a measurable impact on detection performance. Using a placeholder with a recognizable pattern significantly improved classification accuracy compared to a plain white placeholder. The recognizable pattern reduced misclassifications by making it less likely for the network to confuse corrupted textures (stretched, low resolution) with the placeholder itself.
- Confusion matrices indicated that "missing" and "placeholder" textures were generally easier for the network to classify correctly, while "stretched" and "low resolution" textures presented more challenges and higher confusion rates.

**Overall Best Performance:**
The best performance was achieved using ShuffleNetV2 with a patterned placeholder texture, demonstrating an average validation accuracy of 86.8% over the last 20 epochs. For the binary classification problem (glitch vs. normal), the model achieved a recall of 88.1% (detecting 88.1% of actual glitches) with a false positive rate of 6.7%.

**Generalization Across Environments:**
The method exhibited similar performance across different game environments (Book of the Dead, FPS Sample, Viking Village), suggesting its potential applicability to diverse video game artistic styles. However, training a single model on all datasets resulted in slightly lower performance on individual datasets, indicating that training a specialized model for the target environment might be more advantageous.

**Refined Model Explorations:**
- **Class Grouping:** Grouping glitches into fewer classes (e.g., 3-class or binary) did not lead to performance improvements and resulted in a loss of detailed information, confirming the 5-class classification as optimal for this problem.
- **Semantic Segmentation:** Attempts to use semantic segmentation networks (U-Net, FCN, Deep Lab V3) for pixel-wise localization yielded substantially lower detection rates (0.266 to 0.559) compared to the classification approach, suggesting this was not the most effective strategy given the problem and labeling challenges.
- **Image Aggregation:** Aggregating classification probabilities from multiple images of the same object (captured from different angles/distances) improved overall accuracy. For instance, using 20 images for an object increased accuracy to 86.4% from 83.2% with a single image, with diminishing returns as more images were added.
- **Confidence Measures:** Application of the Gauss-Confidence measure demonstrated higher confidence scores for "missing" and "placeholder" glitches (mean ~0.64) and lower scores for "stretched" and "low resolution" textures (mean ~0.2). This suggests its potential utility in filtering false positives and dynamically requesting more images for low-confidence classifications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a viable approach for the automated detection of specific graphical anomalies in video games, offering several significant implications and potential impacts on the video game development industry:

- **Automation of Quality Assurance:** The most direct impact is the partial automation of graphical testing, a process that is currently labor-intensive, time-consuming, and expensive when performed manually. By automating the detection of common texture glitches, development teams can reallocate human resources to more complex or creative tasks, enhancing overall efficiency and reducing quality assurance costs.
- **Improved Game Quality and Player Experience:** Early and consistent detection of graphical glitches ensures higher visual fidelity in the final game product. This directly contributes to a more polished game, preventing visual distractions or immersion-breaking errors that can negatively impact the player's experience.
- **Enhanced Efficiency in Development Cycles:** The ability to perform rapid, automated checks for glitches, even in real-time alongside game rendering (due to the 60 fps execution rate of ShuffleNetV2), allows for quicker feedback loops for artists and developers. This can significantly streamline the debugging process, especially during later stages of development, such as content updates, engine migrations, or multi-platform releases.
- **Versatile and Non-Intrusive Tool:** The methodology's independence from direct game state information and its reliance solely on rendered images provides high versatility. It can be integrated into diverse testing pipelines, including processing recorded gameplay footage, executing scripted camera paths focused on specific assets, or running entirely separately from the game executable.
- **Guidance for Asset Creation and Tooling:** The finding that recognizable patterns in placeholder textures improve automated detection provides practical guidance for game engine and asset pipeline designers. Implementing visually distinct placeholders can aid both human testers and automated systems in identifying missing or incorrectly loaded assets. The observation that low-resolution textures are harder to classify accurately, mirroring human tester difficulties, offers insight into common challenges in visual QA.
- **Foundation for Future Advanced Testing:** While focused on specific texture glitches, this work establishes a baseline for applying DCNNs to game testing. It opens avenues for future research into detecting other types of graphical anomalies, exploring object detection for localization information, and leveraging unsupervised learning for novel glitch discovery.
- **Synergy with Other AI Applications:** The paper suggests potential synergies with other AI approaches, such as integrating glitch detection as a reward signal for reinforcement learning agents that explore game environments, enabling them to actively seek out graphical bugs.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
A primary limitation is the supervised nature of the approach, which requires extensive labeled data for specific glitch types. This makes it less suitable for the very early stages of development where no such data exists or for detecting entirely unforeseen types of glitches. However, its effectiveness increases in later development phases when data generation becomes feasible. The current method also lacks precise localization information, a challenge semantic segmentation attempts did not resolve but which could be addressed by object detection techniques in future work. The study also acknowledges that it focused on a specific subset of glitches in static images, indicating that further research is needed for broader glitch categories and video sequences.

Despite these limitations, the research concludes that DCNNs offer a promising and practical solution to improve video game quality assurance, demonstrating their capacity to partially automate a critical manual process in modern game production environments.
