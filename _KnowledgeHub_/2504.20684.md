# Identifying Uncertainty in Self-Adaptive Robotics with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This research evaluates the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) to systematically identify uncertainties in self-adaptive robotic systems. An empirical study with 10 LLMs across four industrial-scale use cases showed that practitioners agreed with 63% to 88% of the LLM-identified uncertainties, including some previously overlooked ones.

## Problem

- Identifying and managing uncertainties in increasingly complex self-adaptive robots is challenging due to dynamic environments, evolving configurations, and complex interactions.
- Current practices for uncertainty identification often rely on practitioners' intuition and experience, which can be unsystematic and limited by incomplete knowledge of unpredictable operational contexts.
- A lack of comprehensive understanding exists regarding all factors influencing uncertainty across the entire robotic software engineering lifecycle.

## Method

- A novel, systematic LLM-prompting method was developed, instructing LLMs to assume specific roles and leverage robotic requirements to identify uncertainties.
- Ten advanced LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, ==Gemini Pro== 1.5) were selected and used with self-consistency prompting to generate uncertainty responses for four industrial-sized robotic case studies.
- Practitioners from each case study evaluated the anonymized LLM-generated responses using a Likert scale and discussed their findings, followed by quantitative and qualitative data analysis.

## Results

- Practitioners showed substantial agreement, ranging from **63%** to **88%**, with the uncertainties identified by the LLMs across all four industrial case studies.
- Specific LLMs like GPT-4o, Claude **3.5** Sonnet, and LLama **3.3** 70B exhibited high agreement and low disagreement in respective case studies.
- Inter-rater reliability (Cohen's Kappa) and bootstrap confidence intervals confirmed high consistency and reliability in practitioner evaluations of LLM responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs demonstrated a strong understanding of specific robotic domain knowledge and generated logical, contextually relevant responses for identifying uncertainties.
> - LLMs identified realistic uncertainties, some of which were previously unconsidered by practitioners, showcasing their ability to augment human expertise.
> - Practitioner feedback revealed fragmented understanding of uncertainties across different software engineering lifecycle phases, suggesting LLMs could bridge these knowledge gaps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Self-adaptive robotics is an area of increasing focus, with systems designed to autonomously adjust their behavior in response to dynamic environmental conditions and unforeseen events. A foundational aspect of these systems is the MAPE-K (Monitoring, Analysis, Planning, Execution, and Knowledge) loop, which enables continuous data collection, analysis, action planning, and execution refinement. Within this framework, a critical challenge lies in managing uncertainty throughout the entire robotic software engineering lifecycle, from design to operation. Uncertainty can stem from various sources, including unpredictable environments, sensor and actuator limitations, and complex human-robot or robot-robot interactions, all of which influence a robot's dependability and decision-making.

Current practices for identifying and managing uncertainties in self-adaptive robotics often rely on practitioners' intuition and experience with similar systems. This approach is limited by the inherent complexity of self-adaptive robots, their evolving configurations, adaptive behaviors, and a lack of comprehensive knowledge regarding unpredictable operational contexts. Prior research has addressed uncertainty by focusing on specific types, sources, or dimensions of uncertainty, or by using techniques like anomaly detection or digital twins for runtime verification. For example, some studies identify application-dependent uncertainties or propose classifications of anomalies for autonomous missions, while others explore uncertainty quantification for specific robot properties or use LLMs for failure detection in task planning.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by exploring the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to provide a systematic and automated approach for identifying uncertainties in self-adaptive robotics. Unlike previous studies that might use LLMs for reactive failure detection, this research investigates their potential to proactively assist practitioners in identifying potential uncertainties throughout the software engineering lifecycle, aiming to move beyond intuition-based methods. The study evaluates the effectiveness of multiple LLMs across industrial-scale robotics case studies, focusing on system-level uncertainty identification and providing insights into their practical utility and limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing complexity and autonomy of self-adaptive robots, which are expected to operate reliably in highly dynamic and unpredictable environments. For these robots to be dependable, effectively managing uncertainty is critical. However, identifying the various sources and potential impacts of uncertainties within self-adaptive robotic systems, and subsequently defining appropriate mitigation strategies, is a significant challenge. This difficulty arises from several factors:
- **Inherent System Complexity:** Self-adaptive robots involve complex interactions, dynamic configurations, and constantly evolving behaviors, making it difficult to predict all potential uncertainty sources.
- **Lack of Comprehensive Knowledge:** There is often incomplete knowledge regarding the multitude of factors that can influence uncertainty in unpredictable operational contexts and environmental dynamics.
- **Reliance on Intuition:** In current practice, practitioners frequently depend on their intuition and insights gained from past experiences with similar systems to identify and manage uncertainties. This approach lacks systematicity and may overlook critical sources of uncertainty.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this research are detailed as follows:
1. **Evaluate the Potential of LLMs for Uncertainty Identification:** The central objective is to systematically evaluate whether Large Language Models (LLMs) can assist practitioners in identifying uncertainties in self-adaptive robotics throughout the entire software engineering lifecycle. This aims to provide a more systematic and comprehensive method than current intuition-based practices.
2. **Develop a Novel LLM-Prompting Method:** To achieve effective uncertainty identification, the research aims to design and implement a specific LLM-prompting method tailored for uncertainty identification, leveraging robotic requirements as input.
3. **Conduct an Industrial-Scale Evaluation Study:** The study aims to perform an empirical evaluation of various LLMs by engaging practitioners from four distinct industrial robotic domains. This evaluation will gather practitioner perspectives on LLM-generated uncertainty responses.
4. **Derive Actionable Insights and Future Directions:** Based on the evaluation results and practitioner feedback, the research seeks to extract practical insights and propose future research directions for both researchers and practitioners in the field of self-adaptive robotics and LLM applications.

The overall goal is to determine the value LLMs can provide in enhancing the systematic identification of uncertainties, thereby contributing to the development of more dependable self-adaptive robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of Large Language Models (LLMs) in identifying uncertainties in self-adaptive robotics and to determine their practical value for practitioners. The methodology involved a 10-step process, incorporating interactions between the researchers, practitioners, and a selection of LLMs.

**Industrial Context and Case Studies:**
The study was conducted within the framework of RoboSAPIENS, a European Research and Innovation project focused on developing methods and tools for self-adaptive robotic software. The project utilizes the MAPLE-K loop (Monitoring, Analysis, Planning, Legitimate, Execution, and Knowledge), an extension of the traditional MAPE-K loop that integrates a "Legitimate" phase to ensure safety compliance. Four industrial-sized use cases were selected for evaluation:
- **Industrial Disassembly Robot (IDR):** A robot designed for laptop disassembly, involving tasks like unsnapping fittings, unscrewing bolts, and removing cables.
- **Warehouse Robotic Swarm (WRS):** A system of three omnidirectional mobile robots for indoor delivery, focusing on safe autonomous navigation, obstacle detection, and dynamic path planning in the presence of humans.
- **Prolonged Hull of an Autonomous Vessel (PHAV):** A case centered on predicting the motion of autonomous vessels in real-world environments to facilitate dynamic self-adaptation and human decision-making.
- **Human-Robotic Interaction (HRI):** A scenario where robots serve as on-demand workers in manufacturing, loading and unloading workpieces from machines.

**Evaluation Methodology (10 Steps):**
1. **Step ❶: Requirements Collection:** Researchers collected detailed robotic software requirements from practitioners for each use case. These documents described operational contexts, expected behaviors, objectives, constraints, hardware characteristics (sensors, actuators), and software architecture.
2. **Steps ❷–❸: Uncertainty Questionnaire Development:** An initial uncertainty questionnaire, inspired by existing literature on dynamically adaptive systems, was designed. It covered seven aspects: uncertainty sources, identification methods, impacted lifecycle phases, effects on performance/safety, mitigation strategies, real-world failure scenarios, and overall project impact. This questionnaire was then refined based on feedback from practitioners to enhance clarity.
3. **Steps ❹–❻: LLM Interaction and Response Generation:**
 - **Prompt Engineering:** Initial attempts with zero-shot prompting were refined to adopt a role-based prompting strategy. LLMs were instructed to assume specific roles (e.g., engineer, tester) to provide task-specific responses. A detailed prompt (available on Zenodo) with step-by-step instructions was developed, directing LLMs to read requirements, apply robotic knowledge, understand the questionnaire's purpose, and provide concise, justified answers.
 - **LLM Selection:** Ten advanced LLMs with varying capabilities were selected: Gemini Pro 1.5, Perplexity Sonar, Nemotron 70B, Nova Pro, Mistral Large 2411, LLama 3.3 70B, o1 Preview, GPT-4o, Gemini Flash 2.0, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet.
 - **Response Generation:** Each LLM was provided with the specific use case requirement document and the developed prompt. To mitigate hallucination risks, self-consistency prompting was employed, where LLMs were re-prompted three to five times, and the most consistent response was selected for analysis.
4. **Step ❼: Confirmation Questionnaire Development:** All unique LLM-generated responses to the uncertainty questionnaire were compiled into a "confirmation questionnaire." The origin of each LLM response was anonymized. For each response, a five-point Likert scale (from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree") was used to capture practitioners' agreement levels. The questionnaire was developed using Google Forms.
5. **Steps ❽–❾: Practitioner Evaluation Sessions:** Four individual, in-person sessions were conducted, one for each case study. Each session involved two practitioners (one senior, one junior) from the respective case, lasting 40 minutes.
 - **Introduction (10 min):** Overview of activities, presentation of the revised uncertainty questionnaire, and explanation of objectives.
 - **Questionnaire Completion (20 min):** Participants completed the Google Forms confirmation questionnaire, assessing each anonymized LLM response based on their experience and knowledge.
 - **Verbal Discussion (10 min):** An open discussion followed to gather broader feedback on the LLM-generated responses, their understanding of the robotic context, and their potential for uncertainty identification. Informal notes were taken during this phase.
6. **Step ❿: Data Analysis:** The collected data from Google Forms was analyzed, focusing on agreement levels for LLM-generated responses in each case study. Key observations from verbal discussions supplemented this quantitative analysis. Statistical methods, specifically Cohen's Kappa, were used to assess inter-rater reliability between the two practitioners per case study, and the bootstrap method was employed to calculate 95% confidence intervals for the results, ensuring reliability and consistency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved 10 LLMs across four industrial-sized robotic case studies, with feedback gathered from practitioners. The findings indicate the potential of LLMs in identifying uncertainties in self-adaptive robotics, alongside specific observations regarding their performance and practitioner perceptions.

**Overall Practitioner Agreement:**
Across all case studies, practitioners exhibited a substantial level of agreement with the uncertainties identified by the LLMs, ranging from 63% to 88% of the generated responses.

**Case-Specific Results (Figure 2):**
- **Industrial Disassembly Robot (IDR):** GPT-4o demonstrated the highest agreement and lowest disagreement among LLMs, followed by o1 Preview and LLama 3.3 70B. Overall, practitioners agreed with over 60% of the LLM responses, often expressing strong agreement.
- **Warehouse Robotic Swarm (WRS):** Claude 3.5 Sonnet received the most strong agreements. Nemotron 70B, Nova Pro, and o1 Preview also achieved high overall agreement. Disagreement levels were low (11%), and over 80% of LLM responses were agreed upon by practitioners.
- **Prolonged Hull of an Autonomous Vessel (PHAV):** LLama 3.3 70B generated responses with the highest agreement and lowest disagreement, closely followed by Gemini Flash 2.0. Overall, practitioners expressed positive opinions for over 80% of the LLM responses.
- **Human-Robotic Interaction (HRI):** Perplexity Sonar, Mistral Large 2411, and GPT-4o received strong agreement for approximately 48% of their responses, while Nova Pro and o1 Preview achieved the highest overall agreement. Over 70% of LLM responses were agreed upon.

**Reliability and Confidence Analysis (Appendix A & B):**
- **Inter-rater Reliability (Cohen's Kappa):** Cohen's Kappa analysis confirmed unanimous agreement or disagreement between the two practitioners in each case study, indicating a high level of inter-rater reliability with no observed false positives or false negatives (Tables 1-4).
- **Confidence Intervals (Bootstrap Method):** Bootstrap confidence intervals (95% CI) demonstrated high confidence and consistency in the results across all case studies (Tables 5-8). Most categories, particularly 'Strongly Disagree' and 'Strongly Agree,' showed narrow confidence intervals, suggesting stable agreement levels and minimal variation in responses across models.

**Key Insights from Practitioner Discussions (Section 4.6):**
- **LLMs’ Familiarity with Domain:** Practitioners from all four case studies reported that LLMs' responses indicated a strong understanding of the specific case study specifications and domain knowledge, suggesting LLMs' ability to comprehend textual information and leverage related prior knowledge.
- **Logical Responses:** Practitioners uniformly agreed that the LLM-generated responses were logical and contextually relevant to the robotic scenarios, highlighting the analytical capabilities of the LLMs in identifying uncertainties.
- **Overlooked Uncertainties:** A significant finding was that LLMs identified uncertainties that were not only realistic but also, in some instances, previously unconsidered by the practitioners. Examples include variations in laptop components (IDR), LiDAR sensor occlusion (WRS), model transfer issues between vessels (PHAV), and unnecessary interactions leading to replanning (HRI).
- **Knowledge Gaps in Engineering Phases:** Practitioners revealed a fragmented understanding of uncertainties across different software engineering lifecycle phases. Those focused on development or operations often lacked awareness of uncertainties prevalent in other phases (e.g., testing or design), leading to disagreements or neutral assessments of LLM responses outside their direct expertise.
- **Uncertainty in Engineering Phases:** While uncertainties arise in all phases, practitioners emphasized that the testing phase often encounters the most uncertainties. Operational phases, especially in complex environments like autonomous vessels, also experience significant, previously undetected environmental uncertainties. Environmental dynamics were universally confirmed as a major source of uncertainty.
- **Managing Uncertainty:** Current uncertainty management techniques, such as modeling, simulation, digital twins, and uncertainty quantification, were noted to be labor-intensive. Practitioners saw significant potential for LLMs to support automation in these areas, given their demonstrated usefulness.
- **LLM Uncertainty Identification Trends:** LLMs were effective in identifying uncertainties from detailed hardware and software specifications. However, they sometimes broadly applied general uncertainty knowledge, which might not always fit specific system contexts (e.g., human error being less relevant for highly protocolized autonomous vessels but important for robotic swarms). Hardware issues and dynamic environments were identified as primary sources of uncertainty, followed by machine learning models within the MAPLE-K loop.
- **LLM Reasoning:** Reasoning-capable LLMs, such as o1 Preview, produced more detailed and structured responses. General-purpose models like GPT-4o sometimes offered broader, less context-specific reasoning, occasionally leading to practitioner disagreement. Reasoning models, when combined with advanced prompting, were recommended for effective uncertainty analysis.
- **Actionable Recommendations:** The study concluded with recommendations for practitioners, including combining prompting strategies (e.g., few-shot, chain-of-thought), cross-checking LLM responses with teams across different engineering phases to mitigate hallucination, using a second LLM for validation, and removing sensitive information from input documents.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates the potential of Large Language Models (LLMs) to systematically identify uncertainties in self-adaptive robotic systems, addressing a critical challenge in their development and operation. The work offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

Firstly, it provides a **systematic and automated approach** for uncertainty identification, which can significantly improve upon current practices that often rely on human intuition and limited experience. By leveraging LLMs, practitioners can potentially uncover a broader range of uncertainties across the entire software engineering lifecycle, from design to operational phases. This shift towards a more structured method could lead to more robust and dependable self-adaptive robots, particularly those operating in dynamic and unpredictable environments.

Secondly, the study's finding that LLMs can identify **previously overlooked but logically valid uncertainties** is noteworthy. This suggests that LLMs can augment human expertise by offering novel perspectives or drawing connections that might not be immediately apparent to human practitioners. Identifying these latent uncertainties early in the development cycle can prevent costly failures and enhance the overall reliability and safety of robotic systems.

Thirdly, the evaluation across **four industrial-sized robotic case studies** provides practical validation of LLMs in real-world contexts. The high agreement rates (63–88%) between practitioners and LLM-generated responses indicate the practical utility of these models. This empirical evidence can encourage wider adoption of LLM-based tools in industrial settings for uncertainty management, potentially streamlining the software engineering process for complex robotic systems.

Furthermore, the research highlights **knowledge gaps among practitioners regarding uncertainties across different engineering phases**. LLMs, by providing a comprehensive overview of potential uncertainties throughout the lifecycle, could serve as an educational tool or a knowledge repository, helping to bridge these gaps and fostering a more holistic understanding of uncertainty management within development teams.

The insights gained regarding LLM performance, specifically the effectiveness of **reasoning models** and the importance of **advanced prompting techniques**, offer actionable recommendations for both researchers and practitioners. These insights can guide the development of more sophisticated LLM-based tools tailored for specific engineering tasks in robotics, optimizing their deployment and maximizing their utility.

Finally, this work opens several **future research directions**, including comparative studies with human domain experts, large-scale empirical evaluations, and the development of holistic uncertainty identification frameworks. This foundational study paves the way for further advancements in integrating AI into complex engineering workflows, ultimately contributing to the creation of more intelligent, adaptable, and trustworthy autonomous systems.
