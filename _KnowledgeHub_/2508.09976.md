# Masquerade: Learning from In-the-wild Human Videos using Data-Editing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University introduce Masquerade, a framework that processes large-scale, uncurated human videos to create "robotized" demonstrations. This method explicitly closes the visual embodiment gap and employs a co-training strategy, resulting in up to 5-6 times higher zero-shot generalization success rates on novel environments for complex bimanual robot tasks compared to previous baselines.

## Problem

- Robot manipulation research faces a critical data scarcity issue, limiting the training of robust and generalizable policies.
- Abundant 'in-the-wild' human videos offer rich manipulation examples but are difficult to use due to the visual embodiment gap between humans and robots, and the absence of precise robot-action labels.
- Prior methods struggle to fully leverage the vast scale of uncurated human video data due to these explicit visual and labeling discrepancies.

## Method

- A data-editing pipeline transforms raw human videos into "robotized" demonstrations by estimating human hand poses, inpainting human body parts, and overlaying a virtual bimanual robot tracking the inferred actions.
- A vision encoder, pre-trained on these visually-aligned "robotized" human videos, is subsequently co-trained with a diffusion-based policy head on a small set of real robot demonstrations.
- During co-training, an auxiliary 2D keypoint regression loss on edited human videos is continuously optimized alongside the imitation learning loss on real robot data to maintain human knowledge and facilitate robustness.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **74%** across three bimanual kitchen tasks in out-of-distribution scenes, demonstrating a 5-6 times improvement (62 percentage points higher) over state-of-the-art baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed the indispensable role of both the robot overlay in data-editing and the continuous co-training strategy, with their removal causing substantial performance drops.
- Performance on the Stack Pots task monotonically improved with increasing amounts of edited human video data (e.g., from **2%** to **68%** when scaling to **100%** of available data), indicating effective data scaling benefits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly closing the visual embodiment gap through data-editing, even with imperfect overlays, is crucial for effectively transferring knowledge from human videos to robot policies.
> - A continuous co-training strategy that leverages both edited human videos and real robot demonstrations is essential for the vision encoder to retain diverse representations and achieve strong out-of-distribution generalization.
> - In-the-wild human videos, when coupled with a robust data-editing and co-training methodology, can serve as a scalable and effective solution to the data scarcity problem in robot manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation is currently grappling with a critical issue: data scarcity. Unlike natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV), which have been revolutionized by massive, diverse datasets, collecting real-world robot interaction data is slow, expensive, and often dangerous. This limitation severely hinders the development of generalist robot policies capable of operating robustly in diverse, unstructured environments.

In response, a growing body of research has turned to human videos as a potentially vast data source. These videos offer rich, real-world examples of manipulation tasks at a scale unimaginable with robot data. However, two primary challenges arise: the lack of precise robot-action labels and a significant "embodiment gap" – humans look and move differently from robots.

Prior works addressing these challenges can be broadly categorized into:
- **Learning from In-the-wild Human Videos:** These methods typically pre-train vision encoders on large, uncurated internet videos (e.g., R3M, Voltron, MVP, VC-1, HRP) using proxy tasks like time-contrastive learning or masked auto-encoding. Others infer reward functions [6–8], learn world models [9–12], or extract hand-pose trajectories [17–22] or object affordances [23–25]. Crucially, most of these approaches *implicitly* address the embodiment gap, assuming the model will learn correspondences between human and robot embodiments without explicit intervention.
- **Learning from Curated Human Video Demonstrations:** These datasets are collected specifically for robot learning, featuring cleaner, task-focused motions and sometimes more accurate annotations (e.g., depth data, AR/VR). Methods in this category often involve co-training policies on human and robot data (e.g., EgoMimic, PH2D, EgoVLA) or learning motion priors. A smaller subset has explored inpainting or visual editing to bridge the gap in these curated settings (e.g., Whirl, and the group's own Phantom).

"Masquerade" strategically positions itself at the intersection of these two areas. It aims to harness the *massive scale and diversity* of *in-the-wild* human videos, which previous methods struggled to fully leverage due to the embodiment gap. The core innovation lies in *explicitly and automatically closing the visual embodiment gap* in these uncurated videos through a data-editing pipeline inspired by their earlier "Phantom" work, and then combining this with a robust *co-training* strategy. This contrasts with most prior in-the-wild methods that neglect explicit visual alignment and current curated-video methods that lack the scale of internet data. The paper also highlights a concurrent but less effective work (H2R) that uses a similar pipeline but relies solely on finetuning, underscoring the importance of Masquerade's co-training approach. By demonstrating that even imperfect visual alignment yields significant gains, Masquerade pushes the boundary of what is achievable with readily available human video data for robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind Masquerade stems from the persistent problem of data scarcity in robot manipulation research. Despite advancements in language and vision models driven by enormous and diverse datasets, robot learning remains bottlenecked by the difficulty and expense of collecting real-world robot data. Human videos, particularly those "in-the-wild" (uncensored internet footage), represent an unparalleled, virtually limitless reservoir of real-world manipulation scenarios. However, two significant challenges prevent their direct application to robot policy learning:

1. **The Visual Embodiment Gap:** Humans and robots have vastly different visual appearances and physical kinematics. A human hand operating an object looks fundamentally different from a robot gripper performing the same task. This visual discrepancy makes it challenging for vision-based policies, which are often sensitive to domain shifts, to transfer learned representations from human videos to robots.
2. **Missing Precise Action Labels:** In-the-wild human videos typically lack explicit, robot-compatible action labels (e.g., end-effector poses, gripper commands), which are crucial for direct imitation learning.

Given these challenges, the **key objective** of Masquerade is to develop a novel method that explicitly bridges the visual embodiment gap between humans and robots in large-scale, in-the-wild egocentric human videos, thereby unlocking this vast data source to train more robust and generalizable robot manipulation policies.

More specifically, the paper aims to:
- Create a pipeline to automatically "robotize" in-the-wild human videos by estimating human hand poses, inpainting away human body parts, and overlaying a rendered bimanual robot tracking the inferred actions.
- Pre-train a visual encoder on these "robotized" human videos to learn rich, diverse, and visually-aligned features.
- Co-train this pre-trained vision encoder with a diffusion-based policy head on a small set of real robot demonstrations, while continually leveraging the edited human videos, to facilitate robust zero-shot generalization to unseen environments.
- Empirically demonstrate that this explicit embodiment gap closure, even if imperfect, leads to significantly improved generalization performance in long-horizon, bimanual tasks compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- Ablate the contributions of the robot overlay and the co-training strategy to confirm their necessity.
- Investigate the scalability of the method with increasing amounts of edited human video data.

By addressing these objectives, Masquerade seeks to provide a practical and highly effective solution to the data scarcity problem, paving the way for more capable and adaptable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Masquerade employs a three-stage pipeline: data-editing, vision encoder pre-training, and co-training, meticulously designed to bridge the human-robot embodiment gap and leverage large-scale in-the-wild videos for robust robot policy learning.

#### 3.1. Data Processing of In-the-Wild Egocentric Videos

This initial stage transforms raw human video clips (from the Epic Kitchens dataset) into "robotized" demonstrations, addressing both the visual embodiment gap and the lack of explicit robot action labels.

1. **Visual Editing Pipeline:** For each human video frame:
 - **Hand Pose Estimation:** Left and right hands are localized using Epic Kitchens annotations, and 21 anatomical keypoints per hand are estimated using HaMeR.
 - **3D Robot Pose Mapping:** These 2D keypoints are mapped to 3D robot end-effector poses (Cartesian position, orientation, gripper opening) following the Phantom approach. The poses are then temporally smoothed to reduce noise.
 - **Human Arm Inpainting:** Human arms are segmented out using Detectron2 and SAM2 and removed via E2FGVI inpainting.
 - **Virtual Robot Overlay:** A virtual bimanual robot model is rendered, with its end effectors tracking the derived 3D poses, and composited back into the original, in-painted video frame. This creates a video that visually depicts a robot performing the human action, forming the `D'human` dataset.
2. **Extracting Training Labels:** Since monocular human videos lack depth for accurate absolute 3D pose, 2D keypoints are used as auxiliary supervision. The temporally smoothed 3D end-effector positions are projected onto the image plane to obtain 2D action waypoints. The vision encoder is trained to predict a sequence of `H` future 2D waypoints, which are warped to the current frame's view using homographies to correct for egocentric camera motion.
3. **Data Filtering:** Frames with excessive egocentric camera motion (translation > 5 cm, rotation > 0.5 rad per timestep) are filtered out, as are frames with invalid actions due to keypoint errors or kinematic limits. This ensures a stable and reliable dataset, despite acknowledging that many overlays remain imperfect due to dexterous grasp retargeting limitations and lack of depth for occlusion handling.

#### 3.2. Policy Learning

The learning process involves a vision encoder `f(x, z)` (conditioned on language) and a diffusion-based action head `g(·)`.

1. **Vision Encoder Pre-training:**
 - A ViT-Base vision encoder, initialized with ImageNet weights, is pre-trained on the `D'human` dataset.
 - The encoder is conditioned on per-clip natural language annotations (encoded via DistilBERT) using FiLM, allowing semantic modulation of visual features.
 - The pre-training objective `L_2D` minimizes the L2 distance between the encoder's predicted 2D keypoint targets and the actual `H`-waypoint sequences extracted from `D'human`.
2. **Policy Learning using Co-training:**
 - A small set of task-specific real robot demonstrations (`D_robot`) is collected using a dual-Kinova-arm setup with an egocentric ZED mini camera.
 - Crucially, to minimize the visual domain gap, a rendered robot is *also inpainted over the real robot* in `D_robot` during this phase, ensuring consistent visual input for the model.
 - The pre-trained vision encoder `f` and a Diffusion Policy action head `g` are then *co-trained* on a mix of `D'human` and `D_robot`.
 - The total loss `L` combines two objectives:
 - `L_2D`: The auxiliary 2D keypoint regression loss from the `D'human` edited human videos, which *continues to be optimized* during co-training.
 - `L_policy`: An imitation learning loss that minimizes the L2 distance between the policy's predicted Cartesian end-effector actions and the ground-truth actions from `D_robot`.
 - A hyperparameter `λ` (empirically set to 10) balances these two losses. Co-training is emphasized as critical for retaining the knowledge learned from human videos and achieving out-of-distribution robustness.

The hardware setup for evaluation includes two Kinova Gen3 7-DoF arms with Robotiq 2F-85 grippers, controlled in Cartesian space, and a rigidly mounted ZED mini camera for egocentric observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Masquerade presents compelling results that underscore the effectiveness of explicitly bridging the visual embodiment gap and employing a co-training strategy. The experiments are conducted on three challenging long-horizon bimanual kitchen tasks (Stack Pots, Scrape Potato, Sweep Chilis) in three distinct out-of-distribution (OOD) scenes for each task, using a training set of 675K edited human video frames and only 50 real robot demonstrations per task.

1. **Superior Out-of-Distribution Performance:** Masquerade significantly outperforms all baselines (HRP, ImageNet pre-training, and DINOv2) across all three bimanual tasks in OOD scenes. It achieves an average success rate of 74%, which is approximately 5-6 times higher than the baselines' average of 12% (a 62 percentage point difference). This finding highlights the method's strong generalization capabilities to novel environments.
2. **Indispensability of Robot Overlays:** An ablation study comparing Masquerade with a "no-overlay" variant (pre-trained and co-trained on raw human videos) reveals a steep drop in performance for the latter. This demonstrates that explicitly closing the visual embodiment gap by overlaying rendered robots onto human videos is crucial for unlocking the full learning potential from human data. Even imperfect visual alignment dramatically improves cross-embodiment transfer.
3. **Critical Role of Co-training:** Another ablation examines a "no co-training" variant, where the vision encoder is pre-trained on edited human videos but only fine-tuned on the robot policy loss. This variant also experiences a dramatic performance drop. This result confirms that continuing to optimize the auxiliary 2D loss on edited human videos during policy training is essential for the encoder to retain valuable representations learned from human videos, preventing catastrophic forgetting and ensuring robust OOD performance.
4. **Data Scaling Benefits:** Experiments on the Stack Pots task show that increasing the amount of edited human video data during co-training monotonically improves success rates. Performance rises from 2% (no co-training data) to 26% (10%), 47% (50%), and 68% (100% of the data). This logarithmic scaling suggests that Masquerade effectively leverages larger datasets, implying further performance gains could be achieved with even more extensive human video corpora.
5. **Robustness to Scene Shifts:** When comparing in-distribution and OOD performance, Masquerade exhibits the smallest drop in success rate, maintaining similar high performance across seen and unseen scenes. This contrasts sharply with baselines, which suffer significant performance degradation when transferred to OOD environments, further solidifying Masquerade's robust generalization.

In summary, the findings robustly support the paper's core hypothesis: explicitly addressing the visual embodiment gap through data-editing, combined with a continuous co-training strategy, effectively enables robots to learn generalizable policies from readily available in-the-wild human videos.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Masquerade" work marks a significant stride in robot manipulation research, offering a powerful methodology to circumvent the pervasive data scarcity problem that has long plagued the field. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Unlocking a Vast Data Source:** By demonstrating that large-scale, in-the-wild human videos can be effectively leveraged for robot policy learning, Masquerade unlocks an unprecedented and readily available data source. The internet is replete with billions of hours of human activity, and converting a fraction of this into "robotized" demonstrations could provide the diverse, real-world experience robots currently lack. This has the potential to fundamentally change how robot datasets are constructed and scaled.
2. **Addressing the Embodiment Gap Explicitly:** The paper's key insight is the profound impact of *explicitly* closing the visual embodiment gap. While many prior works hoped models would implicitly learn cross-embodiment correspondences, Masquerade shows that even simple 2D inpainting and overlays dramatically boost performance. This principle could inspire new approaches to cross-embodiment transfer, emphasizing active data-editing over passive data ingestion.
3. **Robust Zero-Shot Generalization:** Achieving 5-6x better performance and minimal drops from in-distribution to out-of-distribution scenes is a critical advancement. This strong zero-shot generalization to novel environments is a holy grail in robotics, enabling policies to be deployed in new settings without costly retraining or scene-specific data collection. This capability is vital for real-world applications of robotics beyond highly controlled lab settings.
4. **Scalability and Future-Proofing:** The logarithmic scaling of performance with increasing amounts of edited human video data suggests a clear path for future improvements. As more human videos become available and computational resources grow, Masquerade-like methods are poised to achieve even higher levels of capability and generalization. Furthermore, the reliance on improving hand-pose estimators, depth reasoning, and retargeting algorithms means the method will naturally benefit from advancements in related computer vision and graphics fields.
5. **Foundation for Generalist Robot Policies:** This work moves closer to the vision of "generalist" robot policies, akin to foundation models in NLP and CV. By learning from diverse human experiences, robots can develop a broader understanding of manipulation tasks, objects, and environments, laying the groundwork for more versatile and adaptable robotic systems.
6. **Broader Applicability of Data-Editing:** While Masquerade focuses on vision representation and policy learning, the proposed data-editing pipeline itself is a valuable contribution. It could be extended to other areas of robot learning, such as reward learning from demonstrations, extraction of motion priors, or even for generating synthetic training data for world models, further amplifying its impact.

In conclusion, "Masquerade" is not just an incremental improvement; it presents a paradigm shift in how robot learning systems can leverage human data. By offering a practical, scalable, and effective solution to the data scarcity and embodiment gap challenges, it accelerates the trajectory towards truly autonomous and general-purpose robots capable of operating reliably in the complexity of the real world.
