# A New Human-Likeness and Comfort Index for Robot Movements Along Prescribed Paths

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Salerno introduced a Human-Likeness (HL) index, derived from the sigma-lognormal model of human movements, to quantitatively assess robot movement comfort *a priori*. This index was empirically validated in human-robot interaction experiments, showing a statistically significant correlation between higher HL values and increased user preference for comfortable movements.

## Problem

- The absence of a global, quantitative, and *a priori* index to assess human comfort in human-robot interaction (HRI) movements.
- Existing comfort evaluation methods are either qualitative (*a posteriori*) or rely on local motion characteristics that are insufficient for comprehensive comfort prediction.
- Difficulty in generating robot trajectories that are inherently perceived as comfortable, often requiring post-hoc assessment or trial-and-error.

## Method

- Proposed a **Human-Likeness (HL) index** for robot movements, grounded in the ==kinematic theory of rapid human movements== and its ==sigma-lognormal model==.
- The index quantifies human-likeness by combining the ==Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)== of a movement's sigma-lognormal reconstruction with a penalty for excessive motor "strokes" relative to the path's inherent complexity.
- Conducted human-robot interaction experiments comparing human, uniform, and time-optimal robot trajectories, collecting subjective comfort preferences from 68 participants.

## Results

- The proposed HL index demonstrated global consistency with user preferences, with human movements (higher HL) consistently preferred over uniform (lower HL) and often over scaled time-optimal trajectories (intermediate HL).
- Logistic regression analysis revealed a statistically significant association (**p < 10^-8** for all patterns) between the difference in HL values and the probability of preferring the trajectory with higher human-likeness.
- A threshold of **∆HL > 0.600** was identified, above which human subjects significantly distinguished between movements in terms of comfort, indicating the index's sensitivity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A direct and statistically significant relationship exists between the human-likeness of a robot's movement time law and human perception of comfort during interaction.
> - The sigma-lognormal model accurately characterizes human motor programs, enabling its use as a foundation for a quantitative human-likeness index independent of geometric path.
> - Traditional time-optimal trajectory planning methods can generate movements perceived as "jerky" and less comfortable on paths with high curvature, despite approximating human movement duration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), specifically addressing the growing importance of robot acceptance in various applications. Existing literature in HRI has highlighted that while visual similarity (e.g., humanoid robots) can contribute to acceptance, it is often insufficient, particularly in physical interaction scenarios. Acceptance is intricately linked to human comfort and ergonomics, which in turn are influenced by the perceived quality of robot movement.

Previous studies have explored the "social" dimension of robots, noting that cognitive and perceptual workload for operators can undermine acceptance. A common strategy to enhance acceptance is to design robots that resemble humans in both physical appearance and behavior. This includes advancements in brain-inspired intelligent robots capable of emulating human and animal structures and mechanisms, integrating visual cognition, decision-making, motion control, and musculoskeletal systems. A fundamental premise supporting this approach is the human tendency to prefer interacting with machines in a manner similar to human-human interactions.

The concept of "motor contagion" further informs this work, suggesting that observing and experiencing others' movements involuntarily influences an individual's motor actions. The degree of this effect is modulated by the human-likeness of a robot's movements and appearance. Research indicates that motor contagion occurs with robots, provided the robot's movements do not violate biological laws of motion. This implies that humans rely on recognizing elements of their own motor repertoire in observed robot actions, and that human-like robot movements can lead to easier prediction by human counterparts. Conversely, non-human-like or excessively fast robot movements have been associated with increased anxiety and risk perception among human co-workers. Therefore, time parametrization is identified as a critical aspect in designing motion planning processes for HRI.

Despite this recognized importance, the paper identifies a notable gap in the literature: the absence of a global, quantitative, and *a priori* index for assessing comfort in HRI, particularly one that can be computed without the need for actual movement experience. Existing comfort evaluation methods typically fall into two categories: qualitative, *a posteriori* measures (e.g., questionnaires) that assess comfort after interaction, or quantitative measures focusing on local motion characteristics (e.g., repeatability, smoothness, jerk, proxemics). These quantitative measures often capture necessary but not sufficient conditions for comfort, and a challenge remains in integrating multiple such metrics into a single, comprehensive comfort index that is independent of specific trajectories, control parameters, or hardware. This work aims to bridge this gap by proposing such an index.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish and empirically validate a quantitative index that measures the human-likeness of robot movements along prescribed paths, thereby serving as an *a priori* indicator of human comfort during human-robot interaction.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key considerations:

- **Hypothesis Formulation**: The work is driven by the central Hypothesis 1: "If A and B correspond to, respectively, 'a movement is similar to a human one' and 'a movement is comfortable', then A <=> B." This hypothesis posits a direct equivalence between the human-likeness of a movement's time law and the human's perception of comfort when interacting with that movement. The research aims to provide empirical evidence to validate this hypothesis.
- **Addressing a Gap in Literature**: A significant motivation is the identified absence of a quantitative, objective index for human-likeness that can characterize trajectories *before* their execution. Current methods are often qualitative, require post-interaction assessment, or rely on local motion characteristics that do not universally predict comfort. The proposed index seeks to provide an objective, global measure that is not task-related or dependent on the performance of single interactions.
- **Enabling "Comfortable-by-Design" Trajectory Planning**: By defining an *a priori* comfort index based on human-likeness, the research aims to facilitate the development of trajectory generation algorithms that can inherently produce movements perceived as comfortable. This would allow for "comfortable-by-design" robot movements, circumventing the need for a trial-and-error approach common in data-driven strategies and reducing the burden of post-hoc assessments.
- **Decoupling Geometric Path and Time Law**: The research is specifically interested in evaluating comfort by focusing on the time law of a robot's end-effector movement, while assuming the geometric path is task-dependent and prescribed. This approach is motivated by the understanding that human motor programs, which govern movement kinematics, can be applied to arbitrary geometrical paths. This decoupling extends the applicability of human-like and comfortable movements to industrial and medical scenarios where robots must accurately track specific paths (e.g., collaborative welding, rehabilitation).
- **Improving Robot Acceptance and Collaboration**: Ultimately, the work is motivated by the goal of enhancing human-robot collaboration by improving robot acceptance. By making robot movements more predictable and comfortable for humans, the research aims to reduce anxiety, increase efficiency, and foster more harmonious HRI.

In summary, the key contribution is to introduce and validate a quantitative human-likeness index, based on the sigma-lognormal model of human movements, that serves as an *a priori* measure of comfort, thereby validating the fundamental link between human-like movement kinematics and perceived comfort in human-robot interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study combines theoretical foundations from human motor control, computational modeling, and experimental validation through human-robot interaction.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Foundation: Motor Program and Sigma-Lognormal Model

The research grounds its definition of human-likeness in the concept of the "motor program." Drawing from the phenomenon of "motor equivalence," which describes the ability to achieve complex actions using different end-effectors, the authors posit that the central nervous system stores motor actions as abstract representations, or motor programs, independent of specific motor commands. These programs represent the minimal set of "units of action" (strokes) required to perform a movement accurately while minimizing metabolic energy expenditure.
Proposition 1 states that a sufficient condition for a movement to be human-like is for it to be the realization of a motor program. The kinematic theory of rapid human movements and its sigma-lognormal model are adopted to characterize these motor programs. In this model, individual "strokes" are represented as lognormal impulse responses, generated by the neuromuscular system. Complex human movements are then modeled as a vectorial summation of multiple lognormal elementary movements (strokes) over time (Equation 4), each characterized by parameters like amplitude, time of emission, delay, and response time (Equation 3).
For practical application, the Robust Zero crossing (RX0) algorithm, available in ScriptStudio (SS) software, is chosen for extracting strokes and reconstructing the best sigma-lognormal approximation of a given trajectory. This approach specifically focuses on the velocity profile, decoupling it from the geometric path.

#### 3.2. Phase Plane Representation of Time Laws

To analyze time laws independently of their geometric paths, the study employs phase plane analysis. A path `x(λ)` is defined by a path parameter `λ`, and the time law is represented by the pseudo-velocity `˙λ(λ)` (Phase Plane Trajectory, PPT). The time can be computed by integrating the inverse of `˙λ(λ)` (Equation 6). This framework links task-space velocity and acceleration (`˙x`, `¨x`) to their parametric counterparts (`x'`, `x''`) and `˙λ`, `¨λ` (Equations 7 and 8). The study assumes `x'(λ)` is a unit vector tangent to the path and `x''(λ)` is normal to it (Equations 9 and 10).

#### 3.3. Human-Likeness (HL) Index Definition

The core of the methodology is the definition of the Human-Likeness (HL) index (Equation 12):
`HL = SNR * (¯N / N)`
where:
- `SNR` (Signal-to-Noise Ratio) quantifies the similarity between a given movement's PPT and its sigma-lognormal reconstruction (Equation 11). A higher SNR indicates better reconstruction fidelity.
- `N` is the number of strokes in the sigma-lognormal reconstruction of the given movement.
- `¯N` is the minimum number of strokes necessary to encode the motor program of the *assigned path*, determined by identifying target points or switching points (from max deceleration to max acceleration) on the Maximum Velocity Curve (MVC) in the phase plane. `¯N` represents the inherent complexity of the path itself.

The term `¯N / N` serves as a penalty for reconstructions involving an excessive number of strokes, aligning with the principle that human motor programs are optimized for minimal energy consumption. Proposition 2 states that maximizing HL for a given movement implies it is human-like.

#### 3.4. Time Parametrization of Robotic Paths

To generate artificial trajectories for experimental comparison, the following time parametrization methods are used:
1. **Constant Velocity Profile (Uniform)**: A constant pseudo-velocity `˙λ(λ) = k` (Equation 13) is applied. The constant `k` is chosen to match the duration of corresponding human movements.
2. **Time-Optimal Trajectory Planning (TOTP)**: This method aims to minimize trajectory tracking time subject to task-space acceleration and velocity limits (Equations 14-23). The resulting pseudo-velocity profile `˙λ(λ)` follows a bang-bang control strategy. For this study, `a_max` (maximum acceleration) is set to match the maximum acceleration observed in the recorded human movements, and the time axis is scaled to match human movement duration. This scaled version is referred to as **STOTPAC** (Scaled TOTP with Acceleration Constraints).

#### 3.5. Index Validation Setup

- **Participants**: Two groups were recruited from the University of Salerno:
 - **G1 (8 subjects)**: Involved in recording human movements.
 - **G2 (68 subjects)**: Involved in human-robot interaction experiments for comfort judgment.
 - Participants were pre-selected based on motor skills and absence of upper body injuries.
- **Human Trajectory Generation (G1)**: Subjects drew three simple 2D shapes ("reversed spring" (RS), "5 connected 'l'" (5L), and "bigram eu" (EU)) on a WACOM digitizing tablet. These shapes varied in curvature complexity. Ten repetitions per pattern were recorded.
- **Artificial Trajectory Generation**: For each human-recorded geometric path, two artificial time laws (Uniform and STOTPAC) were generated, preserving the original path geometry.
- **HL Index Evaluation**: Algorithm 2 details the computation. For each trajectory (Human, Uniform, STOTPAC), the `¯N` (from path curvature analysis), `N` (from SS reconstruction), and `SNR` were calculated, leading to the final HL index. Table I and Figure 3 summarize these results.
- **Interaction Experiment (G2)**:
 - **Robot System**: A 6-DOF UR10 robot, velocity-controlled, equipped with a custom 3D-printed end-effector.
 - **Task**: G2 subjects actively maintained contact with the robot's end-effector as it executed handwriting trajectories in its free space, on a virtual horizontal plane. Safety measures were implemented.
 - **Comparison Tests**: Subjects were presented with pairs of movements and asked, "In which of the two movements did you feel more comfortable in interacting with the robot?".
 - **Experimental Campaigns**: Three campaigns were conducted, each performed one week apart with randomized query order:
 1. **Human-Uniform**: 15 pairs (Human vs. Uniform).
 2. **Human-STOTPAC**: 15 pairs (Human vs. STOTPAC).
 3. **Human-Human**: 18 pairs (Human with higher HL vs. Human with lower HL).
 - **Protocol**: A written protocol was provided, and visual references of the patterns were displayed. Pauses were included to mitigate fatigue.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental campaigns and subsequent analyses yielded several key findings:

#### 4.1. Consistency Between HL Index and User Preferences

The results demonstrated a global consistency between the proposed HL index and human comfort perception.
- **HL Index Distributions (Table I, Figure 3)**: Human trajectories generally exhibited higher mean HL values (e.g., RS: 13.99, 5L: 15.17, EU: 13.37) compared to Uniform trajectories (RS: 8.34, 5L: 7.18, EU: 6.07). STOTPAC trajectories showed intermediate HL values (RS: 12.03, 5L: 13.10, EU: 11.35), often closer to Human trajectories, as intended by their design to approximate human movements.
- **Preference Rates (Figure 5)**:
 - **Human-Uniform**: Participants consistently preferred Human trajectories over Uniform ones, with high preference rates for Human movements (e.g., RS: 80-90%, 5L: 90-100%, EU: 60-70%), except for one outlier for the EU pattern.
 - **Human-STOTPAC**: While Human trajectories were still globally preferred, STOTPAC trajectories were chosen as more comfortable in a notable number of trials. This suggests that STOTPAC movements were perceived as more similar to Human movements than Uniform ones, aligning with their intermediate HL values.
 - **Human-Human**: In comparisons between two human trajectories, subjects showed a slight preference for the trajectory with the higher HL index, but no clear, strong preference, indicating that movements with similar high HL values are more difficult to distinguish in terms of comfort.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Jerk

A comparison with Mean Absolute Jerk (MAJ), a common *a priori* comfort metric, revealed that the HL index aligns better with user preference in certain cases. Table II illustrates instances where STOTPAC trajectories, despite having higher MAJ values than Uniform trajectories, were preferred more frequently by users when compared to Human movements. This suggests that MAJ alone might not fully capture the nuanced aspects of comfort perception, whereas the HL index, by capturing more fundamental characteristics of human movement, provided a more consistent measure.

#### 4.3. Impact of Path Curvature on HL Index

The study observed variability in preference for Human trajectories in the Human-STOTPAC campaign, particularly noting that STOTPAC time laws felt less comfortable for the EU pattern. Participant comments indicated that STOTPAC movements for the EU pattern were perceived as "jerky." This is attributed to the bang-bang nature of TOTP, which accentuates acceleration and deceleration at sharp curves. The EU pattern, with tight curves at the "U" letter's upper edges, led to abrupt velocity changes in STOTPAC movements, in contrast to the naturally controlled deceleration/acceleration observed in human movements. While the HL index weakly captured this aspect, it highlights a limitation in fully accounting for jerkiness in highly curved paths when using TOTP.

#### 4.4. HL Index as Comfort Predictor

A logistic regression analysis was performed to assess if the difference in HL (∆HL) between two compared trajectories predicts the preference for the higher HL trajectory (p_h).
- **Statistical Significance**: The results (Table III) indicated a statistically significant association between ∆HL and p_h for all patterns (RS: p = 1.05 * 10^-16, 5L: p = 4.14 * 10^-17, EU: p = 4.71 * 10^-8). This demonstrates that as the difference in human-likeness increases, humans are more likely to perceive the more human-like trajectory as more comfortable.
- **Achieved Power**: A post-hoc power analysis confirmed high statistical power (99.9-100%) for the logistic regression tests, indicating robust detection of the observed effect sizes.

#### 4.5. HL Index Sensitivity

Further analysis of the Human-Human comparisons explored the sensitivity of the HL index. By empirically setting a threshold of ∆HL = 0.600, trajectory pairs were grouped. Pairs with ∆HL > 0.600 showed a significantly higher average preference rate (μh = 63.92%) for the higher-HL trajectory compared to pairs with ∆HL ≤ 0.600 (μl = 40.50%). A two-tailed t-Student test (p = 0.0119) confirmed this statistical difference, suggesting that a sufficient difference in HL values allows humans to distinguish between movements in terms of comfort.

#### 4.6. Accuracy of HL Index in Single Comparisons

The study acknowledged that the HL index is not infallible in predicting comfort for every single comparison, primarily due to inherent user-dependent factors in comfort perception. Instances were observed where subjects preferred a STOTPAC trajectory with a remarkably lower HL index than its Human counterpart. However, the overall results demonstrate that the probability of such an anomaly decreases as the difference in HL between trajectories increases, largely confirming Proposition 3 (that if HL is maximized, the movement is comfortable) and empirically validating Hypothesis 1.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes significantly to the field of Human-Robot Interaction by introducing a novel, quantitative, and *a priori* index for assessing robot movement comfort. Its potential impact spans several areas:

- **Objective and *A Priori* Comfort Evaluation**: The most immediate significance is the provision of an objective metric that can evaluate the comfort level of a robotic trajectory *before* its actual execution. This addresses a critical gap in the literature, which largely relies on qualitative or *a posteriori* assessments. This capability is crucial for efficient robot design and deployment.
- **"Comfortable-by-Design" Robotics**: The HL index enables the development of trajectory generation algorithms that are inherently optimized for human comfort. By maximizing this index, robot planners can create movements that are perceived as more natural and acceptable by human co-workers, moving beyond trial-and-error approaches.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Acceptance and Collaboration**: By fostering the design of more comfortable robot movements, the research directly contributes to increased robot acceptance and improved overall quality of human-robot collaboration. This can lead to safer, more efficient, and more pleasant interaction experiences in various domains.
- **Decoupling of Kinematics and Geometry**: The approach of associating comfort primarily with the movement's time law, independently of its geometric path, is significant. This extends the applicability of human-like movements to tasks requiring precise path tracking (e.g., collaborative welding, surgical assistance, rehabilitation therapies) where the robot must follow a predefined geometry but can still optimize its kinematics for comfort.
- **Applications in Robotics**:
 - **Trajectory Planning and Optimization**: The HL index can serve as a performance benchmark for evaluating and comparing different human-like trajectory generation algorithms.
 - **Exoskeleton Control**: Particularly relevant for rehabilitation, where exoskeletons need to track prescribed paths for functional recovery while ensuring patient comfort.
 - **Energy Efficiency**: Given its correlation with metabolic energy expenditure in human movements, future research could explore a similar link with robot power consumption, potentially leading to more energy-efficient and comfortable robot movements.
 - **Improving Time-Optimal Movements**: The findings highlight limitations of time-optimal parametrizations in highly curved paths, prompting the investigation of new planning algorithms that target HL maximization while mitigating jerkiness.

- **Beyond Robotics**:
 - **Synthetic Handwriting Generation**: The index can be used to guide generative networks in producing more plausible and human-like synthetic handwriting samples, reducing artificial variability in training data.
 - **Medical Diagnostics**: Integrated with motion capture systems, the HL evaluation system could quantify arm motion pathologies by identifying kinematic deviations from healthy human movement patterns, potentially assisting in the diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases or other neurological disorders.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The study acknowledges certain limitations, including the generalizability of results to populations with undeveloped motor programs (e.g., young children) or physical disabilities/stiff muscles (e.g., elderly). It also recognizes that various user-dependent factors (movement habits, physiological disparities, psychological states) can influence comfort perception, though relevant information was collected for future investigation.
Future work will focus on investigating movement features not fully captured by motor program similarity but crucial for comfort, and exploring new trajectory planning algorithms specifically designed to maximize the HL index.
