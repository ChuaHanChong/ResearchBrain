# Learning Impact-Rich Rotational Maneuvers via Centroidal Velocity Rewards and Sim-to-Real Techniques: A One-Leg Hopper Flip Case Study

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KAIST developed a framework for robots to learn and execute impact-rich rotational maneuvers, achieving the first successful front flip on a one-leg hopper in hardware. The approach uses a Centroidal Angular Velocity reward, accurate motor operating region modeling, and transmission load regularization, which led to a 43% increase in rotational velocity and enhanced hardware durability during high-impact landings.

## Problem

- Conventional reinforcement learning rewards for robot rotations often fail to induce true whole-body angular velocity, instead promoting internal joint movements.
- Transferring highly dynamic, high-impact robotic maneuvers from simulation to real hardware faces significant challenges due to actuator limitations and the risk of mechanical failure.
- Existing methods struggle to account for the necessary inertia modulation to amplify rotational speed from generated momentum, crucial for completing complex flips.

## Method

- A ==Centroidal Angular Velocity (CAV)== reward function encourages both momentum generation and mid-air inertia reduction, crucial for achieving high rotational speeds.
- Realistic ==Motor Operating Region (MOR)== modeling is integrated into simulation training to ensure commanded torques are within the physical limits of actuators across varying speeds.
- ==Transmission Load Regularization== is introduced to minimize peak impact forces on mechanical components, using a ==relaxed log barrier== and probabilistic episode termination.

## Results

- The Centroidal Angular Velocity (CAV) reward enabled policies to achieve a full front flip by increasing centroidal angular velocity by **43%** through effective inertia modulation.
- The framework successfully demonstrated the **first** hardware realization of a full front flip on a one-leg hopper, validating its robust sim-to-real transfer capabilities.
- Transmission load regularization reduced peak external torque on the ankle actuator by approximately **19%** and enabled **8** consecutive successful hardware flips without mechanical failure, compared to immediate failure without it.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Centroidal Angular Velocity (CAV) provides a superior reward signal for dynamic rotational maneuvers, as it simultaneously accounts for momentum generation and the critical reduction of moment of inertia.
> - Integrating precise Motor Operating Region (MOR) constraints during policy training is essential for enabling sim-to-real transfer of highly dynamic behaviors that push actuator limits.
> - Regularizing transmission loads effectively mitigates mechanical stress during high-impact landings, allowing for robust and repeatable execution of aggressive maneuvers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has witnessed remarkable progress in recent years, with robots demonstrating increasingly agile and dynamic behaviors, from robust locomotion over challenging terrains to complex acrobatic feats like parkour, cartwheels, and various flips. These advanced capabilities fundamentally rely on the precise generation and dissipation of momentum, often through strong impacts, and the agile modulation of body inertia. However, robotic motions that demand significant whole-body rotation and endure high-magnitude impacts present substantial challenges for both reinforcement learning (RL) and sim-to-real transfer.

Traditional RL approaches for rotational maneuvers often rely on link-level rewards, such as maximizing base angular velocity (BAV). This paper argues and demonstrates that such rewards can be exploited by the robot through internal joint motions, failing to induce true whole-body rotation. While centroidal momentum has been extensively used in model-based control and more recently in RL for balance, its application for *actively generating* dynamic, rotation-intensive behaviors, particularly those involving mid-air inertia modulation, has been less explored. The authors position their "centroidal angular velocity" (CAV) reward as a key advancement over these prior formulations, bridging the gap between momentum generation and effective rotational speed amplification.

Furthermore, deploying learned dynamic policies from simulation to real hardware (sim-to-real transfer) remains a critical hurdle. While methods like domain randomization and noise injection have improved robustness, the extreme conditions of high-impact, high-speed maneuvers demand even more sophisticated techniques that account for real-world actuator limitations and mechanical fragility. This paper builds on recent efforts to incorporate actuator constraints, such as Motor Operating Region (MOR) modeling, and impact-aware planning. By explicitly integrating realistic MOR constraints and a novel transmission load regularization, the work addresses the sim-to-real gap under these severe conditions, setting it apart from approaches that might overlook the granular details of hardware limitations. The choice of a one-leg hopper as a testbed—a minimal, highly underactuated system—highlights the fundamental nature of the challenges tackled, making the lessons learned broadly applicable to more complex legged systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a general framework for **learning and deploying impact-rich, rotation-intensive behaviors** in legged robots that can reliably transfer from simulation to real hardware.

The authors are motivated by several key challenges inherent in such dynamic maneuvers:
1. **Ineffectiveness of Conventional Rewards:** They identify that conventional link-level rewards (e.g., maximizing base angular velocity) often lead to "cheating" behaviors where policies generate high local angular velocities through internal joint movements without achieving meaningful whole-body rotation. Similarly, while centroidal angular momentum (CAM) rewards encourage momentum generation, they fail to incentivize the necessary *reduction in moment of inertia* required to convert momentum into high angular velocity for completing full rotations.
2. **Severe Sim-to-Real Gap:** Dynamic, high-impact maneuvers push actuators to their limits, exacerbating discrepancies between idealized simulations and real-world hardware. Policies trained without accurate models of actuator torque-speed limitations (Motor Operating Regions, MOR) or without consideration for high impact forces can easily fail or even damage real robots.
3. **Hardware Durability:** High-impact landings pose a significant risk of mechanical failure for vulnerable transmission components. Current sim-to-real techniques may not adequately address these specific mechanical stresses, leading to fragile policies.
4. **Challenging Benchmark:** The one-leg hopper front flip is selected as a representative, challenging case study. Its minimal design (single leg, small support polygon, no auxiliary limbs for rotation) makes achieving a full flip particularly difficult, demanding precise impulse generation, nonlinear coupling control, and robust impact absorption. Successfully demonstrating this maneuver on hardware serves as a strong validation for the proposed framework's robustness and generality.

In essence, the research aims to provide a robust, hardware-resilient recipe for learning truly dynamic and acrobatic robot motions by refining reward formulations and enhancing sim-to-real transfer techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates three core components: a novel reward function, realistic actuator modeling, and mechanical load regularization.

#### 3.1. Centroidal Velocity-Based Reward

The paper introduces a **Centroidal Angular Velocity (CAV) reward** as a more effective alternative to conventional link-level (Base Angular Velocity, BAV) or Centroidal Angular Momentum (CAM) rewards.
- **Critique of Alternatives:** BAV rewards are shown to induce internal motions (e.g., knee flexion-extension) without actual whole-body rotation. CAM rewards encourage momentum generation but do not incentivize the reduction of composite inertia, which is crucial for maximizing rotational *speed*.
- **CAV Formulation:** The CAV (w_com) is derived from the system's 6D twist and centroidal composite rigid body inertia (I_G), specifically w_com = I_com^-1 * L_com, where L_com is the angular momentum and I_com is the composite rigid body inertia about the center of mass. This formulation inherently accounts for both angular momentum and the moment of inertia, thus rewarding policies that *generate* momentum and then *reduce inertia* to amplify rotational speed.
- **Reward Structure:** The 2-second flip motion is segmented into takeoff, aerial, and landing phases. During the aerial phase, the reward encourages angular velocity aligned with the desired rotational axis (e.g., pitch for a front flip). During landing, it penalizes residual angular momentum to promote stable contact.

#### 3.2. Sim-to-Real Transfer and Impact Mitigation Techniques

To bridge the sim-to-real gap and ensure hardware robustness for high-energy, high-impact maneuvers, two key techniques are implemented:

- **Motor Operating Region (MOR) Modeling:**
 - Unlike simplified box-type torque-velocity limits, the proposed MOR model accurately reflects the speed-dependent torque capabilities of real motors. This trapezoidal region in the torque-speed plane accounts for voltage limits (where available torque decreases with increasing rotor speed due to back-EMF) and current limits (preventing overheating).
 - During training, commanded torques are clipped to remain within this realistic envelope. This forces the policy to learn executable torque commands, preventing it from exploiting unrealistic torque-speed combinations achievable only in an unconstrained simulation. This is crucial for phases demanding high torque at high speed, such as takeoff and rapid mid-air joint reconfigurations.

- **Transmission Load Regularization:**
 - This technique aims to protect vulnerable hardware components (like gears) from excessive impact-induced loads. It addresses the limitation that simply minimizing contact impulses doesn't guarantee reduced actuator loads, especially in complex closed-loop mechanisms like the hopper's ankle.
 - The instantaneous transmission load (τ_load) is estimated from external contact impulses and the contact Jacobian. This load is then regularized using a relaxed log barrier function, guiding the policy toward landing strategies that distribute forces over time (e.g., rolling landings).
 - Furthermore, a probabilistic episode termination (50% chance) is introduced if τ_load exceeds a critical threshold, realistically modeling irreversible hardware damage and preventing policies from tolerating brief, high-stress violations.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Reward Composition:** The total reward combines "motion rewards" (including CAV and regularization penalties for stability, torque efficiency, and action smoothness) and "barrier rewards" (for joint limits, velocity limits, and transmission load using relaxed log barriers).
- **Policy Architecture:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used with an asymmetric actor-critic structure. The actor receives proprioceptive observations, action history, and phase information. The critic utilizes privileged information (unactuated joint states, body linear velocity).
- **Concurrent Estimation:** To bridge the observation gap for the actor, a concurrent estimation framework is employed, where an auxiliary network predicts privileged states for the actor's input, improving sample efficiency and robustness.
- **Auxiliary Sim-to-Real Methods:** Standard techniques like domain randomization (mass, inertia, COM, PD gains, friction, restitution) and noise injection (on sensor observations) are also applied to enhance robustness against modeling errors and hardware variability. Contact impulse and contact mode regularization are also used to mitigate reliance on inaccurate contact models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides compelling evidence for the effectiveness of its proposed framework through comparative studies and hardware validation.

#### 4.1. Effect of Centroidal Angular Velocity-Based Reward

- **BAV-maximizing policy:** Generates high base angular velocity (8.7 rad/s) through rapid knee flexion-extension but fails to take off or achieve net whole-body rotation. This confirms that link-level rewards can be exploited by internal motions without contributing to global movement.
- **CAM-maximizing policy:** Achieves takeoff and generates substantial centroidal angular momentum (4.3 N·s) but fails to complete a full flip. It maintains a large composite inertia throughout the aerial phase (extended leg), resulting in a low centroidal angular velocity (4.8 rad/s), which is insufficient for a full rotation. This demonstrates the limitation of CAM rewards in not incentivizing inertia reduction for speed amplification.
- **CAV-maximizing policy (Proposed):** Successfully completes a full front flip. It generates significant centroidal angular momentum before takeoff and then rapidly flexes the knee mid-air to reduce composite inertia, increasing centroidal angular velocity by 43% (from 7.4 rad/s to a peak of 10.6 rad/s). This mid-air acceleration enables the robot to achieve a full 360-degree rotation. This clearly validates that optimizing CAV enables both momentum utilization and crucial inertia modulation.

#### 4.2. One-Leg Hopper Front Flip Results and MOR

- **Hardware Realization:** The proposed policy, trained with MOR constraints, achieves the first successful hardware realization of a full front flip on the one-leg hopper platform.
- **Impact of MOR:**
 - A policy trained *without* MOR constraints (only static box-type limits) fails to complete a front flip when evaluated under realistic MOR constraints. This failure is due to commanded torques exceeding the feasible MOR, particularly during takeoff and mid-air reconfigurations where available torque is reduced at high joint velocities. The mismatch leads to unrealizable torque commands and insufficient rotational velocity.
 - The proposed policy, trained *with* MOR constraints, adapts to these realistic actuator limits. Its commanded torques remain within the feasible envelope on hardware, producing the intended angular velocity profile and successfully executing the flip. This demonstrates the critical role of accurate actuator modeling in enabling reliable sim-to-real transfer for dynamic tasks.

#### 4.3. Effect of Transmission Load Regularization

- **Hardware Durability Issue:** In initial hardware trials without transmission load regularization, the robot's ankle actuator sun gear fractured upon landing during the second front flip attempt, highlighting the fragility of components under high-impact loads.
- **Regularized Policy Performance:**
 - In simulation, the regularized policy yields lower and more consistent peak external torques compared to the unregularized policy, which showed a broader range and higher peaks due to unpredictable flat-foot landings. The regularized policy learns to initiate contact at a designated foot corner, followed by a rolling motion to distribute impact forces over a longer duration.
 - In hardware experiments, the estimated peak external torque on the ankle actuator decreased from approximately 21 N·m to 17 N·m with regularization, and the peak occurred later, indicating more compliant impact absorption.
 - This translates directly to improved hardware robustness: the non-regularized policy caused sun gear fracture on the second trial, whereas the regularized policy completed eight consecutive trials without any mechanical failure. This proves that transmission load regularization is crucial for the long-term durability of hardware in impact-rich maneuvers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in the field of dynamic legged robotics and sim-to-real learning, with several important implications:

1. **Breakthrough in Dynamic Maneuvers:** The first successful hardware realization of a full front flip on a minimal one-leg hopper is a notable achievement. This challenging benchmark validates the proposed framework's ability to tackle highly complex, underactuated, and impact-intensive motions.
2. **Novel Reward Formulation:** The introduction of the Centroidal Angular Velocity (CAV) reward provides a powerful and intuitive method for guiding reinforcement learning policies toward truly whole-body, speed-amplifying rotational dynamics. It addresses fundamental limitations of prior reward formulations, offering a transferable recipe for discovering agile, acrobatic behaviors that leverage both momentum generation and inertia modulation.
3. **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Reliability:** The proposed actuator-aware sim-to-real techniques—realistic Motor Operating Region (MOR) modeling and Transmission Load Regularization—are critical for safely and reliably deploying dynamic policies on physical hardware. They address key real-world constraints (actuator limitations and mechanical fragility) that are often overlooked, leading to fragile transfers. This work demonstrates that explicit consideration of these factors is paramount for robustness.
4. **Transferability and Generality:** Although demonstrated on a specific platform and maneuver, the underlying principles of the CAV reward and actuator-aware sim-to-real techniques are broadly applicable. The appendix shows successful learning of yaw-spins and barrel rolls on the one-leg hopper by simply changing the target rotational axis, and even a backflip on a morphologically distinct Unitree Go1 quadruped, suggesting high generalizability to other legged systems and dynamic tasks.
5. **Foundation for Future Work:** This framework provides robust building blocks for developing more complex, sequential, and diverse acrobatic skills in legged robots. It paves the way for robots that can not only perform impressive individual stunts but also link them into fluid, highly dynamic sequences, pushing the boundaries of autonomous robot agility and interaction with complex environments.

While the framework was evaluated primarily on a single platform and specific flip maneuver, its success in handling severe actuation and structural constraints suggests strong potential for broader impact in agile locomotion and acrobatic robotics.
