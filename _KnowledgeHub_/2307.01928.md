# Robots That Ask For Help: Uncertainty Alignment for Large Language Model Planners

> [!summary] Summary
> KnowNo is a framework that calibrates the uncertainty of LLM-based robot planners using conformal prediction. It casts next-step planning as multiple-choice question answering, scores options by next-token likelihood, and builds a prediction set from a calibration quantile; a non-singleton set triggers a clarification question to the human. It provides user-specified task-completion guarantees while cutting human help 10-24% versus baselines.

## Problem

- LLM planners hallucinate confidently and prior language-based planners either never seek clarification or rely on brittle prompt engineering that over- or under-triggers human help.
- No prior approach ties asking for help to a user-specified task success level, so operators cannot control the trade-off between autonomy and reliability.
- Raw sequence likelihoods are length-biased, poor uncertainty scores; earlier conformal work in robotics guaranteed safety only per step, averaged, or via conservative union bounds.

## Method

- ==Multiple-choice generation==: few-shot prompting makes the LLM emit four semantically distinct candidate next steps plus an appended 'an option not listed here', reducing plan scoring to one length-unbiased next-token probability.
- ==Conformal prediction== calibration: on 400 labelled scenarios it computes ==nonconformity scores== one minus the true-option likelihood, takes the dataset-conditional Beta-adjusted quantile, then keeps all options above that threshold; non-singleton sets trigger a human query.
- ==Sequence-level multi-step extension==: calibration lifts to whole trajectories using the minimum per-step score, and a ==causal-reconstruction== claim proves the per-step prediction sets reproduce the sequence set, extending guarantees to multiple acceptable options.

## Results

- In PyBullet tabletop rearrangement over attribute, numeric and spatial ambiguity, KnowNo tracked the **85%** target success most closely across 200 trials per method, cutting help up to **24%** versus Simple Set.
- On a UR5 multi-step sorting task with target success **0.75**, at matched **0.76** plan success KnowNo cut step-wise help from **0.72** to **0.58** and set size from **2.04** to **1.72**.
- Mobile-manipulator kitchen runs cut help **0.81** to **0.67** at equal **0.87** plan success; ablations showed raw-probability and perplexity scoring inflated sets from **2.17** to **4.06** under numeric ambiguity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Coverage guarantees hold regardless of the base model's calibration: a weaker LLM simply triggers more help, so reliability survives model swaps.
> - Reformulating open-ended generation as multiple-choice turns unruly sequence likelihoods into a normalized score that any set-valued calibration method can consume.
> - The guarantee covers planning only; ungrounded perception, an 86% low-level success rate, and unfaithful human answers all break the end-to-end promise.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into robotic systems has rapidly emerged as a highly promising, yet challenging, area of research. LLMs offer unprecedented capabilities for high-level reasoning, step-by-step planning, commonsense understanding, and natural language interaction, which can significantly enhance a robot's ability to interpret complex human instructions and operate in unstructured environments. However, a major bottleneck preventing their reliable deployment in real-world robotics is their propensity for "hallucinations" and overconfidence in incorrect predictions. LLMs can confidently generate plausible but factually incorrect or unsafe plans, which is particularly hazardous in physical systems where errors can lead to damage, inefficiency, or safety risks.

Prior work in language-based planning for robots (e.g., in the references) has explored various ways to leverage LLMs for generating robot actions, writing code, or forming sequential plans. Some approaches attempt to mitigate errors through extensive prompt engineering or by relying on environmental feedback loops and human intervention. However, these methods often lack formal guarantees regarding task success rates and can suffer from over-reliance on human assistance or inefficient interaction strategies. The challenge lies in distinguishing between situations where an LLM is genuinely knowledgeable and where it is merely confident, but wrong.

Simultaneously, the field of uncertainty quantification for LLMs has seen significant growth, with efforts to better calibrate model confidences and identify when models "know what they know". While standard calibration methods typically focus on point-valued outputs, conformal prediction (CP) has gained traction for providing distribution-free, set-valued predictions with rigorous statistical coverage guarantees. CP has been applied in various machine learning contexts and is beginning to find its way into language modeling for tasks like question answering and text infilling. In robotics, CP has been used for fault detection, trajectory prediction, and planning in dynamic environments, but its application to the specific problem of aligning uncertainty in *LLM-based robot planning* with *free-form natural language outputs* is novel.

This paper, "Robots That Ask For Help," directly addresses this critical gap. It stands at the intersection of LLM-based robotics, uncertainty quantification, and human-robot interaction. By proposing KNOWNO, the authors introduce a principled framework that moves beyond heuristic prompt tuning or expensive ensemble methods. It specifically leverages the statistical power of Conformal Prediction to enable LLM-powered robots to quantify their uncertainty in a way that provides formal guarantees on task success and optimizes human intervention. This work represents a significant step towards developing more reliable, autonomous, and safely interactive robots that can operate effectively even when faced with ambiguous instructions or uncertain environmental conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **endow robots with the ability to "know when they don't know" and, consequently, ask for help when needed**. This addresses a fundamental challenge in current LLM-based robotics: the models' tendency to confidently produce plausible but incorrect outputs, or "hallucinate," particularly when faced with ambiguous natural language instructions or novel, unstructured environments. Confidently executing an erroneous plan can lead to undesirable, unsafe, or inefficient outcomes in a physical robot.

The authors formalize this overarching goal into two key desiderata, collectively termed **uncertainty alignment**:

1. **Calibrated Confidence:** The robot should seek sufficient human help to ensure a statistically guaranteed level of task success, as specified by the user. This means if a user desires a 95% task completion rate, the robot's policy (which includes asking for help) should achieve at least that rate for new, unseen scenarios. This provides a quantifiable assurance of reliability, which is crucial for safety-critical applications.
2. **Minimal Help:** The robot should minimize the overall amount of help it seeks from a human by efficiently narrowing down possible ambiguities in a task. Unnecessary or excessive requests for clarification can be burdensome for humans and reduce the efficiency and autonomy of the robot system. The goal is to provide just enough information to the human to resolve ambiguity without overwhelming them or constantly interrupting the task flow.

The motivation stems from the inherent ambiguities in human language and real-world environments. For instance, an instruction like "place the bowl in the microwave" might be ambiguous if multiple bowls are present, or if one of them is unsafe for a microwave (e.g., a metal bowl). Rather than making a potentially dangerous assumption, the robot should recognize its uncertainty and seek clarification. Current LLM-based planners often either proceed without clarification or rely on extensive, brittle prompt engineering to ask for help, without any guarantees on the success rate or efficiency of intervention. KNOWNO aims to provide a robust, theoretically-backed solution to these issues, making LLM-powered robots more trustworthy and efficient partners.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

KNOWNO addresses uncertainty alignment by integrating Large Language Model (LLM) planning with Conformal Prediction (CP), employing a novel multi-step extension and a multiple-choice question-answering (MCQA) formulation.

#### 3.1. Planning as Multiple-Choice Q&A (MCQA)

A core challenge in applying uncertainty quantification methods to LLMs for robotics is the variable length and free-form nature of generated plans. Directly evaluating the probability of an entire generated sequence (`p(y)`) can be unreliable due to length biases. To circumvent this, KNOWNO uses a clever "trick":
1. **Candidate Generation:** Given an environment context (robot observations, user instruction) and a few-shot prompt, the LLM is first prompted to generate a diverse set of semantically distinct candidate next steps (e.g., "Put plastic bowl in microwave," "Put metal bowl in microwave"). An additional option, "An option not listed here," is always appended to cover possibilities not explicitly generated.
2. **MCQA for Scoring:** The task then transforms into a multiple-choice question: "Which option is correct?" The LLM is asked to predict the most likely *label* (A, B, C, D, E). This converts the variable-length plan prediction problem into a single next-token prediction task, which aligns well with LLM log-likelihood loss functions and MCQA-style training data. The probabilities assigned by the LLM to these labels serve as normalized scores, `f(x)y`, representing the LLM's confidence in each option. This method primarily addresses semantic uncertainty rather than subtle linguistic variations.

#### 3.2. Calibrating Confidence with Conformal Prediction (CP)

Conformal Prediction is a non-parametric statistical method that provides finite-sample guarantees on prediction accuracy without making strong assumptions about the underlying data distribution or model `f`.
1. **Nonconformity Scores:** For a set of calibration data `Z = {(x_i, y_i)}` (augmented contexts and their true labels), CP computes a nonconformity score `κ_i = 1 - f(x_i)y_i` for each `(x_i, y_i)` pair. A higher score indicates less conformity to the true label.
2. **Quantile Calculation:** Based on these scores and a user-specified error level `ϵ`, CP calculates an empirical quantile `ˆq`. This quantile is determined such that a certain proportion of the calibration data's true labels are "covered" (i.e., their nonconformity scores are below `1-ˆq`). The paper uses a dataset-conditional guarantee to ensure the desired `1-ϵ` coverage for each new test sample.
3. **Prediction Set Generation:** For a new, unseen test scenario `x_test`, CP generates a prediction set `C(x_test)` by including all candidate labels `y` for which the LLM's confidence `f(x_test)y` is greater than or equal to `1-ˆq`. This prediction set is guaranteed to contain the true label `y_test` with a probability of at least `1-ϵ`.
4. **Triggering Human Help:** If `C(x_test)` is a singleton (contains only one option), the robot executes that action confidently. If `C(x_test)` contains multiple options, the LLM is deemed uncertain, and the robot asks for human help, presenting the human with the options in `C(x_test)`. This mechanism ensures calibrated confidence.
5. **Minimal Prediction Set Size:** A theoretical property of CP is that, if `f` models true conditional probabilities, it minimizes the average size of the prediction sets among all prediction schemes that achieve the `1-ϵ` coverage guarantee. This directly addresses the "minimal help" desideratum.

#### 3.3. Multi-Step Uncertainty Alignment

A key theoretical contribution of KNOWNO is extending CP to multi-step planning, where sequential actions introduce dependencies and violate the i.i.d. assumption of standard CP.
1. **Sequence-Level Calibration:** Instead of individual `(x_t, y_t)` pairs, data points for calibration are lifted to sequences `(x, y)` where `x = (x_0, ..., x_{T-1})` is a sequence of augmented contexts and `y = (y_0, ..., y_{T-1})` is the corresponding sequence of true labels. The nonconformity score for a sequence `(x, y)` is defined as `1 - min_t f(x_t)y_t`, effectively considering the "weakest link" in the sequence.
2. **Causal Reconstruction:** At test time, the robot observes contexts `x_t` causally (one at a time). KNOWNO demonstrates that the prediction set for a full sequence `C(x_test)` can be causally reconstructed by computing individual step-level prediction sets `C_t(x_t_test)` using the same `ˆq` from sequence-level calibration. This ensures that the sequence-level coverage guarantee `P(y_test ∈ C(x_test)) ≥ 1-ϵ` holds for the entire plan.
3. **Multiple Acceptable Options:** The framework is further extended to handle scenarios where multiple actions can be considered "correct" at a given step (e.g., picking up either a Coke or a Sprite if the instruction is "get me a soda"). This is managed by ensuring the prediction set contains at least one acceptable option, preserving the task completion guarantee.

This methodology provides a robust, statistically sound framework for enabling LLM-powered robots to manage uncertainty, achieve desired reliability, and interact efficiently with humans.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate KNOWNO's effectiveness across a variety of simulated and real-world robotic setups, consistently outperforming baselines in achieving calibrated confidence and minimizing human intervention.

#### 4.1. Simulation: Tabletop Rearrangement

- **Settings:** Experiments were conducted in a PyBullet simulator with a robot arm, involving tabletop rearrangement tasks with three types of ambiguity in user instructions:
 - **Attribute:** Ambiguous object descriptions (e.g., "receptacle" for "bowl").
 - **Numeric:** Under-specified quantities (e.g., "a few blocks").
 - **Spatial:** Vague spatial relationships (e.g., "next to" with human preference over specific placement).
- **Achieving Target Success Rate:** KNOWNO consistently achieved the target task success rate (e.g., `1-ϵ = 0.85`) with the least deviation across all three ambiguity settings (Fig. 3). Baselines like Simple Set and Ensemble Set struggled to provide consistent coverage, and prompt-based methods (Prompt Set, Binary) and No Help offered no explicit control over the error rate, leading to larger deviations.
- **Efficiency and Help Reduction:** As shown in Fig. 4, KNOWNO achieved higher task success rates with significantly lower average prediction set sizes and human help rates compared to Simple Set. For instance, in more ambiguous scenarios (like Spatial), KNOWNO reduced human help by as much as 24% at certain success levels compared to Simple Set (Fig. A10).
- **Ensemble Set Performance:** While Ensemble Set could achieve high success rates with relatively low help at higher `ϵ` levels, it was computationally expensive (20x inference time) and could fail in scenarios where the LLM assigned zero probability to the correct option even with randomized prompts.

#### 4.2. Hardware: Multi-Step Tabletop Rearrangement & Mobile Manipulator

- **Multi-Step Tabletop Rearrangement:** On a UR5 robot arm sorting toy food items based on simulated human preferences (a multi-step setting with multiple acceptable options), KNOWNO reduced step-wise human help by 14% and trial-wise help by 8% compared to Simple Set, while maintaining the same planning success rate (Table 1). It also reduced the average prediction set size.
- **Mobile Manipulator in a Kitchen:** This experiment involved a mobile manipulator in an office kitchen, tackling tasks with complex ambiguities including Winograd Schemas and potentially unsafe actions (e.g., putting a metal bowl in a microwave). In this single-step, multi-label setting, KNOWNO again reduced human help by 14% and decreased the average prediction set size compared to Simple Set (Table 2).
- **Robustness to LLM Choice:** KNOWNO's target success guarantee remained robust even when using different LLMs (PaLM-2L-IF, GPT-3.5). While GPT-3.5 exhibited significant biases, KNOWNO compensated by triggering more human intervention to maintain the `1-ϵ` success rate, demonstrating the strength of CP's coverage guarantees regardless of the underlying LLM's calibration or accuracy.

#### 4.3. Additional Findings

- **MCQA vs. Sentence-Level Scores:** Experiments in Section A9 confirmed the hypothesis that using sentence-level probabilities (raw or perplexity-normalized) directly with CP leads to worse performance compared to the MCQA setup, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed MCQA trick for eliciting better calibrated confidences from LLMs.
- **Bimanual Manipulation:** Additional results in Section A5 for a bimanual setup further validated KNOWNO's ability to model ambiguities (e.g., choice of arm due to reachability) and trigger clarifications efficiently.

In summary, KNOWNO consistently delivered on its objectives: providing statistically guaranteed task completion rates while significantly minimizing human intervention across diverse robotic tasks and ambiguity types. Its out-of-the-box compatibility with LLMs and theoretical assurances mark it as a powerful and practical framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Robots That Ask For Help" paper presents KNOWNO, a significant advancement in bridging the gap between the impressive planning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) and the stringent reliability requirements of real-world robotics. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Robot Reliability and Safety:** The primary impact of KNOWNO is its ability to provide **statistical guarantees on task completion rates** for LLM-powered robots. This is a critical step towards deploying robots safely and reliably in unstructured and dynamic environments, especially in safety-critical applications. By rigorously quantifying uncertainty and ensuring that the robot knows when it doesn't know, KNOWNO mitigates the risks associated with LLM hallucinations and overconfidence.
- **Optimized Human-Robot Interaction:** KNOWNO addresses the challenge of efficient human-robot collaboration by ensuring **minimal human help**. By providing prediction sets that are provably the smallest possible to achieve a given coverage guarantee, the robot asks for clarification only when genuinely necessary and offers a focused set of options, reducing human cognitive load and improving the overall efficiency of the interaction. This fosters a more natural and productive partnership between humans and robots.
- **Out-of-the-Box LLM Compatibility:** A major practical advantage of KNOWNO is its ability to work with **pre-trained LLMs "out of the box," without requiring model fine-tuning**. This makes it a highly scalable and accessible solution, as it can leverage the ever-growing capabilities of foundation models without costly and data-intensive retraining. This "lightweight" approach to uncertainty modeling is crucial for rapid deployment in diverse applications.
- **Novel Theoretical Contribution:** The paper's **multi-step extension of Conformal Prediction** is a notable theoretical advancement. By developing a method for sequence-level calibration and causal reconstruction of prediction sets, it addresses the fundamental challenge of dependent observations in sequential decision-making problems. This extends the applicability of CP to complex, temporally extended planning tasks common in robotics.
- **Broader Applicability to AI Systems:** While demonstrated in robotics, the principles of uncertainty alignment, MCQA-based confidence scoring, and multi-step CP could be applicable to other AI systems that rely on LLMs for planning and decision-making, particularly in domains where human oversight and reliability are paramount.
- **Future Research Catalyst:** The authors acknowledge limitations, such as the assumption of fully grounded environments and successful low-level actions, and perfect human help. These open avenues for future research, including integrating perception and low-level control uncertainty into the CP framework, modeling human error, and exploring active preference learning for more sophisticated queries. This work serves as a strong foundation and inspiration for further efforts towards building more robust, intelligent, and trustworthy autonomous systems.

In essence, KNOWNO provides a principled, practical, and statistically sound framework for managing uncertainty in LLM-based robot planning, pushing the boundaries of what is possible for safe, reliable, and user-friendly human-robot interaction.
