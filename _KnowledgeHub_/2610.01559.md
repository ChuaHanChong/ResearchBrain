# Completion Aware Guidance for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Completion Aware Guidance (CAG) is a training-free sampling method for World Action Models that fixes task-incomplete imagination, where predicted video and actions agree on a plausible future that skips the step needed to finish the task. CAG measures which instruction tokens the current chunk attends to and boosts their cross-attention keys and values. Task success rises 64%->70% on RoboTwin 2.0 and 69%->75% zero-shot.

## Problem

- World Action Models produce visually coherent, action-consistent chunks that leave out the transition that finishes a phase, for example holding an object above its target instead of releasing it.
- Fine-tuning a fixed-horizon video model for short-chunk control never requires a phase to finish inside one horizon, so repeated regeneration can keep putting off the completing transition.
- Earlier WAM work never separated this failure from corrupted video or video-action misalignment, and external subgoal guidance does not make sure the policy actually performs the transition.

## Method

- ==Task-incomplete imagination diagnosis==: with the same video backbone, the native 5-second horizon produces the completion event while 1.6-second autoregressive chunks skip it, which points to the adaptation, not the backbone.
- ==KL-regularized completion objective==: chooses a chunk distribution that maximizes a phase-completion indicator while staying close to the frozen WAM prior, intervened on through ==token-wise scaling== of ==instruction cross-attention== keys and values.
- ==Attention-affinity proxy==: since the completion signal and its gradient are unavailable at sampling time, CAG averages each instruction token's attention over chosen layers and applies ==sparse-gated scaling== of 1 + alpha*psi(r).

## Results

- On nine non-saturated RoboTwin **2.0** tasks with Fast-WAM, average success rises from **64.4%** to **70.0%**, for example Stack Bowls Three **67**->**87** and Move Stapler Pad **57**->**73**.
- On three DreamZero DROID zero-shot simulation scenes, average success improves from **69%** to **75%**, with gains on every task, including Can to Mug **60**->**72**.
- Among failed DreamZero rollouts, the share caused by task-incomplete imagination falls from **79%** to **40%**, using one fixed alpha of **0.15** with no per-task tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fine-tuning a pretrained model to a shorter generation horizon can hide skills it already has; a failure may come from objective mismatch rather than missing capacity.
> - Plausible local continuations can postpone discrete events indefinitely in receding-horizon generation, so chunked controllers need an explicit push toward finishing the current phase.
> - Cheap inference-time reweighting of attention on conditioning tokens can bring out latent behaviour from a frozen generative policy without retraining, and it works across different WAM designs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot is told to pick up a can and put it in a mug. A World Action Model (WAM) generates both a predicted visual future and the robot actions meant to realize it. In the paper's example, a standard WAM predicts a locally plausible continuation and matching actions. It moves the can toward the mug but keeps holding it instead of releasing it, resulting in task failure. The authors' fix is to strengthen the instruction cues that the current prediction already uses, so the next chunk is more likely to include the missing transition. The WAM is not retrained. In simulated evaluations, this guidance raises reported task success on two different WAM designs.

Explanatory schematic for "Pick up the can and put in the mug." The standard WAM's actions are labeled "Grasp," "lift," "hold," "hold," ending in task failure. With CAG, highlighted instruction tokens ("Pick," "up," "put," "mug") accompany "Grasp," "lift," "move," "release," and the task graph marks success.

### 2. Why a plausible chunk can fail to finish

WAMs generate video-action chunks. Each chunk is executed, and the next is regenerated from new observations. A locally plausible continuation, such as holding or hovering, can remain compatible with the instruction even when the current phase requires release. Because no single chunk has to finish that phase, the transition can be deferred again.

The authors call this "task-incomplete imagination." It is a coherent, instruction-relevant, action-consistent prediction that omits the transition needed to complete the active phase. It is not a corrupted video, and it is not a disagreement between predicted video and action. Their proposed explanation is that WAMs' short, repeatedly regenerated control chunks can defer a completion event that a video model can represent over a longer trajectory.

Completion Aware Guidance (CAG) is a training-free sampling intervention. During denoising, it looks at which instruction tokens the video-action queries already attend to. It averages that attention affinity over selected layers and queries, then sparsely boosts the selected tokens' key and value signals. This attention signal is a proxy for relevant instruction components. It does not directly detect task completion, and it is not a new task reward. In the reported experiments, $\alpha$ is fixed at 0.15 across tasks and backbones.

### 3. What the horizon and task tests show

The first comparison uses the same video diffusion backbone at two generation horizons: standard 1.6-second chunks versus extended 5-second generation. Completion rates are higher at the extended horizon for each of the three plotted tasks:

- Cube to Bowl: 40% to 65%
- Can to Mug: 35% to 70%
- Banana to Bin: 60% to 85%

This supports the authors' interpretation that longer generation can elicit transitions omitted by short chunks. It does not establish that horizon mismatch is the only cause of task-incomplete imagination.

The second comparison measures task success. Each task's success rate is the reported proportion of successful rollouts, and the aggregate is the average across tasks.

- **RoboTwin 2.0 with Fast-WAM.** The evaluation covers nine tasks whose published Fast-WAM Random-setting success rates were below 90%, with 30 rollouts per task. The aggregate rises from 64.4% to 70.0% with CAG. Two of the nine tasks decline at the task level.
- **DreamZero zero-shot tasks.** On three zero-shot simulation tasks, with 50 rollouts per task, the aggregate rises from 69% to 75%.

A separate diagnostic on DreamZero looks only at failed rollouts. The share of failures attributed to task-incomplete imagination falls from 79% to 40% with CAG. This figure is a share of failures, not the percentage of all rollouts that fail. The paper reports no comparison against generic instruction strengthening, random token selection, or another mechanism-specific control. The results therefore do not isolate attention-based token selection as the only effective ingredient.

(a) Completion rate, Baseline (1.6s) vs. Extended (5s): 40/65%, 35/70%, 60/85%. (b) DreamZero vs. CAG zero-shot success: Cube to Bowl 90/92, Can to Mug 60/72, Banana to Bin 56/60. (c) Proportion of failed rollouts attributed to task-incomplete imagination: 79% vs. 40%.

### 4. Make the transition count

A predicted future can be plausible and action-consistent and still leave out the transition that finishes the task. CAG gained on both DreamZero, which uses explicit future generation, and Fast-WAM, which uses video-based world modeling differently for action prediction. These results suggest the guidance can apply across these two designs. The evaluations are simulation benchmarks, and the paper reports success rates rather than real-world deployment results.
