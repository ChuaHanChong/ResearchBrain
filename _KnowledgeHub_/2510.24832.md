# Scheduling Your LLM Reinforcement Learning with Reasoning Trees

> [!summary] Summary
> An algorithm called Re-Schedule was introduced at ICLR 2026, using a novel Reasoning Score (r-score) based on the structural properties of a query's reasoning tree to dynamically schedule training data for Large Language Models (LLMs) in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). This method achieved new state-of-the-art average scores, reaching 48.5% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, by providing a more effective measure of learning difficulty compared to path-based metrics.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) continue to face challenges in mastering complex reasoning tasks.
- Existing data scheduling methods in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) define query difficulty using path-based metrics, primarily final solution accuracy.
- Accuracy alone is an insufficient indicator of a query's true learning difficulty, overlooking the inherent structural complexity of the reasoning process.

## Method

- The method constructs an approximate ==reasoning tree== for each query, representing intermediate reasoning steps and potential solution paths.
- A Reasoning Score (r-score) is calculated for each query, quantifying its learning potential by estimating the maximum accuracy gain achievable with a limited 'node editing' budget within its reasoning tree.
- Re-Schedule uses these r-scores to dynamically weight queries during RLVR training, prioritizing structurally simple queries in early stages and gradually shifting towards more complex ones.

## Results

- Re-Schedule achieved new state-of-the-art average scores on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks, reaching **48.5%** on Qwen2.5-Math-**7B** and **44.5%** on Qwen2.5-**7B**.
- The method consistently outperformed existing scheduling baselines (e.g., ACC_sigmoid by up to **3.2%**) and classical RLVR methods (e.g., OPO/GRPO by up to **3.8%**) on Qwen2.5-Math-**7B**.
- The approach demonstrated generalizability by improving performance for different model architectures (Qwen3-**4B**-Base) and extending its effectiveness to code generation tasks on the LiveCodeBench v5 benchmark.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL training can be effectively modeled as an optimization process of a reasoning tree, where the model refines its policy at critical decision nodes.
> - The structural complexity of a query's reasoning tree, as captured by the r-score, provides a more effective and principled measure of its 'learnability' than simple path-based accuracy metrics.
> - An 'easy-to-hard' curriculum, guided by the r-score, allows LLMs to acquire reasoning skills more efficiently by starting with queries that offer higher potential for rapid accuracy gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work is situated within the field of Large Language Model (LLM) optimization, specifically focusing on enhancing their complex reasoning capabilities, particularly in domains such as mathematical problem-solving. Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a prominent paradigm for this purpose, with methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) being a notable example. In this context, LLM training can be conceptualized as a process of refining a "Reasoning Tree" associated with each query, where the model's policy is dynamically adjusted at each node (token) of the tree.

A critical component for improving data efficiency and accuracy in LLM training, including RLVR, is data scheduling, which draws principles from curriculum learning. Traditional data scheduling strategies prioritize queries based on predefined "difficulty" metrics, typically progressing from easier to harder examples. However, the existing methods for defining difficulty in RLVR commonly rely on path-based metrics, such as the final solution accuracy. This approach, as identified by the authors, overlooks the inherent structural complexity of the reasoning process itself. Current metrics often treat reasoning as a flat sequence, which may not accurately reflect the true learnability or optimization potential of a query.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing a novel approach to data scheduling that explicitly considers the structural properties of a query's reasoning tree. By moving beyond simple accuracy-based metrics, the research aims to provide a more principled and effective way to sequence training data, thereby improving the efficiency and performance of RLVR for LLMs in complex reasoning tasks. The contribution lies in re-conceptualizing "difficulty" to incorporate the topological structure of the solution space, rather than solely relying on outcome-based proxies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for optimizing Large Language Models (LLMs) on complex reasoning tasks, such as mathematical problem-solving. This is pursued through the development of a novel data scheduling algorithm that leverages the structural characteristics of reasoning processes.

The motivation for this work arises from several observations regarding the limitations of existing RLVR training paradigms:

1. **Challenge of Complex Reasoning:** LLMs continue to face significant challenges in mastering complex reasoning tasks. While RLVR, particularly with policy optimization algorithms like GRPO, has shown promise, there is a continuous need for enhancements to push performance further.
2. **RLVR as Node-Editing a Reasoning Tree:** The authors conceptualize the RLVR process as dynamically "node-editing" a "Reasoning Tree." In this framework, each node represents an intermediate reasoning step, and paths represent potential solution trajectories. The model refines its decision policy at each node by rewarding correct paths and penalizing incorrect ones, gradually pruning suboptimal branches.
3. **Limitations of Current Data Scheduling:** Data scheduling is acknowledged as a critical factor in model performance, often based on curriculum learning principles where models learn more effectively from an organized sequence of training examples. However, existing data scheduling methods in RLVR typically define query "difficulty" using path-based metrics, primarily final solution accuracy.
4. **Inadequacy of Accuracy as a Difficulty Metric:** The core motivation of the paper is that accuracy alone is an insufficient and potentially misleading indicator of a query's true learning difficulty.
 - A query might have low initial accuracy due to numerous incorrect solution trajectories, but its reasoning tree structure could be simple, meaning that modifying a few key decision nodes could yield substantial accuracy gains. Such a query would exhibit high learning efficiency despite poor initial performance.
 - Conversely, a query might achieve higher initial accuracy, but if its correct paths are fragmented across disparate subtrees, it could require extensive edits across many nodes. This fragmented structure would result in higher training difficulty and lower learning efficiency, despite the seemingly better initial performance.
 - Existing accuracy-based metrics would misinterpret the first type of query as "hard" and the second as "easy," leading to an inefficient training curriculum that prioritizes queries that are genuinely harder to optimize.
5. **Need for Structural Understanding:** This discrepancy highlights a fundamental gap: the oversight of richer, query-level characteristics, specifically the structural complexity of the reasoning tree. The authors posit that a structural understanding of the reasoning tree provides a more accurate and principled measure of a query's "learnability" or "learning potential."

Therefore, the central research question motivating this paper is: "How can we move beyond path-based metrics to directly quantify a query’s true learning difficulty from its reasoning-tree structure?" The objective is to design a new metric and a scheduling algorithm that leverage this structural insight to create a more effective "easy-to-hard" curriculum for RLVR training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, named Reasoning Tree Schedule (Re-Schedule), introduces a novel metric called Reasoning Score (r-score) to quantify a query's learning difficulty based on its reasoning tree structure. This r-score then guides a dynamic weighting scheme for RLVR training. The approach consists of three main stages: Tree Construction, R-score Calculation, and Dynamic Weighting.

#### 3.1. Tree Construction

To analyze the reasoning process, the authors first construct an approximate reasoning tree for each query.
- **Approximation Necessity:** A complete reasoning tree for complex tasks is computationally intractable due to the combinatorial explosion of possible solution paths. Therefore, an approximated, fixed-structure `k`-ary tree `T` is used.
- **Parameters:** This approximation is defined by a branching factor `k` (number of sub-paths at each split), a maximum depth `d`, and a token interval `l` (e.g., `k=4, d=4, l=200`).
- **Process:** The construction starts at the root node (the query `q`). A base LLM generates responses. A branch is triggered at the beginning of the response and subsequently at every `l`-token interval. At each trigger point, the current path splits into `k` independent sub-paths that generate tokens in parallel. This recursive branching continues until the maximum depth `d` is reached.
- **Computational Efficiency:** To mitigate the computational overhead of multi-path sampling, a Key-Value (KV) Cache is utilized, as sibling branches often share common prefixes.
- **Node Quality Definition:** The quality of any intermediate node `n_i` is defined as the average accuracy of its leaf descendants, `L(n_i)`. This is formalized by `ACC(S) = (sum_{n_j in S} I(n_j is correct)) / |S|`, where `S` is a set of leaf nodes and `I(·)` is an indicator function for correctness. This allows for assessing quality at different levels of reasoning.

#### 3.2. R-score Calculation

The r-score quantifies the learning potential of a query based on its tree structure, rather than its current accuracy. It represents the maximum achievable accuracy gain within a limited "node editing budget."
- **Node-level R-score `R(n_i)`:** For any non-leaf node `n_i`, its r-score is defined as the maximal accuracy gain achievable by selecting its single best child branch and pruning all other branches originating from `n_i`. This is calculated as `R(n_i) = max_{n_child in C(n_i)} ACC(N_{leaf} \ L(n_i) U L(n_child)) - ACC(N_{leaf})`. This effectively measures how much the overall query accuracy could improve if the model learned to follow the best single branch from `n_i`. A higher `R(n_i)` indicates a simpler structure for potential improvement within that subtree.
- **Query-level R-score `R(q)`:** The overall r-score for a query `q` estimates the total accuracy gain possible under a predefined maximum budget of `M` node modifications. It is calculated as the maximum sum of `R(n_i)` scores from any set of `M` non-conflicting nodes (where non-conflicting means one node is not in a subtree implicitly pruned by another's optimal branch selection). This formulation identifies the most impactful `M` edits across the entire reasoning tree.
- **Interpretation:** A higher `R(q)` signifies a more tractable reasoning structure, indicating that substantial accuracy improvements can be made by correcting a limited number of critical reasoning steps. This represents a structurally simple and efficient-to-learn query.

#### 3.3. Dynamic Weighting

The calculated r-scores are then integrated into the RLVR training process to form a dynamic curriculum.
- **Adaptive Weights:** Queries are assigned adaptive weights `ω` that depend on both their r-score `R(q)` and the current training epoch `t`.
- **Curriculum Design:** The scheduling framework prioritizes queries with high r-scores (structurally simple, lower learning difficulty) in the early stages of training. As training progresses, the weighting gradually shifts towards queries with lower r-scores (structurally complex, higher learning difficulty). This aims to stabilize initial RL learning and then enhance generalization by tackling more challenging problems.
- **Weighting Function:** The training weight `ω` for each query is formulated using an intermediate term `α(R(q), t) = (1 − γ(t))R(q) + γ(t)(1 − R(q))`. This `α` function monotonically varies, down-weighting high-scoring queries over time while up-weighting lower-scoring ones. The final weight `ω` is then derived from `rank(α)` within the dataset, normalized between `ω_min` and `ω_max`.
- **Scheduling Function `γ(t)`:** The progression of the curriculum is controlled by `γ(t)`, which can be a linear mapping (`t/T`) or a sigmoid function (`σ(t/T - 0.5)`), where `T` is the total number of epochs.
- **RL Objective Modification:** The original GRPO objective function is modified to include `ω(q, t)`, effectively weighting the contribution of each query to the overall loss.

#### 3.4. Training Setup

- **Base Framework:** The work builds upon the standard GRPO framework for policy optimization.
- **Training Data:** DAPO-Math-17k dataset, focused on math problems with integer ground-truth answers.
- **Model Architectures:** Experiments conducted on Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Qwen2.5-7B, with additional generalization tests on Qwen3-4B-Base and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B for code generation.
- **Evaluation:** Models evaluated on six widely used mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH-500, Minerva Math, OlympiadBench), reporting average accuracy (`avg@32`). Math-Verify is used for verification.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings that support the proposed Reasoning Score (r-score) and the Re-Schedule algorithm's effectiveness in optimizing LLM reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Training as Reasoning Tree Optimization

An empirical validation was conducted to confirm the core assumption that the RL training process optimizes reasoning trees. The Minimum Corrective Nodes (MCN) metric, defined as the minimum node modifications needed to achieve a target accuracy, was tracked. The results showed a consistent downward trend in the average MCN across the training set as training progressed, regardless of the target accuracy. This indicates that RL effectively refines the model's policy at critical decision nodes, thereby validating the conceptualization of training as reasoning tree optimization.

#### 4.2. Relationship between R-score and Learning Difficulty

An experiment comparing different metrics for identifying valuable queries for early-stage training demonstrated the efficacy of the r-score.
- Models were trained on subsets comprising the top one-third of data selected by either accuracy (ACC-based), r-score, or random selection.
- The model trained on the r-score-selected subset rapidly surpassed the ACC-based method in training accuracy, despite the latter initially showing higher accuracy. This suggests that the r-score is more effective at identifying queries with inherent low learning difficulty.
- On the test set, the model trained on r-score-selected queries consistently outperformed both ACC-based selection and random selection (GRPO baseline). This outcome supports the claim that r-score identifies queries that provide a more effective learning signal, leading to better generalization and performance improvement.

#### 4.3. Main Experiment Results

Re-Schedule was evaluated against several classical RLVR methods and other scheduling baselines on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks using Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Qwen2.5-7B models.
- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** Re-Schedule consistently achieved new state-of-the-art average scores, reaching 48.5% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B and 44.5% on Qwen2.5-7B.
- **Superiority over Baselines:** It significantly outperformed existing scheduling baselines (e.g., ACC_sigmoid by up to 3.2% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B) and classical RLVR methods (e.g., OPO/GRPO by up to 3.8% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B).
- **Validation of Core Claim:** These results empirically validate the central claim that the reasoning tree's structure, quantified by the r-score, provides a more effective measure of a query's true learning difficulty compared to path-based metrics like accuracy.

#### 4.4. Ablation Experiments

Several ablation studies were conducted to assess the impact of different design choices.
- **Tree Construction Parameters (k, d):** The default configuration of branching factor `k=4` and depth `d=4` yielded the best average performance (48.3% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B). While larger values for `k` and `d` could theoretically offer more accurate tree approximations, they also incur higher computational costs.
- **Weight Function Hyperparameters (ω_min, ω_max):** The default setting of `ω_min=0.5` and `ω_max=2.0` achieved the highest average score (48.5%). A sufficiently large weighting range was found to be crucial for effective differentiation between easy and hard samples, while overly extreme ranges (e.g., `ω_min=0.2`) could degrade performance by excessively under-weighting difficult queries.
- **Metric Selection (Node-level vs. Branch-level):** Comparing the proposed node-level 'Fix' (single node modification) with a branch-level 'Pruning' (pruning an entire sub-branch) showed that 'Fix' consistently outperformed 'Pruning'. This indicates that the fine-grained node-level modification aligns more consistently with the policy optimization process in reinforcement learning.
- **Token Interval `l`:** The Re-Schedule method demonstrated robustness to the choice of token interval `l` within the range of. However, a significant performance drop was observed at `l=1200`, suggesting that excessively large intervals lead to a too coarse reasoning tree approximation.
- **Node Modification Budget `M`:** The performance of the method was relatively robust to changes in the node modification budget `M`, with the default `M=10` yielding optimal results, but variations between `M=5` and `M=15` not leading to significant degradation.

#### 4.5. Analysis Experiments

- **Computational Cost:** The offline tree construction cost for larger trees (e.g., 4^4) was higher (e.g., +48.54% relative to total training time) but remained manageable in relation to the substantial performance gains achieved, demonstrating a favorable trade-off.
- **Impact of Ordering:** A comparison between the "easy-to-hard" curriculum (Re-Schedule) and a "hard-to-easy" (Reverse Schedule) confirmed that prioritizing structurally simple samples first is crucial for effective learning, as the Reverse Schedule led to a significant performance drop.
- **Generalization to Different Model Architectures:** Re-Schedule consistently outperformed baselines (GRPO, ACC) when applied to the Qwen3-4B-Base model, indicating its broad applicability beyond the specific Qwen2.5 family.
- **Dynamic R-score Calculation:** Experimentation with dynamically recomputing r-scores during training showed comparable performance to the static approach (computed once before training). Given the high computational cost of dynamic updates, the static r-score was deemed a sufficient and efficient prior.
- **Generalization to Code Generation:** The method's effectiveness extended to code generation tasks, outperforming GRPO and ACC baselines on the LiveCodeBench v5 benchmark, suggesting the broader applicability of structural reasoning insights across different LLM domains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces a conceptual shift in how learning difficulty is assessed for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) in Large Language Models (LLMs). By moving beyond traditional path-based metrics like accuracy, the paper establishes a new framework based on the structural properties of a query's reasoning tree.

The key significance and potential impacts include:

1. **More Principled Data Scheduling:** The introduction of the Reasoning Score (r-score) provides a more theoretically grounded and effective metric for quantifying the "learnability" of queries. This offers a principled foundation for creating curricula that adapt to the inherent structural complexity of reasoning tasks, rather than relying on potentially misleading performance proxies.
2. **Enhanced LLM Reasoning Capabilities:** The consistent outperformance of Re-Schedule across multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks, against both classical RLVR and other scheduling methods, demonstrates its ability to significantly improve LLM accuracy. This suggests a pathway to developing LLMs with more robust and advanced reasoning abilities for complex problem-solving.
3. **Improved Data Efficiency:** By prioritizing queries based on their learning potential (ease of optimization), the Re-Schedule algorithm can lead to more efficient use of training data and computational resources. This is particularly relevant for LLM training, which is typically resource-intensive.
4. **Generalizability Across Domains and Architectures:** The successful application of Re-Schedule to different Qwen model architectures (Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-4B-Base) and its generalization to the code generation domain (LiveCodeBench v5) suggest that the underlying principle of structural learnability is broadly applicable. This broad applicability could enable similar performance gains in other complex generative tasks where a reasoning-tree structure can be approximated.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens avenues for future research into:
 - **Refined Tree Construction:** Investigating more sophisticated or adaptive methods for approximating reasoning trees, potentially balancing fidelity with computational cost more optimally.
 - **Advanced R-score Formulations:** Exploring alternative definitions or computational methods for the r-score that might capture even finer-grained aspects of structural difficulty.
 - **Dynamic Curriculum Adaptation:** While static r-scores were found efficient, future work might explore more advanced dynamic scheduling strategies that can adapt the r-score itself as the model's capabilities evolve during training, without incurring excessive computational burden.
 - **Application to New Modalities:** The demonstrated generalization to code generation suggests that tree-based structural metrics could be adapted to improve multi-modal data generation pipelines and self-instructed compositional code captioning, where "reasoning trees" might represent decision spaces for diverse outputs.

In summary, this research contributes a novel, structurally-aware approach to data scheduling in RLVR, offering a significant step forward in optimizing LLM performance on complex reasoning tasks and providing a robust framework for future innovations in curriculum learning for advanced AI systems.
