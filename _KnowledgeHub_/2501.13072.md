# AdaWM: Adaptive World Model based Planning for Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> AdaWM finetunes a pretrained world-model-based driving policy by first identifying whether dynamics-model mismatch or policy mismatch dominates the performance gap after distribution shift, then applying low-rank updates to only that component. On CARLA tasks it lifts success rate from 0.35-0.40 (best baseline) to 0.66-0.82.

## Problem

- Pretrain-finetune world model RL degrades sharply online because naive initialization ignores which component, dynamics model or policy, is actually misaligned with the new task.
- Existing finetuning strategies alternate model and policy updates or fix on one alone, without diagnosing which mismatch dominates the observed performance drop.
- Prior end-to-end driving systems (VAD, UniAD, Think2Drive) either skip online finetuning entirely or use rigid, single-focused adaptation strategies with no unified framework.

## Method

- **AdaWM**'s ==Mismatch Identification==: estimates ==total-variation distance== between pretrained and current dynamics models and between pretrained and current policies via state-action visitation, then a theorem-derived threshold C picks the dominating cause each step.
- ==Alignment-driven Finetuning==: routes updates to only the diagnosed mismatch instead of blindly alternating, avoiding wasted gradient steps on an already-aligned component.
- ==LoRA==-style ==low-rank dynamics-model update== (NoLa-style base-vector factorization) plus policy decomposition into a convex combination of sub-units, updating only the combination weights for cheap finetuning.

## Results

- On CARLA Leaderboard v2 / Bench2Drive, AdaWM reaches SR **0.82** and TTC **2.05** on ROM03 versus DreamerV3's **0.40** SR and **0.95** TTC, the strongest baseline.
- On the hardest task LTD03 (left turn, dense traffic), AdaWM hits SR **0.70** and TTC **1.92** while VAD and UniAD collapse to SR **0.04-0.10** and TTC below **0.1**.
- Ablation shows AdaWM beats fixed model-only, policy-only, and alternating model+policy finetuning across all four tasks, and is robust to threshold C over roughly a **2-50** range.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diagnosing which sub-model is misaligned before updating beats blindly alternating updates, a general lesson for any two-component pretrain-finetune system.
> - A single dominating mismatch can explain most of the performance gap, so cheap TV-distance proxies can substitute for expensive full retraining diagnostics.
> - Low-rank, selective updates recover most performance in about an hour of online interaction, suggesting cheap finetuning is viable once you know where to apply it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous vehicles (AVs) represent a significant development in future mobility, promising advancements in safety and efficiency. However, developing AVs capable of reliably navigating diverse and complex real-world scenarios, particularly unforeseen situations, remains a substantial challenge. Traditional modular architectures often struggle with the intricate dynamics of environments and timely decision-making, such as emergency braking.

World model (WM) based reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising approach in this domain. These end-to-end methods map sensory inputs directly to control outputs, aiming for improved efficiency and robustness compared to modular systems. By learning a latent dynamics model from observations and actions, WM-based systems can predict future events and optimize policy decisions, which can enhance generalization across various environments. Recent developments in WM-based RL, such as DreamerV2 and DreamerV3, have demonstrated performance in both 2D and 3D environments.

A significant hurdle for WM-based RL, especially in autonomous driving with its vast state spaces and complex environments, is the time-consuming nature of learning a world model and planning policy from scratch. Furthermore, models learned offline may not perform robustly in unseen scenarios due to generalization limitations. To mitigate these issues, the pretraining-finetuning paradigm is frequently employed. This approach involves pretraining models on large offline datasets to capture general features, followed by finetuning with task-specific data to adapt to new environments.

However, the effectiveness of the pretrain-finetune paradigm is dependent on the finetuning strategy. A naive application of finetuning can lead to performance degradation during online interactions in a new task, primarily due to distribution shifts between the pretraining and new tasks. Existing research on finetuning strategies in RL has explored model-only or policy-only adaptations, or lacks online finetuning capabilities in autonomous driving contexts (e.g., supervised learning methods like VAD and UniAD). While studies have shown the general benefits of finetuning in robotic learning, a unified framework for dynamic adaptation in autonomous driving systems has been less explored.

This work addresses the specific challenge of performance degradation during finetuning by analyzing its root causes and proposing an adaptive strategy. It aims to bridge the gap between effective offline pretraining and robust online adaptation in autonomous driving by offering a dynamic approach to finetuning both the world model and the planning policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The development of autonomous vehicles is complex, requiring systems that can learn intricate environmental dynamics, predict future scenarios accurately, and make timely decisions. World model-based reinforcement learning (RL) has shown promise in this regard by enabling agents to learn an internal model of the environment and use it for planning. However, directly applying WM-based RL to autonomous driving tasks faces significant challenges:
1. **High Computational Cost:** Learning a comprehensive world model and an effective planning policy from scratch for the vast and complex state spaces encountered in autonomous driving environments is computationally intensive and time-consuming.
2. **Generalization Limitations:** Models trained on specific datasets or environments may not generalize well to novel or unseen scenarios, leading to performance degradation when deployed in different conditions or tasks.

To address the training efficiency and generalization issues, the pretrain-finetune paradigm has been adopted. In this approach, a model is first pretrained on a large dataset, often offline, to acquire general capabilities, and then finetuned on specific tasks to adapt to new environments. However, the authors observed that a direct or unoptimized application of finetuning can lead to a *decline in performance* during online interactions with a new task.

A motivating example presented in the paper illustrates this issue: an agent pretrained for right turns at an intersection, when finetuned for left turns, initially experiences performance degradation. Different finetuning strategies (alternating model and policy updates, model-only, policy-only) yield varying recovery speeds. The observation was that model-only finetuning led to a significantly faster recovery. This suggested that for the right-to-left turn transition, the primary challenge was the dynamics model's adaptation to new spatial-temporal relationships of vehicles, and that a misalignment in the dynamics model disproportionately impacts policy decisions. This highlights that effective finetuning requires identifying and prioritizing the *dominant mismatch* rather than uniformly applying updates.

This motivation leads to the central objective of the research:
**To determine when and how the pretrained dynamics model and planning policy should be finetuned to effectively mitigate performance degradation resulting from distribution shift.**

The specific contributions outlined to achieve this objective are:
- **Quantifying Performance Degradation Causes:** To analyze the performance gap observed during finetuning and identify two primary root causes: (1) mismatch of the dynamics model (inaccurate environment predictions) and (2) mismatch of the planning policy (suboptimal actions). The work then assesses the impact of each mismatch on finetuning performance.
- **Introducing AdaWM for Adaptive Finetuning:** To propose AdaWM (Adaptive World Model based planning), which incorporates two key steps:
 - **Mismatch Identification:** At each finetuning step, AdaWM quantifies the degree of distribution shift to identify the dominating mismatch causing performance degradation.
 - **Alignment-driven Finetuning:** Based on the identified dominant mismatch, AdaWM selectively updates either the dynamics model or the policy. This selective approach prioritizes the most critical misalignment. Furthermore, AdaWM integrates efficient low-rank update methods: LoRA-based adaptation for dynamics model finetuning (updating only low-dimensional vectors) and decomposition of the policy network into a convex ensemble, updating only the weights of these sub-units for policy finetuning.
- **Experimental Validation:** To validate AdaWM through extensive experiments on challenging CARLA autonomous driving tasks, demonstrating its ability to improve finetuning, leading to more robust and efficient performance in terms of routing success rate (SR) and time-to-collision (TTC).

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on developing an adaptive finetuning strategy for world model-based reinforcement learning (RL) in autonomous driving, specifically designed to counter performance degradation caused by distribution shifts.

#### 3.1. Basic Setting and Pretraining

The agent's decision-making problem is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by states (S), actions (A), state transition dynamics (P), rewards (r), and a discounting factor (γ).
In the pretraining phase, the objective is to learn a dynamics model, denoted WMϕ, that captures the latent dynamics of the environment and a planning policy, πω. This involves:
- **Latent State Representation:** Observations (st) are encoded into a latent state (zt) using an autoencoder (qϕ). A hidden state (ht), derived from a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), incorporates historical information to form a comprehensive model state (xt = [ht, zt]).
- **Dynamics Model Training:** The dynamics model WMϕ predicts future states by forecasting the next hidden state (ht+1) based on the current action (at) and model state (xt). This predicted hidden state is then used to predict the next latent state (ˆzt+1). The model is trained by minimizing the prediction error between predicted future states and actual observations, incorporating reward prediction loss. The model parameters are denoted by ϕ.
- **Policy Learning:** Once the dynamics model is trained, it guides policy learning. The planning policy πω is learned by maximizing a value function (VπωWMϕ(xt)), which combines cumulative rewards from a K-step lookahead using the learned dynamics model and a Q-function for the terminal state. The policy parameters are denoted by ω.

#### 3.2. Finetuning with Pretrained Dynamics Model and Policy

The core challenge addressed is finetuning the pretrained WMϕ and πω to adapt to a new task while minimizing performance degradation due to distribution shift. At each finetuning step, the agent interacts with the environment, collects new samples, and uses the current policy and dynamics model for planning.

#### 3.3. Impact of Mismatches on Performance Degradation

The paper first formally analyzes the performance gap (η − ˆη) between the pretrained task performance (η) and the performance on the new task (ˆη). This gap is attributed to:
- **State Prediction Error (ϵk):** The difference between the true state representation in the new task (xk) and the state representation predicted by the pretrained dynamics model (ˆxk). This error is decomposed into:
 - **(xk − ¯xk):** Capturing the distribution shift between the current and pretraining tasks.
 - **(¯xk − ˆxk):** Representing the prediction error of the pretrained RNN model on the pretraining task itself (generalization error).
- **Policy Sub-optimality:** The pretrained policy πω, optimized for the pretraining tasks, may not select optimal actions in the new task due to differing objectives or dynamics.

The paper provides a theoretical upper bound for this performance gap (Theorem 1), which quantifies the compounded effect of both dynamics model mismatch (represented by terms involving Emax and EˆP) and policy mismatch (represented by Eπ).

#### 3.4. Determining the Dominating Mismatch

Based on the theoretical analysis of the performance gap, a criterion is established to determine which mismatch (dynamics model or policy) is more dominant at each finetuning step. This criterion involves comparing the Total Variation (TV) distance of the dynamics model (DTV(P | ˆP)) and the policy (DTV(πt | πω)).
- If DTV(P | ˆP) ≥ C * DTV(πt | πω), where C is a constant derived from theoretical bounds, the dynamics model mismatch is considered dominant, indicating that improving the model's accuracy will most effectively reduce the performance gap.
- Otherwise, if DTV(πt | πω) is more dominant, refining the policy is prioritized.

#### 3.5. AdaWM: Mismatch Identification and Alignment-driven Finetuning

AdaWM implements an iterative finetuning process (Algorithm 1) with two key components:

- **Mismatch Identification:**
 - **Dynamics Model Mismatch:** Estimated using the TV distance between the dynamics model by measuring the state-action visitation distribution (Janner et al., 2019). This quantifies the model's inability to accurately predict the current task's state transitions.
 - **Policy Mismatch:** Calculated as the TV distance between the state visitation distributions or policy distributions from the pretraining phase and the current task.

- **Alignment-driven Finetuning:** Once the dominant mismatch is identified, AdaWM selectively updates either the dynamics model or the policy using efficient methods:
 - **Finetuning the Dynamics Model:** A LoRA-based (Low-Rank Adaptation) strategy, also referred to as NoLa, is employed. The model parameters (ϕ) are decomposed into low-dimensional vectors (latent representation base vector Z and vector Φ). During finetuning, only the weight (B) of the base vector is updated (B' ← B, ϕ' = (B'Z)⊤Φ), significantly reducing computational overhead compared to full model updates.
 - **Finetuning Planning Policy:** The policy network (ω) is decomposed into a convex combination of sub-units (Ω = Σ δiωi). During finetuning, only the weight vector (Δ = [δ1..., δD]) of these sub-units is updated (Δ' ← Δ), streamlining the process.

This adaptive and selective approach ensures that computational resources are focused on the most impactful component for performance improvement, thereby minimizing degradation and accelerating adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates AdaWM's effectiveness through extensive experiments within the CARLA autonomous driving simulator, focusing on its ability to mitigate performance drops during finetuning and the impact of its adaptive strategy.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Environment:** CARLA, a high-fidelity 3D urban driving simulator. Observations are processed as Bird-Eye-View (BEV) semantic segmentation images (128x128). The action space is discrete, encompassing acceleration ([-2, 0, 2]) and steering angle ([-0.6, -0.2, 0, 0.2, 0.6]). Rewards are a weighted sum of safety, comfort, driving time, velocity, orientation, and distance to waypoints.
- **Baselines:** Three state-of-the-art autonomous driving algorithms:
 - UniAD (Hu et al., 2023) and VAD (Jiang et al., 2023): Supervised learning methods, evaluated using offline-trained checkpoints from the Bench2Drive dataset without finetuning, adhering to their original design.
 - DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023): A world model-based RL algorithm, adapted from Think2Drive (Li et al., 2024).
- **Pretrain-Finetune Protocol:** AdaWM is pretrained for 12 hours on a single V100 GPU using the Pre-RTM03 task (Right Turn in Moderate traffic in Town 03). Finetuning is then conducted for 1 hour on a single V100 GPU for each new task.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Time-to-Collision (TTC) (higher is better) and Success Rate (SR, percentage of successful goal completions without collisions) (higher is better).

#### 4.2. Evaluation Tasks

The models were evaluated on a series of increasingly difficult CARLA tasks, designed to test generalization capabilities to new scenarios and traffic conditions.
- **Core Tasks:**
 - **ROM03:** Roundabout, Moderate traffic, Town 03 (same town as pretraining, new maneuver).
 - **RTD12:** Right Turn, Dense traffic, Town 12 (new town, new traffic density).
 - **LTM03:** Left Turn, Moderate traffic, Town 03 (same town/traffic, new maneuver).
 - **LTD03:** Left Turn, Dense traffic, Town 03 (most challenging, new maneuver, dense traffic).
- **Supplementary Tasks (Appendix G):**
 - HC13 (Highway Cut-In), HE12 (Highway Exit), YE12 (Yield to Emergency Vehicle), BI12 (Blocked Intersection), VT11 (Vehicle Turning Route Pedestrian).

#### 4.3. Performance Comparisons

- **Impact of Finetuning in Autonomous Driving (Table 2, 7, 8):**
 - AdaWM consistently outperformed all baseline algorithms (UniAD, VAD, DreamerV3) across all evaluation tasks in terms of both TTC and SR.
 - For instance, in the ROM03 task, AdaWM achieved a TTC of 2.05 and an SR of 0.82, significantly higher than DreamerV3 (TTC 0.95, SR 0.40), VAD (TTC 0.95, SR 0.15), and UniAD (TTC 0.92, SR 0.13).
 - In the most challenging task, LTD03, AdaWM maintained robust performance with TTC 1.92 and SR 0.70, while DreamerV3 achieved TTC 0.15 and SR 0.35, and supervised learning methods performed even lower.
 - Similar trends were observed in supplementary tasks, with AdaWM demonstrating superior adaptation in highway maneuvers (HC13, HE12), emergency response (YE12), and complex intersections (BI12, VT11). This indicates that finetuning, specifically AdaWM's adaptive approach, is crucial for mitigating performance drops in diverse autonomous driving scenarios.

- **Effectiveness of Finetuning Strategy (Table 3, 9):**
 - Comparing AdaWM's alignment-driven strategy against conventional finetuning methods (no finetuning, policy-only, model-only, and alternating model+policy), AdaWM consistently achieved better performance.
 - In ROM03, AdaWM yielded TTC 2.05 and SR 0.82, surpassing model-only (TTC 0.95, SR 0.60) and policy-only (TTC 0.46, SR 0.72) strategies.
 - This demonstrates that AdaWM's adaptive adjustment of either the model or policy based on identified mismatches provides a more robust performance profile than rigid or fixed finetuning approaches.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Determine the Dominating Mismatch during Finetuning (Figure 4):**
 - Analysis of TV distances showed that model-only finetuning often caused a deterioration in policy performance (increased policy TV distance), suggesting the policy struggled to adapt to the newly adjusted model.
 - Conversely, policy-only finetuning reduced policy mismatch but led to an increased dynamics model mismatch.
 - AdaWM's alignment-driven approach effectively managed both mismatches, preventing either from escalating dramatically and maintaining stability in both model and policy alignment throughout finetuning.

- **The Impact of Parameter C (Table 4, Figure 5):**
 - The parameter C, which balances the importance of dynamics model mismatch vs. policy mismatch, was investigated.
 - Extremely large C values (e.g., C=100) led to performance degradation, as this effectively reduced AdaWM to policy-only finetuning, neglecting critical dynamics model updates.
 - Very small C values also resulted in suboptimal performance due to insufficient dynamics model updates.
 - AdaWM showed strong performance across a range of C values (e.g., 2 to 50), demonstrating its robustness. Visualizations (Figure 5) illustrated that AdaWM adaptively prioritized fine-tuning the dynamics model when its mismatch was greater (C=10, 50) and the policy when its mismatch was greater (C=2).

In summary, the results demonstrate that AdaWM's adaptive and selective finetuning strategy, informed by mismatch identification and utilizing efficient update mechanisms, significantly enhances the performance and robustness of world model-based planning for autonomous driving across various challenging scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces AdaWM, an Adaptive World Model based planning method, which addresses a critical challenge in the application of world model-based reinforcement learning (RL) to autonomous driving: mitigating performance degradation during the finetuning of pretrained models. By offering an intelligent and efficient finetuning strategy, AdaWM holds significant implications for the development and deployment of autonomous driving systems.

**Significance:**
- **Addressing Distribution Shift:** AdaWM directly tackles the problem of performance degradation caused by distribution shifts when a pretrained model is adapted to a new task. This is a fundamental challenge in transferring learned behaviors to diverse real-world conditions.
- **Theoretical Grounding for Finetuning:** The work provides a theoretical analysis that quantifies the performance gap and identifies two root causes: dynamics model mismatch and policy mismatch. This theoretical understanding informs the development of the adaptive finetuning strategy, moving beyond heuristic approaches.
- **Adaptive and Efficient Finetuning:** The core contribution of AdaWM lies in its ability to dynamically identify the dominant mismatch (either in the dynamics model or the policy) and then selectively apply efficient low-rank updates. This adaptive approach ensures that computational resources are concentrated on the most impactful component, improving both finetuning efficiency and effectiveness.
- **Improved Robustness and Safety:** Experimental results in the challenging CARLA environment demonstrate that AdaWM consistently achieves higher success rates (SR) and improved time-to-collision (TTC) compared to baselines and alternative finetuning strategies. This indicates enhanced robustness and safer decision-making in complex driving scenarios.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Development and Deployment:** By enabling faster and more reliable adaptation of pretrained models, AdaWM can significantly reduce the time and computational resources required to develop and deploy autonomous driving systems in various operational design domains (ODDs). This could accelerate the transition of advanced prototypes to real-world applications.
- **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** The adaptive finetuning mechanism allows autonomous vehicles to generalize more effectively to unforeseen conditions, new traffic patterns, or previously unencountered maneuvers, thereby increasing their reliability and capabilities beyond their initial training data.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work emphasizes the importance of intelligent finetuning strategies in embodied AI. It opens avenues for future research into further optimizing mismatch identification techniques, exploring the generalization of AdaWM to other complex domains beyond autonomous driving, and extending the framework to multi-agent settings to account for complex interactions, which are crucial for real-world scenarios.
- **Contribution to Ethical AI:** The paper includes an ethics statement acknowledging the importance of safe operation, minimizing biases, and maintaining transparency. By improving the adaptability and robustness of autonomous systems, AdaWM contributes to the responsible development of technology that can operate more safely and predictably in dynamic environments. The reproducibility statement also enhances the scientific impact by providing a clear path for verification and further exploration.

In essence, AdaWM offers a principled and practical solution for making world model-based RL more effective and efficient for real-world autonomous driving, paving the way for more adaptable, robust, and ultimately safer intelligent transportation systems.
