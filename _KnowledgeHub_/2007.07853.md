# Active World Model Learning with Progress Curiosity

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces γ-Progress, a curiosity signal for active world model learning that enhances efficiency and robustness in complex multi-agent environments. The method facilitates faster world model construction and leads to the spontaneous emergence of attention towards animate agents, demonstrating improved performance over prior curiosity-driven approaches.

## Problem

- Developing AI agents that can efficiently learn robust predictive models of their environment, analogous to human curious exploration.
- Overcoming the "white noise problem," where curiosity-driven agents fixate on unlearnable, noisy stimuli due to consistently high prediction errors.
- Designing a scalable and effective method for estimating learning progress that is applicable to high-dimensional, continuous action spaces and deep neural networks.

## Method

- Proposed **γ-Progress**, a novel ==curiosity signal== that measures ==prediction gain== by comparing the current world model to an ==exponentially decaying weighted mixture== of all past models, ensuring constant memory usage.
- Developed a custom 3D virtual environment featuring a range of inanimate (static, periodic, noise) and complex animate (e.g., chasing, mimicry) agent behaviors.
- Utilized a ==disentangled world model architecture== that independently predicts ==forward dynamics== for behaviorally interdependent groups of external agents, along with a ==DQN==-based controller trained to maximize **γ-Progress**.

## Results

- γ-Progress demonstrated superior sample complexity and end performance, achieving faster world model loss reduction and lower final loss across most behaviors in both mixture and noise-rich environments, outperforming baselines such as Adversarial, Disagreement, and RND.
- Agents driven by γ-Progress consistently displayed strong animate attention, spending significantly more time observing animate agents compared to inanimate ones, a preference often lacking or misdirected in baseline methods.
- A pilot human study indicated that the animate attention patterns generated by the γ-Progress policy were similar to those observed in human participants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - γ-Progress effectively mitigates the "white noise problem" by directing exploration towards learnable and complex dynamics, improving learning efficiency and robustness.
> - An appropriate learning progress signal can naturally induce agents to prioritize attention towards animate, socially-relevant dynamics without explicit, hard-coded biases.
> - Disentangled world model architectures are beneficial for complex multi-agent prediction, suggesting the importance of structural inductive biases for efficient learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in artificial intelligence: enabling agents to learn robust predictive models of their environment, known as "world models," through self-supervised exploration. Humans naturally acquire such world models by curiously exploring their surroundings, leading to compact abstractions of sensory input, long-horizon planning capabilities, and an understanding of other agents' behaviors. Devising algorithms that replicate this efficiency is crucial for developing advanced, socially-integrated AI and robotic systems.

Within the AI literature, this research builds upon existing work in forward dynamics prediction for world models, where models predict future states or latent representations based on past observations and actions. It also integrates concepts from active learning and curiosity-driven exploration in reinforcement learning. Traditional active learning focuses on efficiently selecting data for supervised tasks with costly labels. More recently, this has extended to self-supervised reinforcement learning, where agents generate their own data through curiosity-driven policies to train world models. Prior approaches to curiosity include:
- **Novelty metrics**: Directing agents to under-explored parts of the state-space (e.g., count-based methods, Random Network Distillation (RND)). These methods act as proxies for learning progress but may not be consistently accurate.
- **Adversarial curiosity**: Maximizing current world model error (e.g., Pathak et al., 2017). This is susceptible to the "white noise problem," where agents fixate on unlearnable, noisy stimuli.
- **Model Disagreement**: Using the variance across an ensemble of models as a proxy for uncertainty and potential learning gain (e.g., Pathak et al., 2019). This can also suffer from the white noise problem in complex target functions.
- **Information Gain/Learning Progress**: Directly estimating how much information an action will provide or how much the model's performance will improve (e.g., Oudeyer et al., 2007). These methods are often computationally intensive, especially in high-dimensional continuous action spaces. δ-Progress is a prior approximation that has limitations regarding memory and tuning.

From a cognitive science perspective, the work connects to human intuitive physics and object-based priors, where humans intuitively predict object dynamics and allocate attention based on object permanence. It also draws inspiration from human curiosity and active learning, where infants prioritize stimuli that are complex yet predictable, and selectively attend to animate agents. The paper investigates whether an intelligent curiosity signal can naturally lead to "animate attention" and contribute to foundational abilities akin to Theory of Mind, which involves predicting others' behaviors by inferring their mental states.

This paper positions itself by proposing a scalable and effective progress-based curiosity signal that aims to overcome the limitations of prior methods, particularly the "white noise problem," while learning world models in complex, agent-rich environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to formalize and study Active World Model Learning (AWML), specifically focusing on developing a directed exploration policy that efficiently constructs robust world models in environments rich with diverse and interactive agents.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and challenges:
1. **Human Learning Efficiency**: Humans, even as infants, efficiently learn complex world models through curious exploration. This process allows them to abstract high-bandwidth sensory inputs, plan over long horizons, and understand the behavior of other agents. Replicating this efficiency in AI systems is a significant step towards more capable and adaptive artificial intelligence.
2. **Diverse Environmental Dynamics**: Real-world environments contain a wide spectrum of dynamics, ranging from simple and learnable (e.g., static objects, periodic motions) to unlearnable noise (e.g., rustling leaves) and complex, rule-driven but learnable dynamics (e.g., animate agents). An effective AWML system must navigate this diversity.
3. **The "White Noise Problem"**: A major challenge for curiosity-driven exploration is the "white noise problem." Agents driven by mere prediction error or novelty can become perpetually fascinated by unlearnable, random stimuli, wasting computational resources and failing to learn meaningful dynamics.
4. **Scalable Learning Progress Estimation**: While directly maximizing learning progress is theoretically appealing, existing methods for estimating it (e.g., Information Gain, δ-Progress) face computational or algorithmic limitations that hinder their application to high-dimensional, continuous action spaces and deep neural networks.
5. **Emergence of Social Attention**: The paper seeks to investigate whether an appropriate curiosity signal can naturally induce an agent to prioritize attention towards animate, socially-relevant dynamics without explicit, hard-coded biases for animate features. This could provide insights into the development of social cognition.

To address these motivations, the authors aim to:
- Construct a controlled yet realistic 3D virtual environment with a variety of learnable and unlearnable agent behaviors.
- Formalize AWML within a reinforcement learning framework, defining the objective as minimizing world model loss through effective data collection.
- Propose a novel, scalable, and effective learning progress-based curiosity signal called γ-Progress.
- Demonstrate that a γ-Progress-driven controller can overcome the white noise problem and achieve superior AWML performance compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- Analyze the emergent attentional policies of agents, particularly their focus on animate versus inanimate stimuli.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted approach involving a custom 3D virtual environment, a formalization of Active World Model Learning (AWML) as a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem, a novel curiosity signal (γ-Progress), and a deep learning architecture for the world model and controller.

#### 3.1. Multi-Agent Virtual World Environment

A custom 3D virtual environment was developed to replicate key properties of real-world learning challenges:
- **Diverse Dynamics**: The environment features a curious agent at the center of a room surrounded by various external agents (colored spheres) in four quadrants. These external agents exhibit a spectrum of hard-coded behaviors:
 - **Inanimate**: Static, Periodic, Noise (random motion).
 - **Animate**: Reaching (deterministic/stochastic), Chasing (deterministic/stochastic), Peekaboo (deterministic/stochastic), Mimicry (deterministic/stochastic). These animate behaviors represent complex, rule-driven, and interactive dynamics.
- **Partial Observability**: The curious agent has a limited field of view and can only observe one external agent behavior at a time by rotating its gaze. This necessitates an attention allocation mechanism.
- **Contingency**: The amount and type of learning are contingent on the curious agent's actions and interactions.

The curious agent can choose from 9 actions to rotate its gaze by varying degrees (12°, 24°, 48°, or 96°) left/right, or to stay put.

#### 3.2. Active World Model Learning Formalization

AWML is formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the agent's state space is augmented to include the environment state, the history of visited state-actions, and the current world model parameters. The goal is to learn a world model ω_θ_ that predicts future states (or observations) with minimal error.
The learning objective is framed as maximizing a reward function `r` that measures the reduction in world model loss (L_μ(θ)) as a result of collecting new data. Specifically, `r(s, a, s') = L_μ(θ) - L_μ(θ')` represents the "prediction gain." However, direct computation of this reward is intractable due to the unknown or diffuse validation distribution `μ`. This necessitates the use of heuristic "curiosity signals."

#### 3.3. Curiosity Signals

The paper critically analyzes existing curiosity signals and their limitations:
- **Information Gain**: Computationally expensive for deep nets.
- **Adversarial Curiosity**: Prone to the "white noise problem" by fixating on unlearnable stimuli due to perpetually high prediction error.
- **Disagreement**: Uses an ensemble of models, but memory usage scales linearly with ensemble size and it can still suffer from the white noise problem if the ensemble does not converge.
- **Novelty**: Rewards under-explored states but can be inefficient if novelty does not correlate with learnability or if it prefers noisy data.
- **Progress (δ-Progress)**: Measures loss reduction over a fixed number (δ) of model updates. This approach is sensitive to the choice of δ, and memory usage scales linearly with δ, making it impractical for deep models with δ > 3.

#### 3.4. γ-Progress: The Proposed Curiosity Signal

To overcome the limitations of δ-Progress, the paper introduces **γ-Progress**. This signal also measures prediction gain but defines the "old" model (θ_old) as an exponentially decaying weighted mixture of all past models, rather than a fixed δ-step old model:
θ_old ← γθ_old + (1 − γ)θ_new
where θ_new is the current model. The curiosity reward is then:
`r(s, a, s') = log(ω_θ_new(s'|s, a)) - log(ω_θ_old(s'|s, a))` (or `L(θ_old) - L(θ_new)`).
This formulation offers two key advantages:
1. **Tuning Simplicity**: γ (the progress horizon) is easier to tune than δ, with the authors using a fixed value across experiments.
2. **Constant Memory Usage**: Memory usage is constant with respect to γ, eliminating the O(δ) memory constraint of δ-Progress and allowing for a longer effective progress horizon.

#### 3.5. World Model Architecture

The world model `ω_θ` is designed as an ensemble of component networks `{ω_θ_k}`. This "disentangled" architecture independently predicts forward dynamics for behaviorally interdependent groups of external agents. For example, if two agents are involved in a "chasing" behavior, their dynamics are predicted by one component network. If an agent is alone, it gets its own component network.
- **Input**: The model receives an object-oriented feature vector `x_t` from an oracle encoder, containing masked positions of external agents, auxiliary object positions, and the curious agent's orientation. Partial observability is maintained as the encoder only provides coordinates for agents in view.
- **Components**: Each `ω_θ_k` is a two-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network followed by a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). The number of hidden units scales with the number of agents being modeled by that component.
- **Loss Function**: The world model loss combines mean squared error for predicted coordinates (only for observed agents) and cross-entropy loss for observability masks.

#### 3.6. Progress-driven Controller

The controller `π_φ` is a two-layer fully-connected neural network that takes the agent's recent observation history (`x_t-2:t`) and outputs estimated Q-values for the 9 possible gaze rotation actions. It is trained using the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm, with `γ-Progress` (Eq. 12) serving as the cost function to be minimized (or reward to be maximized).

#### 3.7. Training Protocol

World model and policy updates are interleaved. Both are updated with 10 gradient steps per 40 environment steps, after an initial buffer filling period. Adam optimizer is used for both networks. The old model weights in `γ-Progress` are periodically synchronized with the new model weights to prevent excessively large progress rewards.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated γ-Progress against several baseline curiosity methods in two distinct virtual environments: a "Mixture world" (static, periodic, noise, and animate agents) and a "Noise world" (three noise agents and one animate agent), across various deterministic and stochastic animate behaviors. Performance was measured by end performance (inverse of final world model loss) and sample complexity (rate of loss reduction).

#### 4.1. AWML Performance

- **Mixture World**: γ-Progress demonstrated superior sample complexity, achieving faster world model loss reduction than δ-Progress, Disagreement, Adversarial, and Random baselines across all 8 tested behaviors. It also outperformed RND in 7/8 behaviors and performed comparably in one (stochastic chasing). In terms of end performance, γ-Progress achieved lower final world model loss on 6/8 behaviors.
- **Noise World**: This environment specifically tested robustness to unlearnable noise. γ-Progress exhibited greater robustness, achieving lower sample complexity and higher end performance than all baselines across all 8 behaviors. Baselines frequently performed worse than a random policy due to fixation on the noise agents.
- **Aggregate Performance (Table 1)**: γ-Progress showed a mean ratio of 7.83 (Mixture World) and 13.79 (Noise World) for baseline end performance over Random baseline, significantly higher than other methods (e.g., δ-Progress: 2.2 and 2.46; RND: 1.25 and 0.85).

#### 4.2. Attention Control Analysis

- **Emergent Animate Attention**: γ-Progress agents consistently displayed strong animate attention. They spent substantially more time observing animate agents compared to other external agents (static, periodic, or noise). This often manifested as an early attentional "bump" towards animate agents, followed by a decrease as prediction accuracy improved. This emergent animate preference was observed across 7/7 animate behaviors in the Mixture world (excluding the noise agent from this ratio).
- **Baseline Failure Modes (Table 2)**:
 - **Attentional Indifference**: δ-Progress frequently exhibited attentional indifference, with its animate-to-inanimate attention ratio being statistically indistinguishable from a random policy (7/8 behaviors). This was attributed to the new and old models, separated by a fixed time difference, being too similar to generate a strong curiosity signal.
 - **White Noise Fixation**: RND, Disagreement, and Adversarial baselines suffered from white noise fixation.
 - RND, a novelty-based method, focused on noise agents because they generated the most diffuse state distributions, which RND interprets as high novelty (4/8 behaviors).
 - Disagreement models found noise behaviors highly interesting because their ensemble of models failed to converge to a single prediction for these unlearnable dynamics (7/8 behaviors).
 - Adversarial curiosity, driven by prediction error, consistently fixated on noise behaviors as they yielded the highest prediction errors (8/8 behaviors).
 - White noise fixation was particularly detrimental to sample complexity for RND, Disagreement, and Adversarial, leading to performance worse than random in the Noise world.

#### 4.3. Disentangled World Models

A pilot study comparing the agent-centric disentangled world model with an "entangled" or "joint" LSTM architecture (which processes all external agents together) revealed that the disentangled architecture significantly outperformed the joint version in asymptotic model performance across various reaching, chasing, and mimicry behaviors (Figure 5). This suggests that explicitly separating the modeling of independent or causally linked groups of agents is beneficial.

#### 4.4. Human Behavior Modeling (Pilot Study)

A pilot human study showed that adult participants displayed a clear animacy preference, quickly directing their gaze to animate spherical robots (Spheros) over static, periodic, or noise stimuli. The attention pattern generated by the γ-Progress policy was found to be similar to that observed in human participants (Figure 6a).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a significant contribution to the field of artificial intelligence and offers potential implications for cognitive science and developmental diagnostics.

#### 5.1. Advancement in Active World Model Learning

The primary significance lies in the introduction of **γ-Progress**, a novel curiosity signal that demonstrates improved performance and robustness compared to existing state-of-the-art methods. Its key advantages—constant memory usage and easier tuning—address practical limitations of previous progress-based signals like δ-Progress, making it more applicable to complex deep neural network models and real-world scenarios. By effectively mitigating the "white noise problem," γ-Progress enables agents to focus on learnable and complex dynamics, leading to more efficient world model construction.

#### 5.2. Emergence of Animate Attention

A notable finding is that γ-Progress naturally gives rise to an exploration policy that prioritizes animate agents. This emergence of animate attention without explicit, hard-coded biases suggests that effective learning progress signals can implicitly guide agents toward rich, rule-driven social dynamics. This capability is critical for developing AI systems that can effectively learn in social environments.

#### 5.3. Disentangled Architectures and Theory of Mind

The finding that an agent-centric disentangled world model architecture outperforms a joint approach highlights the importance of structural inductive biases for complex multi-agent prediction. The authors draw a loose connection to the cognitive science concept of Theory of Mind (ToM), suggesting that separate predictive models for individual (or interacting) non-self agents might be a computational function enabling statistical disentangling in partially observed settings. This opens avenues for exploring how to build more sophisticated ToM features into AI models.

#### 5.4. Future Directions in AI and Robotics

The research points to several future directions for expanding the capabilities of AWML agents:
- **Realistic Embodiment and Interaction**: Moving beyond gaze-driven interaction to full motility, articulated effectors, and social cues would enable more complex proto-social behaviors (e.g., mutual gaze, pointing) and extend the framework to imitation learning.
- **Real-world Inputs and Partial Observability**: Integrating pixel-based visual inputs and robust working memory systems to handle dynamic partial observability is a crucial next step towards deploying AWML on real-world robotic platforms.
- **Multi-Agent Learning**: Investigating scenarios where multiple agents are simultaneously running curious policies, leading to richer interactive learning environments.
- **Learning Interaction Graphs**: Developing methods for agents to infer dependencies between agents (interaction graphs) from observations, rather than relying on prior knowledge.

#### 5.5. Quantitative Models of Human Behavior and Developmental Variability

The work proposes a framework for using AWML as a quantitative model of human behavior. The pilot human study provides initial evidence that γ-Progress-driven attention patterns align with human animate preferences. This connection could be extended to:
- **Modeling Developmental Timecourses**: Comparing the learning curves of AWML agents to empirical developmental timecourses of social attention and acuity in children.
- **Understanding Developmental Variability**: Using variability in curiosity signals within the AWML framework to model inter-subject differences in social development, potentially linking to conditions like Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). The "computational etiology" hypothesis suggests that differences in intrinsic motivation mechanisms could explain variability in social acuity and attention allocation.
- **Model-Driven Diagnostics**: The "Attention Differential Indicator" model, which predicts late-stage performance from early attention patterns, offers a theoretical pathway for developing early, objective diagnostics for developmental conditions like ASD. This could enable earlier interventions during critical developmental periods, addressing current limitations of subjective and late diagnoses.

In conclusion, this paper presents γ-Progress as a robust and efficient curiosity signal for active world model learning in complex, agent-rich environments, demonstrating superior performance and the natural emergence of animate attention. Its implications span AI development, cognitive modeling, and potentially, early diagnostics for developmental disorders.
