# Sparse-WAM: Accelerating World Action Models via Action-Guided Sparse Imagination

> [!summary] Summary
> Sparse-WAM is a training-free method that speeds up world action model inference by computing only the action-relevant future-frame tokens during joint visual-action denoising. It scores tokens by how much the action tokens attend to them. It keeps frame-specific core tokens plus shared spatial anchors, and its Pilot engine reuses the selections across denoising steps. It reaches about 2.0x speedup on LIBERO and 1.8x on RoboLab-120 while largely preserving task success.

## Problem

- World action models denoise dense high-resolution future-frame tokens at every step, so imagined frames dominate the token sequence, inflate latency and limit robot control frequency.
- Existing speedups either drop future imagination at inference or compress future tokens uniformly, which still spends compute on regions that have little relevance to the predicted actions.
- Token pruning and caching methods from VLAs and video diffusion rank tokens by relevance to the observed input or by visual quality, not by which evolving noisy future regions actually inform actions.

## Method

- ==Action-Guided Token Selection== scores each future frame using ==attention== from its time-aligned group of action queries, averaged over the layers that score highest on attention mass times one minus ==spatial entropy==.
- Each frame keeps its own top-scoring core tokens to follow shifting hotspots, plus shared spatial anchors ranked by mean over ==coefficient of variation==, which preserve consistent cross-frame context at a fixed sequence length.
- The **Pilot** engine extracts scores from the first dense step reusing the original ==log-sum-exp normalizers==, reuses selections and packing metadata across steps, and fills omitted regions with ==cached velocity predictions==.

## Results

- On LIBERO with FastWAM-Joint, Sparse-WAM reaches **1.98x** speedup at **49.65%** of dense FLOPs and **98.45%** success versus **98.75%** dense, ahead of WorldCache's **1.62x**, ToCa, C3ache and SpecPrune-VLA.
- On RoboLab-120 it gives **1.85x** on Cosmos 3 Edge (**23.00%** vs **22.90%** dense) and **1.81x** on Nano (**35.50%** vs **36.75%**). On a real robot, latency drops from **501 to 242 ms**.
- At an equal token budget, core plus anchor selection scores **35.5%** versus **31.7%** for random selection. On LIBERO-Plus, success drops to **63.04%** from **70.89%** dense but beats action-only's **51.96%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dense future imagination is largely redundant for action prediction. Attention from action tokens is a cheap relevance signal, so compute should be allocated by action relevance rather than visual quality.
> - Attention hotspots stay spatially stable across denoising steps even as the latents change (81-98% overlap), so token selections can be computed once per action chunk and reused.
> - Fewer FLOPs does not guarantee lower latency: fixed sequence lengths and reusable metadata that allow compiled execution matter. Sparse imagination also gives up some robustness under perturbations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

World-action models (WAMs) jointly denoise future visual states and action sequences so imagined futures can inform robot control. A robot policy repeatedly refines a predicted future and an action chunk. Repeatedly processing dense future-frame tokens makes inference slow, which matters when a robot needs to choose its next action promptly. The paper's illustrative instruction is “Pick up the banana and place it in the bowl.” The imagined future of such a task keeps changing during refinement. Even so, the authors observe that attention from action tokens to future-frame tokens can overlap substantially between consecutive steps, which gives the system a selection it can reuse.

For the instruction “Pick up the banana and place it in the bowl,” hotspots shift spatially across future frames. Adjacent-frame overlap bars are visibly higher with hotspots excluded. ### 2. Follow the action's attention

A denoising step is one refinement pass over the predicted future and action chunk. Action-to-future attention measures how strongly action-token queries attend to future-frame tokens, and the authors use it as a proxy for action relevance. The authors compared normalized spatial-attention distributions by summing the smaller value at each position. On Cosmos 3 Edge, mean consecutive-step overlap is 81.11%, compared with 68.89% mean cross-frame overlap. This supports reusing selections across steps while selecting frame-specific regions. It does not show that relevant regions never change.

Sparse-WAM, the authors' method, applies this observation in three steps:

1. At a dense conditional forward pass, it uses the action queries aligned with each future frame to score spatial positions. The alignment is approximate, because queries near group boundaries may attend to neighboring frames.
1. It keeps frame-specific core tokens at that frame's attention hotspots. It also keeps shared spatial anchors at positions with strong, relatively consistent attention across frames. The positions are shared, but each frame's representation remains separate.
1. A component called Pilot derives the scores from query–key products in the already available dense forward pass. This avoids another network forward or a full attention matrix. Pilot then reuses the selections across later steps in the same action chunk.

Sparse-WAM sparsifies computation on future-frame tokens while preserving joint visual–action denoising. It retains all observation and action tokens. Omitted future positions still receive sampler updates using cached predictions.

### 3. Test the selection at a fixed budget

The controlled ablation is the clearest test of this mechanism. On Cosmos 3 Nano Policy, every sparse variant retains 184 of 360 tokens per future frame. The combined method reaches 35.5% success, compared with 34.5% for core-only, 34.2% for stable-anchor-only, and 31.7% for random selection. Dense inference reaches 36.8%. At the same token budget, the combination beats these sparse alternatives, but it remains below dense success.

Sparse-WAM, Figure 5. (a) Success rate (%, left axis) and speedup (×, right axis): dense 36.8/1.000, core-only 34.5/1.814, stable-only 34.2/1.809, random-only 31.7/1.818, core+stable 35.5/1.809. (b) Future-token prune rates of 28.89%, 48.89%, 57.78%, and 68.89% give 37.5/1.451, 35.5/1.803, 30.8/1.995, and 28.3/2.356.

In this tested Nano Policy setting, more aggressive pruning corresponds to higher speedup and lower success.

### 4. Faster inference, with conditions

The headline result uses FastWAM-Joint on LIBERO. Here, average success is the mean success rate across four equally sized suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. Sparse-WAM reports 98.45% average task success, versus 98.75% for dense inference. It also reports a 1.98× inference speedup, with FLOPs at 49.65% of dense.

This speedup compares optimized Sparse-WAM inference with dense eager inference on an NVIDIA RTX 4090. It therefore includes execution optimizations, not only token selection. A timing ablation on Cosmos 3 Edge reports 1.55× for Sparse-WAM eager versus dense eager, and 1.56× for Sparse-WAM versus equally optimized dense. Optimized Sparse-WAM reaches 1.85× against dense eager. The timing includes profiling, scoring, selection, packing/restoration, cache operations, and sampler updates. These results concern inference per action chunk. They do not establish fewer robot attempts or a measured increase in control frequency.

### 5. What the evidence supports

The paper's case is that action-guided selection can reduce repeated computation while retaining joint imagination. It does not show that every future token is dispensable. Its main boundary is robustness: on LIBERO-Plus, Sparse-WAM reaches 63.04% success versus 70.89% for dense FastWAM-Joint, a 7.85 percentage-point loss. The results also come from one GPU and three WAMs, so broader hardware and architecture coverage remains open.
