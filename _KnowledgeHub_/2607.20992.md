# Distributed Model-Based Diffusion For Scalable Multi-Robot Trajectory Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> DMBD is a framework for scalable multi-robot trajectory optimization in non-convex, non-differentiable environments that leverages a distributed, iterative denoising process. The system enables coordination among robots using only local information, achieving over 90% success rates in complex scenarios like Parking and Rush Hour and solving tasks for up to 20 robots within sub-second planning times, where centralized methods often encountered memory limits.

## Problem

- Existing multi-robot trajectory optimization methods, particularly sampling-based ones, suffer from a "curse of dimensionality" in high-dimensional joint trajectory spaces, leading to poor sampling efficiency and high computational costs for large teams.
- Centralized Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) requires global access to all robots' objectives, dynamics, and constraints, incurring substantial communication overhead and heavy computational/memory burdens on a central unit.
- Planning for multi-robot systems in highly non-convex, non-linear, and non-differentiable environments remains challenging, as many optimization approaches are prone to local minima.

## Method

- **DMBD** distributes the ==reverse (denoising) process== of ==Model-Based Diffusion== across a server-robot framework, decomposing the high-dimensional joint optimization into parallel ==local conditional denoising== processes for each robot.
- Each robot plans its own trajectory using a local cost function and local constraints, conditioning only on aggregated state trajectories of other robots received from a central server, rather than requiring full global objective or dynamic models.
- The **DMBD** framework employs an iterative process where robots perform local candidate trajectory sampling, weighted averaging, and updates guided by ==local conditional score functions==, facilitating coordination without explicit knowledge sharing.

## Results

- DMBD achieved **sub-second** planning times for up to **20** robots in goal-swapping tasks, a significant improvement over centralized baselines (CEM, MPPI, MBD, D4orm) which often failed due to out-of-memory errors at high robot counts.
- The framework demonstrated success rates exceeding **90%** in complex multi-robot coordination scenarios, including Parking and Rush Hour puzzles, consistently solving them within **sub-second** planning times.
- DMBD's scalability and planning efficiency advantages became more pronounced in complex scenarios involving non-smooth collision constraints and heterogeneous robot dynamics, where baseline methods struggled.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing a high-dimensional joint trajectory optimization problem into lower-dimensional, parallel, conditional local optimizations significantly enhances scalability for multi-robot systems by improving sample efficiency.
> - Effective multi-robot coordination can emerge from localized iterative denoising processes, where robots condition their planning on aggregated state estimates of others, thereby eliminating the need for explicit global objective or dynamic knowledge sharing.
> - DMBD's inherent zeroth-order optimization approach provides robustness, making it well-suited for trajectory planning in complex environments characterized by non-differentiable, non-convex, and non-linear cost functions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the critical challenge of trajectory optimization for multi-robot systems, particularly in environments characterized by non-convex, non-linear, and non-differentiable characteristics. Historically, gradient-based methods have been employed for trajectory optimization, but these approaches often struggle with local minima and high computational costs in complex, non-convex settings.

To circumvent these limitations, sampling-based optimization (SBO) methods have emerged as an alternative, framing trajectory planning as a probabilistic inference problem. Examples include the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM), Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), and variational Model Predictive Control (MPC) techniques. These SBO methods have demonstrated efficacy in navigating complex environments.

More recently, Model-Based Diffusion (MBD) has been introduced as a promising SBO paradigm. Inspired by denoising diffusion probabilistic models, MBD optimizes over multi-modal trajectory distributions through an iterative denoising process. Unlike other diffusion-based planners that rely on learned score networks from offline datasets, MBD directly utilizes known system dynamics and task objectives during inference, enabling learning-free trajectory generation. Prior extensions of MBD have focused on incorporating gradual constraint enforcement and strict safety guarantees for single-agent systems.

Despite MBD's capabilities in single-agent settings and its appeal for multi-robot planning due to its ability to handle multi-modal distributions (which can help mitigate deadlocks arising from local minima), its direct application to multi-robot systems faces two primary challenges:
1. **Curse of dimensionality**: The dimension of the joint trajectory space scales with the number of robots, leading to reduced sampling efficiency and optimization performance, a common issue for SBO methods in large-scale multi-robot scenarios. While some efforts like D4orm have attempted to mitigate this with iterative deformation updates, the underlying reverse diffusion process often remains centralized. Other approaches combine individual MBD planning with centralized deconfliction, which may necessitate multiple rounds of deconfliction and replanning in congested environments.
2. **Centralized information access**: MBD requires global access to all robot objectives, constraints, and dynamics to perform denoising. In practical multi-robot systems, particularly heterogeneous teams, aggregating this diverse information can lead to substantial communication overhead and significant computational and memory burdens on a single processing unit, thereby limiting scalability.

This paper positions its proposed method, Distributed Model-Based Diffusion (DMBD), as a solution to these specific limitations, aiming to provide a scalable and decentralized approach to multi-robot trajectory optimization building upon the strengths of MBD.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a scalable multi-robot trajectory optimization framework that can operate effectively in challenging, non-convex, non-linear, and non-differentiable environments. This objective is motivated by the inherent limitations of existing trajectory optimization methods, particularly Model-Based Diffusion (MBD), when applied to multi-robot systems.

The specific motivations are twofold:

1. **Addressing the Curse of Dimensionality in Multi-Robot Settings**: Current centralized MBD implementations, like many sampling-based optimization (SBO) methods, face a significant challenge when scaled to multiple robots. The dimensionality of the joint trajectory space for *N* robots increases with *N*, leading to an exponential decrease in sampling efficiency and overall optimization performance. This "curse of dimensionality" makes it computationally intractable to plan for large teams, as a centralized processor must sample and denoise trajectories in a very high-dimensional space. The paper aims to overcome this by decomposing the problem, thereby reducing the effective dimensionality faced by individual computing agents.

2. **Eliminating the Need for Centralized Global Information Access**: Traditional MBD requires a single entity (e.g., a server or a central controller) to have complete knowledge of all robots' individual objectives, dynamics, and local constraints. This centralized information access poses practical difficulties in real-world multi-robot deployments, especially in heterogeneous teams where robots may have distinct characteristics and proprietary information. Aggregating and processing such vast amounts of data can incur substantial communication overhead and place excessive computational and memory demands on a central unit. The motivation is to enable a more modular and scalable framework where robots can plan using primarily their local information, enhancing the autonomy and distributed nature of the system.

In summary, the key objectives are to achieve scalable multi-robot coordination while retaining the benefits of MBD (such as efficiency and multi-modal trajectory generation), by developing a distributed framework that decomposes the optimization problem, mitigates the curse of dimensionality, and reduces the reliance on global information aggregation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is Distributed Model-Based Diffusion (DMBD), a server-robot framework designed to overcome the scalability and centralized information requirements of traditional MBD for multi-robot trajectory optimization. DMBD maintains the same forward (noising) process as vanilla MBD but introduces a distributed approach for the reverse (denoising) process.

#### 3.1. System Model and Problem Formulation

The system comprises *N* robots connected to a server. Each robot *k* has its own dynamics, state, and control input. The global state and control input are stacked vectors of all robots' states and inputs. A planning horizon *T* defines the control trajectories for each robot and the global system. Each robot *k* aims to minimize a local objective function, which includes terminal and stage costs. Robots are also subject to local constraints, which can be coupled, meaning they may depend on the state trajectories of other robots. An Assumption 1 states that coupled constraints are symmetric with respect to participating robots, meaning all involved robots share knowledge of the constraint's functional form and parameters. The overall centralized trajectory optimization problem is formulated as minimizing the sum of local objectives subject to system dynamics and various constraints. This constrained problem is then recast into an unconstrained probabilistic inference problem by defining a global target distribution *p_0(Y)* proportional to *exp(-ϕ(Y)/λ)*, where *ϕ(Y)* is a global cost function combining objectives and weighted constraint violations.

#### 3.2. Distributed Model-Based Diffusion (DMBD) Process

DMBD decomposes the joint reverse diffusion process into local conditional denoising processes. The framework operates iteratively in a server-robot architecture:

1. **Local Conditional Target Distribution**: For each robot *k*, a local conditional target distribution *p_k^0(Y_k | τ_-k)* is defined. This distribution depends on robot *k*'s local cost function *ϕ_k(Y_k)*, which includes its local objective and local constraints, conditioned on the state trajectories of all other robots (*τ_-k*).

2. **Initialization**: At the start of the denoising process (step *M*), each robot *k* samples its initial control trajectory *Y_k^(M)* from a Gaussian distribution. It then rolls out its state trajectory *τ_k^(M)* and transmits it to the server. The server aggregates all *τ_k^(M)* into a global trajectory *τ^(M)* and broadcasts this aggregated information back to all robots.

3. **Iterative Local Conditional Denoising**: For each denoising step, from *i = M* down to 1:
 - **Server Aggregation and Broadcast**: The server aggregates the current state trajectories from all robots (*τ^(i)*) and broadcasts the state trajectories of other robots (*τ_-k^(i)*) to each individual robot *k*.
 - **Parallel Local Denoising by Robots**: Upon receiving *τ_-k^(i)*, each robot *k* performs the following steps in parallel:
 - **Candidate Trajectory Sampling**: Robot *k* samples *S_k* candidate control trajectories (*{Y_(k,s)^(i)}*) for itself from a Gaussian distribution, conditioned on its current trajectory estimate.
 - **Weighted Average Computation**: It computes a locally weighted average (*Y_k^(i)*) of these candidate trajectories. The weights are determined by the conditional likelihood induced by its local target distribution *p_k^0(· | τ_-k^(i))*. This step ensures that the sampled trajectories are evaluated based on robot *k*'s local objectives and constraints, considering the current state of other robots.
 - **Score Function Estimation**: Robot *k* estimates its local conditional score function *∇_(Y_k^(i)) log p_k^i(Y_k^(i) | τ_-k^(i))*. This score function guides the update towards lower-cost trajectories.
 - **Trajectory Update**: Robot *k* updates its control trajectory from *Y_k^(i)* to *Y_k^(i-1)* using the estimated local conditional score function.
 - **State Trajectory Transmission**: After updating its control trajectory, robot *k* rolls out its new state trajectory *τ_k^(i-1)* and transmits it back to the server.

This cycle continues until the final denoised trajectory (*i=1*) is obtained.

#### 3.3. Distinguishing Features

- **Reduced Inference Complexity**: DMBD reduces the optimization dimension from the joint trajectory space (which scales with the team size *N*) to individual robot trajectory spaces, improving sample efficiency and scalability.
- **Modularity and Information Hiding**: Each robot only uses its local objective, dynamics, and constraints, without sharing them with the server or other robots. This enhances modularity and reduces the need for global information storage and communication.
- **Communication Efficiency**: Unlike other distributed sampling-based approaches that exchange multiple trajectory samples, DMBD communicates only a single trajectory (the aggregated state trajectories) per denoising iteration.
- **Zeroth-Order Optimization**: DMBD is entirely zeroth-order, making it suitable for non-differentiable cost functions, unlike gradient-based methods.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Characterization

The paper provides Proposition 1, which bounds the discrepancy between the centralized score function and the local conditional score function at each denoising step. This bound depends on the deviation of other robots' optimal trajectories from their current estimates and a Lipschitz constant (*H_k*) that quantifies the sensitivity of robot *k*'s denoiser to other robots' trajectories. When constraints are fully decoupled, *H_k* becomes zero, and DMBD effectively reduces to individual MBD instances. The authors acknowledge that this bound is for exact score functions, and finite sample approximations introduce additional errors.

#### 3.5. Simulation Setup

The methodology is evaluated using simulations with two robot dynamics models (double integrator and kinematic bicycle) and two robot geometries (circular and rectangular). Collision avoidance constraints are implemented for both inter-robot and robot-obstacle interactions. DMBD is compared against CEM, MPPI, MBD, and D4orm using metrics of success rate (collision-free, goal-reaching) and planning time. Scenarios include goal-swapping (circular and rectangular robots), multi-floor coverage (heterogeneous rectangular robots), parking, and rush-hour puzzles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation results reported in the paper demonstrate DMBD's performance across various multi-robot scenarios, particularly addressing its scalability and ability to achieve coordination with distributed knowledge.

#### 4.1. Q1. Scalability with Respect to the Number of Robots (N)

DMBD was compared against CEM, MPPI, MBD, and D4orm in goal-swapping and multi-floor coverage tasks, varying the number of robots (N).
- **Superior Performance**: DMBD consistently achieved the best performance in terms of both success rate and planning time as *N* increased.
- **Sub-second Runtimes**: DMBD successfully solved goal-swapping tasks, even for 20 robots, within sub-second planning times.
- **Baseline Limitations**: In contrast, baseline methods exhibited a rapid increase in planning time and a decrease in success rate with increasing *N*. For the goal-swapping scenario, these baselines failed at N=20 due to out-of-memory (OOM) errors, attributed to their need to sample from a high-dimensional joint trajectory space.
- **D4orm Comparison**: D4orm showed comparable performance to DMBD in the circular-robot goal-swapping scenario but required approximately 10 times longer planning time and also eventually failed due to OOM errors at N=20.
- **Complexity Impact**: The performance gap between DMBD and baselines widened as task complexity increased (e.g., from circular robots to rectangular robots and multi-floor coverage scenarios, which introduce more non-smoothness in collision constraints and dynamics).
- **Reason for Scalability**: This scalability advantage is attributed to DMBD's decomposition of the high-dimensional joint trajectory space (dimension * (Σ m_k)T*) into lower-dimensional per-robot trajectory spaces (dimension *m_k T*), which significantly improves sample efficiency.

#### 4.2. Q2. Coordination Without Global Knowledge

To evaluate DMBD's ability to achieve global coordination without centralized knowledge, two challenging scenarios were considered: Parking and Rush Hour. These tasks require robots to make strategic, sometimes locally suboptimal, decisions without explicit knowledge of other robots' goals, operating within highly non-convex and non-smooth environments.
- **High Success Rates**: DMBD achieved success rates exceeding 90% in both the Parking and Rush Hour tasks.
- **Efficient Planning**: These tasks were solved within sub-second planning times.
- **Emergent Coordination**: The results indicate that the distributed inference approach, where each robot plans using only local information conditioned on the aggregated state estimates of others, is sufficient to generate globally coordinated behaviors. This demonstrates that sharing global objectives or dynamics explicitly is not a prerequisite for effective multi-robot coordination with DMBD.

In summary, the main findings highlight DMBD's ability to scale effectively to a larger number of robots and to achieve complex coordination behaviors in challenging environments while operating under a distributed information paradigm, outperforming existing centralized and some distributed baseline methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of multi-robot systems by introducing Distributed Model-Based Diffusion (DMBD), a framework that addresses key limitations of existing trajectory optimization methods, particularly Model-Based Diffusion (MBD), in scalable multi-robot applications.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Enhanced Scalability for Multi-Robot Trajectory Optimization**: DMBD directly tackles the curse of dimensionality inherent in centralized trajectory optimization for large multi-robot teams. By decomposing the high-dimensional joint inference problem into lower-dimensional local problems, the method demonstrates superior scalability. This allows for planning in scenarios with a significantly higher number of robots than previously feasible with existing MBD or other sampling-based baselines, which often suffer from out-of-memory errors or prohibitive planning times. This can enable the deployment of larger and more complex robot swarms in real-world applications.

2. **Decentralized Operation with Reduced Information Requirements**: DMBD eliminates the need for a central entity to possess global knowledge of all robots' dynamics, objectives, and constraints. Each robot operates with its local information, only conditioning on aggregated state trajectory estimates from other robots, which are broadcast by a server. This design reduces communication overhead and the computational/memory burden on a central unit, fostering a more modular and robust system architecture. This is particularly relevant for heterogeneous robot teams or systems where privacy of individual robot objectives or capabilities is desired.

3. **Robustness in Complex Environments**: The ability of DMBD to operate efficiently and effectively in highly non-convex, non-linear, and non-differentiable environments is significant. These types of environments are common in real-world robotics, where collision avoidance, complex dynamics, and diverse task requirements introduce planning challenges that gradient-based methods often struggle with due to local minima. DMBD's sampling-based, zeroth-order nature makes it well-suited for such complexities.

4. **Emergent Coordinated Behavior**: The demonstration that DMBD can achieve complex coordinated behaviors (e.g., in Parking and Rush Hour scenarios) without explicit sharing of global objectives or complex coordination protocols among robots indicates a promising avenue for designing intelligent multi-robot systems. The coordination emerges from the iterative local conditional denoising process, simplifying the design of coordination algorithms.

5. **Theoretical Foundation for Distributed Diffusion Models**: The derivation of a theoretical bound on the discrepancy between global and local conditional score functions provides a formal understanding of the distributed approximation. This theoretical insight can guide future developments and analyses of distributed diffusion-based optimization methods.

In practical terms, DMBD has the potential to impact applications such as automated warehouses, delivery systems, environmental monitoring, and search-and-rescue operations, where large numbers of robots need to coordinate in dynamic and constrained environments. By offering a scalable, distributed, and efficient trajectory optimization solution, this work contributes to advancing the autonomy and deployment capabilities of multi-robot systems.
