# ViKiNG: Vision-Based Kilometer-Scale Navigation with Geographic Hints

> [!summary] Summary
> ViKiNG, developed by UC Berkeley, enables kilometer-scale robot navigation in unseen outdoor environments by integrating a learned vision-based local traversability model with an A*-like search algorithm guided by approximate geographic hints. The system successfully navigated up to 3 kilometers in real-world settings, achieving a 100% success rate and demonstrating robustness to inaccurate or outdated map information.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic navigation methods (geometric reconstruction or end-to-end learning) struggle with long-range, kilometer-scale navigation in novel, unstructured outdoor environments.
- Geometric methods are computationally intensive and require high-fidelity maps, while learning-based approaches often have high sample complexity and limited generalization beyond short distances (tens of meters).
- Existing hybrid graph-based planning methods typically perform uninformed search or require extensive prior mapping, lacking effective guidance for distant goals using external knowledge.

## Method

- **ViKiNG** integrates a deep neural network-based ==latent goal model== for local traversability estimation and immediate action prediction, which can also propose feasible subgoals.
- An A*-like physical search algorithm, **ViKiNG-A***, incrementally builds a ==topological graph== and uses a learned heuristic to guide navigation over kilometer-scale distances.
- The search heuristic is learned from "==macro-trajectories==" and incorporates approximate geographic hints (e.g., satellite images, roadmaps, noisy GPS) to estimate the favorability of distant waypoints.

## Results

- ViKiNG achieved **100%** success across various difficulty categories, navigating to goals up to **3 kilometers** away in unseen real-world environments, significantly outperforming baseline methods.
- The system demonstrated emergent behaviors such as intelligent path following, dynamic obstacle avoidance, and effective rerouting from dead ends, even with outdated or incorrect map information.
- Ablation studies confirmed that while approximate GPS provides some guidance, the learned overhead map heuristic is crucial for efficient long-range navigation, as uninformed search failed to reach distant goals.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining a learned local visual controller with a global planning heuristic derived from imperfect geographic information enables robust kilometer-scale navigation in novel environments.
> - The low-level, vision-based controller provides resilience to real-world dynamic obstacles and inaccuracies in high-level geographic hints by prioritizing immediate visual traversability.
> - Learning a goal-directed heuristic from composite "macro-trajectories" allows effective long-range planning, even when individual training trajectories are short.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic navigation systems have traditionally been approached through two primary paradigms: geometric reconstruction and planning, or end-to-end learning. Geometric methods involve constructing a 3D model of the environment and then planning a path within this model. While robust for known or accurately mapped environments, these methods can be computationally intensive and may not generalize well to novel or complex terrains where geometry alone is insufficient for traversability (e.g., distinguishing traversable tall grass from non-traversable mud).

Conversely, end-to-end learning-based approaches aim to directly map sensor observations to actions or traversability estimates, often bypassing explicit geometric reconstruction. These methods can learn complex patterns and affordances from data, potentially enabling navigation in challenging unstructured environments. However, a significant limitation of many learning-based methods, particularly those formulated as "PointGoal" tasks using simulation and reinforcement learning, is their high sample complexity and difficulty in scaling to long-range, kilometer-scale navigation in real-world, novel environments. They often struggle with global planning and reasoning over vast distances, typically performing well only over tens of meters.

A hybrid approach involves combining learned models with graph-based planning, often using topological representations. These methods may assume access to existing environmental graphs or require extensive data collection in the target environment. Recent work, such as RECON, has explored using learned representations of feasible subgoals within a search procedure to incrementally build a topological graph in novel environments for exploration. However, these methods typically perform uninformed search, lacking mechanisms to effectively guide the robot towards distant goals using external knowledge.

This work, ViKiNG, addresses the challenge of long-range navigation by integrating the strengths of both learning and planning. It proposes a system that utilizes a learned, image-based local traversability model in conjunction with a heuristic planner that incorporates approximate geographic hints (e.g., schematic roadmaps, satellite images, noisy GPS). This approach is motivated by human navigation, which often combines learned patterns with imperfect mental maps or external geographic cues, without requiring perfectly accurate geometric reconstruction. By using geographic information as a planning heuristic rather than a strict geometric model, ViKiNG aims to enable robust, kilometer-scale navigation in novel, real-world outdoor environments, extrapolating beyond the scales seen in training data and being resilient to inaccuracies in external information. This positions ViKiNG as an advancement in developing generalizable, vision-based navigation systems capable of operating autonomously over large distances in previously unseen outdoor settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic navigation system, named ViKiNG, capable of operating autonomously over kilometer-scale distances in previously unseen real-world environments. This system is designed to achieve a user-designated goal, specified by an egocentric image of the destination and an approximate GPS location.

The core motivations underpinning this objective are multifaceted:
1. **Bridging Local Perception and Global Reasoning:** Existing navigation paradigms often excel at either local traversability (learning-based methods) or global path planning (geometric methods), but rarely both effectively for long-range, real-world tasks. The authors identify a need to integrate these capabilities, allowing robots to leverage both detailed visual information for immediate obstacle avoidance and general geographic knowledge for overall direction.
2. **Utilizing Imperfect Geographic Knowledge:** Humans routinely navigate using imprecise "mental maps" or simplified paper maps, demonstrating that perfect geometric accuracy is not always necessary for effective long-range navigation. Current robotic systems, particularly those relying on Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), often demand high-fidelity maps or highly accurate GPS, which may not be available or reliable in all real-world scenarios. ViKiNG is motivated to exploit readily available but potentially noisy and unreliable geographic hints (such as schematic roadmaps or satellite imagery, and approximate GPS) as planning heuristics, without requiring them to be perfectly accurate or complete.
3. **Generalization to Novel Environments:** The goal is for the robot to navigate effectively in environments it has never encountered before. This necessitates learning general navigational affordances and common-sense patterns from a diverse dataset collected in *other* environments, rather than requiring re-training or extensive mapping in each new operational area.
4. **Scaling to Long Distances with Limited Training Data:** Traditional learning-based methods often struggle to generalize to path lengths significantly greater than those observed during training. A key motivation for ViKiNG is to overcome this limitation, enabling navigation over kilometers even when the training data only contains trajectories up to tens of meters.
5. **Exhibiting Complex Navigational Behaviors:** Beyond merely reaching a goal, the system is motivated to display intelligent behaviors such as probing potential paths, backtracking from dead ends, and implicitly following natural or artificial pathways (like roads or hiking trails), which are indicative of robust, adaptable navigation.
6. **Robustness to Real-World Conditions:** The system needs to be robust to common real-world challenges, including dynamic obstacles not present on maps, variations in lighting or season, and environments with dense canopies where satellite imagery provides limited information about ground-level traversability. The design ensures that the low-level controller primarily relies on egocentric image observations, making it resilient to such discrepancies.

In essence, ViKiNG seeks to equip robots with a navigation capability analogous to human explorers, combining learned visual intuition with approximate, high-level geographic guidance to achieve distant goals in previously unknown and complex outdoor settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ViKiNG's methodology integrates a learned low-level controller with a higher-level heuristic planning mechanism, leveraging geographic hints to achieve kilometer-scale navigation in novel environments. The overall architecture is depicted in Figure 3.

**A. Low-level Control with a Latent Goal Model:**
At its foundation, ViKiNG employs a deep neural network, referred to as the latent goal model, for local control and traversability estimation (Figure 4, left). This model takes two primary inputs: the robot's current egocentric camera observation (`o_t`) and a potential subgoal observation (`o_w`). From these inputs, it predicts three key outputs:
1. **Temporal Distance (`d_w_t`):** The estimated time required to reach the subgoal `w` from the current location `o_t`.
2. **First Action (`a_w_t`):** The optimal immediate action the robot should take to move towards `w`.
3. **Spatial Offset (`x_w_t`):** The approximate GPS coordinate offset between `o_t` and `w`.

Beyond predicting these values for given subgoals, the model is also designed to *propose* potential reachable subgoals. This is achieved by sampling latent representations (`z_w_t`) from a prior distribution, which are then used with `o_t` to predict `d_w_t`, `a_w_t`, and `x_w_t` for these candidate waypoints.

**Training the Latent Goal Model:**
The model is trained on a large dataset of robot trajectories collected in diverse environments. For each pair of time steps within a trajectory, the earlier image serves as `o_t`, and the later image as `o_w`. The number of time steps between them provides supervision for `d_w_t`, the first action taken for `a_w_t`, and the GPS difference for `x_w_t`. The training objective is a Variational Information Bottleneck (VIB) loss (Equation 1), which comprises:
- A term ensuring accurate prediction of `{a, d, x}_w_t`.
- A KL-divergence term regularizing the waypoint encoder (`p_φ`) to produce latent distributions close to a fixed prior (`r(z_w_t) ~ N(0, I)`). This regularization allows the model to sample valid latent waypoints from the prior during deployment, representing feasible locations without needing an explicit image `o_w`.
The latent representation `z_w_t` encodes relative information about the subgoal from the current context (`o_t`), promoting generalization to new environments.

**B. Informed Search on a Topological Graph (ViKiNG-A*):**
To navigate kilometer-scale distances beyond the range of the low-level model, ViKiNG employs an A*-like algorithm called ViKiNG-A* (Algorithm 1) for physical search. Unlike standard graph search algorithms, physical search involves the robot incrementally building a topological graph (`T`) by driving to selected subgoals, which incurs physical costs and updates the graph with new observations.

The algorithm maintains an "open set" ($\Omega$) of unexplored candidate waypoints. In each iteration, it selects the waypoint `w_t` with the lowest estimated total cost (`f(w)`), drives to it, observes the environment (`o_t`), adds `w_t` to the graph `T`, and samples new candidate waypoints using the latent goal model.

The cost function `f(w)` for a waypoint `w` in the open set is composed of four terms (Equation 14 in the paper, line 14 in Alg. 1):
1. `g(t, w)`: The accumulated cost (temporal distance) to reach the parent of `w` (which is part of the established graph `T`).
2. `d_w_Pr[w]`: The estimated temporal distance from `w`'s parent to `w` itself, predicted by the low-level model.
3. `h(w)`: A learned heuristic cost estimate of reaching the final goal from `w`, derived from geographic hints.
4. `v(Pr[w])`: A novelty bonus term (`C * N(Pr[w])`, where `N(Pr[w])` is the visitation count of the parent), encouraging exploration of novel states.

The `DriveTo` procedure navigates the robot to the selected waypoint `w` by planning the shortest path through the existing graph `T` and using the low-level model to execute actions until `w` is reached.

**C. Learning a Goal-Directed Heuristic for Search:**
The heuristic `h(w)` is crucial for guiding the A*-like search using geographic side information (`c_t`), which can be an overhead satellite image or a roadmap. This heuristic estimates the favorability of a candidate waypoint `w` for reaching the goal `G`.

ViKiNG learns this heuristic `h_over(x_w, x_G, x_t, c_t)` based on an estimator for `p_over(w -> G | x_w, x_G, x_t, c_t)`, representing the probability that `w` lies on a valid path to `G`. The heuristic `h_over` is then defined as `λ_over * (1 - p_over)`.

**Training the Heuristic Model:**
The `p_over` model (Figure 4, right) is a neural network that takes the overhead image `c_t` (centered at current location `x_t`) and the GPS coordinates of the current location (`x_t`), waypoint (`x_w`), and destination (`x_G`). It is trained using the InfoNCE objective (Equation 2), a contrastive learning method.
- **Positive Examples:** Pairs of locations (`x_w+`, `x_E`) within a "macro-trajectory" where `x_w+` is on a path from `x_S` to `x_E`.
- **Negative Examples:** Randomly sampled locations (`x_w-`) from other trajectories.
To enable kilometer-scale reasoning, overlapping short trajectories from the training dataset are combined into much longer "macro-trajectories" based on their GPS positions. This allows the heuristic to learn about long-horizon paths, even if individual training trajectories are short.

The entire system operates on a Clearpath Jackal UGV, utilizing its forward-facing RGB camera, approximate GPS, and queried overhead images. The offline training dataset comprises 30 hours of autonomously collected data and 12 hours of human-teleoperated data, ensuring diverse navigational behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative findings demonstrating ViKiNG's capabilities in kilometer-scale navigation within previously unseen environments.

**A. Kilometer-Scale Navigation and Emergent Behaviors:**
ViKiNG successfully navigated to goals up to 3 kilometers away in real-world environments (university campuses, city roads, nature parks) despite being trained only on trajectories not exceeding 80 meters. This demonstrates significant extrapolation capabilities.
Qualitative observations revealed several emergent complex behaviors:
- **Path Following:** The system exhibited preferences for following existing roads (with roadmap hints) or hiking trails (with satellite hints), a behavior learned from the teleoperated portion of the training dataset.
- **Obstacle Avoidance and Rerouting:** ViKiNG was observed to identify and plan around large obstacles (e.g., buildings) rather than pursuing a greedy straight-line path. When encountering dead ends or new obstacles (like a truck blocking a previously clear path), the robot demonstrated backtracking behavior and found alternative routes to the goal, highlighting its robustness.
- **Navigation in Challenging Terrains:** In environments like forests with dense canopies where satellite imagery provided limited utility, the local image-based controller enabled the robot to navigate successfully using only egocentric visual observations.
- **Sequential Goal Execution:** ViKiNG was able to follow a sequence of six checkpoints on a 2.73 km hiking trail with 70m elevation gain and perform visual inspection tasks across 2.65 km in a suburban environment, reusing its topological graph.

**B. Quantitative Evaluation and Comparisons:**
ViKiNG was quantitatively compared against four baseline methods: Behavioral Cloning (BC), Offline PPO, Generalized Computation Graphs (GCG), and RECON-H (a modified RECON with GPS and satellite inputs). Evaluations were conducted in 10 unseen environments across three distance categories: <50m (Easy), 50-150m (Medium), and 150-500m (Hard).

- **Success Rate (Table I):** ViKiNG achieved a 100% success rate across all difficulty categories (3/3 Easy, 4/4 Medium, 3/3 Hard), successfully reaching all goals. In contrast, RECON-H achieved 3/3, 3/4, 1/3; GCG achieved 3/3, 2/4, 0/3; and BC and Offline PPO performed poorly, particularly at longer ranges (0/3 for Hard category). This indicates ViKiNG's superior capability in navigating to distant goals.
- **Efficiency Metrics (Table II):** ViKiNG demonstrated high efficiency, with an average velocity of 1.36 m/s, comparable to the training data's average of 1.68 m/s. Its average displacement before disengagement was over 250m. RECON-H, while reaching some distant goals, exhibited a much lower average velocity (0.41 m/s), suggesting it took less efficient, more exploratory paths. BC and PPO frequently resulted in collisions or getting stuck, leading to low average displacements and velocities.
- **Trajectory Visualization (Fig. 8):** Visualizations showed ViKiNG following smooth, intelligent paths around obstacles (e.g., buildings), while RECON-H often got stuck or explored inefficiently. Other baselines frequently resulted in collisions.

**C. Role of Geographic Hints:**
Experiments specifically investigated the impact and robustness of geographic hints:
- **Type of Hints:** When trained with roadmap hints, ViKiNG preferred following marked roads. With satellite imagery, it often found more direct paths by cutting across traversable terrain like meadows (Figure 9), suggesting the heuristic model can extract richer information from detailed satellite images.
- **Outdated Hints:** ViKiNG demonstrated robustness to outdated maps. When a large truck (not present in the satellite image) blocked a path, the robot's local controller identified the obstacle and successfully rerouted without collision (Figure 10). This confirms that the low-level visual control maintains primacy over potentially inaccurate map information.
- **Incorrect Hints:** Even with GPS measurements offset by approximately 5km (leading to a completely incorrect overhead map), ViKiNG's learned heuristic still guided the robot along the features of the *incorrect* map. If these features corresponded to traversable open space, the robot could still reach the goal, albeit via a circuitous path (Figure 11). This highlights the system's reliance on learned heuristics, even if they are based on faulty global information, as long as local traversability is maintained.
- **Ablation Studies (Disoriented ViKiNG):**
 - **No Overhead Image (GPS only):** Using only a simple Euclidean distance heuristic from GPS, ViKiNG could still reach the destination but took significantly longer (2.6x slower) and explored dead ends before finding the goal (Figure 12). This shows that while GPS alone provides some guidance, the learned overhead map heuristic is vital for efficiency.
 - **No GPS (Uninformed Search):** Without any GPS or overhead map information, ViKiNG reverted to an uninformed search (akin to RECON). In this scenario, the robot was unable to reach the goal within 30 minutes, performing aimless exploration. This result critically underscores the necessity of geographic hints for kilometer-scale navigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of ViKiNG represents a significant advancement in robotic navigation, particularly for kilometer-scale operations in novel, unstructured outdoor environments. Its primary impact stems from its ability to effectively integrate learning-based visual perception for local traversability with approximate geographic knowledge for global path planning.

**Key areas of significance include:**

1. **Addressing Kilometer-Scale Navigation in Novel Environments:** ViKiNG tackles a long-standing challenge in robotics by enabling autonomous navigation over distances significantly exceeding typical learning-based system capabilities and training data scales. This is crucial for real-world applications where robots need to cover large areas without prior detailed mapping.
2. **Robustness to Imperfect Information:** A core contribution is the demonstrated robustness to various forms of unreliable geographic hints—outdated maps, incorrect GPS, and low-fidelity schematics. This capability is paramount for practical deployment in dynamic and uncertain outdoor settings where perfect data is often unavailable or difficult to maintain. By prioritizing the learned visual controller for immediate action, ViKiNG ensures safety and adaptability even when global guidance is flawed.
3. **Enhanced Generalization:** The system's ability to generalize to completely unseen environments and much longer trajectories than those it was trained on indicates a powerful learning paradigm. This suggests that robots can acquire transferable navigation skills from diverse, previously collected datasets, reducing the need for environment-specific training.
4. **Efficiency and Autonomy:** ViKiNG achieves complex, long-range navigation using only offline training data, obviating the need for extensive, often prohibitively expensive, online reinforcement learning or simulation in the target environment. Its comparable average velocity to training data demonstrates efficient execution during real-world deployment.
5. **Human-Inspired Navigation:** The approach aligns with human cognitive strategies for navigation, combining learned visual patterns with approximate global knowledge. This bio-inspired methodology offers a promising direction for developing more intuitive and adaptable robotic systems.

**Potential impacts of this work include:**

- **Field Robotics:** Enabling autonomous ground robots for tasks such as environmental monitoring, inspection of remote infrastructure, search and rescue operations in partially mapped areas, and long-range logistics in diverse outdoor terrains (e.g., agricultural fields, construction sites, wilderness areas).
- **Autonomous Exploration:** Providing a robust framework for robots to explore and map unknown environments more efficiently by intelligently biasing their search towards distant objectives.
- **Reduced Infrastructure Dependency:** By relying on approximate GPS and readily available overhead imagery (or even schematic maps), ViKiNG reduces the dependency on high-precision localization systems or pre-built high-definition maps, broadening the scope of deployable environments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays groundwork for incorporating other forms of imperfect semantic information, such as natural language instructions or symbolic world models, into similar learning-based navigation frameworks. Further exploration of how different types of geographic information influence learned behaviors could lead to more nuanced and context-aware navigation systems.

Overall, ViKiNG presents a robust, generalizable, and efficient solution to a critical problem in robotics, bringing autonomous navigation closer to real-world deployment in challenging, open-world environments.
