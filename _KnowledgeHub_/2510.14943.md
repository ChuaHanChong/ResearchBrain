# LaSeR: Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding

> [!summary] Summary
> The LaSeR framework enables Large Language Models to efficiently perform self-verification and enhance their reasoning capabilities by deriving a 'last-token self-rewarding score' directly from the final predicted token's probability distribution. This approach integrates self-assessment at nearly zero additional inference cost, improving reasoning performance while achieving high F1 scores for distinguishing correct from incorrect solutions.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) often struggle with complex reasoning tasks and can produce plausible but incorrect outputs, requiring robust verification.
- Existing Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods depend on ground-truth verifiers which are unavailable during real-world inference.
- Current self-verification approaches are computationally inefficient, either requiring separate external verifier models or sequential generation steps that significantly increase inference cost.

## Method

- **LaSeR** introduces a theoretical derivation that simplifies the RL objective for verification, linking the true verification reward to a 'last-token self-rewarding score' (r_s) from the policy model's final token probability distribution.
- It integrates an MSE loss to train the model to align its predicted r_s with true verifier-based rewards, adding this loss to the standard RLVR objective for joint optimization.
- The framework employs practical techniques like simplifying the reference log-probability to a pre-calculated constant and integrating both verifier-based and self-rewarding-based advantages for stable and efficient training.

## Results

- LaSeR-trained models consistently showed improved reasoning performance across various LLM architectures and mathematical benchmarks, with Open-Reasoner-Zero-**7B** increasing average accuracy from **45.0%** to **45.5%**.
- Models achieved high self-verification F1 scores (around **80%** on average in math tasks), outperforming or matching equally sized state-of-the-art external reward models in evaluating their own generated solutions.
- The optimized self-rewarding scores significantly enhance inference-time scaling, with weighted majority voting (RM@K) outperforming standard majority voting (Maj@K) for solution aggregation, improving overall accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A model's inherent assessment of its own solution can be effectively captured within the log-probability ratio of a specific 'last token' at the end of the generated sequence.
> - Self-verification can be achieved with minimal computational overhead (nearly zero additional inference cost) by leveraging this 'last-token self-rewarding score' during standard generation.
> - Jointly optimizing reasoning and self-rewarding capabilities through a unified loss function leads to improved overall reasoning performance and highly accurate internal self-assessment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has marked a significant advancement in AI, with capabilities spanning various domains from text generation to complex problem-solving. However, despite their successes, LLMs often struggle with intricate reasoning tasks, frequently producing plausible but incorrect outputs. This limitation has driven intense research into methods for enhancing LLM reasoning and ensuring the reliability of their outputs.

One of the most promising paradigms to emerge in this space is **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)**. Methods like OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1 have demonstrated RLVR's effectiveness in incentivizing LLMs to generate more deliberate reasoning chains by providing binary rewards based on the consistency between final outcomes and ground-truth answers. This approach helps mitigate issues like reward hacking, where models might learn superficial patterns rather than true reasoning.

However, a critical challenge for standard RLVR arises during test-time inference. In real-world scenarios, ground-truth answers are often unavailable, rendering the deterministic verifier useless. To address this, the existing research landscape has explored two main avenues:

1. **External Verifiers:** This involves training separate models—either scalar reward models (like Outcome-supervised Reward Models or Process-supervised Reward Models) or generative verifiers (LLMs that critique reasoning in natural language)—to evaluate solutions. While effective, these methods incur significant overhead, requiring additional training for the verifier model and separate inference steps during deployment, which adds to computational cost and reduces efficiency.
2. **Self-Verification:** This paradigm aims to unify the generator and verifier roles within a single policy model. The LLM is trained to both generate solutions and verify its own outputs. While conceptually appealing, current self-verification methods typically require the LLM to sequentially generate a solution and then a separate self-verification using distinct prompt templates. This sequential process essentially doubles the inference cost per sample, severely impacting efficiency, especially for applications demanding high throughput or low latency.

"LaSeR" directly addresses the inefficiency bottleneck inherent in existing self-verification approaches. Instead of separate generation steps for solution and verification, LaSeR seeks to embed the self-verification signal directly within the *final token's predicted probability distribution* during solution generation. This innovation fundamentally shifts the computational burden, moving from multiple inference passes to nearly zero additional cost, thereby advancing the practicality and scalability of LLM self-verification. By doing so, LaSeR positions itself as a critical step towards more autonomous, robust, and economically deployable LLM systems that can reason and validate their own outputs efficiently.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the LaSeR framework are rooted in addressing the practical limitations of current LLM reasoning and self-verification paradigms.

1. **Enhance LLM Reasoning Capability:** The overarching goal is to equip LLMs with stronger complex reasoning abilities, building upon the successes of RLVR.
2. **Enable Efficient Self-Verification at Test Time:** To allow LLMs to reliably assess the correctness of their own generated solutions even when ground-truth answers are unavailable during inference. This is crucial for real-world applications, agentic systems, and self-correction mechanisms.
3. **Overcome Inefficiency of Existing Self-Verification Methods:** Specifically, to address the computational burden of prior approaches that either require training and deploying external verifiers or demand sequential generation of solutions and verifications using multiple prompt templates, thereby significantly increasing inference cost.
4. **Jointly Optimize Reasoning and Self-Rewarding Capabilities:** To integrate the self-verification training seamlessly into the RLVR process, ensuring that the model simultaneously improves its ability to generate correct reasoning paths and accurately evaluate them.

**Motivation:**

The core motivation for LaSeR stems from the recognition that while RLVR has been transformative for LLM reasoning, its dependency on ground-truth verifiers limits its utility in real-world deployment. Current attempts to enable self-verification, whether through external verifiers or joint optimization within the LLM, introduce substantial inefficiency.

The authors motivate their work by highlighting two specific inefficiencies:
- **External verifiers:** Require "additional training on a separate LLM" and separate inference steps.
- **Joint optimization with sequential generation:** "Doubles the per-sample inference cost and reduces generation efficiency" by needing "two separate prompt templates."

LaSeR's motivation is to develop a "lightweight and highly effective algorithm" that achieves joint optimization of reasoning and self-verification "at nearly zero additional cost." This is driven by a fundamental insight: a model's inherent "assessment in its own solution can be captured in the last token’s predicted probability distribution." By theoretically formalizing this intuition and developing an efficient mechanism to leverage it, LaSeR aims to unlock the full potential of self-verification for LLMs, making it a viable and scalable solution for real-world reasoning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LaSeR (Reinforcement Learning with Last-Token Self-Rewarding) introduces a novel and efficient approach to jointly optimize the reasoning and self-verification capabilities of Large Language Models. Its methodology is built upon a theoretical insight that significantly simplifies the self-verification objective.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Foundation: Deriving the Last-Token Self-Rewarding Score

The core of LaSeR's methodology lies in a theoretical derivation that transforms the RL objective for verification into a remarkably simple form.

1. **RL Objective for Verification (Eq. 6):** The authors start by formalizing the problem of training a model to verify solutions. Given a problem `x` and a candidate solution `y` (generated by `π_g`), the model `π_θ` generates a verification `z`. The objective `V_π_θ` maximizes a verification reward `ˆr(x, y, z)` while regularizing with a KL divergence to a reference model `π_ref`. The reward `ˆr(x, y, z)` (Eq. 8) is 1 if `z` correctly identifies the true correctness of `y` (based on a ground-truth verifier `r_v(x, y)`), and 0 otherwise. For simplicity, `z` is chosen as a single token, `z_c` for correct and `z_i` for incorrect.
2. **Closed-Form Solution to RL Objective (Eq. 9):** Drawing from prior work on implicit rewards, the optimal solution to `V_π_θ` can be expressed as `ˆr(x, y, z) = β_v log[π_θ(z|x, y)/π_ref(z|x, y)] + β_v log Z(x, y)`, where `Z(x, y)` is a partition function.
3. **Simplification of the Partition Function `Z(x, y)` (Eq. 10):** A crucial insight is that `π_ref(z|x, y)` for specific verification tokens (`z_c`, `z_i`) is extremely small, especially after the generation of a complete solution (e.g., after an `<EOS>` token). Numerical analysis (Figure 5 in Appendix) validates this, showing `π_ref(z_c|x, y)` is often less than `e^-9` (and `e^-20` for unused special tokens). Because of these tiny probabilities, the partition function `Z(x, y)` approximately simplifies to 1, making `log Z(x, y) ≈ 0`.
4. **The Simplified Self-Rewarding Formula (Eq. 11, 12):** This simplification leads to the profound discovery that the true verification reward can be directly linked to the log-probability ratio: `ˆr(x, y, z) = β_v log[π_θ(z|x, y)/π_ref(z|x, y)]`. Specifically, for a correct solution, the true reasoning reward `r_v(x, y)` (which equals `ˆr(x, y, z_c)`) is equal to `β_v log[π_θ(z_c|x, y)/π_ref(z_c|x, y)]`. The authors term `r_s = β_v log[π_θ(z_c|x, y)/π_ref(z_c|x, y)]` as the **last-token self-rewarding score**. This score is derived from the policy model's predicted next-token probability distribution at the last token of the generated solution.

#### 3.2. Optimization Strategy: MSE Loss Integration

Instead of explicitly performing a separate RL optimization for self-verification, LaSeR directly trains the model to align its `last-token self-rewarding score` with the true verifier-based reasoning reward.

1. **MSE Loss (Eq. 13):** A Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss is introduced: `L = E[(r_s - r_v(x, y))^2]`. This loss encourages the model to generate `r_s` values that accurately reflect `r_v(x, y)`.
2. **Joint Optimization (Eq. 14):** This MSE loss is directly added to the standard RLVR objective (e.g., using PPO or GRPO's KL-regularized objective). This creates a unified loss function that simultaneously optimizes the LLM's reasoning capabilities and its self-rewarding (self-verification) ability. The combined objective is:
 `S_π_θ = max_π_θ E_x,y [r_v(x, y) - βD_KL(π_θ(y|x)||π_ref(y|x)) - α * (r_s - r_v(x, y))^2]`
 where `α` is a loss balancing coefficient.

#### 3.3. Practical Techniques for Efficiency and Stability

LaSeR incorporates several techniques to further enhance its efficiency and training stability:

1. **Simplification of Reference Log-Probability:** The term `log π_ref(z_c|x, y)` is observed to be nearly constant across various inputs and outputs (Figure 5). This allows it to be replaced by a pre-calculated constant `c_ref`, eliminating the need for forwarding the solution `y` through the reference model during training and inference. Furthermore, an *unused special token* is chosen as `z_c` to ensure `π_ref(z_c|x,y)` is extremely small, making `c_ref` more stable and minimizing its interference.
2. **Self-Rewarding Loss Re-Weighting (Eq. 15):** To counter the imbalance between correct and incorrect solutions in a batch, a class-level re-weighting strategy is applied to the MSE loss. This ensures balanced learning for self-verification on both positive and negative examples.
3. **Integration of Verifier-based and Self-Rewarding-based Advantages (Eq. 16):** During the RL phase, the advantage function `A_t` (which guides policy updates) is combined. A weighted average of advantages derived from the deterministic verifier (`r_v`) and the self-rewarding scores (`r_s`) is used. This allows `r_s` to provide finer-grained feedback and potentially mitigate misjudgments by rule-based verifiers, improving overall learning. A filtering strategy is applied to stabilize training if `r_s` variance is too low.
4. **Separate Warm-Up of Capabilities:** Training is structured with warm-up phases. When starting from base models, standard RLVR for reasoning is performed first. After this, a warm-up phase dedicated to the self-rewarding MSE loss is initiated, followed by the full integration of both advantages. This sequential approach helps stabilize training by gradually introducing the new self-rewarding objective.

#### 3.4. Inference-Time Operation

At inference time, after the policy model generates a solution, `r_s` is computed based on the next-token log-probability of the pre-specified token `z_c` at the final response token (e.g., `<EOS>`). This computation requires **only one additional token inference**, making it significantly more efficient than previous methods that require an entirely separate generation step for verification. The calculated `r_s` can then be used for self-verification (e.g., by comparing to a threshold) or for more robust answer aggregation methods like weighted majority voting.

By grounding its approach in theoretical reduction and bolstering it with practical optimizations, LaSeR offers a highly efficient and effective mechanism for infusing LLMs with robust self-verification capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted for LaSeR demonstrate its effectiveness in both improving LLM reasoning performance and equipping models with strong self-rewarding capabilities at minimal cost.

#### 4.1. Enhanced Reasoning Performance

- **Consistent Improvement:** Across various LLM architectures (LLaMA3.2 and Qwen2.5), including pre-trained, mid-trained, and reinforced variants, LaSeR consistently yields better reasoning performance compared to the GRPO baseline. For instance, OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base improved from 30.8% average accuracy (GRPO) to 32.8% (LaSeR); Qwen2.5-7B-Base from 41.8% to 42.7%; and Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B from 45.0% to 45.5%. These improvements, though sometimes marginal, are consistent across multiple benchmarks (MATH500, AMC23, AIME24/25, OlympiadBench).
- **Reasons for Improvement:** The authors attribute this to two factors:
 1. **Improved Confidence Calibration:** LaSeR encourages the model to encode its assessment of the solution in the final response token, leading to better internal confidence calibration, which positively impacts learning.
 2. **Finer-grained Advantage Estimation:** The integration of self-rewarding-based advantages (via `τ`) into the RL process provides more granular feedback than binary verifier rewards alone, facilitating more effective learning. The ablation study (Table 1, `-SWA` rows) shows that removing the self-rewarding advantage integration (`τ=0`) generally leads to slightly lower reasoning accuracy, confirming its contribution.

#### 4.2. Remarkable Self-Verification (Self-Rewarding) Capability

- **High F1 Scores:** A standout finding is the substantial enhancement of the model's self-verification (self-rewarding) capability. LaSeR-trained models achieve remarkably high F1 scores (around 80% on average) across diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks. For example, OctoThinker-3B-Short-Base saw its average F1 score jump from 51.0% (GRPO) to 72.5% (LaSeR); Qwen2.5-7B-Base from 49.2% to 79.6%; and Open-Reasoner-Zero-7B from 38.8% to 77.6%. This indicates that the models can accurately distinguish between their own correct and incorrect solutions.
- **Outperforming External Reward Models:** Crucially, LaSeR (as a self-rewarding mechanism) performs comparably to, and in some cases even *outperforms*, equally sized state-of-the-art external reward models (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B-PRM800K, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B) when evaluating its *own generated solutions* (Table 2). It even matches the performance of a much larger 72B reward model in certain scenarios. This highlights the efficiency gain, as LaSeR achieves this with minimal additional inference cost (one token inference).

#### 4.3. Effective Inference-Time Scaling

- **Improved Weighted Majority Voting:** The optimized self-rewarding scores prove highly effective in improving inference-time scaling performance. When multiple solutions are sampled for a problem, using the self-rewarding scores to perform *weighted majority voting* (RM@K) consistently outperforms standard *majority voting* (Maj@K) (Figure 2). This demonstrates the practical utility of LaSeR's self-rewarding capability in aggregating answers for higher overall accuracy, especially crucial in competitive tasks like math reasoning where multiple attempts are common.

#### 4.4. Impact of Reference Log-Probability Simplification

- **Efficiency Without Performance Loss:** The methodological decision to simplify `log π_ref(z_c|x, y)` to a constant `c_ref` (Table 3) is validated. Experiments show that this simplification has a negligible impact on both reasoning and self-verification performance. This confirms that significant computational cost can be saved without sacrificing accuracy, reinforcing LaSeR's claim of efficiency.

#### 4.5. Generalizability to General Reasoning Tasks

- **Comparable Reasoning, Useful Self-Rewarding:** LaSeR demonstrates generalizability beyond mathematical reasoning. On general reasoning benchmarks like MMLU-Pro and GPQA-Diamond, LaSeR-trained models maintain comparable average reasoning accuracy to baselines (Figure 3a). While the self-verification F1 scores are not as high as in math tasks (due to more overlap in score distributions for correct/incorrect solutions, Figures 3b, 3c), the optimized self-rewarding scores still provide useful signals.
- **Enhanced Weighted Majority Voting in General Domains:** Even with somewhat lower F1 scores, the self-rewarding scores still lead to better weighted majority voting results compared to unweighted majority voting in general reasoning tasks (Figure 3a), indicating that the signal, though noisier, remains valuable for inference-time aggregation.

Overall, the main findings strongly support LaSeR's efficacy as a lightweight, efficient, and dual-purpose algorithm that significantly enhances both the reasoning and self-verification capabilities of LLMs, with clear practical benefits for inference-time deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LaSeR framework presents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model research, particularly in the domain of complex reasoning and self-correction. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Theoretical Breakthrough in Self-Verification:** LaSeR's core contribution is its elegant theoretical derivation, which reduces the complex RL objective of self-verification to a remarkably simple "last-token self-rewarding score." This score, directly derived from the policy model's predicted next-token probability distribution at the end of a generated sequence, simplifies a previously computationally intensive problem into a highly efficient calculation. This theoretical foundation could inspire similar simplifications in other LLM self-assessment tasks.

2. **Unprecedented Efficiency for Self-Verification:** The most impactful aspect of LaSeR is its ability to perform self-verification with "nearly zero additional cost"—requiring at most one additional token inference, and potentially even zero by leveraging the `<EOS>` token position. This dramatically improves upon prior methods that either demanded separate external verifier models or sequential generation steps, effectively doubling inference costs. This efficiency makes real-time self-correction and confidence estimation for LLMs much more feasible in production environments.

3. **Dual Enhancement of LLM Capabilities:** LaSeR is unique in its ability to jointly optimize both the reasoning capability and the self-verification (self-rewarding) capability of an LLM. This leads to:
 - **Improved Reasoning Performance:** By fostering better confidence calibration and enabling more fine-grained advantage estimation during RL, LaSeR helps LLMs produce more accurate reasoning paths.
 - **High-Fidelity Self-Assessment:** The model gains a robust ability to assess the correctness of its own outputs, achieving high self-verification F1 scores that are competitive with, or even surpass, dedicated external reward models of similar size.

4. **Enabling Robust Inference-Time Operations:**
 - **Enhanced Answer Aggregation:** The accurate self-rewarding scores empower advanced inference-time techniques like weighted majority voting. As demonstrated, weighting solutions by their self-rewarding scores significantly boosts accuracy compared to simple majority voting, especially critical in complex tasks where multiple reasoning paths might be explored.
 - **Trust and Reliability:** Providing LLMs with internal mechanisms for self-assessment increases their trustworthiness. Users can rely on the model's own confidence scores, reducing the need for external human validation or separate verification systems in many scenarios.

5. **Paving the Way for More Autonomous AI Agents:** The ability for an LLM to efficiently evaluate its own outputs is a foundational step towards building more autonomous and intelligent AI agents. Such agents could:
 - **Self-Correct:** Identify and correct their own errors without external human feedback.
 - **Prioritize Tasks:** Allocate computational resources more effectively by identifying high-confidence solutions.
 - **Manage Uncertainty:** Understand when they are uncertain about an answer and seek clarification or further computation.

6. **Broader Applicability:** While initially validated on mathematical reasoning, the generalizability experiments suggest that LaSeR's principles can extend to broader reasoning domains. Further research into refining its performance in general reasoning could unlock even wider applications.

In conclusion, LaSeR represents a significant leap forward in making self-verification for LLMs both powerful and practical. By innovatively embedding self-assessment within the core generation process, it addresses a critical efficiency gap, pushing LLMs closer to truly autonomous and reliable intelligent systems.
