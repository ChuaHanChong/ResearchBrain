# ReIL: A Framework for Reinforced Intervention-based Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The ReIL framework from researchers at the Asian Institute of Technology and The University of Tokyo presents a method for training robot agents by integrating imitation learning and reinforcement learning, efficiently utilizing human interventions. It addresses the value overestimation bias in intervention-based methods and uses a multi-task model, demonstrating stable performance in real-world mobile robot navigation without performance degradation over time.

## Problem

- Traditional imitation learning often requires extensive supervised data or unintuitive feedback, and many systems further rely on task-specific feature engineering or bespoke architectures - a bottleneck for non-expert training and general applicability.
- Existing intervention-based imitation learning methods can suffer from value overestimation bias, which might incentivize agents to purposefully induce supervisor interventions.
- Reinforcement learning typically demands vast amounts of agent-generated trial-and-error exploration, which is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and potentially unsafe for physical robots.

## Method

- Developed **ReIL**, an intervention-based learning algorithm that reformulates the problem into a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with a custom reward function inherently penalizing supervisor interventions.
- Integrated TD3 (Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient) with a modified ==Behavior Cloning== objective, differentially weighting supervisor actions more heavily for better sample efficiency and stable learning.
- Introduced MimeticSNAIL, a multi-task imitation learning model based on SNAIL, augmented with an intervention flag, ALiBi for temporal awareness, and a learned task-termination flag for generalizability across tasks.

## Results

- In a simulated CartPole environment, ReIL successfully completed 20 out of 20 runs, averaging **270.7** supervised steps, outperforming IARL (16/20 runs, **411.27** steps) and an RL-only baseline (3/4 runs, **978.667** steps).
- In real-world mobile robot navigation, ReIL maintained stable performance over time without the deterioration or increased oscillatory behavior observed in Behavior Cloning-only methods.
- ReIL demonstrated superior offline training performance for obstacle avoidance, achieving lower action error (**0.247** vs. **0.307**), fewer supervised steps (**0.27** vs. **1.00**), and smoother trajectories (angular acceleration **0.056** vs. **0.158**) compared to HG-DAgger.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Value overestimation bias in intervention-based reinforcement learning can be effectively mitigated by designing a reward function that assigns a constant negative reward upon intervention, specifically lower than any possible task reward, thus intrinsically penalizing such events.
> - Combining off-policy reinforcement learning (TD3) with behavior cloning, while carefully weighting expert demonstrations and agent-generated data, significantly enhances sample efficiency and learning stability in intervention-based scenarios.
> - A general, memory-aware model architecture, like an adapted SNAIL, can process diverse temporal inputs (demonstrations, past experiences, current observations) to reduce task-specific engineering and enable multi-task robot policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of robotic learning, specifically focusing on imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) techniques for training robotic agents. A primary goal in this area is to enable non-expert users to train robots for various tasks, thereby facilitating wider adoption of robotic technologies.

Traditional IL methods, such as DAgger and DART, often require substantial amounts of supervised training data or employ data collection approaches that can be unintuitive for human supervisors. This creates a bottleneck for practical application. Intervention-based Imitation Learning (IIL) emerged as a more natural paradigm, where a human supervisor intervenes only when the agent deviates towards an undesirable state, providing corrections. This approach reduces supervisor burden over time and naturally generates agent-generated data, which can be leveraged for further policy optimization.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) excels at optimizing agents based on agent-generated data and has achieved successes in complex domains. However, a significant drawback of RL for real-world robotic applications is its requirement for extensive agent-generated trial-and-error exploration, which can be time-consuming, resource-intensive, and potentially unsafe in physical environments. Furthermore, many RL tasks and robotic systems historically demanded task-specific feature engineering or different model architectures, posing challenges for non-expert users.

Existing IIL algorithms address some of these limitations but introduce others. For instance, some methods, like "Learning to Drive in a Day" (L2D), primarily utilize RL on agent-generated data, while others, like HG-DAgger and IWR, rely predominantly on supervised learning from supervisor corrections. Methods solely relying on supervised learning might require pre-training and often struggle with performance deterioration as the agent's actions produce more data, as they do not explicitly learn to avoid actions leading to interventions. Conversely, RL-only approaches can be sample-inefficient.

A critical issue identified in some prior intervention-based RL approaches, such as EGPO and IARL, is the potential for value overestimation bias. This can occur if the value function incorrectly estimates the future reward of actions that lead to supervisor interventions, potentially incentivizing the agent to "cheat" by purposefully entering states that elicit corrections. IARL addresses this by introducing a heuristic to differentiate rewards for supervisor and agent actions, while EGPO uses constrained optimization, adding complexity. Other methods, like EIL, avoid value estimators entirely, transferring the responsibility of action quality assessment to the user through manual hyperparameters.

The proposed ReIL framework positions itself by attempting to combine the strengths of both IL and RL. It aims to efficiently utilize both supervisor-generated demonstrations and agent-generated data, resolve the value overestimation bias without resorting to additional heuristics, and introduce a general multi-task model to minimize task-specific engineering. This situates ReIL as an advancement seeking to bridge the gap between theoretical learning paradigms and practical, user-friendly robot training in real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that facilitates the training of real-world robot agents by non-expert users with minimal supervision and fine-tuning, across multiple tasks. This overarching goal is underpinned by several specific motivations and technical objectives:

- **Enhance Sample Efficiency and Reduce Supervisor Burden:** A significant motivation is to improve the efficiency of the data collection and training process compared to traditional IL methods. By adopting an intervention-based approach, the framework aims to reduce the continuous supervision required, allowing the human operator to intervene only when necessary. Furthermore, the ability to train offline is crucial, as real-world robot training is often labor-intensive and expensive.
- **Integrate Imitation Learning and Reinforcement Learning:** The authors seek to combine the advantages of imitation learning (specifically, Behavior Cloning) and reinforcement learning (specifically, TD3). This integration is motivated by the understanding that IL excels at learning from expert demonstrations, while RL is adept at optimizing policies based on trial-and-error experiences. The goal is to leverage both supervisor-generated corrections and agent-generated data concurrently for robust learning.
- **Address Value Overestimation Bias in Intervention-Based RL:** A core technical motivation is to mitigate the "cheating" problem observed in some existing IIL methods where agents might learn to induce interventions due to an overestimation of value for actions leading to supervisor corrections. The framework aims to design a reward function and value estimation strategy that naturally discourages such behavior without relying on ad-hoc heuristics or complex constrained optimization, unlike approaches like IARL and EGPO.
- **Optimize Task Performance While Minimizing Interventions:** The framework aims to enable agents to perform arbitrary tasks optimally, as defined by a task-specific reward function, while simultaneously ensuring that the agent minimizes the need for supervisor interventions. This dual objective is crucial for an effective and autonomous agent.
- **Develop a General, Multi-Task Policy Model:** To reduce the need for task-specific feature engineering and allow for broader applicability, a key objective is to develop a policy model capable of handling diverse tasks. This model is designed to process demonstrations, past experience, and current observations simultaneously. Such a model would be beneficial for memory-dependent tasks, allow the agent to learn from historical corrections, and potentially generalize similarities across different tasks, enabling a single model to be trained for multiple objectives.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ReIL framework consists of two main components: a general intervention-based learning algorithm and a multi-task imitation learning model.

**A. Intervention-based Imitation Learning Algorithm**

The algorithm is designed to address the IIL problem by reformulating it into a Markov Decision Process (MDP) that can be solved using reinforcement learning techniques, while also incorporating aspects of imitation learning.

1. **MDP Formulation and Reward Design:**
 - The environment is defined by (S, A, P, R_task, π_s), where R_task is the task-specific reward and π_s is the supervisor's policy. An IIL trajectory includes (s_t, a_t, f_demo_t), where f_demo_t indicates if the action was supervisor-generated (1) or agent-generated (0).
 - To prevent value overestimation bias, the algorithm defines a surrogate IIL objective function: the expected cumulative discounted future reward is calculated only up to the point of supervisor intervention or task termination. This ensures that actions leading to interventions are not overvalued.
 - A custom reward function R is introduced: R(s_t, a_t) = (1 - Ω_int_t) * R_task(s_t, a_t) + Ω_int_t * r_int. Here, Ω_int_t is a binary flag indicating supervisor intervention. `r_int` is a constant negative reward applied upon intervention, explicitly set to be lower than the minimum possible discounted R_task reward. This design intrinsically penalizes interventions, encouraging the agent to avoid them.
 - The authors claim this MDP formulation is equivalent to optimizing the original IIL objective for any sub-trajectory not leading to intervention, making it compatible with existing RL algorithms for maximizing R_task.

2. **Algorithm Integration (TD3 + BC):**
 - The MDP is solved using TD3 (Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient), an off-policy RL algorithm suitable for continuous action spaces.
 - To enhance sample efficiency and incorporate expert knowledge, Behavior Cloning (BC) is integrated with TD3. The TD3 actor's objective function is modified: J(θ) = α * Q_φ(s, π_θ(s)) - (f_demo + β * (1 - f_demo)) * ||π_θ(s) - a||².
 - The first term is the standard TD3 objective.
 - The second term is the BC objective, which minimizes the squared difference between the agent's action and the observed action.
 - `α` is a hyperparameter balancing the RL and BC objectives.
 - `f_demo` weights supervisor actions (f_demo=1) more heavily for BC.
 - `β` (where β < 1) applies a reduced weight to BC for agent-generated actions (f_demo=0). This prevents early training hindrance from imitating potentially poor initial agent actions with full fidelity. This modification is critical for IIL, distinguishing it from standard offline RL methods like TD3+BC.

**B. Multi-Task Imitation Learning Model (MimeticSNAIL)**

To ensure high sample efficiency and generality, the framework employs a specialized model architecture capable of processing diverse temporal inputs.

1. **SNAIL Adaptation:** The model, named MimeticSNAIL, adapts SNAIL (Simple Neural Attentive Meta-Learner). SNAIL combines convolutional neural networks and attention mechanisms with dense connections, making it effective for memory-dependent tasks and processing temporal data.
2. **Architectural Modifications:**
 - The previous `f_demo_t-1` flag is appended to the latent vector fed into SNAIL, allowing the model to explicitly condition on whether the previous action was a supervisor intervention.
 - The attention mechanism is augmented with ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases). This introduces a recency bias and increases the relative attention given to demonstration time frames in proximity to the currently queried time frame, improving temporal awareness.
3. **Task Termination Flag:** The actor model includes a task-termination flag, `f_tf_θ(s)`. This flag can be learned by imitating the supervisor's task termination signal (`f_s`) during training, allowing for the automatic sequencing of shorter sub-tasks to achieve arbitrarily long tasks. An additional objective function `J_tf(θ)` is added for this purpose.
4. **Real-world Implementation Details:** For visual navigation tasks, two MimeticSNAIL models (one for actor, one for critic) are used. These process 40x30 RGB images through convolutional layers, fully-connected layers, and then the SNAIL-specific TCBlock and AttentionBlock, before outputting actions or Q-values.

**C. Experimental Setup**

Experiments were conducted in two environments:
- **Simulated CartPole-V1:** A continuous action space version of OpenAI's CartPole-V1, using a simple R_task=1 and r_int=1. A pre-trained TD3 expert simulated the supervisor. Comparisons were made between ReIL, IARL (modified with TD3), HG-DAgger, and ReIL (Only RL, essentially L2D). Metrics included success rate and total supervised steps.
- **Real-World Mobile Robot Navigation:** A ROS2-enabled mobile robot with a front-facing monocular camera (40x30 RGB images). Tasks included obstacle avoidance (OA) and target hitting (TH). A universal reward function R_task=1 (except at task completion) and r_int=0 was used. Algorithms compared were ReIL, ReIL (Only BC), ReIL (Pre-trained with BC), and HG-DAgger. Experiments included online and offline training modes. Metrics involved average episode length, average absolute angular acceleration (smoothness), number of supervised steps, and action error (normalized RMSE between agent and supervisor actions).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides insights into ReIL's performance compared to other IIL and RL methodologies across both simulated and real-world environments.

**Simulated Cartpole Environment:**
- ReIL successfully completed 20 out of 20 runs, requiring an average of 270.7 supervised steps.
- HG-DAgger also achieved 20 out of 20 successful runs with a comparable number of supervised steps (251.6).
- IARL achieved success in 16 out of 20 runs, requiring a higher average of 411.27 supervised steps for successful runs.
- ReIL (Only RL), which functionally represents L2D in this context, was successful in 3 out of 4 runs, but demanded a substantially larger amount of supervisor corrections, averaging 978.667 supervised steps.
- These results suggest that, in a simple simulated environment, both BC-augmented methods (ReIL and HG-DAgger) demonstrated higher success rates and lower supervisory burden compared to RL-only or IARL, with ReIL performing comparably to HG-DAgger.

**Real-World Mobile Robot Navigation (Obstacle Avoidance and Target Hitting):**
- **Performance of BC-only Methods:**
 - ReIL (Only BC) showed a trend of increasing episode length over time in the obstacle avoidance task, indicating a deterioration in performance. This was attributed to the imitation of sub-optimal agent-generated data when solely relying on Behavior Cloning.
 - HG-DAgger exhibited an increase in trajectory oscillations as the number of supervisor corrections grew. This behavior is consistent with HG-DAgger's mechanism of learning corrections from undesirable states without explicitly learning to avoid entering such states.
- **Performance of ReIL:**
 - In contrast to the BC-only methods, ReIL did not exhibit a deterioration in performance over time or increased oscillatory behavior in the real-world navigation tasks. This stability is attributed to ReIL's critic component, which evaluates the relative quality of both agent-generated and supervisor-generated data based on the defined reward function.
 - **Offline Training:** ReIL demonstrated superior performance in the offline training setting for obstacle avoidance. It achieved a lower action error (0.247 vs. 0.307), required fewer supervised steps (0.27 vs. 1.00), and produced smoother trajectories (angular acceleration 0.056 vs. 0.158) compared to HG-DAgger when both were trained on the same dataset. This finding supports the viability and advantages of offline training with ReIL.
 - **Online Training:** In online training for both obstacle avoidance and target hitting, ReIL performed comparably to or slightly better than other algorithms in terms of action error and supervised steps. ReIL (Pre-trained) and HG-DAgger showed marginally better initial angular acceleration in some online scenarios.
- **General Model Applicability:** The MimeticSNAIL model, utilized for visual navigation, functioned without requiring specific manual fine-tuning for different tasks, supporting its intended capability for multi-task learning.

Overall, the results indicate that while methods relying primarily on Behavior Cloning can be effective in simple or early training stages, they tend to suffer from performance degradation and instability in more complex, real-world scenarios. ReIL, by integrating reinforcement learning's evaluative capacity with imitation learning's data efficiency, maintains performance and robustness over time, particularly highlighting its benefits in offline training environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ReIL framework offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic learning and its practical applications:

- **Enhanced Performance and Trainability:** ReIL's combination of imitation learning and reinforcement learning demonstrates improved agent performance and trainability in real-world navigation tasks compared to methods relying solely on one paradigm. This suggests a more robust and effective learning process for robotic agents.
- **Increased Sample Efficiency:** By effectively utilizing both supervisor-generated data (corrections/demonstrations) and agent-generated data, and enabling offline training, ReIL addresses a critical challenge in real-world robotics: the high cost and labor intensity of data collection. This efficiency can accelerate the development and deployment of robotic systems.
- **Resolution of Value Overestimation Bias without Heuristics:** The framework provides a principled approach to mitigate the value overestimation bias that can lead to "cheating" behavior in some intervention-based RL methods. By designing a reward function and value estimation that inherently penalizes interventions without ad-hoc heuristics or constrained optimization, ReIL simplifies the training process and enhances the reliability of the learned policies.
- **Generalizability and Reduced Feature Engineering:** The development of the MimeticSNAIL model, capable of processing diverse temporal information (demonstrations, past experience, current observations), contributes to the creation of more general-purpose robot policies. This can reduce the need for task-specific feature engineering, making it easier for non-expert users to train robots for a variety of tasks with a single model.
- **Facilitating Widespread Robot Adoption:** By making robot training more intuitive, sample-efficient, and robust, ReIL has the potential to lower the barrier for non-expert users to train and deploy robotic systems. This could lead to a broader adoption of robotics in various settings, including homes and workplaces, for tasks that currently require significant human expertise or intervention.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work lays a foundation for future advancements, such as using ReIL's value estimator to predict and proactively propose supervisor interventions. This could further optimize the training process by guiding the agent away from mistakes even before they occur, potentially reducing the need for extensive environmental exploration.

In summary, ReIL represents a step towards making robot training more practical and accessible, addressing key limitations of existing IL and RL methodologies, and thereby contributing to the broader integration of robotics into everyday environments.
