# Distribution-Agnostic Robust Trajectory Optimization via Chance-Constrained Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework named Distribution-Agnostic Robust Trajectory Optimization via Chance-Constrained Reinforcement Learning (RCCRL) was developed to robustify spacecraft trajectories against various uncertainties by combining deterministic optimal control with reinforcement learning. The system demonstrated a 5.50% reduction in upper-tail fuel cost and significantly tighter terminal dispersion in an Earth-Mars transfer, while successfully adapting to different uncertainty distributions and maintaining sub-meter accuracy in a pinpoint rocket landing scenario.

## Problem

- Spacecraft trajectory design faces challenges from uncertainties like navigation errors and unmodeled perturbations, leading to mission compromises or failures.
- Traditional robust optimization methods rely on restrictive assumptions (e.g., Gaussian uncertainty, linear dynamics) that are inadequate for complex, non-Gaussian, or bounded uncertainties.
- Standard reinforcement learning (RL) optimizes expected return rather than probabilistic feasibility and often yields unstructured policies difficult to integrate into safety-critical aerospace systems.

## Method

- A Robust Chance-Constrained ==Reinforcement Learning== (RCCRL) framework was proposed, integrating an offline-computed deterministic nominal trajectory with an RL-learned affine robustification layer.
- The RL policy outputs a structured control law consisting of feedforward corrections and time-varying linear feedback gains to adapt the nominal path and reshape uncertainty ensembles.
- Probabilistic feasibility and terminal dispersion are enforced empirically using Monte Carlo rollouts, upper-tail quantile evaluations, and ensemble-level covariance penalties, avoiding analytical reformulations of chance constraints.

## Results

- In an Earth-Mars transfer, RCCRL achieved a **5.50%** reduction in 95th-percentile total impulse cost and 42-**78%** tighter terminal dispersion compared to a robust optimal control benchmark under Gaussian uncertainty, maintaining **100%** mission success probability.
- The framework adapted effectively to bounded uniform uncertainties, showing a modest **3.3%** increase in cost from its Gaussian performance while still outperforming the benchmark.
- For an atmospheric pinpoint rocket landing, RCCRL demonstrated less than **1%** fuel overhead relative to the deterministic baseline and maintained sub-meter terminal accuracy under both Gaussian and bounded uniform uncertainties, showcasing portability across different problem types.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling nominal trajectory design from robust augmentation allows leveraging established optimal control methods while using RL specifically for handling uncertainties in a structured manner.
> - A structured affine closed-loop control policy provides both interpretability for aerospace applications and the ability to systematically adjust the trajectory and contract uncertainty spreads.
> - Empirical, sample-based evaluation of probabilistic constraints enables the framework to handle distribution-agnostic uncertainties and highly nonlinear dynamics without restrictive analytical approximations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Spacecraft trajectory design is fundamentally impacted by various uncertainties, including navigation errors, control execution inaccuracies, unmodeled perturbations, and atmospheric variability. These factors can lead to deviations from nominal paths, potentially causing fuel overruns, reduced terminal accuracy, or mission failure. Traditional approaches to managing these uncertainties include appending conservative margins or late-stage feedback tuning, which are often inefficient or insufficient for missions with stringent requirements and tight fuel budgets, such as interplanetary rendezvous, pinpoint landings, and proximity operations.

Stochastic optimal control offers a framework for trajectory design under uncertainty by optimizing performance over a distribution of possible system evolutions. Within this domain, chance-constrained formulations enable the probabilistic enforcement of path and terminal requirements, allowing a quantified trade-off between conservatism and risk. Related covariance steering and control methods aim to regulate the dispersion of the state distribution, which is beneficial for missions requiring precise terminal accuracy. While powerful, classical robust optimization techniques often rely on restrictive assumptions such as Gaussian uncertainty models, linear or locally linear covariance propagation, and analytical reformulations of probabilistic constraints into deterministic surrogates. These assumptions can become inadequate for bounded or non-Gaussian uncertainties, significant nonlinear dispersion growth, or constraint metrics that are difficult to reformulate analytically, potentially leading to overly conservative or brittle approximations.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) presents an alternative approach by learning control policies directly from interactions with stochastic simulation environments. This flexibility allows RL to accommodate nonlinear dynamics, non-convex constraint geometries, and simulation-based uncertainty models without requiring closed-form problem reformulations. However, standard RL typically optimizes for expected return rather than probabilistic feasibility. Furthermore, unstructured end-to-end RL policies can be challenging to interpret, inefficient to train, and often misaligned with the structured optimization architectures preferred in safety-critical aerospace systems.

This research aims to bridge the gap between classical robust optimization and reinforcement learning. It proposes a framework that leverages the strengths of both by integrating a deterministic optimal-control backbone with sample-based probabilistic evaluation within an RL paradigm. The objective is to provide a robustification methodology that can handle a broader range of uncertainty models and nonlinearities than classical methods, while maintaining a structured and interpretable control architecture that is more aligned with aerospace engineering practices than typical end-to-end RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work is to address the limitations inherent in existing methods for spacecraft trajectory optimization under uncertainty, specifically targeting scenarios where both traditional robust control and standard reinforcement learning fall short. The key objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Develop a Distribution-Agnostic Robust Trajectory Optimization Framework:** The paper aims to create a framework that can handle various types of uncertainty (e.g., Gaussian, bounded uniform) without requiring them to conform to a specific closed-form distribution amenable to analytical reformulation. The only requirement for the uncertainty model is that it must be capable of being sampled in a simulation environment. This is the definition of "distribution-agnostic" in this context.

2. **Integrate Deterministic Optimal Control with Reinforcement Learning for Robustification:** The objective is not to replace established deterministic trajectory optimization (TO) tools with an unconstrained learned controller. Instead, the framework seeks to compute a deterministic nominal trajectory offline using conventional methods (e.g., Sequential Convex Programming, SCP) and then use RL specifically to *robustify* this baseline solution against uncertainties.

3. **Propose a Structured Affine Closed-Loop Correction Law:** To maintain interpretability and compatibility with safety-critical aerospace systems, the RL policy is designed to output a structured control adjustment. This includes a feedforward control correction (`Δu_k`) and time-varying linear feedback gains (`K_k`). This affine structure allows the controller to explicitly shape robustness by adjusting the nominal path and contracting or reshaping the uncertainty ensemble around it.

4. **Enforce Probabilistic Feasibility Empirically:** The framework aims to move away from analytical reformulations of chance constraints, which can be restrictive. Instead, probabilistic feasibility is enforced empirically through Monte Carlo (MC) rollout-based upper-tail quantile evaluations of mission-relevant metrics. This approach allows for accommodating nonlinear dynamics and non-Gaussian uncertainty models directly through simulation.

5. **Regulate Terminal Dispersion via Covariance-Feasibility Penalties:** In addition to chance constraints on path and terminal conditions, the framework intends to regulate the overall spread of the terminal state distribution by imposing penalties based on ensemble-level terminal covariance statistics. This helps ensure that the vehicle not only reaches its target probabilistically but also does so with a desired terminal accuracy.

6. **Demonstrate Portability Across Heterogeneous Trajectory Planning Problems:** A crucial objective is to validate that the core robustification architecture is not problem-specific. The framework is motivated by the need for a general scaffold that can be applied to materially different spacecraft trajectory design problems without requiring a redesign of its fundamental stochastic-control structure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Robust Chance-Constrained Reinforcement Learning (RCCRL), operates on three core design principles to achieve distribution-agnostic robust trajectory optimization.

#### 3.1. Finite-Horizon Stochastic Problem Setting

The problem is formulated as a discrete-time, finite-horizon stochastic control problem over `N` decision nodes. The system state `x_k` and control `u_k` are considered, with uncertainty originating from sampled initial conditions (`x^(i)_0 ∼ D_0`) and, optionally, sampled process disturbances (`w^(i)_k ∼ D_w,k`). The framework's "distribution-agnostic" nature stems from the sole requirement that these uncertainty models `D_0` and `D_w,k` can be sampled.
For an ensemble of `N_s` samples, the dynamics are propagated as `x^(i)_k+1 = f_k(x^(i)_k, u^(i)_k, w^(i)_k)`. Empirical mean `ˆx_k` and covariance `ˆP_k` are computed from the propagated ensemble at each node, serving as both policy observations and statistics for terminal dispersion assessment.
The conceptual problem minimizes an expected cost subject to dynamics, uncertainty, chance constraints `P(g_ℓ({x_k}, {u_k}) ≤ 0) ≥ 1 − β_ℓ`, and deterministic ensemble-level constraints `h_m(ˆx_N, ˆP_N) ≤ 0`. RCCRL does not solve this via analytical stochastic programming but through a sample-based, penalized objective.

#### 3.2. Decoupled Nominal Backbone and Affine Robustification

A central feature of RCCRL is the separation of nominal trajectory design from robust augmentation.
1. **Nominal Trajectory Generation**: A deterministic reference trajectory, `{x_nom_k, u_nom_k}`, is first computed offline using a problem-appropriate nominal design method (e.g., Sequential Convex Programming, SCP). This forms the "nominal backbone."
2. **RL Policy Output**: At each node `k`, the RL policy outputs an action `a_k = [Δu_k^T, vec(K_k)^T]^T`.
 - `Δu_k`: A feedforward correction vector to the nominal control `u_nom_k`. The corrected nominal feedforward control is `¯u_k = u_nom_k + Δu_k`.
 - `K_k`: A time-varying linear feedback gain matrix `n_u x n_x`.
 - `¯x_k`: A policy-adjusted reference trajectory is propagated using `¯u_k` under disturbance-free dynamics.
3. **Applied Control**: For each sample `i` in the ensemble, the control applied is `u^(i)_k = ¯u_k + K_k (x^(i)_k - ¯x_k)`. This affine structure provides an interpretable and structured feedback parameterization, allowing the policy to introduce systematic corrections (`Δu_k`) and reshape/contract the uncertainty ensemble (`K_k`). The learned controller acts as a robustification layer, not a replacement for the nominal trajectory design.

#### 3.3. Empirical Approximation of Chance Constraints

RCCRL avoids closed-form analytical reformulations of probabilistic constraints. Instead, it uses empirical methods:
1. **Sample-Wise Constraint Metrics**: For each probabilistic constraint, a scalar sample-wise metric `z^(i)_ℓ` is computed from the `i`-th trajectory realization.
2. **Quantile-Based Penalties**: The values `{z^(i)_ℓ}` are sorted, and the empirical `(1 − β_ℓ)`-quantile `ˆq_ℓ(1 − β_ℓ)` is calculated. The chance constraint is then approximated by the empirical condition `ˆq_ℓ(1 − β_ℓ) ≤ 0`. This approach allows accommodating non-Gaussian or bounded uncertainty directly through simulation. The trade-off is that guarantees are empirical, relying on simulation fidelity and sample size.
3. **Covariance-Feasibility Conditions**: Ensemble-level terminal dispersion is enforced through deterministic constraints `c_m = h_m(ˆx_N, ˆP_N) ≤ 0` applied directly to the empirical terminal mean and covariance.

#### 3.4. Observation and Action Parameterization

1. **Observation (`o_k`)**: The policy observes a compact summary at each node: `o_k = [ˆx_k^T, vec_up(ˆP_k)^T, u_nom_k^T, τ_k]^T`, where `ˆx_k` and `ˆP_k` are empirical moments, `u_nom_k` is the nominal control, and `τ_k` is normalized time-to-go. The policy does not observe individual MC samples directly, keeping input dimension fixed.
2. **Action (`a_k`)**: As described, it's `[Δu_k^T, vec(K_k)^T]^T`. The policy learns both feedforward corrections and feedback gains.

#### 3.5. Penalized RL Objective and Training Workflow

The RL policy `π_θ(a_k | o_k)` is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a stochastic actor-critic algorithm suitable for continuous action spaces and episodic, penalty-based optimization.
1. **Reward Function**: The per-episode return `R` is defined as the negative of a total penalized cost `J_tot = J_perf + Φ_cc + Φ_ens`.
 - `J_perf`: Aggregated mission objectives (e.g., fuel expenditure).
 - `Φ_cc`: Penalties for chance constraint violations based on empirical quantiles, `∑ λ_ℓ [ˆq_ℓ(1 − β_ℓ)]_+`.
 - `Φ_ens`: Penalties for deterministic ensemble-level constraint violations (e.g., covariance bounds), `∑ η_m [c_m]_+`.
2. **Training Workflow (Algorithm 1)**:
 - Compute nominal reference trajectory offline.
 - Initialize policy parameters.
 - In each training iteration:
 - Generate MC ensemble rollouts by sampling initial conditions and disturbances.
 - At each time step, apply the learned affine control law using the current observation (mean, covariance, nominal control, time-to-go).
 - Propagate the ensemble.
 - At the end of the episode, evaluate `J_tot` and update policy parameters via PPO to maximize `R`.

This workflow iteratively refines the policy by exposing it to uncertainties through simulations and penalizing deviations from probabilistic and deterministic feasibility requirements, while concurrently optimizing performance metrics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The RCCRL framework was assessed on two distinct spacecraft trajectory design problems to evaluate its performance and portability.

#### 4.1. Case Study I: Earth–Mars Transfer

This case study involved a three-dimensional multi-impulse heliocentric transfer, characterized by a long horizon and impulsive maneuvers. It served as a direct benchmark against a recent robust trajectory optimization formulation (ROCP–CL by Marmo et al.).

- **Gaussian Uncertainty Benchmark**: When trained and evaluated under Gaussian initial uncertainty and process noise, RCCRL demonstrated competitive performance.
 - **Fuel Cost**: RCCRL achieved a 95th-percentile total impulse cost (∆v_tot,95) of 12.6594 km/s. This represented a 5.50% reduction in upper-tail total cost compared to the Monte Carlo (MC) validated ROCP–CL benchmark (13.3960 km/s).
 - **Terminal Feasibility**: The policy maintained a 100% empirical Mars Sphere of Influence (SOI) capture probability (p_SOI = 1.00) over 100,000 rollouts, indicating that cost reduction did not compromise mission success probability.
 - **Terminal Dispersion**: RCCRL yielded substantially tighter terminal dispersion compared to the ROCP–CL (MC) benchmark, with reductions of approximately 42% in x-component position standard deviation and 78% in y-component position standard deviation at arrival.
 - **Control Allocation**: The 95th-percentile per-node impulse magnitude remained below the defined limit at all nodes, confirming satisfaction of the per-impulse chance constraint. The largest control effort spread was observed early in the transfer.

- **Bounded Uniform Uncertainty**: To assess behavior beyond Gaussian assumptions, the framework was evaluated with bounded uniform initial uncertainty, matching the first two moments of the Gaussian case.
 - **Fuel Cost**: The 95th-percentile total cost was 13.0806 km/s, a modest increase (3.3%) compared to the Gaussian RCCRL case, but still lower than the ROCP–CL (MC) benchmark. This suggests that the framework adapts to different uncertainty distributions.
 - **Feasibility**: All probabilistic requirements, including per-impulse bounds, SOI capture, and terminal covariance, remained satisfied, with p_SOI = 1.00.
 - **Control Strategy**: The policy showed a shift towards more systematic feedforward biasing under the bounded non-Gaussian uncertainty, indicating an adaptive strategy for maintaining robustness.
 - **Terminal Dispersion**: Terminal dispersion was redistributed across components rather than uniformly inflated, reflecting a different balance of corrective authority.

- **Robustness to Unmodeled Process Noise**: The Gaussian-trained policy's robustness was tested against varying levels of unmodeled process noise.
 - **Feasibility Degradation**: Feasibility was primarily lost due to violations of the terminal covariance constraint, followed by the SOI capture condition, rather than control authority saturation.
 - **Sensitivity**: Robustness was more sensitive to position-noise scaling than velocity-noise scaling, indicating that positional diffusion was a dominant factor.
 - **Robustness Radius**: The policy maintained feasibility and performance up to a process noise scaling factor of approximately 15 times the nominal level. Beyond this, covariance violation occurred, and capture probability degraded at even higher noise levels (around 48.9 times nominal). This indicated a finite robustness radius related to dispersion growth between decision nodes.

#### 4.2. Case Study II: Atmospheric Pinpoint Rocket Landing

This case study explored the portability of RCCRL to a short-horizon continuous-thrust powered descent problem, including drag, mass depletion, and state-dependent glide-slope constraints.

- **Gaussian Uncertainty**:
 - **Fuel Cost**: Under noise-free evaluation, the mean propellant consumption was 4,922.23 kg, representing an overhead of less than 1% compared to the deterministic SCP baseline.
 - **Feasibility**: Node-wise 95th-percentile thrust remained below the maximum, and the mean glide-slope corridor was satisfied.
 - **Terminal Dispersion**: Achieved sub-meter positional spread and sub-0.2 m/s velocity spread at touchdown in the noise-free setting. When nominal process noise was enabled during evaluation, terminal dispersion increased to sub-meter position and sub-0.3 m/s velocity, but covariance feasibility remained satisfied. Process noise primarily influenced terminal accuracy rather than fuel expenditure.

- **Bounded Uniform Uncertainty**:
 - **Fuel Cost**: Exhibited slightly higher propellant usage (4,928.24 kg mean) than the Gaussian-trained policy but still a small overhead relative to the SCP baseline. Process noise had a negligible effect on mean fuel usage.
 - **Feasibility**: All constraints, including thrust limits and glide slope, remained satisfied.
 - **Terminal Dispersion**: The touchdown-ellipse geometry differed from the Gaussian case, demonstrating that the policy redistributed corrective authority differently even with similar fuel usage. Terminal dispersion inflated with process noise to a similar overall scale as the Gaussian case but with modest anisotropy differences.

In both case studies, the RCCRL framework consistently demonstrated its capacity to manage uncertainty, satisfy probabilistic and deterministic constraints, and achieve competitive performance without requiring fundamental structural changes to its core architecture.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents RCCRL as a "reusable robustification architecture" for spacecraft trajectory optimization, rather than a method tailored to specific mission types. The significance and potential impact of this framework stem from its ability to address limitations of both traditional robust optimal control and standard reinforcement learning in practical aerospace applications.

#### 5.1. Methodological Contributions

- **Distribution-Agnostic Control Design**: RCCRL’s capacity to handle various uncertainty models (Gaussian, bounded uniform, or other simulation-defined distributions) by only requiring their sampleability provides a notable advantage over classical methods that often depend on restrictive analytical assumptions. This expands the applicability of robust trajectory optimization to scenarios with complex or poorly characterized uncertainties.
- **Structured and Interpretable Control Law**: By augmenting a deterministic nominal trajectory with a structured affine closed-loop policy (feedforward corrections and linear feedback gains), RCCRL offers a control solution that is more interpretable and aligned with engineering practices in safety-critical domains compared to opaque, end-to-end learned policies. This maintains the benefits of established trajectory optimization for nominal mission geometry while leveraging RL for robust adaptation.
- **Empirical Enforcement of Chance Constraints**: The use of Monte Carlo rollout ensembles and upper-tail quantile statistics for probabilistic feasibility bypasses the need for complex and potentially conservative analytical reformulations of chance constraints. This enables the framework to directly address nonlinear dynamics and complex, non-Gaussian uncertainty distributions.

#### 5.2. Demonstrated Performance and Portability

- **Competitive Performance**: In the Earth-Mars transfer case study, RCCRL demonstrated competitive or superior performance in upper-tail fuel cost while fully preserving probabilistic capture feasibility when benchmarked against an established chance-constrained robust trajectory optimization method under Gaussian uncertainty.
- **Cross-Domain Portability**: The framework's successful application to both a long-horizon, multi-impulse interplanetary transfer and a short-horizon, continuous-thrust atmospheric rocket landing problem highlights its versatility. The consistent effectiveness across such materially different dynamical regimes, control parameterizations, and constraint geometries suggests that the core design logic of RCCRL (nominal backbone, affine augmentation, sample-based evaluation) is transferable.

#### 5.3. Practical Implications

- **Enhanced Robustness for Complex Missions**: RCCRL offers a practical approach for designing robust trajectories for missions where analytical tractability is limited due to high nonlinearity or complex uncertainty characteristics. This could be particularly relevant for future missions involving autonomous rendezvous, on-orbit servicing, or precision planetary landings.
- **Decision-Making with Quantified Risk**: By enforcing probabilistic feasibility through empirical quantiles, the framework allows mission planners to design trajectories with quantified risk levels, moving beyond overly conservative designs without fully sacrificing reliability.
- **Integration with Existing Tools**: The modular design, which separates nominal trajectory generation from RL-based robustification, facilitates integration with mature deterministic trajectory optimization tools (e.g., SCP), allowing for a smoother adoption path in aerospace engineering workflows.

#### 5.4. Limitations and Future Directions

The paper acknowledges that the probabilistic guarantees provided by RCCRL are empirical rather than analytical. Their credibility is contingent on the fidelity of the simulation environment, the realism of the assumed uncertainty model, and the accuracy of finite-sample quantile estimates. Additionally, the moment-conditioned policy input may not fully distinguish between distributions with identical first two moments but different higher-order statistics.

Future work could focus on extending the framework to incorporate higher-fidelity dynamics, more sophisticated disturbance and actuation models, and an explicit treatment of onboard state-estimation uncertainty. Further research into the trade-offs between nominal optimality, feedback authority, and probabilistic robustness across a wider array of spacecraft trajectory design and optimization problems would also contribute to the framework's development.

In summary, RCCRL represents a significant step towards practical and robust spacecraft trajectory optimization by coherently combining structured control policies with simulation-based learning, offering a valuable tool for missions operating under complex and varied uncertainties.
