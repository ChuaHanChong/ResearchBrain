# Keep the Future, Drop the Rollout: RIFT for World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Rift (Rollout-free Imagination via Future Tokens) is a world-action model that keeps explicit test-time future conditioning but removes iterative video rollout. Learned anticipation tokens fill the complete future-position key/value cache in one video-backbone pass, shaped by conditional flow matching, and the action expert reads that cache through the unchanged interface. LIBERO success reaches 98.8% while action-chunk latency drops 68.2% to 89.1%.

## Problem

- Rollout-based world action models pay 3.3x to 9.6x current-only deployment latency, since iterative video generation must finish before every action chunk executes.
- Efficient variants like Fast-WAM and PFD delete the explicit future read at deployment, leaving a residual success gap to policies that read a generated future.
- Prior comparisons change future availability and its iterative producer together and report only end-task success, so the two contributions stay unresolved.

## Method

- A ==paired closed-loop intervention protocol== records the action-independent future ==K/V cache==, then masks, shuffles, noises, or final-clean-replays it, scoring end-effector average displacement error and success.
- **Rift** places learned ==anticipation tokens== at future spatiotemporal indices; one ==VideoStack cache prefill== writes their complete per-layer K/V, retaining Fast-WAM-Joint's original future-read attention interface.
- Training combines native video ==flow loss==, deployment-matched action flow loss, a ==conditional flow-matching== head over anticipation states, and a ==stopped-gradient linear probe==, both auxiliary heads dropped at deployment.

## Results

- Masking the future read cut success from **98.4%** to **9.7%** at **18.7cm** drift, while replaying one final-clean cache preserved **97.9%** success at **1.9cm**.
- Rift reached **98.8%** LIBERO success against **98.4%** to **98.6%** for rollout-based methods and **96.8%** current-only, costing **247.9ms** per action chunk versus **780** to **2270ms**.
- LIBERO-Plus out-of-distribution success hit **81.1%**, **+9.7** points over Fast-WAM-IDM; RoboTwin 2.0 gave **92.9/92.6%** clean/randomized; conditional flow matching beat the L2 recipe **98.8** versus **98.37**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Causal intervention on internal caches, scored by physical trajectory drift, tests whether a policy actually uses a representation; matched success rates cannot.
> - Separate what a module consumes from how that content is produced: an expensive iterative producer can be replaced while the consumption interface stays fixed.
> - For multimodal future targets, distributional objectives such as conditional flow matching beat L2 regression, which averages distinct valid futures into a blurred mean.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot control, specifically focusing on World Action Models (WAMs). WAMs represent a class of robot policies that integrate future video prediction with action generation. These models typically condition robot actions on an imagined future video sequence, which has been observed to yield higher task success rates compared to policies that only consider the current observation.

However, a notable challenge with conventional WAMs is the computational overhead associated with iterative video generation, known as "rollout," during deployment. This iterative process can significantly increase latency, making rollout-based systems substantially slower (reported 3.3 to 9.6 times slower) than policies that operate solely on current observations.

The existing research landscape can be broadly categorized into:
- **Vision-language-action (VLA) policies with current-only deployment:** These policies map observations and language directly to actions, often using pre-trained vision-language backbones. They achieve low latency but typically do not explicitly incorporate future-position state, sometimes resulting in a performance gap compared to future-conditioned approaches. Examples include direct visuomotor policies using diffusion or flow-matching action heads.
- **World Action Models (WAMs) with explicit future prediction:** These models generate future video at deployment to inform actions. They can be further divided by their interface:
 - **Imagine-then-execute systems** (e.g., IDM), which generate a complete future video and then apply inverse dynamics, allowing the action expert to read a fixed, clean future representation.
 - **Mixture-of-transformers systems** (e.g., Joint), which denoise video and action simultaneously through shared attention, meaning the action expert reads a future representation that evolves at each denoising step. While these two designs expose the future differently, they have shown similar success, yet both incur the high cost of iterative video generation.
- **Implicit-future policies:** These methods aim to mitigate the latency issue by utilizing future prediction only during training, without an explicit visual rollout at inference time. Approaches such as Fast-WAM remove the explicit future representation at deployment, while others like PFD distill the effect of a generated future into a lightweight current-only correction. These methods reduce latency but may still exhibit a performance gap relative to policies that explicitly read a generated future.

This paper addresses a specific gap in the understanding of WAMs: whether action generation necessitates the *evolving trajectory* produced by iterative rollout, or if only the *representation of the future* is required. Previous comparisons often modified both the availability of the future representation and its production process, obscuring their individual contributions. This work seeks to disentangle these factors, aiming to preserve the benefits of explicit future conditioning without the high latency of iterative video generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research is to resolve the trade-off between task success and deployment latency in World Action Models (WAMs). While WAMs that explicitly condition actions on predicted futures generally achieve higher success rates in robotic manipulation tasks, their reliance on iterative video rollout for future generation introduces significant computational latency, rendering them less suitable for real-time applications.

The core objective of the study is to investigate whether the performance benefits of future conditioning in WAMs are derived from the dynamic, evolving nature of the future representation as it is constructed through iterative rollout, or if they depend solely on the static, final state of that future representation. This central question leads to several specific objectives:

1. **To understand the role of the future-position Key/Value (K/V) cache in WAMs:** The researchers aimed to determine how action experts consume future information by systematically intervening on the K/V cache, which serves as the channel from predicted futures to action tokens. This involved asking:
 - Is the explicit future read essential for successful task execution, or can it be omitted at test time without substantial performance degradation?
 - Do the spatial and temporal positions of the future values within the cache hold specific significance for action generation, or is the content treated as an unordered pool?
 - Is the *iterative evolution* of the K/V cache throughout the denoising process a critical component for action prediction, or would a fixed, final representation of the future be sufficient if available?

2. **To develop and validate a rollout-free mechanism for future conditioning:** Based on the insights gained from the cache intervention study, a secondary objective was to design a novel architectural component, named Rift (Rollout-free Imagination via Future Tokens). This component is intended to construct a complete future K/V cache in a single pass, thus eliminating the need for iterative video generation at deployment while retaining the explicit future-read interface.

3. **To demonstrate that the proposed rollout-free approach can maintain high task success rates while significantly reducing latency:** The ultimate goal was to show that Rift could achieve performance comparable to or exceeding existing rollout-based WAMs and current-only policies, but with deployment latency similar to or only slightly higher than current-only methods. This would validate the hypothesis that explicit future conditioning can be achieved efficiently without iterative video rollout.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a two-pronged approach: an intervention study to analyze how WAM action experts utilize future representations, followed by the development and evaluation of a novel architectural design, Rift, based on these findings.

#### 3.1. Intervention Study on Future K/V Caches

To dissect the role of the future representation, a paired closed-loop intervention protocol was designed. This protocol focused on the future-position K/V cache, a per-layer channel from predicted futures to action tokens. The key property leveraged was that, due to the attention mask preventing video tokens from attending to action tokens, this cache is action-independent given the observation, language, and video-generation randomness.

The methodology involved:
1. **Recording:** During normal execution of unmodified WAM policies (Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM, Cosmos Policy, and LingBot-VA), the per-layer future K/V cache at every action-denoising step was recorded.
2. **Replay and Intervention:** Subsequent action denoising steps were replayed with non-target inputs fixed, while specific interventions were applied to the recorded future cache:
 - **Masking the future read:** The future read was entirely prevented.
 - **Editing future values under recorded keys:**
 - **Spatial permutation:** Future values were shuffled within individual frames.
 - **Temporal swapping:** Future frames' values were exchanged (not applicable to Cosmos-2, which inherently exposes only one future timestep).
 - **Norm-matched noise:** Future values were replaced with random noise.
 - **Frozen present:** Future values were replaced with non-predictive values derived from the current observation.
 - **Final-clean K/V replay:** For models like Joint and Cosmos-2, which typically use an evolving cache, the *entire* future-cache trajectory was replaced with one fixed final-clean K/V cache, derived from the *final* output of the original iterative video generation, and reused at every action-denoising step. This specific intervention was structurally "Not Applicable" (N/A) for IDM and LingBot-VA, as these models already operate by generating a final clean future once and then conditioning actions on it.
3. **Evaluation:** Each intervention was evaluated in 2,000 paired closed-loop trials across all 40 LIBERO tasks. The metrics used were:
 - **End-Effector Average Displacement Error (EE-ADE):** The average L2 distance between the end-effector trajectory of the intervened policy and the unmodified (Original) policy over their common executed prefix.
 - **Success Rate (SR):** The percentage of tasks successfully completed.

#### 3.2. Rift: Rollout-free Imagination via Future Tokens

Based on the intervention study's findings, Rift was proposed as a new approach to construct the future interface in one pass, eliminating iterative rollout.

1. **Architecture:** Rift largely retains the architecture and future-read interface of Fast-WAM-Joint. It replaces the rolled-out future tokens with *learned anticipation tokens* (E ∈ R^(m×d)). These tokens are designed to inherit corresponding future spatiotemporal indices. For example, on LIBERO, m = 196 tokens were used for full alignment.
2. **Cache Production:** The key innovation is a single video-backbone pass that maps the first-frame tokens (f₀(o)) from the observation (o), along with the learned anticipation tokens (E), and the language instruction (l), to produce the complete per-layer future-position K/V cache (C_ϕ). This cache then serves as a fixed input for the action expert throughout its denoising process, without further video diffusion or decoding.
3. **Training Objectives:** Rift's training involves two forwards through the same shared video expert per optimization step:
 - **Native video supervision (L_vid):** The first forward uses a standard video-flow loss on clean-first-frame clips with noised future latents, similar to existing WAMs. This maintains dynamics supervision.
 - **Deployment-matched action training (L_act):** The second forward uses the [f₀; E] input to produce the cache for the action expert, trained with a flow-matching loss.
 - **Conditional Flow-Matching (FM) supervision (L_FM):** An auxiliary distributional objective is applied to shape the anticipation states (S_ϕ). This head predicts the velocity for ground-truth future latent patches, conditioned on S_ϕ, using a conditional flow matching loss. This helps avoid conditional-mean optima that can average distinct valid futures.
 - **Stopped-gradient linear probe (L_probe):** A diagnostic linear probe (L2 regression) is also trained from the anticipation states to ground-truth future latents, with its gradients detached to only update the probe itself. This allows for a deterministic future-latent readout and can be used for runtime uncertainty monitoring (not part of deployment latency).
 - The overall loss is a sum of these components, with auxiliary weights for L_FM and L_probe decaying over the curriculum.
4. **Deployment:** At test time, only one cache prefill (using f₀(o) and E) is performed, followed by the standard action flow, with no iterative video diffusion or VAE decoding.

#### 3.3. Benchmarks and Comparisons

The evaluation was conducted on:
- **LIBERO:** A benchmark with 40 robotic manipulation tasks.
- **LIBERO-Plus:** An out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness benchmark featuring 10,030 variants.
- **RoboTwin 2.0:** A benchmark with 50 bimanual tasks in both clean and domain-randomized scenes.

Rift was compared against several existing methods, including current-only policies (Fast-WAM, PFD) and rollout-based WAMs (Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM, Cosmos Policy, LingBot-VA), often using shared backbones and training data for matched comparisons. Latency measurements were reported in milliseconds per action chunk on one A800 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded two primary findings from the intervention study and demonstrated the performance characteristics of the proposed Rift model.

#### 4.1. Findings from the Future-Cache Intervention Study

The systematic intervention on the future-position K/V cache in four different WAMs provided empirical evidence regarding how action experts utilize future information:

- **Finding 1: WAM action experts are sensitive to future values and their assigned positions.**
 - **Necessity of future read:** Masking the future read (removing the future cache entirely) resulted in a substantial increase in EE-ADE (ranging from 11.8 cm to 20.4 cm across models) and a significant reduction in success rates (from 98.4–98.6% down to 0.0–32.0%). This indicates that an explicit future read is critical for high performance.
 - **Content matters:** Replacing future values with norm-matched noise or non-predictive "frozen present" values also led to high EE-ADE (10.5–21.3 cm) and severely reduced success (at most 40.9% and 6.5%, respectively). This shows that the specific content of the future values is meaningful to the action expert.
 - **Position matters:** Permuting values spatially within frames (spatial shuffle) or exchanging entire value frames temporally (temporal swap) also degraded performance. Spatial shuffle yielded 5.0–19.8 cm EE-ADE and 0.0–84.5% success, while temporal swap resulted in 15.6–16.3 cm EE-ADE and 0.0–69.0% success (where applicable). These results demonstrate that the action expert relies on future content being present at its assigned spatial and temporal positions.

- **Finding 2: For certain WAMs, one fixed final-clean K/V cache can nearly preserve execution.**
 - For Fast-WAM-Joint and Cosmos-2, which originally operate with an *evolving* K/V cache during iterative denoising, replacing the entire cache trajectory with *one fixed final-clean K/V cache* (derived from the original model's final video generation step) resulted in minimal performance degradation. Specifically, it led to only 1.9 cm and 1.7 cm EE-ADE, respectively, and maintained high success rates of 97.9% and 98.2%, very close to their unmodified (Original) success rates of 98.4%. This finding suggests that, for these models, the *content* of the final clean future is sufficient for action generation, and the iterative *evolution* of the cache is not strictly necessary for consumption-side sufficiency.

#### 4.2. Rift Performance and Results

Building on the intervention findings, the Rift model demonstrated improvements in both performance and efficiency:

- **LIBERO Benchmark Performance:**
 - **Success Rate:** Rift achieved an overall success rate of 98.8% on the 40 LIBERO tasks. This performance is competitive with, and in some cases slightly surpasses, rollout-based WAMs such as Fast-WAM-Joint (98.4%), Fast-WAM-IDM (98.6%), and LingBot-VA (98.5%).
 - **Deployment Latency:** Rift operated with a latency of 247.9 ms per action chunk. This represents a substantial reduction (68.2% to 89.1%) compared to rollout-based alternatives (which ranged from 780.2 ms to 2270.3 ms). Crucially, Rift's latency was comparable to current-only policies like Fast-WAM (235.7 ms) and PFD (257.0 ms), while improving success by 2.0 and 1.5 percentage points over them, respectively.

- **LIBERO-Plus Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Robustness:**
 - When evaluated on the 10,030 variants of LIBERO-Plus without retraining, Rift achieved the highest overall success rate of 81.1%. This was a 9.7 percentage-point gain over Fast-WAM-IDM, demonstrating enhanced robustness to various OOD perturbations, difficulty levels, and source suites.

- **RoboTwin 2.0 Benchmark Performance:**
 - On the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, Rift achieved success rates of 92.9% on clean scenes and 92.6% on randomized scenes. These results were the highest observed among the evaluated methods, outperforming PFD (92.5%/92.1%), rollout-based LingBot-VA (92.4%/91.4%), and Fast-WAM (91.9%/91.6%).

- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Anticipation-token supervision:** The full conditional flow-matching (FM) recipe for Rift yielded 98.8% success, slightly outperforming a direct L2 regression baseline (Rift-L2) at 98.37%, while maintaining the same latency.
 - **Number of anticipation tokens:** Performance on LIBERO increased with the number of anticipation tokens (m), with full alignment (m=196) achieving the best results for both L2 and conditional-FM supervision. Even small interfaces (e.g., m=4) demonstrated an improvement over current-only baselines.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Results

- **Matched Imagined Futures:** Diagnostic visualizations showed that Rift's one-pass anticipation decodes produced imagined futures that were qualitatively similar in evolution (at frames 0, 4, and 8) to the iterative rollout decodes from Fast-WAM-Joint on both LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0.
- **L2-FM Uncertainty Warning:** An optional diagnostic monitor, comparing the L2 probe's point prediction with the conditional-FM samples, demonstrated the ability to detect potential policy failures. Across 33 failed LIBERO rollouts, this L2-FM discrepancy-based CUSUM warning system raised an alarm an average of 210 steps before the detected failure point.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic control and world models through both empirical analysis and architectural innovation.

#### 5.1. Empirical Insights into World Action Models

The intervention study provides empirical evidence that clarifies how World Action Models utilize future information. It systematically demonstrates that:
- Explicit future conditioning is critical for high task success, as its removal leads to substantial performance degradation.
- The content and specific spatiotemporal positioning of future values within the K/V cache are significant for action generation.
- For certain WAM architectures, the iterative *evolution* of the future K/V cache during denoising is not a necessary condition for successful consumption by the action expert, provided a high-quality fixed "final clean" future representation is available. This finding separates the *consumption* requirements from the *production* process of the future representation.

#### 5.2. Architectural Innovation for Efficient Future Conditioning

The development of Rift represents a significant architectural advancement. By replacing iterative video rollout with a single-pass anticipation token prefill, Rift demonstrates a method to produce a complete future K/V interface efficiently. This design maintains the benefits of explicit future conditioning without the associated high latency.

#### 5.3. Practical Implications and Potential Impact

The quantitative results highlight several practical implications:
- **Reduced Deployment Latency:** Rift achieves success rates comparable to high-performing rollout-based WAMs while drastically reducing deployment latency (by 68.2% to 89.1%). This makes sophisticated future-conditioned robot policies more viable for real-time applications where rapid decision-making is essential.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** The observed out-of-distribution robustness on LIBERO-Plus, where Rift significantly outperforms other evaluated methods, suggests that its single-pass future representation may contribute to better generalization capabilities in varied or perturbed environments.
- **Broad Applicability:** Demonstrated success across two distinct and challenging benchmarks (LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0) indicates the generalizability of the Rift approach to different robot embodiments and task complexities.
- **Inspiration for Future WAM Design:** The findings challenge the implicit assumption that iterative video generation is inseparable from effective future conditioning in WAMs. This could inspire the development of further efficient WAM architectures that focus on the quality and structure of the future representation rather than its iterative construction.

In summary, this work advances the understanding of future conditioning in robot policies and provides a practical, efficient solution that bridges the performance gap between low-latency current-only policies and high-performance, but slow, rollout-based WAMs. The proposed Rift model facilitates the deployment of capable robotic systems in time-sensitive environments while maintaining strong performance and robustness.
