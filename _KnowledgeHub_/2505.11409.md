# Visual Planning: Let's Think Only with Images

> [!summary] Summary
> A new paradigm, Visual Planning, enables AI models to perform multi-step reasoning solely through sequences of images, eliminating the need for textual mediation. The Visual Planning via Reinforcement Learning (VPRL) framework, applied to vision-first navigation tasks, achieved an 80.6% average Exact Match rate and 84.9% Progress Rate, outperforming text-based reasoning methods by 27%.

## Problem

- Current multimodal large language models (MLLMs) predominantly rely on text as the medium for reasoning, even with visual inputs, creating a 'modality gap'.
- Translating rich visual information into textual descriptions for reasoning often fails to accurately capture intricate visual features and dynamic state transitions in 'vision-first' tasks.
- Existing multimodal reasoning paradigms, even those incorporating visual aids, remain fundamentally text-driven, constraining AI's ability to genuinely 'think' visually.

## Method

- The paper proposes a =='Visual Planning' paradigm== where reasoning is defined as the generation of a sequence of intermediate images, without any textual mediation, using a generative vision model.
- They introduce ==Visual Planning via Reinforcement Learning (VPRL)==, a two-stage framework leveraging ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== to train a Large Vision Model (LVM-7B) exclusively on visual data.
- VPRL incorporates a reward function with a ==rule-based dynamics interpreter== and a ==progress estimator== to guide optimal visual state transitions, penalizing invalid actions and encouraging goal progression.

## Results

- VPRL achieved an average Exact Match rate of **80.6%** and a Progress Rate of **84.9%** across three grid-based navigation tasks, demonstrating a **27%** higher average EM rate compared to the best text-based reasoning methods.
- Language-based planning methods, including proprietary MLLMs (Gemini 2.5 Pro) and fine-tuned open-source models, consistently underperformed, with proprietary models yielding average EM below **50%**, supporting the 'modality gap' hypothesis.
- VPRL exhibited superior robustness and generalization, maintaining higher accuracy on larger, unseen grids (e.g., **82.4%** EM on 6x6 FROZENLAKE compared to **38.8%** for Gemini 2.5 Pro) and reducing the invalid-failure ratio by at least **24%** across tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Purely visual reasoning offers a more intuitive and effective approach for 'vision-first' tasks, overcoming the limitations imposed by language-mediated reasoning's 'modality gap'.
> - A two-stage reinforcement learning process, involving policy initialization through random trajectories followed by goal-aligned optimization, is critical for developing robust visual planning capabilities in large vision models.
> - Directly planning with image sequences allows for improved generalization, particularly in out-of-distribution scenarios and with increased task complexity, as models learn underlying visual strategies rather than relying on textual translations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and their multimodal extensions (MLLMs) have significantly improved machine reasoning capabilities across various tasks. However, a prevalent characteristic of these models is their reliance on text as the primary medium for both structuring and expressing reasoning, even when visual information is integral to the task. MLLMs, despite incorporating visual inputs, typically ground this visual data into textual descriptions or symbolic representations and then perform reasoning in a purely linguistic format. Examples include generating captions for visual content or producing verbal rationales for decisions.

This reliance on language-centric reasoning, even for "vision-first" tasks, has been observed to introduce a modality gap. This gap can hinder the model's ability to accurately capture intricate visual features, spatial relationships, and state transitions, particularly in domains involving geometry, physics, or complex spatial dynamics. Benchmarks in multimodal reasoning have indicated that purely language-based approaches may fall short in these specific areas.

The proposed work positions itself as a departure from this text-dominant paradigm by investigating whether models can perform planning directly within a non-verbal modality, specifically using images. This concept draws motivation from cognitive science, particularly Dual Coding Theory, which suggests that human cognition processes information through both verbal and nonverbal channels. While some MLLMs have incorporated interleaved text and images in reasoning steps, these approaches often remain fundamentally text-driven, utilizing visualizations as auxiliary tools rather than as the primary medium for reasoning itself. This paper aims to explore a "truly visual-only reasoning paradigm" that explicitly avoids any text-based reasoning proxies, thereby filling an underexplored niche in multimodal AI research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central argument motivating this research is that language may not always represent the most natural or effective modality for reasoning, especially in tasks fundamentally rooted in visual information, such as those requiring spatial or geometrical inference. The authors identify a key limitation in current MLLMs: despite processing visual inputs, these models predominantly "think" in text. This introduces a modality gap, where visual information must be converted into a linguistic format before reasoning can occur. This conversion process can lead to inaccuracies and an inability to fully capture the nuances of visual features and dynamic state transitions.

The primary objective of this work is to propose and validate a novel reasoning paradigm, termed "Visual Planning," which enables planning through purely visual representations. This paradigm is conceived as a supplementary channel to traditional language-based reasoning, specifically tailored for "vision-first" tasks. The core idea is to execute planning via sequences of images that visually encode step-by-step inference, mimicking human processes like sketching or mentally visualizing future actions.

The specific objectives include:
- **Investigating the feasibility of pure visual planning:** To determine if artificial intelligence models can achieve multi-step planning entirely through visual representations without the mediation of language.
- **Developing a novel framework for visual planning:** To introduce a robust training mechanism that enables large vision models to perform autoregressive image generation for planning purposes.
- **Empirically demonstrating the effectiveness of visual planning:** To evaluate the proposed paradigm on representative visual navigation tasks and compare its performance against established language-based reasoning methods and multimodal baselines.
- **Analyzing the generalization capabilities:** To assess whether a visual planning model can learn underlying planning rules and generalize effectively to novel or more complex scenarios.
- **Understanding the benefits of reinforcement learning in visual planning:** To explore how reinforcement learning, particularly a two-stage approach, can enhance exploration, reduce invalid actions, and improve overall planning performance in the visual domain.

Ultimately, the research seeks to provide evidence that visual planning can serve as a viable and promising alternative or complement to language-based reasoning, opening new avenues for tasks that inherently benefit from intuitive, image-based inference.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the **Visual Planning paradigm**, where reasoning is structured as a sequence of images without explicit language mediation. The approach involves generating a trajectory of intermediate visual states, `ˆT = (ˆv_1..., ˆv_n)`, given an initial image `v_0`. Each subsequent visual state `ˆv_i` is sampled autoregressively, conditioned on the initial state and previously generated states: `ˆv_i ~ π_θ(v_i | v_0, ˆv_1..., ˆv_{i-1})`. In this paradigm, actions are not explicitly predicted as discrete tokens but are implicitly represented by the transitions between visual states.

To implement this, the authors propose **Visual Planning via Reinforcement Learning (VPRL)**, a novel two-stage reinforcement learning framework utilizing Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to post-train Large Vision Models (LVMs). An LVM, specifically LVM-7B, trained exclusively on images and video frames without textual data, serves as the backbone model to ensure a clean investigation of purely visual reasoning.

**VPRL Framework Stages:**

1. **Stage 1: Policy Initialization (Supervised Learning):** The LVM policy model `π_θ` is initially trained on a dataset of random trajectories generated by random walks within the environment. This stage aims to enable the model to generate valid sequences of visual states and foster exploration capabilities. For training, input-target pairs `(v_≤i, ˜v_{i+1})` are extracted from trajectories, where `v_≤i` is a prefix sequence and `˜v_{i+1}` is a randomly sampled valid next state from all possible valid next states. The model minimizes a loss function `L_VPFT(θ) = -E_{(v_≤i, ˜v_{i+1})} [log π_θ(˜v_{i+1} | v_≤i)]`. This stage acts as a warm-up, improving visual coherence and generation quality while ensuring sufficient stochasticity for subsequent RL.

2. **Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning for Visual Planning (GRPO):** Building on the explorative policy from Stage 1, this stage optimizes the model's planning capabilities. Given an input prefix `v_≤i`, the behavior model `π_θ_old` samples a group of `G` candidate next visual states `{ˆv_{i+1}^{(1)}..., ˆv_{i+1}^{(G)}}`. Each candidate response `ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}` is scored using a composite reward function `r(v_i, ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)})`. GRPO is then employed to compute relative advantages `A^{(k)}` by comparing rewards within the group, thereby providing efficient training signals without requiring a learned critic. The policy `π_θ` is updated by maximizing an objective function `J_VPRL(θ)` which incorporates the relative advantage and a KL divergence penalty to balance exploration and exploitation.

**Reward Design:**
The reward function `r(v_i, ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)})` is crucial for guiding the model toward goal-aligned visual planning. It emphasizes two aspects: adherence to environment constraints (validity) and progress toward the goal. This involves two components:
- **Dynamics Interpreter D:** A function `D: V × V → A ∪ E` that parses the transition between a current state `v_i` and a generated candidate state `ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}`. It identifies the implicit action and determines if it is valid (`a ∈ A`) or invalid (`a ∈ E`, e.g., violating physical constraints). For implementation, this is a rule-based system that compares pixel-wise features and uses Intersection-over-Union (IoU) to identify agent movement and object interactions (pick/drop in MINIBEHAVIOR).
- **Progress Estimator P:** A function `P: V → N` that quantifies the remaining steps or effort to reach the goal from a given visual state `v`. This is implemented using Breadth-First Search (BFS) to pre-compute a "progress map" for all grid positions.

Based on the interpretation, actions are categorized into three disjoint subsets:
- `A_opt`: Optimal actions that reduce the distance to the goal (`P(ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}) < P(v_i)`).
- `A_nopt`: Non-optimal but still valid actions (`P(ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}) ≥ P(v_i)`).
- `E_inv`: Invalid actions.
The reward function is defined as `α_opt * I(D(v_i, ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}) ∈ A_opt) + α_nopt * I(D(v_i, ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}) ∈ A_nopt) + α_inv * I(D(v_i, ˆv_{i+1}^{(k)}) ∈ E_inv)`. In experiments, coefficients are set to `α_opt = 1`, `α_nopt = 0`, and `α_inv = -5`.

**Tasks and Baselines:**
The method is evaluated on grid-based navigation tasks: FROZENLAKE, MAZE, and MINIBEHAVIOR, representing spatial planning. Synthetic datasets with varying complexities are constructed.
- **Visual Planning Variants:** VPFT (supervised fine-tuning on optimal trajectories) and VPRL (the proposed two-stage framework).
- **Textual Baselines:** Qwen 2.5-VL-Instruct-7B (Direct, Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on action sequences, and RL variants with progress reward or PR metric reward) and proprietary models like Gemini 2.0 Flash (Direct, CoT) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (thinking model).

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Exact Match (EM):** Measures if the entire predicted trajectory `ˆT` is identical to any shortest optimal trajectory `T^(m)`. State equality is based on the underlying environment configuration, not pixel-wise matching.
- **Progress Rate (PR):** A softer metric, calculating the ratio of consecutive correct steps from the start that align with at least one optimal trajectory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments produced several key findings regarding the performance and characteristics of visual planning compared to language-based reasoning:

**Performance of Visual Planning (VPRL vs. VPFT):**
- VPRL significantly outperformed its supervised counterpart, VPFT. On average, VPRL achieved a 20% higher Exact Match (EM) rate.
- Specifically, VPRL attained near-perfect accuracy on FROZENLAKE (91.6% EM, 93.2% PR). It also demonstrated strong performance on MAZE (74.5% EM, 77.6% PR) and MINIBEHAVIOR (75.8% EM, 83.8% PR).
- The improvements in VPRL were attributed to the Stage 2 outcome-based optimization. Stage 1 (policy initialization) alone resulted in near-random behavior, confirming its role in fostering exploration rather than direct planning.

**Comparison with Textual Baselines:**
- **Proprietary Models:** Gemini 2.0 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Pro exhibited average EM rates below 50% and PR rates only marginally above 50%, even with advanced "thinking" capabilities. Gemini 2.5 Pro, despite generating over 7,000 thinking tokens in a case study, failed to provide a correct answer for a relatively simple FROZENLAKE instance.
- **Open-Source MLLMs:** Qwen 2.5-VL-Instruct-7B in its direct and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) modes performed poorly. Even with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on text-based planning, Qwen-7B achieved 68.6% EM and 84.4% PR on FROZENLAKE, which was surpassed by VPRL.
- **Textual Reinforcement Learning:** Applying RL (GRPO) to text-based planning with multimodal inputs yielded limited performance gains compared to the SFT baseline (e.g., 54.4% EM on FROZENLAKE with VPRL-like progress reward). The authors attribute this to a persistent "modality gap," where grounding visual information into text introduces inaccuracies that hinder effective learning in the language domain. Error analysis showed 25.7% of coordinate-based layout descriptions and 22.3% of ASCII-based representations generated by textual planning systems mismatched ground-truth layouts.

**Robustness and Generalization:**
- **Scaling Complexity:** VPRL demonstrated superior robustness when tasks scaled in difficulty (e.g., larger grid sizes in FROZENLAKE). While language-based methods experienced sharp declines in performance, visual planning methods, especially VPRL, exhibited much flatter performance curves, indicating greater stability. For example, VPRL maintained 82.4% EM on 6x6 FROZENLAKE grids, compared to Gemini 2.5 Pro's 38.8%.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD):** VPRL showed a greater degree of generalization to unseen, larger grid sizes compared to VPFT. Although overall performance dropped on OOD data, VPRL consistently outperformed VPFT in both EM and PR, suggesting it captured underlying planning strategies rather than merely memorizing training patterns.
- **Perturbed Inputs:** Qualitative analysis showed that VPRL could produce coherent planning traces even when portions of input images were masked, preserving structural consistency with visible regions.

**Mechanism and Contribution of VPRL Components:**
- **Exploration (Stage 1):** The ablation study confirmed that Stage 1 random policy initialization was crucial for effective exploration. VPFT, trained via teacher-forcing, suffered from rapidly declining entropy and limited exploration. VPRL Stage 1, conversely, maintained higher entropy, balancing exploration with a low invalid action ratio.
- **Invalid Action Reduction:** VPRL significantly reduced the "invalid-failure ratio" (percentage of failed trajectories caused by at least one invalid action) by at least 24% across all tasks compared to VPFT. This indicates that VPRL not only improved success rates but also encouraged planning within valid action spaces.
- **Image Quality and Robustness to Noise:** The study found that artifacts in generated images originated from the LVM's tokenizer rather than the prediction process itself. Importantly, the visual planner was robust to this visual noise; performance remained similar whether self-generated images or high-quality ground-truth images were used as intermediate inputs, indicating that sufficient semantic information was preserved for effective planning.

**Computational Cost:**
- Visual planning introduced a computational overhead due to repeated image generation. However, this cost was found to be computationally feasible, roughly 3 times that of Qwen 2.5-VL-Instruct-7B with CoT and about 6 times that of Gemini 2.0 Flash with CoT, on average. Some "thinking" models, such as Gemini 2.5 Pro, generated a larger number of tokens, suggesting visual planning is not universally the most expensive option.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of multimodal artificial intelligence by introducing and empirically validating **Visual Planning** as a novel reasoning paradigm. By demonstrating that models can successfully reason and plan solely through visual state transitions, without relying on textual intermediaries, the work challenges the prevailing assumption that language is the indispensable medium for complex inference in AI systems, especially for visually-oriented tasks.

The key implications and potential impacts are:

- **Expanded AI Reasoning Capabilities:** The findings establish visual planning as a viable and effective alternative to language-based reasoning for "vision-first" tasks, particularly those involving spatial, geometrical, and physical dynamics. This expands the repertoire of how AI systems can process and reason about information, offering a more intuitive approach for problems that are inherently visual.
- **Addressing the Modality Gap:** The research highlights the inherent limitations of grounding visual information into text, which often leads to inaccuracies and constraints in reasoning. Visual planning directly circumvents this "modality gap," allowing models to operate within the native visual domain, potentially leading to more accurate and robust reasoning in visually complex environments.
- **Advancement in Reinforcement Learning for Vision Models:** The introduction of VPRL, a two-stage reinforcement learning framework utilizing GRPO for large vision models, represents a novel application of RL to sequential image generation for goal-oriented planning. This methodology provides a blueprint for training generative vision models to acquire complex decision-making capabilities.
- **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The observed superior generalization of VPRL to increasing task complexity and out-of-distribution scenarios, along with its robustness to perturbed inputs, indicates that purely visual reasoning can capture fundamental environmental dynamics and planning strategies more effectively than textual counterparts in these domains. This suggests more adaptable and reliable AI agents.
- **New Avenues for Multimodal Research:** This work opens up a rich new direction for research into holistic multimodal thinking systems. It provides a foundation for developing AI agents that can integrate both verbal and non-verbal reasoning channels, potentially leading to more human-like cognitive architectures. Future work could explore how to effectively interleave visual and textual reasoning for even broader applicability.
- **Practical Applications in Embodied AI:** The demonstrated capabilities in visual navigation tasks (FROZENLAKE, MAZE, MINIBEHAVIOR) have direct implications for embodied AI, robotics, and assistive technologies. In these domains, perception and decision-making are tightly coupled, and the ability to plan directly from visual inputs could lead to more intuitive, efficient, and robust navigation and interaction systems. For instance, robots could plan their movements by visualizing trajectories, much like humans might.

In summary, this research underscores the power of intuitive, image-based inference and offers a compelling argument for strengthening the visual component in future multimodal AI systems, paving the way for more flexible and powerful reasoning across a wide range of domains.
