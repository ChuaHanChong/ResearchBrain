# Towards Hierarchical Multi-Step Reward Models for Enhanced Reasoning in Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Hierarchical Reward Models (HRM) and Hierarchical Node Compression (HNC) were developed to enhance the reliability and scalability of reward models for multi-step mathematical reasoning in LLMs. HRM combats reward hacking by evaluating reasoning at both individual and consecutive multi-step levels, leading to more stable and generalizable reasoning, while HNC efficiently augments training data for improved robustness.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently struggle with detecting and correcting intermediate errors in complex multi-step reasoning, leading to error propagation.
- Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) suffer from delayed feedback, credit assignment issues, and susceptibility to spurious reasoning.
- Process Reward Models (PRMs) are vulnerable to reward hacking and incur high computational and human annotation costs for training data generation.

## Method

- Introduced ==Hierarchical Reward Model (HRM)== that evaluates reasoning at both ==fine-grained (individual steps)== and coarse-grained (merged consecutive steps) levels, promoting coherence and self-correction.
- Developed ==Hierarchical Node Compression (HNC)==, a data augmentation strategy that merges consecutive nodes in MCTS-generated trees to enhance data diversity and robustness with minimal computational overhead.
- Applied a ==self-training strategy== for policy models, fine-tuning them on high-quality reasoning data identified by HRM and regularized with ==KL divergence== to maintain generalization capabilities.

## Results

- HRM achieved **80%** accuracy on PRM800K, outperforming traditional ORM and PRM (**74.4%**) which showed fluctuations, with policy models fine-tuned on HRM-identified data showing improved robustness.
- HRM, when trained with HNC-augmented data, consistently showed greater robustness and stability across various policy models, with HNC requiring only 30 minutes of CPU for augmentation.
- HRM demonstrated superior generalization, achieving **74.2%** accuracy on the out-of-domain MATH500 dataset compared to PRM's **65.6%** at N=64.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Evaluating reasoning hierarchically, by considering both individual steps and multi-step coherence, effectively combats reward hacking and fosters more reliable LLM reasoning.
> - An efficient data augmentation method like HNC can significantly improve the diversity and robustness of autonomously generated training data for reward models without substantial computational cost.
> - Careful regularization with KL divergence is critical during policy model self-training to prevent catastrophic forgetting of general capabilities when fine-tuned on task-specific, high-quality data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advancements in various generative tasks, including aspects of mathematical reasoning. Techniques such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Tree-of-Thought (ToT) have been developed to enhance these reasoning capabilities by enabling LLMs to generate intermediate steps. However, existing CoT models often lack mechanisms for detecting and correcting intermediate errors, which can lead to error propagation. ToT methods, while offering structured exploration, do not inherently verify every intermediate step or guarantee the retrieval of optimal reasoning trajectories.

To address these limitations, researchers have explored reward mechanisms to guide LLM reasoning. Two primary approaches are the Outcome Reward Model (ORM) and the Process Reward Model (PRM). ORM assigns rewards based on the final output, suffering from delayed feedback and credit assignment problems, making it difficult to identify which specific steps contribute to success or failure. This can also lead to "spurious reasoning," where a correct answer is achieved through flawed intermediate steps. PRM, in contrast, provides finer-grained supervision by evaluating each reasoning step individually. Despite its advantage in granularity, PRM is often model-based, making it susceptible to "reward hacking," where models exploit reward signals without genuinely improving their reasoning. Furthermore, the manual annotation required for PRM training data is costly and resource-intensive, limiting its scalability and broad deployment.

This work addresses the specific limitations of PRMs, focusing on mitigating reward hacking and reducing the annotation cost for training data. It introduces a novel reward model approach that integrates hierarchical supervision to improve reasoning coherence and a data augmentation strategy to enhance the efficiency of training data generation. By doing so, this research aims to develop more reliable, robust, and scalable reward models that can guide LLMs in complex multi-step reasoning tasks, particularly in mathematical domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to enhance the reliability and scalability of reward models for guiding multi-step reasoning in Large Language Models, specifically by addressing key challenges identified in existing Process Reward Models (PRMs).

The motivations underpinning these objectives are:
- **Mitigating Reward Hacking in PRMs:** Current PRMs, due to their model-based nature, are vulnerable to reward hacking. This phenomenon occurs when a reinforcement learning agent identifies loopholes or ambiguities in the reward function, leading it to optimize for the reward signal rather than the intended task performance. This undermines the genuine improvement of reasoning abilities in complex tasks and can result in unreliable model behavior. The motivation is to design a reward model that is more robust to such exploitation, ensuring that improvements in reward scores directly correspond to improved reasoning quality.
- **Addressing High Annotation Costs for PRM Training Data:** Training effective PRMs requires extensive datasets of manually annotated reasoning steps. This annotation process is prohibitively expensive and labor-intensive, making large-scale deployment and continuous improvement of PRMs challenging. The researchers are motivated to find scalable and efficient methods for generating PRM training data autonomously, thereby reducing reliance on costly human annotation.
- **Improving Reasoning Coherence and Self-Correction:** Existing PRMs often penalize any incorrect step without considering whether subsequent steps might correct an earlier error. This atomistic evaluation overlooks the overall coherence of a multi-step reasoning process. The motivation is to develop a reward mechanism that can assess reasoning from a broader perspective, allowing for the identification and acknowledgment of self-correction mechanisms within a reasoning chain, leading to a more robust and forgiving evaluation.
- **Enhancing Generalization and Robustness:** Reward models, especially when trained on specific datasets, need to generalize effectively to new, unseen reasoning tasks and domains. The research is motivated by the need to develop a reward model that maintains stable and reliable performance across diverse mathematical reasoning problems, ensuring its utility beyond the training distribution.

In summary, the work aims to move "towards hierarchical multi-step reward models" to overcome the fragility and cost associated with current PRM approaches, ultimately leading to more capable and trustworthy LLM reasoners.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces two principal methodological contributions: the Hierarchical Reward Model (HRM) and Hierarchical Node Compression (HNC), coupled with a self-training strategy for policy models.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Reward Model (HRM)

The HRM is proposed to combine the fine-grained supervision of PRM with a broader assessment of reasoning coherence, addressing the limitations of reward hacking and error propagation.
- **Hierarchical Supervision:** HRM integrates a hierarchical supervision signal during its training phase. It evaluates reasoning processes at two distinct granularities: fine-grained (individual, single-step) and coarse-grained (consecutive multi-step sequences). This layered approach is designed to capture both local accuracy and global coherence in reasoning trajectories.
- **Training Data Construction:** The HRM training dataset extends that of a traditional PRM. While a PRM dataset consists of individual steps paired with their rewards ($D_{PRM} = \{(s_i, R(s_i)) | 1 \le i \le N\}$), the HRM dataset includes these single-step evaluations along with evaluations of merged, consecutive steps. Specifically, it includes pairs of two consecutive steps $(s_i + s_{i+1})$ and their combined reward ($D_{HRM} = D_{PRM} \cup \{(s_i + s_{i+1}, R(s_i + s_{i+1})) | 1 \le i < N\}$). The paper focuses on two-step merging as a minimal yet effective form of hierarchical supervision. Labeling rules for merged steps, particularly in the PRM800K dataset, dictate how positive, negative, and neutral labels combine (e.g., positive + positive = positive, positive + neutral/negative = negative). This strategy aims to encourage concise and goal-directed reasoning by penalizing non-progressive steps.
- **Error Correction and Coherence:** Unlike PRM, which penalizes an incorrect single step without considering potential corrections, HRM assesses coherence across multiple steps. This allows the model to identify and incorporate later steps that might rectify earlier errors, leading to a more robust evaluation that accounts for self-reflection.
- **Inference Mechanism:** Despite its hierarchical training, HRM operates step-wise during inference, similar to PRM. It assigns a reward based solely on the current step $s_i$. This design maintains fine-grained step-level evaluation while smoothing annotation noise from ambiguous step boundaries.
- **Implementation Details:** The Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math model is fine-tuned as the Reward Model. The reward score is derived from the softmax probability of a step being correct. Techniques like FlashAttention, DeepSpeed, and mixed-precision training are employed, with `max_grad_norm` set to 0.01 to stabilize training, particularly in the PRM800K domain where numerical precision issues were observed. Reasoning steps are defined by formula completion using specific stop tokens.

#### 3.2. Hierarchical Node Compression (HNC)

To address the computational inefficiency of autonomously annotating PRM training data using Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), the Hierarchical Node Compression (HNC) data augmentation method is introduced.
- **Purpose:** HNC aims to increase the diversity and robustness of MCTS-generated training data for reward models while minimizing computational overhead.
- **Mechanism:** HNC consolidates two consecutive nodes (representing reasoning steps) from different depths within the MCTS tree structure into a single new node. This creates a new branch without requiring additional MCTS rollouts.
- **Effect on Data and Robustness:** By merging nodes, HNC effectively expands the training dataset. It introduces controlled noise into the MCTS-based scoring: while the expectation of the parent node's score remains unchanged, the variance increases from $\frac{\sigma^2}{N}$ to $\frac{\sigma^2}{N-1}$ (where N is the number of child nodes). This controlled noise injection is intended to enhance label robustness.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The HNC augmentation process is computationally lightweight (approximately 30 minutes on CPU) compared to the MCTS simulation itself (thousands of GPU-hours), making it an efficient data augmentation strategy.
- **MCTS Configuration:** For autonomous annotation, MCTS is configured with 5-6 child nodes per parent and a maximum tree depth of 7 to balance computational feasibility and reliability.

#### 3.3. Self-Training for Policy Models

The research also includes a self-training component to improve the policy model's reasoning performance.
- **Data Filtering:** High-quality reasoning data is filtered from MCTS-generated trajectories using the Monte Carlo Score (MC-Score) to ensure reliable supervision and mitigate reward bias.
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Instead of reinforcement learning methods, supervised fine-tuning is used to train the policy model. The objective function is defined as $L = L_{LM} + \lambda \log D_{KL}(P || Q)$, where $L_{LM}$ is the causal language modeling loss on high-quality sequences, and $D_{KL}(P || Q)$ is the KL divergence between the policy model's output distribution P and a reference model's output distribution Q.
- **KL Regularization:** A logarithmic scaling is applied to the KL divergence term to stabilize the loss landscape and ensure a balanced trade-off. This addresses an imbalance where raw KL loss could range from 0 to 20000 while LM loss remains 0 to 12, preventing the policy model from rapidly losing generalization ability by excessively minimizing KL divergence. A weighting factor $\lambda$ (set to 0.5) balances task-specific adaptation and retention of general capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across different evaluation scenarios, demonstrating the performance of HRM and HNC.

#### 4.1. HRM Performance with Manually Labeled Data

- **Dataset:** Evaluated on the PRM800K dataset, which contains manually annotated reasoning processes.
- **Policy Model:** Qwen2.5-72B-Math-Instruct was used as the policy model, employing a best-of-N search strategy.
- **Stability and Reliability:** HRM consistently demonstrated superior stability and reliability in reward evaluations compared to both ORM and PRM. As the value of N (number of candidate paths) increased in the best-of-N strategy, the accuracy of the policy model guided by HRM stabilized at 80%. In contrast, policy models using ORM and PRM exhibited significant performance fluctuations, with accuracy often degrading as N grew.
- **Statistical Robustness:** The evaluations, conducted on datasets with thousands of test problems (e.g., 1k in GSM8K, 500 in MATH500, over 800k annotated steps in PRM800K) and utilizing best-of-N (up to N=512) for hierarchical exploration, implicitly averaged over a large number of trajectories, contributing to the statistical robustness of the results.

#### 4.2. HRM and HNC Performance with Autonomously Annotated Data

- **Dataset:** PRM800K questions and ground truths were used, with MCTS (using Qwen2.5-7B-Math-Instruct) employed for automatic reasoning trajectory annotation. HNC was applied to augment this MCTS-generated data.
- **Computational Efficiency of HNC:** The MCTS simulation for generating trajectories required approximately 2,457 A100 GPU-hours. In contrast, the HNC augmentation process took only about 30 minutes on a CPU, representing a minimal computational overhead for data expansion. HRM training, incorporating HNC-augmented data, added about 30% more samples and increased training time from 84 to 120 GPU hours, which was considered negligible compared to the MCTS generation cost.
- **Robustness Across Policy Models:** When trained on auto-annotated data, HRM demonstrated greater stability and robustness compared to PRM across various policy models, including Qwen2.5-7B-Math-Instruct, DeepSeek-Math-7B, and Qwen2.5-72B-Math-Instruct, under the best-of-N strategy on the PRM800K test set.

#### 4.3. Self-Training Results for Policy Models

- **KL Loss Regularization:** Experiments on self-training highlighted the critical role of KL divergence regularization and its logarithmic scaling. Without proper scaling or with a very small weighting factor ($\lambda = 0.001$), the policy model rapidly lost its generalization ability, with KL loss escalating significantly (e.g., to 20,000) while the causal language modeling loss remained low. A balanced $\lambda$ (e.g., 0.5) effectively prevented this divergence and ensured stable training.
- **Enhanced Reasoning Capability:** Fine-tuning policy models (Qwen2.5-7B-Math-Instruct) on high-quality reasoning data (MC-score > 0.9) filtered from MCTS-generated PRM and HRM training sets improved their reasoning capabilities. Policy models fine-tuned with HRM-derived data (Qwen-7B-HRM) consistently showed greater robustness compared to those fine-tuned with PRM-derived data (Qwen-7B-PRM).

#### 4.4. Generalization Across Different Domains

- **Datasets:** HRM and PRM models, trained solely on PRM800K, were evaluated on out-of-domain mathematical reasoning tasks from MATH500 and GSM8K.
- **Superior Generalization:** HRM exhibited superior generalization and robustness, particularly on the MATH500 dataset, which consists of complex high-school and university-level mathematical problems. It effectively handled these tasks, outperforming PRM.
- **Marginal Difference on Simpler Tasks:** On the GSM8K dataset, which primarily involves simpler arithmetic problems, the performance difference between HRM and PRM was marginal. This is attributed to the fact that strong policy models can often solve these problems in few steps, reducing the impact of HRM's advantages in multi-step coherence and self-reflection. Nevertheless, HRM still maintained a slight performance edge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the development of reward models for Large Language Models, particularly for tasks requiring multi-step reasoning. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Enhanced Reliability and Robustness of LLM Reasoning:** By introducing the Hierarchical Reward Model (HRM), the research offers a more robust and reliable method for evaluating multi-step reasoning. HRM's ability to assess both individual and consecutive steps, coupled with its capacity to account for self-correction, mitigates the issue of reward hacking observed in traditional Process Reward Models (PRMs). This can lead to LLMs that exhibit more genuinely improved reasoning abilities rather than merely optimizing for superficial reward signals.
- **Improved Multi-Step Reasoning Coherence:** The hierarchical supervision in HRM encourages LLMs to generate reasoning trajectories that are not only locally accurate but also globally coherent. This addresses a critical limitation where LLMs might propagate errors without correction. The mechanism for recognizing and rewarding self-correction could lead to more sophisticated and human-like reasoning processes in AI.
- **Scalable Data Generation for Reward Models:** Hierarchical Node Compression (HNC) provides an efficient and lightweight data augmentation strategy for Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)-based autonomous annotation. This significantly reduces the reliance on expensive human-annotated data, making the training of high-quality reward models more scalable and accessible. The minimal computational overhead of HNC, compared to full MCTS rollouts, democratizes the development of advanced reward models.
- **Better Generalization Across Diverse Reasoning Tasks:** The demonstrated superior generalization of HRM to out-of-domain datasets like MATH500 highlights its potential for broader applicability. This suggests that reward models trained with hierarchical supervision may be more adaptable to various complex reasoning challenges, enhancing the utility of LLMs in different scientific and technical domains.
- **Effective Policy Model Self-Training:** The self-training framework, incorporating KL divergence regularization with logarithmic scaling, offers a principled approach to fine-tuning policy models using autonomously generated high-quality reasoning data. This method allows for continuous improvement of LLMs' reasoning capabilities while preserving their general language abilities, providing a pathway for iterative self-enhancement without constant human intervention.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in hierarchical reward modeling, potentially exploring deeper levels of step merging, alternative forms of hierarchical supervision, and its application to other structured reasoning domains beyond mathematics. It also encourages further exploration into efficient autonomous data generation techniques.
