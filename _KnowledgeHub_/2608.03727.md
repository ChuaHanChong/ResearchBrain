# Track4Action: Distilling World-Centric 3D Tracker into Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Track4Action introduces a method to distill comprehensive world-centric 3D motion and geometry from a training-only 3D tracker into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. This approach enables VLA policies to implicitly understand action consequences in the 3D environment, achieving a 7.6-point average gain in zero-shot robustness on LIBERO-Plus and at least a 20-point improvement in real-world bimanual tasks compared to alignment-free baselines.

## Problem

- Conventional Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies primarily learn what robot commands to execute but lack explicit information about how these actions alter the 3D environment, such as scene geometry, object motion, or contact events.
- This implicit understanding of world transitions limits VLA policies' robustness and generalization, especially in complex or out-of-distribution scenarios.
- Existing approaches that incorporate geometric or future content often require geometric predictions or tracker execution at deployment, adding computational overhead or dependencies during inference.

## Method

- **Track4Action** formulates a mechanism to distill world-centric 3D cues (trajectories, motion, visibility, camera parameters) from a frozen, pre-trained `Track4World` 3D tracker into a VLA policy during training, using action-aligned video clips as ==privileged supervision==.
- A set of learnable '==track queries==' within the VLA policy's hidden states is designed to infer the likely 3D world transition from current observations and is trained with an ==L2 alignment loss== against the tracker's features.
- The learned world-transition representation from the track queries conditions the action generation process, allowing the `Track4World` model and the alignment branch to be entirely discarded for ==tracker-free deployment==.

## Results

- Achieved **97.0%** average success on the LIBERO benchmark, with a **9.6-point** improvement in the LIBERO-Long suite (from **86.2%** to **95.8%**) compared to the alignment-free variant.
- Demonstrated **82.3%** average success on LIBERO-Plus zero-shot robustness, outperforming competitive baselines by **3.0 points** and showing a **7.6-point** gain over the alignment-free variant across all seven perturbation categories.
- Obtained **80.44%** (clean) and **81.48%** (domain randomized) success on the 50-task RoboTwin 2.0 bimanual benchmark, exceeding the strongest reported baseline by up to **4.48 points**, and achieved **67.5%** average success on real-world bimanual tasks, improving by at least **20 points** over the alignment-free control.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Rich 4D scene dynamics, encompassing geometry, motion, and visibility, implicitly present in demonstration videos, can be leveraged as powerful action-aligned privileged supervision to enhance VLA policies' understanding of cause-and-effect in 3D environments.
> - Distilling 3D tracker features into VLA policies through an 'action-facing track-query pathway' significantly improves robustness and generalization capabilities without introducing any computational overhead or dependency on complex 3D tracking models at inference time.
> - The benefits of world-transition supervision are particularly pronounced in long-horizon tasks and under zero-shot distribution shifts, where a deeper, action-aware understanding of dynamic interactions is crucial for task success.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies represent a class of robot control systems that translate visual observations and natural language instructions directly into robot commands. These policies are designed for generalist control across various tasks, datasets, and embodiments. Current VLA policies primarily rely on expert action likelihood or denoising objectives, which specify *what* robot commands to execute but do not explicitly encode *how* these commands alter the 3D environment, including scene geometry, object motion, contact events, or changes in camera visibility. This implicit understanding of world transitions can limit their robustness and generalization capabilities, particularly in complex or out-of-distribution scenarios.

Existing research has explored various avenues to enrich action learning with future and geometric information. One category involves world-model policies that predict future images, geometry, or visual features to guide action generation. Another focuses on geometry-aware policies that encode 3D structure, learn scene flow, or align policy features with frozen spatial representations. Furthermore, methods utilizing point tracking and motion representations have emerged, where tracks or flow are either predicted, planned, or used as an intermediate representation for control.

The paper identifies a gap in these existing approaches: they typically target future content, explicit geometric variables, or spatial features extracted from a single state. They do not leverage a *motion-specialized representation of the exact segment realized by a demonstrated action chunk* as privileged supervision during training. While demonstration videos inherently contain this physical evidence across K frame transitions corresponding to K actions, existing VLA policies do not fully exploit this rich, action-aligned information regarding scene geometry, 3D motion, visibility, and camera changes.

Track4Action positions itself by addressing this specific gap. It proposes a mechanism to distill this "realized transition" information from a frozen, world-centric 3D tracker into a current-observation VLA policy. Unlike methods that require geometric predictions or tracker execution at deployment, Track4Action uses the tracker *only* during training as a supervisor, ensuring tracker-free execution. This approach aims to provide the VLA policy with a deeper understanding of the world dynamics associated with actions, without imposing a computational burden or dependency on complex 3D tracking models during inference. The work seeks to strengthen action representations by shaping them with world-transition supervision, while maintaining compatibility with diverse VLA backbones and action decoders.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for Track4Action stems from a limitation in conventional Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies: while action labels provide precise motor commands for a robot to imitate, they inherently lack information about the *consequences* of these commands in the 3D world. An action chunk specifies *how* a robot should move (e.g., end-effector displacement), but it does not explicitly convey *which* scene points move with it, whether contact occurs, or how visibility changes throughout the execution. The same robot command can lead to vastly different physical outcomes depending on the surrounding 3D structure, such as moving through free space, transporting an object, or altering the camera's view. These distinctions are critical for task success but are not encoded by typical action labels.

The authors identify that this "missing supervision" is, however, implicitly present within the demonstration video itself. For any given action chunk comprising K commands, the corresponding K+1 frames in the aligned demonstration clip capture the scene before and during these K transitions. A robust world-centric 3D tracker can process these frames to explicitly organize and estimate the scene's geometry, 3D motion, visibility, and camera parameters during the executed action.

The key objective of this work is to leverage this rich, action-aligned physical evidence as *privileged supervision* for training a VLA policy. Specifically, the paper aims to answer the question: Can an action-aligned 3D tracker effectively supervise a VLA policy during its training phase, and subsequently be entirely removed, allowing the robot to act without any dependency on the tracker at deployment?

To achieve this, the primary objectives of Track4Action are:
1. **Formulate world-centric 3D tracker feature distillation for VLA policies:** To transform the information about the realized action segment—how the world changes during an action—into a supervisory signal for a policy that only observes the current state.
2. **Introduce an action-facing track-query pathway:** To design a mechanism where learnable "track queries" extracted from the VLA policy's hidden states serve a dual purpose: they must learn to match the distilled tracker target *and* they must condition the action generation process, ensuring that the learned world dynamics directly influence control decisions. This pathway must facilitate tracker-free deployment.
3. **Demonstrate broad applicability and robustness:** To evaluate the proposed distillation interface across diverse robotic manipulation benchmarks, including zero-shot visual shifts (LIBERO-Plus), 50-task bimanual simulation (RoboTwin 2.0), and real-world physical bimanual manipulation tasks, confirming its effectiveness across different observations, embodiments, and action spaces.

By achieving these objectives, the research seeks to enhance the VLA policy's understanding of task-relevant 4D scene dynamics, leading to improved robustness, generalization, and overall success in complex manipulation tasks, without incurring additional computational cost at inference time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Track4Action's methodology is structured around distilling world-centric 3D tracker features into a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy during training, ensuring that the deployed policy operates without the tracker. The framework integrates a training-only "teacher" (the 3D tracker) with a "student" (the VLA policy).

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

At time $t$, the VLA policy receives a language instruction $l$, current RGB observations $I_t$, and optionally, the robot state $s_t$. Its objective is to predict a normalized action chunk $A^\star_t = (a_t..., a_{t+K-1})$, consisting of $K$ actions. During training, the system also utilizes a primary-view video clip $V_{t:t+K} = (o_t..., o_{t+K})$, which contains $K+1$ frames aligning with the $K$ actions. Crucially, this clip is exclusively for constructing privileged supervision during training and is not available to the policy at deployment. The deployed policy $\pi_\theta$ directly predicts $b_{A_t} = \pi_\theta(l, I_t, s_t)$ based solely on current inputs.

#### 3.2. World-Centric Tracker Supervision

The core idea is to leverage a frozen, pre-trained world-centric 3D tracker, `Track4World`, as a teacher. Given the action-aligned clip $V_{t:t+K}$, `Track4World` estimates a comprehensive set of world-centric cues, including 3D trajectories, scene motion, visibility, and camera parameters. These outputs are then pooled into a compact `tracker feature` ($f^{trk}_t$). A `stop-gradient` operation (`sg`) is applied to $f^{trk}_t$, preventing gradients from flowing back into the `Track4World` model. This ensures that `Track4World` functions purely as a source of privileged information and is not modified during the VLA training. The resulting $f^{trk}_t$ summarizes the 3D structure and motion recovered from the *exact realized action segment*, making it a precise target for world-transition supervision.

#### 3.3. Distilling Tracker Features into the VLA

The student VLA policy must learn to infer the information contained in $f^{trk}_t$ using only its current observations, without access to the full action-aligned clip or the `Track4World` model.
First, the VLA backbone encodes the current images and language instruction into a sequence of hidden states, $H_t = E_\theta(l, I_t)$.
The framework then introduces a set of learnable `track queries` (Q). These queries interact with the VLA hidden states $H_t$ through cross-attention, producing a refined sequence $Z_t = D_\theta(Q, H_t)$. This sequence $Z_t$ acts as an "action-facing bottleneck" representation.
$Z_t$ is subsequently pooled and mapped to a shared alignment space, where it is compared with the pooled tracker feature $f^{trk}_t$. An L2 loss, $L_{align} = ||\bar{z}_t - \bar{f}^{trk}_t||_2^2$, is used to train the track queries. This alignment objective encourages the track queries to infer the likely 3D world transition solely from the current scene context and task instruction.

#### 3.4. Action Generation and Tracker-Free Deployment

For the distilled tracker knowledge to influence action generation, the aligned representation $Z_t$ must be integrated into the action decoding process. The track queries $Z_t$ are fused with the original VLA sequence $H_t$ through a learned `feature-wise gate`. This gated fusion generates a conditional input $H^{cond}_t$ for the action head. The gating mechanism is designed to selectively expose the distilled tracker cues while preserving the semantic context from $H_t$. Importantly, the teacher feature $f^{trk}_t$ itself never directly enters $H^{cond}_t$ or the action head.

The policy uses a flow-matching action head, which is optimized with a standard conditional velocity objective, $L_{act}$. The total training objective is a weighted sum of the action loss and the alignment loss: $L = \lambda_{act}L_{act} + \lambda_{align}L_{align}$. The action loss guides the policy on *what* to execute, while the alignment loss informs its internal representation about *which* world transition is associated with that execution.

During **deployment**, the `Track4World` model, the action-aligned clips, the tracker target, and the alignment branch are all discarded. The policy operates solely on its current observations ($l, I_t, s_t$), computes $H_t$ and $Z_t$ via the VLA backbone and track queries, fuses them to form $H^{cond}_t$, and generates the action chunk from noise. This ensures that the policy remains entirely tracker-free at execution.

Two crucial design choices underscore this approach: (1) **Temporal alignment** directly links the tracker target to the exact transitions induced by the action chunk, providing action-specific supervision. (2) **Action-facing track queries** serve as both the student representation for alignment and an input to the action head, ensuring that the learned world-transition understanding directly shapes control.

The experimental setup involved training a Qwen3.5-2B VLA backbone with a DiT-B flow-matching action head. Different action spaces and observation types were handled across benchmarks (7-D delta end-effector actions for LIBERO, 14-D bimanual joint and gripper commands for RoboTwin and physical tasks). An "alignment-free" variant was used as a controlled comparison, maintaining the same model scale, data, optimizer, and training schedule but without the tracker-distillation pathway.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Track4Action was conducted across simulation and physical environments, focusing on in-distribution performance, zero-shot robustness to distribution shifts, and bimanual manipulation.

#### 4.1. LIBERO Results (In-Distribution Simulation)

On the LIBERO benchmark, Track4Action achieved an average success rate of 97.0% across the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. This performance surpassed that of competitive VLA policies, including $\pi^{0.5}$ (96.9%) and $F_1$ (95.7%). Track4Action recorded the highest scores on the Object and Long suites and remained within one point of the best results on the Goal suite.

A direct comparison with the alignment-free variant of Track4Action revealed a 2.8-point improvement in average success (97.0% vs. 94.2%). This gain was primarily concentrated in the LIBERO-Long suite, where success increased by 9.6 points (from 86.2% to 95.8%). In contrast, gains on Spatial, Object, and Goal suites were smaller (1.0, 0.4, and 0.2 points, respectively). This suggests that tracker supervision is particularly beneficial for long-horizon tasks requiring the preservation of task-relevant state across multiple stages.

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Robustness on LIBERO-Plus

For zero-shot robustness, models were trained only on original LIBERO demonstrations and tested on seven perturbation dimensions in LIBERO-Plus without adaptation. Track4Action achieved an average success rate of 82.3%, outperforming LaMP (79.3%) by 3.0 points. It ranked first in Camera, Light, Noise, and Layout categories, and second in Robot, Language, and Background, indicating consistent robustness across various visual shifts.

The tracker alignment mechanism improved performance across all seven perturbation categories compared to the alignment-free variant (82.3% vs. 74.7%), resulting in a 7.6-point average gain. The most substantial improvements were observed in Camera (18.3 points), Noise (9.0 points), and Robot (8.7 points). The absence of performance regression in any category suggests that the overall gain was not attributed to specific favorable perturbations.

#### 4.3. Bimanual Evaluation on RoboTwin 2.0

On the 50-task RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, designed for bimanual manipulation, Track4Action achieved 80.44% success under clean conditions and 81.48% under domain randomization. These results exceeded the strongest reported baseline, Motus w/o Pretrain, by 2.88 points (clean) and 4.48 points (randomized), and X-VLA by 7.64 and 8.64 points, respectively.

Notably, Track4Action's success rate *increased* by 1.04 points from clean to randomized conditions, whereas Motus showed a slight decrease. This indicates that the advantage of Track4Action is retained or even enhanced under scene randomization, demonstrating robustness to varied environments.

#### 4.4. Cross-Benchmark Effect of Tracker Distillation

The ablation studies consistently showed that the complete tracker-distillation pathway provided performance improvements across all tested benchmarks. The average success rate increased by 2.8 points on LIBERO, 7.6 points on LIBERO-Plus, and significantly by 39.12 points (clean) and 41.58 points (randomized) on RoboTwin 2.0 compared to the alignment-free variants. The most pronounced gains were observed in settings with greater demands on world state and motion, such as long-horizon tasks and zero-shot distribution shifts.

#### 4.5. Real-World Experiments (Physical Bimanual Manipulation)

Physical experiments were conducted on an AgileX ALOHA-style platform for four bimanual tasks, utilizing the same training-only teacher and tracker-free deployment protocol. Track4Action achieved an average success rate of 67.5%, surpassing $\pi^{0.5}$ (65.0%) and the alignment-free variant (42.5%). Tracker supervision led to at least a 20-point improvement over the alignment-free control on every task, with the largest gains (30 points) on multi-stage tasks like "drawer" and "cabbage."

The average process score for Track4Action was 75.0, comparable to $\pi^{0.5}$ and 14.4 points higher than the alignment-free variant (60.6). Gains in process score ranged from 7.5 to 25.0 points across tasks. While Track4Action improved drawer success from 60% to 80% over $\pi^{0.5}$, it achieved 40% on towel folding compared to $\pi^{0.5}$'s 70%, identifying deformable manipulation as a persistent challenge.

Under held-out out-of-distribution (OOD) physical changes (new object layout, unseen towel color, textured desk background), tracker supervision improved average success from 23.3% to 50.0%. For instance, chili-transfer success rose from 30% to 60% with a new layout, and cabbage-task success increased from 30% to 70% on a textured desk. However, physical OOD deformable manipulation (unseen-color towel) remained difficult at 20% success.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in Track4Action demonstrates a method for enhancing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by distilling world-centric 3D tracker features as privileged supervision during training. This approach addresses a fundamental limitation in VLA policies, which typically receive action labels but lack explicit information about the 3D world transitions caused by those actions.

The primary significance lies in showing that rich 4D scene dynamics, encompassing geometry, motion, visibility, and camera changes, can be leveraged from demonstration videos to robustly supervise VLA policies. This is achieved without incurring any computational overhead or dependency on complex 3D tracking models at deployment time, as the tracker is entirely discarded after training. This "tracker-free deployment" is a key advantage, maintaining the simplicity and efficiency of traditional VLA inference while benefiting from sophisticated 3D world understanding during learning.

The consistent performance improvements observed across diverse benchmarks underscore the impact of this method:
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** Track4Action demonstrated superior performance on zero-shot visual shifts (LIBERO-Plus) and in randomized simulation environments (RoboTwin 2.0). The ability to consistently improve across different perturbation categories suggests that the distilled 3D world understanding contributes to a more generalized and robust policy.
- **Enhanced Performance in Complex Tasks:** Significant gains in long-horizon tasks (LIBERO-Long) and multi-stage physical manipulation indicate that world-transition supervision is particularly beneficial when tasks require maintaining task-relevant state and understanding dynamic interactions over extended periods.
- **Real-World Applicability:** The successful application to physical bimanual manipulation tasks, with notable improvements over alignment-free baselines and competitive performance against other state-of-the-art VLA models, validates the practical utility of the approach in real-world robotics. The gains observed even in out-of-distribution physical settings highlight its potential for deploying more adaptable robots.
- **Modular and Compatible Framework:** The distillation interface is designed to be compatible with different VLA backbones and action decoders, suggesting that it can be integrated into various existing and future VLA architectures to augment their capabilities.
- **Novel Use of Privileged Supervision:** The formulation of action-aligned 3D tracker features as privileged supervision represents a novel method for extracting and utilizing implicit information from demonstration data, moving beyond simple action imitation to incorporating a deeper understanding of cause-and-effect in the physical world.

The potential impact of Track4Action is broad. It offers a pathway to develop robotic manipulation policies that are more adept at understanding and reacting to their dynamic environments, leading to more reliable task completion in varied and unstructured settings. This could accelerate the development of generalist robots capable of performing a wider range of tasks with higher success rates and greater adaptability. By enriching action representations with scene dynamics, the work contributes to building more intelligent and physically aware robot control systems.
