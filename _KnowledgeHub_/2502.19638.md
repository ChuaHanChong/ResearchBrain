# Sensor-Invariant Tactile Representation

> [!summary] Summary
> University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign researchers developed Sensor-Invariant Tactile Representations (SITR) to achieve zero-shot transferability of machine learning models across diverse optical tactile sensors. Their method, leveraging physics-based simulations and sensor calibration images, demonstrated over 81% accuracy for inter-sensor object classification and reduced 3-DoF pose estimation RMSE to 0.80 mm on real-world datasets.

## Problem

- Machine learning models trained on one tactile sensor exhibit poor generalization to other sensors due to significant variations in optical design and manufacturing processes.
- The substantial cost and effort required to collect large, diverse tactile datasets for each unique sensor type hinder widespread adoption and research.
- Existing representation learning methods often struggle to provide zero-shot generalization to entirely unseen or uncharacterized tactile sensors.

## Method

- A transformer-based architecture learns Sensor-Invariant Tactile Representations (SITR) by combining tactile images with a small set of sensor-specific calibration images to account for optical and physical variations.
- The model is pre-trained on a large-scale synthetic dataset (1 million examples across 100 sensor configurations) generated using physics-based rendering, providing diverse contact geometries and ground-truth normal maps.
- Training incorporates a normal map reconstruction loss to preserve geometric information and a geometry-focused supervised contrastive learning loss that pulls together features from similar contact geometries across different sensors.

## Results

- SITR achieved 81.94% average accuracy for inter-sensor object classification, outperforming ImageNet-pre-trained ViT-Base by over 33 percentage points (48.10%) under zero-shot transfer conditions.
- The method demonstrated robust 3-DoF pose estimation with an inter-sensor Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 0.80 mm, representing an approximate 50% reduction compared to baseline ViT models (e.g., 1.58 mm for ImageNet-pre-trained).
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the number and diversity of calibration images, as well as the synergistic combination of normal map reconstruction and supervised contrastive learning losses, were crucial for achieving high transfer performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging easily acquirable sensor calibration images provides critical sensor-specific context, enabling a unified model to effectively normalize diverse tactile inputs.
> - Training with supervised contrastive learning, where positive pairs share contact geometry but originate from different sensors, effectively aligns feature spaces and facilitates zero-shot transfer.
> - A large, diverse physics-based simulated dataset can serve as a robust pre-training environment, providing sufficient data variance and precise ground truth for learning sensor-invariant features that generalize to real-world sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Tactile sensing is an important modality for intelligent systems in areas such as embodied perception and robotic manipulation. Vision-based tactile sensors, including the GelSight series and its variants, have gained prominence due to their capacity to capture detailed contact information, which is valuable for tasks such as object shape detection, material recognition, and fine-grained manipulation.

However, a significant limitation within this field is the lack of transferability of learned models between different tactile sensors. This challenge arises from sensor variance, where differences in optical design, manufacturing processes, or material properties result in substantial discrepancies in the tactile signals produced. Consequently, machine learning models trained on data from one sensor often exhibit poor generalization performance when applied to another. This issue is compounded by the substantial cost and effort associated with collecting large, diverse tactile datasets for each sensor type, creating a barrier to widespread adoption and advancement.

Previous research efforts have attempted to address this transferability challenge. Some approaches involved collecting diverse datasets using multiple GelSight sensors, though with limited gains in generalization. More recent methods, such as T3 (Zhao et al., 2024), explored pre-training transformer models across various sensors and tasks, but their reliance on category-specific encoders limited their ability to generalize to entirely unseen sensors. Other works have applied general representation learning techniques from computer vision, like contrastive learning (Yuan et al., 2017b; Yang et al., 2022) or auto-encoders (Cao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024), to tactile data. However, many of these approaches often overlooked the substantial domain gap between different sensors, leading to models that perform well only on the specific sensor or sensor type they were trained on. UniT (Xu et al., 2024) aimed for unified multimodal tactile representations by encoding sensor types as tokens within large language model frameworks, while Sparsh (Higuera et al., 2024) utilized self-supervised learning with masking and self-distillation. The common limitation among many existing methods is their dependence on large, existing datasets and their tendency to treat sensor types as fixed categories, which restricts zero-shot generalization to new or uncharacterized sensors and variations within the same sensor type.

This work aims to advance the field by directly tackling sensor variance to enable zero-shot transferability. It focuses on learning representations that are robust to these variations, thereby facilitating the application of models trained on one sensor to a wide array of others without extensive retraining.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a novel methodology for extracting Sensor-Invariant Tactile Representations (SITR) that facilitates zero-shot transferability across optical tactile sensors. The central aim is to enable machine learning models to generalize effectively to new, previously unseen sensors, thereby overcoming the limitations imposed by sensor variance arising from differences in optical design and manufacturing.

The motivation for this work is rooted in several observations and challenges within tactile sensing:
1. **Addressing Sensor Variance and Domain Gaps:** The significant and unpredictable domain gaps between different GelSight-like sensors hinder the transfer of learned models. Existing approaches often struggle to generalize to unseen sensors because they do not adequately account for the wide range of variations that can occur. The researchers hypothesize that learning truly sensor-invariant representations is crucial for robust generalization.
2. **Focus on Contact Geometry:** The authors operate on the belief that contact geometry constitutes one of the most critical features for a majority of tactile-driven tasks, including object shape recognition, texture classification, and contact localization. By prioritizing geometric accuracy and designing the representation to preserve this information while factoring out sensor-specific artifacts, the goal is to develop representations that are universally applicable.
3. **Reducing Data Collection Burden:** The high cost, time, and effort involved in collecting real-world tactile datasets for every new sensor configuration or application represent a major barrier to progress. The research is motivated by the desire to leverage physics-based simulations to efficiently generate diverse tactile datasets, thereby reducing the reliance on costly real-world data collection and allowing for robust model training with precise ground truth.
4. **Enabling Zero-Shot Transfer:** The ultimate motivation is to allow machine learning models and knowledge learned from one sensor or dataset to be seamlessly transferred to others in a zero-shot manner. This capability would accelerate development in tactile perception and robotics by making models more adaptable and reusable across heterogeneous sensing platforms.

To achieve these objectives, the research introduces a framework incorporating three core innovations:
1. **Sensor Characterization via Calibration Images:** Utilizing a small set of easily acquirable calibration images to characterize individual sensors, which are then effectively combined with tactile readings via a transformer-based encoder.
2. **Geometry-Focused Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL):** Employing SCL, supervised by geometric accuracy, to emphasize the geometric aspects of tactile data and encourage the clustering of similar contact geometries regardless of the originating sensor.
3. **Large-Scale Synthetic Dataset Generation:** Developing a substantial synthetic dataset (1 million examples across 100 sensor configurations) using a physics-based simulator. This dataset provides the necessary diversity for robust model training and precise ground truth for contact geometries.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on developing Sensor-Invariant Tactile Representations (SITR) using a transformer-based architecture trained with a combination of calibration images, normal map reconstruction, and supervised contrastive learning on a large-scale simulated dataset.

#### 3.1. Input and Pre-processing

For each tactile image $x_i$, a set of $K$ calibration images $c_{i,k}$ (from the same sensor) are also utilized. All input images are resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels. Distortions in GelSight Wedge sensor images undergo affine transformation correction. Background subtraction is applied to all images to isolate the tactile signal, followed by normalization based on the mean and standard deviation of the simulated dataset. Batched data augmentations, including color jitter and Gaussian blur, are applied during training.

#### 3.2. Calibration Images for Sensor-Specific Information

Calibration images are used to inform the model about sensor-specific characteristics, such as optical properties and physical variations. The method involves pressing two objects—a 4mm diameter ball and a cube corner—at nine distinct locations each (forming a 3x3 grid) across the sensor surface, generating 18 calibration images per sensor. These images guide the encoder to identify and account for sensor-specific features. To efficiently encode multiple calibration images, they are reshaped and stacked into a single representation. Both the tactile image $x_i$ and the stacked calibration images $c_i$ are then patchified and linearly projected into sequences of tokens, similar to a Vision Transformer (ViT). These token sequences, along with a learnable class token $z_i$, are concatenated and passed as input to the transformer encoder. Positional encoding is added to the tokens based on their 2D coordinates.

#### 3.3. Network Architecture and Training

The core of the SITR framework is a modified Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder. This encoder processes both the tactile image tokens and the calibration image tokens. During the pre-training phase, the encoder is trained using two distinct supervision signals:

- **Normal Map Reconstruction Loss ($L_{normal}$):** A lightweight decoder attached to the output patch tokens of the encoder reconstructs the contact surface as a normal map. Normal maps capture the orientation of each 3D point on the contact surface, providing rich geometric information that is invariant to sensor-specific variations. A pixel-wise Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss is applied between the predicted normal map ($\hat{n}$) and the ground truth normal map ($n$).
- **Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) Loss ($L_{SCL}$):** Applied to the class token $z_i$. SCL leverages label information to learn more effective representations by pulling together similar samples and pushing apart dissimilar ones in the embedding space. In this context, positive pairs are defined as tactile images exhibiting the *same contact geometry* but captured by *different sensors*. Negative pairs consist of images from different contact geometries or locations. The training batch includes two views for each sample (same contact, different sensors) to facilitate the learning of discriminative features that are robust to sensor variations. The SCL loss function utilizes a temperature parameter $\tau$ to scale similarity values.

The total loss for SITR pre-training is a weighted sum: $L = \lambda_{normal} \cdot L_{normal} + \lambda_{SCL} \cdot L_{SCL}$, where both $\lambda_{normal}$ and $\lambda_{SCL}$ are set to 1.

#### 3.4. Datasets

Three datasets are employed for training and evaluation:

- **Simulated Tactile Dataset:** This large-scale synthetic dataset is used to train the SITR encoder. It is constructed using Physics-based Rendering (PBR) in Blender, simulating GelSight sensors with parameterized optical designs (light properties, gel properties, camera properties). This approach generates 100 unique sensor configurations. Object diversity is achieved by using 50 high-resolution 3D meshes of common household objects, randomly scaled, rotated, and placed on the gel pad. For each contact geometry, tactile images are rendered across all sensor configurations, paired with ground-truth surface normal maps. This process yields 10,000 unique contact configurations, resulting in a total of 1 million synthetic examples designed to be sensor-aligned for SCL.
- **Real-World Tactile Datasets (for downstream tasks):** Two datasets are collected across seven different real sensors (four GelSight Minis, GelSight Hex, GelSight Wedge, and DIGIT) for evaluating zero-shot transferability on downstream tasks.
 - **Object Classification Dataset:** Comprises 16 distinct objects, each pressed in various poses and depths. 1,000 tactile images are recorded per object, repeated across all seven sensors, totaling 112,000 images (16,000 per sensor).
 - **Pose Estimation Dataset:** Collected using a modified Ender-3 Pro 3D printer for precise ground truth of 3-DoF (x, y, z) contact poses. Six different indenters are pressed at various locations and depths on the sensor surface, generating 1,000 samples per indenter across four sensors, resulting in 24,000 tactile images (6,000 per sensor) with precise pose labels.

#### 3.5. Downstream Task Evaluation

For evaluating downstream tasks, the pre-trained SITR encoder is frozen, and only a task-specific decoder head is trained on data from a *single* sensor. The model's performance is then evaluated on the remaining sensors in a zero-shot manner.

- **Shape Reconstruction:** Qualitatively assessed by reconstructing normal maps for objects from the real-world classification dataset and integrating them into height maps, demonstrating the preservation of geometric and textural details.
- **Object Classification:** Measures top-1 accuracy on 16 object classes. The decoder consists of a ResNet-18 network applied to unpatchified output tokens, concatenated with the class token, followed by a 3-layer MLP.
- **Pose Estimation:** Estimates 3-DoF (x, y, z) position change between an initial and final tactile image. Two tactile images of the same object are fed separately into the frozen SITR encoder, their features concatenated, and then passed to a modified ResNet-18 with a 6-channel input, followed by a linear projection to a 3-dimensional output.

#### 3.6. Baselines and Ablation Studies

SITR is compared against various baselines, including ViT-Base (trained from scratch and ImageNet pre-trained), ViT-Large (ImageNet pre-trained), T3 (Zhao et al., 2024), and UniT (Xu et al., 2024).

Ablation studies are conducted to investigate:
- The impact of the number and type of calibration images (ranging from none to 18 images of a ball and cube corner).
- The contribution of SCL and varying contrastive temperatures ($\tau$).
- The individual contributions of normal map loss and SCL loss.
- The effectiveness of SITR's supervision signals compared to alternatives like Masked Auto-Encoders (MAE) or VQGAN.
- The effect of simulation dataset size, including the number of sensor variations and samples per sensor, on transfer performance.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of SITR in achieving zero-shot transferability across various vision-based tactile sensors for different downstream tasks.

#### 4.1. Shape Reconstruction

Qualitative evaluation of shape reconstruction shows that SITR successfully preserves fine-grained geometry and texture details. Reconstructed normal maps, integrated into 3D height maps, accurately represent contact surfaces for objects interacting with various real sensors, including GelSight Mini, DIGIT, GelSight Hex, and GelSight Wedge. This indicates the robustness of SITR in modeling contact geometry despite diverse sensor inputs.

#### 4.2. Object Classification

SITR significantly outperforms all baseline models in object classification accuracy under zero-shot transfer conditions.
- **Transfer Performance:** SITR achieves 90.23% average accuracy in intra-sensor transfer (across different GelSight Minis) and 81.94% in inter-sensor transfer (across GelSight Mini, Wedge, Hex, DIGIT). In contrast, a ViT-Base pre-trained on ImageNet achieved 73.22% intra-sensor and 48.10% inter-sensor accuracy, while ViT-Base trained from scratch yielded 36.90% intra-sensor and 24.02% inter-sensor accuracy. Baselines like T3 and UniT also showed lower transferability (e.g., T3-Medium: 38.66% intra-sensor).
- **No Transfer Performance:** Most models performed well in the no-transfer setting (training and testing on the same sensor), with SITR achieving 99.72% accuracy, indicating that the challenge lies in generalization across sensor domains rather than within a single sensor.
- **Feature Analysis:** t-SNE visualizations of SITR features demonstrate that supervised contrastive learning effectively clusters similar contacts from different sensors, confirming SITR's ability to align tactile signals and eliminate sensor-variant features.
- **Limitations:** Features from the DIGIT sensor were observed to be somewhat more challenging to cluster with other sensors, suggesting that DIGIT's distinct optical design may not be fully represented within the current simulation dataset.

#### 4.3. Pose Estimation

SITR shows robust performance in 3-DoF (x, y, z) pose estimation when transferring across different sensors.
- **Inter-sensor Transfer:** SITR achieved a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 0.80 mm, which represents an approximate 50% reduction compared to baselines. For instance, ViT-Base pre-trained on ImageNet achieved 1.58 mm RMSE, and ViT-Base trained from scratch yielded 1.63 mm RMSE.
- **No Transfer Performance:** All models exhibited similar RMSE errors in the no-transfer setting (around 0.5 mm), indicating sub-millimeter inaccuracies that might stem from the data collection process itself, but also serving as an upper bound for transfer performance.
- **Comparison to Baselines:** Pre-training ViT on ImageNet provided only marginal improvements for pose estimation, suggesting that features learned from natural images are not optimally transferable for precise tactile regression tasks.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Number and Type of Calibration Images:** Increasing the quantity and diversity of calibration images (e.g., adding a cube alongside a ball) consistently improved SITR's performance, particularly in inter-sensor transfer tasks, leading to increases in classification accuracy by over 20% compared to using no calibration. The gains diminished when adding more images of the same object but were substantial with diverse object geometries.
- **Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) and Temperature:** The inclusion of SCL significantly enhanced performance, especially for classification tasks, by helping align features across sensors. An optimal contrastive temperature of $\tau=0.07$ (intra-sensor) or $\tau=0.03$ (inter-sensor) was identified for classification. SCL had a negligible impact on pose estimation RMSE, indicating that pose estimation relies more heavily on fine-grained geometric information.
- **Contribution of Loss Terms:** Both the normal map reconstruction loss and the SCL loss independently serve as effective supervision signals, but their synergistic combination yielded the strongest overall results in classification accuracy.
- **Choice of Supervision Signal:** When evaluated on the simulated dataset, SITR's combined normal map and SCL supervision consistently outperformed alternative supervision signals like MAE and VQGAN, demonstrating the benefits of SITR’s architectural and training pipeline.
- **Effect of Simulation Dataset Size:** Increasing both the number of sensor variations (from 10 to 100) and the number of samples per sensor (from 1K to 10K) in the simulated dataset directly correlated with improved classification transfer performance, validating the importance of a diverse and large-scale synthetic training dataset.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Sensor-Invariant Tactile Representations (SITR) marks a notable development in the field of tactile sensing. The work addresses the fundamental challenge of sensor variance, which has historically limited the transferability of machine learning models across different tactile sensing platforms.

The primary significance of SITR lies in its ability to enable **zero-shot transfer** of learned models to unseen optical tactile sensors. This capability has several potential impacts:
1. **Reduced Data Collection Burden:** By allowing models to generalize without extensive retraining or new data collection for each sensor, SITR can substantially reduce the financial and temporal costs associated with developing and deploying tactile-enabled robotic systems. This democratizes access to advanced tactile perception for researchers and developers.
2. **Accelerated Research and Development:** The ability to transfer models seamlessly fosters faster iteration cycles in research. Knowledge and models developed for one sensor can be readily applied to new sensor designs or existing sensors, accelerating advancements in areas like robotic manipulation, object recognition, and material characterization.
3. **Enhanced Model Robustness and Generalizability:** SITR's focus on extracting robust, geometry-preserving features, independent of sensor-specific artifacts, results in more generalizable models. This contributes to the development of more versatile intelligent systems capable of adapting to diverse physical interaction scenarios.
4. **Foundation for a Unified Tactile Modality:** This work represents a step towards a unified framework for tactile sensing, where disparate sensor outputs can be mapped into a common, invariant representation. Such a framework could bridge the gap between different tactile technologies, allowing for more comprehensive understanding of physical interactions.
5. **Future Expandability:** The methodology, particularly the physics-based rendering environment, is designed for expandability. It can be adapted to model a broader range of flat tactile sensors by incorporating new optical parameters. The potential for future generalization to more complex optical sensors (e.g., GelSight Svelte, DIGIT 360) and even traditional array-based tactile sensors by establishing connections between depth maps and force distributions further highlights its long-term impact. The incorporation of marker-based tactile information (force and torque) through adapted calibration methods could also broaden the model's applicability.

In summary, SITR provides a robust solution to a critical bottleneck in tactile perception, paving the way for more adaptable, cost-effective, and powerful tactile-enabled robotic systems and furthering the development of embodied perception.
