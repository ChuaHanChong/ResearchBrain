# Rethinking On-Policy Self-Distillation for Thinking Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Princeton University researchers found that privileged-context on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) consistently degraded the performance of "thinking models" on long reasoning tasks, demonstrating average accuracy drops of 0.8 to 6.4 percentage points. The study revealed this degradation is caused by "fork suppression," where privileged teacher feedback discourages the exploratory branching and self-correction vital for these models' extended deliberation.

## Problem

- The effectiveness of on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) for "thinking models," which rely on test-time deliberation and long reasoning traces, remained systematically underexplored.
- A prevailing assumption existed that privileged self-distillation universally benefits model performance, particularly for models designed for deep, iterative reasoning.
- Existing OPSD studies typically focused on instruction-tuned models or contexts where the deliberative nature of thinking models was not fully leveraged, especially with extended generation budgets.

## Method

- Evaluated privileged-context ==on-policy self-distillation (OPSD)== on thinking models (Qwen3 and OLMo families) using math reasoning and Countdown datasets.
- Compared OPSD with varied privileged teacher contexts (full gold demonstration, final answer only) against ==vanilla on-policy distillation== (without privileged context).
- Employed diagnostic techniques including ==fork-lock analysis==, per-token distillation signal analysis, and deliberation marker analysis to understand underlying mechanisms.

## Results

- OPSD training consistently degraded `avg@16` performance for all **five** evaluated thinking models by **0.8 to 6.4 percentage points**, while improving instruction-tuned models.
- Performance decline was specific to privileged teacher context and concentrated at longer rollout budgets (**32,000 to 38,912 tokens**), with OPSD models producing substantially shorter responses.
- Diagnostic analysis showed privileged context significantly lowered "fork rates" and raised "lock rates" in thinking models and assigned negative advantage to self-correction and uncertainty-marking tokens.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Privileged-context OPSD degrades the performance of strong thinking models, particularly over long reasoning traces, contrary to its positive effects on instruction-tuned models.
> - The degradation is attributed to "fork suppression," where privileged teacher feedback suppresses exploratory branching and penalizes self-correction/uncertainty markers at high-entropy decision points.
> - The extent of performance degradation correlates with the density of privileged information, with full gold demonstrations causing more severe issues than final-answer-only context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) has emerged as a strategy for self-improvement in large language models (LLMs). This technique involves a single model acting as both student and teacher, where the teacher component is augmented with privileged information, such as a gold solution or a final answer, to guide the student's learning. Prior research, including works by Zhao et al., Shenfeld et al., and Hübotter et al., has reported positive outcomes for OPSD, primarily in the context of instruction-tuned models, settings with short generation budgets, or for tasks like continual learning and reasoning compression.

Thinking models, characterized by their ability to engage in test-time deliberation through branching, verification, hedging, and error recovery, represent a distinct class of LLMs that are well-suited for self-improvement. Their inherent capacity for complex reasoning, often involving long and intricate reasoning traces, makes them intuitive candidates for methods that aim to enhance their deliberative capabilities. However, a systematic investigation into the effects of privileged-context self-distillation on these specific models, particularly when they operate with extended generation budgets, has been largely absent from the literature. Existing studies have typically disabled thinking during training or focused on contexts where the deliberative nature of thinking models is not fully leveraged.

This paper addresses this gap by specifically examining how privileged-context OPSD impacts thinking models, especially concerning their performance over long reasoning traces. It posits that while privileged information might seem beneficial, it could inadvertently interfere with the exploratory and self-corrective mechanisms that thinking models rely upon. The research positions itself against the prevailing assumption that privileged self-distillation universally benefits model performance, particularly for models designed for deep, iterative reasoning. It extends the diagnostic frameworks for analyzing reasoning traces, such as those identifying "forking positions" (high-entropy decision points where multiple continuations are plausible), as introduced by Bigelow et al. and Zhang et al.. Concurrent work by Kim et al. has also explored self-distillation failures, attributing degradation to the suppression of epistemic verbalization. This paper builds on such insights by proposing "fork suppression" as a broader mechanism, examining it through a comprehensive lens that includes budget curves, fork/lock diagnostics, token-level signal analysis, and shifts in deliberation markers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether existing privileged-context on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) methods are beneficial for improving the performance of "thinking models," particularly when these models engage in long reasoning traces. The authors specifically aim to determine if such distillation preserves or degrades the complex test-time search behaviors that thinking models employ.

The motivation for this study stems from several observations and hypotheses:

- **Apparent Suitability of Thinking Models:** Thinking models, which are post-trained to deliberate, branch, verify, backtrack, and self-correct at test time, naturally appear to be strong candidates for self-improvement techniques like OPSD. These models inherently leverage complex reasoning processes, making the idea of using privileged information to refine these processes appealing.
- **Gap in Existing Research:** While OPSD has shown promise for instruction-tuned models and in scenarios with shorter generation budgets, its effectiveness and impact on thinking models that depend on long, deliberative generation traces remained largely unexplored. Previous applications of OPSD had not fully considered this specific operational regime of thinking models.
- **Hypothesis of Potential Degradation:** Contrary to the intuitive expectation that privileged information would universally improve models, the authors specifically hypothesized that privileged context might *suppress* the very deliberative and exploratory behaviors that thinking models rely on for their gains at longer rollout budgets. This forms the core of their investigative motivation: to test this hypothesis and understand the underlying mechanisms if degradation occurs.
- **Mechanistic Understanding:** Beyond simply observing performance changes, a key motivation was to conduct mechanistic exploration at the token level. This involved understanding how privileged teacher context reshapes the learning signal, especially at "high-entropy forking positions" – points where multiple reasoning paths are plausible and exploration is crucial. The authors sought to identify how the distillation process might affect tokens related to self-correction, uncertainty, and branching decisions.
- **Informing Future Self-Improvement Strategies:** By identifying potential failure modes and their causes, the research aims to provide insights that can guide the development of more effective self-distillation methods tailored for strong thinking models, ensuring that privileged feedback enhances problem-solving without undermining critical reasoning processes.

In essence, the research was motivated by a desire to rigorously test the applicability of a widely adopted self-improvement technique (OPSD) to a specific class of highly capable LLMs (thinking models), anticipating a potentially counterintuitive negative outcome and seeking a detailed explanation for it.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The researchers evaluated the impact of privileged-context on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) on thinking models by comparing various models under different distillation and evaluation conditions.

**Distillation Objective:**
The core of the methodology is defined by the on-policy distillation (OPD) objective:
$L_{OPD} = \sum_t D(\pi_T(\cdot | x, c, y_{<t}), \pi_S(\cdot | x, y_{<t}))$.
Here, $y_{<t}$ represents the prefix before token $t$, $\pi_S$ is the student policy being updated, and $\pi_T$ is the teacher policy. The variable $c$ denotes teacher-only privileged context. The divergence $D$ was instantiated as JSD (Jensen-Shannon Divergence).
- **Vanilla OPD:** Corresponds to $c = \emptyset$, meaning the teacher receives no privileged context beyond the problem itself.
- **Privileged-Context OPD:** Sets $c$ to additional information, such as a final answer or a full gold demonstration.
- **OPSD:** A specific case of privileged-context OPD where the teacher and student share the same architecture and typically start from the same checkpoint, with the teacher initialized as a copy of the initial student and receiving privileged context.

**Training Details:**
- **Training Completion Cap:** Unless otherwise noted, training rollouts were capped at 4,096 completion tokens.
- **Epochs and Batch Size:** Training was conducted for one epoch with an effective batch size of 64.
- **Hyperparameters:** Default OPSD training hyperparameters were used, including a cosine decay learning-rate schedule, warmup ratio of 0.1, max gradient norm of 1.0, and bf16 precision.
- **Hardware:** Experiments utilized 8 H100 GPUs (80GB/GPU).

**Training Data:**
Two domains were used for training:
- **Math Reasoning:** An OpenThoughts 15k subset was used. The student prompt was the problem statement, while the privileged teacher context was the full reference solution.
- **Countdown:** 15k examples from jasonrqh/Countdown-CoT-20k were used. The privileged context consisted of the post-thinking solution suffix. A 500-example held-out set was reserved for in-domain evaluation.

**Models and Comparisons:**
The study utilized models from the Qwen3 and OLMo families.
- **Thinking Models:** Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B-Think-2507, and OLMo-7B-Think. These models were primarily used in the OpenThoughts-trained setting.
- **Instruction-tuned Models:** Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, primarily for Countdown tasks, to compare OPSD behavior between model types.
- **Controls:**
 - Comparison of OPSD to vanilla OPD (larger unprivileged teacher).
 - Experiments disabling thinking during OPSD training while keeping it enabled at evaluation.

**Evaluation:**
- **Benchmarks:** Transfer evaluation was performed on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT 2025 (30 problems each). Countdown-trained models were evaluated on the held-out Countdown split.
- **Generation Cap:** A long generation cap of 38,912 tokens per sample was used for main evaluations, with 16 samples generated per problem. This is significantly larger than the training completion cap, designed to stress the models' long-reasoning capabilities.
- **Metrics:** The primary metric was `avg@16` accuracy (mean sample correctness over 16 rollouts per problem). `pass@1` and unbiased `pass@16` were also reported.
- **Evaluation Settings:** Temperature of 0.6-0.7 and top-p of 0.8-0.95 were used depending on the model.

**Diagnostic Techniques:**
To understand the observed degradation, the researchers employed several diagnostic techniques:
1. **Fork-Lock Analysis:** Distinguished "fork" positions (high-entropy, multiple plausible continuations, top candidate probability < 0.25) from "lock" positions (locally constrained, top candidate probability > 0.65) using teacher distributions on sampled rollouts.
2. **Per-Token Distillation Signal Analysis:** Inspected the sign and magnitude of the per-token teacher–student log-ratio along fixed sampled rollouts to identify how privileged context altered the credit assigned to self-correction and uncertainty markers.
3. **Deliberation Marker Analysis:** Quantified the usage of specific lexical markers (e.g., "wait", "hmm", "check", "maybe") related to verification, backtracking, and hedging in generated student rollouts, both as raw counts and length-normalized frequencies. This was done by comparing paired base and OPSD rollouts for the same model, benchmark, problem, and sample index across 7,200 paired rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents five primary observations establishing a failure mode for privileged-context on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) in thinking models:

1. **OPSD's Differential Impact on Instruction-tuned vs. Thinking Models:**
 - OPSD training improved instruction-tuned models (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and OLMo-7B-Instruct) on the Countdown dataset, showing gains in `avg@16` performance.
 - In contrast, for thinking models trained on OpenThoughts, OPSD consistently led to a degradation in `avg@16` performance across all five evaluated thinking models (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B-Think-2507, and OLMo-7B-Think). The average drops ranged from 0.8 to 6.4 percentage points.
 - A control experiment revealed that this degradation was specific to training with *thinking enabled*. When thinking was disabled during OPSD training for Qwen3-4B, performance was preserved, but enabling thinking during training caused a drop.

2. **Degradation is Specific to Privileged Teacher Context:**
 - Vanilla on-policy distillation (OPD), where a larger Qwen3-8B teacher was used without privileged context, *improved* the Qwen3-1.7B thinking student's `avg@16` performance from 0.372 to 0.392.
 - However, when this same larger teacher was conditioned on a privileged gold demonstration (OPD gold demo), the performance decreased to 0.350, reversing the gains from vanilla OPD. OPSD with a full gold demonstration further reduced performance to 0.308.
 - This indicates that the degradation is not a general failure of on-policy distillation or an artifact of the short training budget, but specifically arises when the teacher scores the student's trajectory while having access to information unavailable to the student at test time.

3. **Degradation Manifests at Longer Rollout Budgets:**
 - The performance decline in thinking models was concentrated at long rollout budgets (32,000 to 38,912 tokens). At shorter budgets (4,000–8,000 tokens), OPSD models often performed comparably to or even slightly better than their base counterparts.
 - At the longest budgets, OPSD models consistently produced substantially shorter responses compared to base models. This suggests that OPSD, while potentially compressing reasoning for short-budget efficiency, eliminated the gains thinking models typically obtain from longer, exploratory rollouts.

4. **Students Inherit Shorter Rollouts but Not Teacher's Performance Benefits:**
 - When the base model (acting as the OPSD teacher) was given a gold demonstration in context, its own rollouts became significantly shorter, yet its `pass@k` performance remained high and often improved.
 - The OPSD-trained student also produced shorter responses, similar to the privileged teacher. However, the student's `pass@k` gains were smaller at short budgets and disappeared or reversed at longer budgets, failing to inherit the teacher's long-budget performance advantages.

5. **Impact of Privileged Context Density:**
 - The degree of degradation depended on the density of the privileged context provided to the teacher. Full gold demonstrations caused more substantial long-budget performance reversals and stronger length compression (roughly 0.8x the base length at 38k tokens).
 - Conversely, final-answer-only context resulted in milder short-budget gains, preserved long-budget behavior closer to the base model, and induced less length compression. This suggests that the "harm" scales with the amount of privileged information the teacher receives.

**Mechanistic Analysis (Fork Suppression):**
The research identified "fork suppression" as a mechanism contributing to the observed failures:
- **Shift in Fork–Lock Distribution:** Privileged context significantly lowered "fork rates" (high-entropy decision points) and raised "lock rates" (locally determined continuations) in thinking-model rollouts. This effect was pronounced for thinking models but minimal for instruction-tuned models, indicating a suppression of exploratory branching.
- **Reversal of Signal on Fork Markers:** Token-level analysis showed that privileged teachers assigned *negative* advantage (discouraged) self-correction and uncertainty-marking tokens (e.g., "But wait", "Hmm", "Maybe") even when these tokens appeared in trajectories that ultimately led to the correct answer. The privileged teacher instead shifted positive credit towards locally fluent, but potentially incorrect, continuations.
- **Fewer Deliberation Markers:** OPSD-trained students produced fewer verification, backtracking, and hedging markers in their evaluation rollouts, even after normalizing for length. This directly correlates with a reduction in explicit deliberative behaviors.

These findings collectively indicate that privileged-context self-distillation, rather than enhancing long-range reasoning for thinking models, appears to suppress their critical deliberative and exploratory behaviors, leading to performance degradation, particularly in scenarios requiring extended reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant finding that challenges conventional assumptions regarding the universal applicability of privileged-context on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) for language model self-improvement. The identified failure mode, termed "fork suppression," has several implications:

- **Re-evaluation of OPSD for Thinking Models:** The core finding—that privileged-context OPSD can degrade the performance of strong thinking models, especially over long reasoning traces—necessitates a re-evaluation of current self-improvement recipes. It highlights that techniques beneficial for instruction-tuned models may not be directly transferable or optimally designed for models that rely on extensive test-time deliberation and exploration. This could prevent misapplication of OPSD in contexts where it would be detrimental to complex reasoning capabilities.

- **Mechanistic Understanding of Failure Modes:** By pinpointing "fork suppression" as a mechanism, the study contributes to a deeper, token-level understanding of why self-distillation might fail. The observation that privileged feedback suppresses exploration, branching, and self-correction at high-entropy decision points, even by penalizing markers like "wait" or "maybe" that can lead to correct solutions, provides granular insight into the learning process. This understanding is valuable for diagnosing similar issues in other LLM training paradigms.

- **Guidance for Future Self-Improvement Design:** The findings suggest that future self-distillation methods for thinking models must be more nuanced. Instead of broad, privileged supervision, designers may need to:
 - Explicitly account for "forking positions" and ensure that privileged feedback *improves* solution-directed behavior without *collapsing* the crucial test-time search space.
 - Control the *density* and *application points* of privileged information. The result that sparse, final-answer-only context is less detrimental than dense, full-demonstration context offers a practical guideline for balancing guidance with the preservation of exploratory capacity. This is particularly relevant for training long-horizon agents, where targeted textual feedback is an emerging approach.

- **Implications for Long-Horizon Agents and Exploration:** The research connects directly to the training of long-horizon agents, which often face exploration challenges. The observation that privileged context can harm a model's ability to explore effectively has broad implications for reinforcement learning and other interactive agent training paradigms. It suggests that while privileged information can guide an agent, its unchecked application can inadvertently stifle the very exploratory behaviors necessary for complex problem-solving.

- **Complementary to Existing Work:** This work complements concurrent research on self-distillation failures, such as those related to epistemic verbalization suppression. By framing the issue as a broader "fork suppression" mechanism affecting high-entropy decision points, it provides a more comprehensive perspective on how token-level signals can reshape the entire reasoning process, not just explicit uncertainty language.

While the study focuses on verifiable math tasks and identifies limitations in not proposing a solution, its detailed analysis of a significant failure mode provides a foundation for developing more robust and context-aware self-distillation techniques for sophisticated language models.
