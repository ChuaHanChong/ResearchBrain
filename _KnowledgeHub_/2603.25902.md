# Chasing Autonomy: Dynamic Retargeting and Control Guided RL for Performant and Controllable Humanoid Running

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive pipeline for performant and controllable humanoid running leverages dynamically optimized human motion data and control-guided reinforcement learning. This approach enabled a Unitree G1 robot to run at speeds up to 3.3 m/s and execute real-time obstacle avoidance through integration with a layered autonomy stack.

## Problem

- RL-generated humanoid locomotion often lacks the generalization and precise velocity tracking necessary for autonomous deployment in varied environments.
- Existing methods for generating reference motions, such as purely kinematic retargeting or dynamic optimization without human input, often compromise dynamic feasibility, periodicity, or natural agility.
- Agile locomotion policies typically operate in isolation, hindering their seamless integration into higher-level robotic autonomy stacks that manage perception and planning.

## Method

- A multiple-shooting dynamic optimization routine retargets human running data onto the robot, enforcing dynamic feasibility and periodicity to create a library of gaits at various speeds.
- Reinforcement learning is employed with Control Lyapunov Function (CLF)-guided rewards, combined with explicit goal conditioning, to train policies for robust reference and velocity tracking.
- The developed controller is deployed on a Unitree G1 robot and integrated into a full autonomy stack, utilizing a Model Predictive Control (MPC)+Control Barrier Function (CBF) layer for real-time obstacle avoidance.

## Results

- The Unitree G1 humanoid robot achieved dynamic running speeds of up to 3.3 m/s on a treadmill and demonstrated endurance by traversing over 250 meters consecutively outdoors.
- Simulation ablation studies showed that CLF-RL rewards consistently resulted in lower mean squared tracking errors compared to mimic-style rewards, improving reference and velocity tracking.
- The running controller successfully integrated into an autonomy stack for real-time obstacle avoidance, enabling the robot to dynamically dodge detected obstacles at approximately 2 m/s in an outdoor setting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically optimized human motion data, which ensures dynamic feasibility and periodicity, yields more accurate and performant reference trajectories for RL than purely kinematic retargeting or dynamic optimization alone.
> - Control Lyapunov Function (CLF)-based reward structures, particularly when combined with goal conditioning for velocity targets, significantly improve both reference tracking and overall velocity tracking performance in RL policies.
> - The integration of a robust dynamic running controller with a layered autonomy stack, including real-time perception and planning, facilitates practical humanoid navigation and obstacle avoidance in complex environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen progress in enabling dynamic locomotion, including agile running. A persistent challenge remains in generalizing and controlling these motions for autonomous operation over extended durations and in varied environments. Historically, approaches to bipedal locomotion have included classical control methods, such as the Raibert heuristic for footstep placement and the hybrid zero dynamics (HZD) methodology, which stabilizes nonlinear systems around reference trajectories often derived from dynamic trajectory optimization or human data.

More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a prominent tool for synthesizing dynamic controllers for bipedal and humanoid robots. RL's ability to leverage full nonlinear dynamics, manage contact interactions, and use domain randomization for robustness has led to controllers capable of complex behaviors, including running and agile maneuvers. However, many current RL-based methods, particularly those that "mimic" human motions, are often limited to single motion playback. While these mimic-style controllers achieve high agility by tracking kinematically retargeted human data, they typically lack the versatility and control required for autonomous locomotion, where robots need to respond to external commands (e.g., velocity targets) and integrate into higher-level planning and perception stacks.

This paper addresses the gap between highly dynamic, human-like locomotion generated by RL and the practical requirements for autonomous deployment. It builds upon previous RL work in reference tracking and goal-conditioned rewards, while also drawing from model-based control concepts like Control Lyapunov Functions (CLFs) to enhance stability and controllability. Furthermore, it contributes to the area of motion retargeting, where human motion data is adapted for robotic platforms, by proposing a method that ensures dynamic feasibility and periodicity, moving beyond purely kinematic retargeting or dynamic optimization without human input. The work aims to develop locomotion controllers that can accurately track commanded velocities, thereby facilitating their integration into layered autonomy architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the disparity between the dynamic capabilities demonstrated by RL-controlled humanoids and their limitations in autonomous, long-duration, and controllable locomotion. While RL has shown promise in generating agile human-like motions, these behaviors often lack the necessary generalization and precise velocity tracking required for deployment within complex robotic systems, such as those involving perception and planning for obstacle avoidance. Autonomous robots typically rely on layered architectures where locomotion controllers receive velocity commands from higher-level planners. If a locomotion policy cannot accurately track these commands, its utility in such an architecture is significantly diminished. The paper seeks to bridge this gap by developing performant and controllable humanoid running capabilities.

The research addresses three main questions, which also serve as its key objectives:

1. **How can agile reference motions be generated and designed to yield optimal reference tracking performance?**
 - **Motivation:** Existing methods for obtaining reference motions often involve kinematically retargeting human data, which may not be dynamically feasible for a robot, can be noisy, and generally lacks periodicity for steady-state gaits. Alternatively, purely dynamic trajectory optimization can generate feasible motions but may lack the natural agility derived from human data. The objective is to develop a method that leverages the strengths of human data while ensuring dynamic feasibility and improving tracking accuracy.

2. **How does the reward structure influence both the ability to track references and the ability to track desired velocities?**
 - **Motivation:** Reward design is a critical aspect of RL, and a suboptimal choice can hinder performance or training efficiency. For reference tracking RL, various reward structures exist, including "mimic-style" rewards that track multiple components (joints, body poses) and more principled control-guided rewards, such as those based on Control Lyapunov Functions (CLFs). Additionally, different strategies for enabling velocity tracking—such as kinematically adjusting reference trajectories or using goal-conditioned rewards—have varying impacts on the robot's ability to follow external commands. The objective is to rigorously compare these reward structures and velocity tracking mechanisms to identify the most effective combination.

3. **Can dynamic motions, such as running, be integrated into a layered autonomy stack?**
 - **Motivation:** The ultimate goal for advanced humanoid locomotion is autonomous operation in real-world environments. This requires not only dynamic and robust motion but also the ability to interface seamlessly with higher-level perception, planning, and decision-making modules. Current agile locomotion policies often operate in isolation. The objective is to demonstrate that the developed controller, capable of dynamic running, can receive and execute commands from an autonomy stack, specifically for tasks like obstacle avoidance, thereby validating its practical utility.

These objectives collectively aim to advance humanoid locomotion from a performance demonstration to a component capable of supporting full autonomy in complex, dynamic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper involves a multi-stage pipeline, beginning with optimized reference trajectory generation, followed by reinforcement learning (RL) controller synthesis using specific reward structures, and culminating in hardware deployment and integration into an autonomy stack.

**A. Hybrid Systems Model and Control Lyapunov Functions (CLFs)**
The underlying mathematical framework for dynamic retargeting utilizes an explicit hybrid system model to represent legged locomotion. This model accounts for both continuous dynamics (e.g., during single support or flight phases) and discrete events (e.g., foot impacts, take-offs). The system is defined by its state manifold, input space, guards (conditions for transitions), reset maps (state changes at transitions), and continuous dynamics. For running, the model considers single support phase (SSP) and flight phase (FLT) domains, with associated reset maps for take-off and impact.

Control Lyapunov Functions (CLFs) are a component of the reward structure. For a given output tracking error, a quadratic Lyapunov function is constructed. The reward formulation incorporates terms that incentivize the decrease of this Lyapunov function, thereby encouraging stable behavior and convergence of the robot's state to the reference trajectory.

**B. Reference Generation Through Optimized Dynamic Retargeting**
To address the limitations of direct human data usage (embodiment gap, noise, lack of periodicity), a multiple-shooting dynamic optimization routine is employed to retarget human motion data.
1. **Human Data Selection:** A segment of human running data (from the LAFAN dataset) is kinematically retargeted onto the robot's kinematics. A single, periodic stride, consisting of both a single support and a flight phase, is programmatically extracted.
2. **Multiple-Shooting Optimization:** This optimization problem encourages the robot's motion to mimic the human data while enforcing hard constraints.
 - **Dynamics and Constraints:** The optimization uses discretized hybrid dynamics, with fixed mode sequences (SSP, FLT). Holonomic constraints and friction cones are enforced during SSP, and ground reaction forces are zero during FLT. Joint limits, torque limits, and state constraints are incorporated.
 - **Periodicity:** Crucially, periodicity for a steady-state gait is enforced by applying impact equations on a reflected state, meaning only one stride needs to be optimized, with the other being its mirror image.
 - **Cost Function:** The objective function minimizes the squared tracking error between the robot's state (base position, orientation, joint angles, and velocities) and the desired human reference state.
 - **Gait Library Generation:** By constraining forward step length and using warm starts from faster gait solutions, a library of dynamically feasible running gaits at various speeds (1.2 m/s to 3.6 m/s) is generated from a single human demonstration.
3. **Trajectory Representation:** The optimized trajectories are then fitted with Bezier curves, representing desired "outputs" (joint angles, pelvis poses, end-effector poses, and their velocities), which serve as the targets for the RL controller.

**C. Reward Structure and RL Design**
Two primary reference tracking reward types are investigated:
1. **CLF-RL Rewards:** This approach embeds a Lyapunov function and its decrescent condition into the reward. It includes a reward for tracking the reference based on the exponential decay of the Lyapunov function ($exp(-V_t/\sigma_v)$) and a penalty for not satisfying the CLF decrescent condition.
2. **Mimic Rewards:** These rewards incentivize tracking of various components of the reference trajectory separately, typically including joints, base position, base orientation, and end-effector positions/orientations, along with their velocities. These are often structured as Gaussian-like terms ($exp(-\|z - z_d\|^2/\sigma_z)$) for each component.
In addition to these, **regularization rewards** (e.g., for torque, action-rate, torque limits, joint limits) and **contact rewards** (for holonomic position and velocity during contact) are used across all methods.

**Goal Conditioning and Velocity Tracking:** To enable the robot to track desired linear and angular velocities beyond the straight-line reference, two methods are explored:
1. **Kinematic Adjustment:** The reference trajectory is kinematically adjusted in the training loop to incorporate yaw or lateral motions.
2. **Goal Conditioning Reward:** An explicit reward term encourages the robot's root velocity (linear x, y, and yaw rate) to match the commanded velocity, even if it deviates from the reference motion.

**RL Design and Training:**
- **Simulation Environment:** IsaacLab and IsaacSim provide GPU-accelerated physics simulation.
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used with RSL-RL's implementation.
- **Network Architecture:** Asymmetric actor-critic setup with fully connected feedforward networks (three hidden layers, ELU activations).
- **Observations:** The actor receives base angular velocity, projected gravity, commanded velocity, joint angles, joint velocities, previous action, and sine/cosine phases. The critic receives additional privileged information, including reference trajectory data and contact states.
- **Velocity Command Training:** The policy is trained with diverse velocity commands (open loop, heading control, P controller for heading/lateral position) to learn robust tracking.
- **Initialization:** Episodes are initialized from the reference trajectory corresponding to the desired speed. To encourage transition learning, 40% of environments start from a slower walking reference (1 m/s). The running policy covers speeds from 1.1 to 3.7 m/s.
- **Domain Randomization:** Friction, mass, and motor properties are randomized during training, along with periodic velocity-based pushes, to enhance sim-to-real transferability.
- **Hardware Control:** The learned policy outputs joint angle setpoints at 50 Hz, which are then tracked by PD controllers with over-damped gains to prevent high-frequency chatter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from simulation ablation studies and real-world hardware deployments to validate the proposed methods.

**A. Reference Motion and Tracking Reward Ablation Study**
This simulation study compared different reference motion generation methods and reward structures using 200 robots in IsaacSim, each tracking a single velocity command for 7 seconds. The error metric was the mean squared tracking error averaged across environments and time.

- **Reference Motion Quality:** The dynamically optimized human data references consistently resulted in more accurate reference tracking compared to kinematically retargeted human data and dynamic optimization without human data. This suggests that incorporating dynamic feasibility and periodicity into the human data retargeting process is beneficial for tracking performance. The optimized human data also outperformed hand-crafted dynamic optimization without human data, indicating the value of leveraging human motion characteristics.
- **Reward Structure Effectiveness:** For every reference type, the CLF-RL rewards yielded a lower tracking error than the mimic-style rewards. This finding indicates that the control-guided reward structure, which explicitly encourages theoretically sound stability to the reference, can lead to more precise tracking performance.
- **Conclusion for Q1 & Q2 (partially):** Dynamically optimized human data provides the most effective reference motions, and CLF-RL rewards outperform mimic rewards in reference tracking. The quality of the reference trajectory is a critical factor for accurate tracking.

**B. Velocity Tracking Ablation Study**
This study investigated the impact of reference type, reward structure, and goal conditioning on velocity tracking performance in Mujoco, with policies trained to track a range of forward, lateral, and yaw velocities. Domain randomization was applied (torso mass, CoM position, D gains), and 50 trials were logged per speed. Key metrics included forward velocity error ($e_{v_x}$), lateral velocity error ($e_{v_y}$), and yaw rate error ($e_{\dot{\psi}}$).

- **Goal Conditioning vs. Kinematic Adjustment:** While kinematically adjusting the trajectory showed some tracking capability, explicit goal conditioning in the reward provided a stronger signal during training, generally leading to better velocity tracking.
- **CLF Rewards for Velocity Tracking:** The CLF-RL reward structure generally facilitated better overall velocity tracking compared to mimic rewards.
- **Dynamic Optimized Reference Library vs. Single Kinematic Trajectory:** Even when increasing the goal conditioning weight threefold for kinematically retargeted human data, the policies trained with a dynamic optimized reference library (with goal conditioning) yielded superior velocity tracking performance. This highlights the benefit of having a library of dynamically feasible references for various speeds, generated through optimization, rather than relying on a single, potentially less versatile, kinematically retargeted trajectory.
- **Performance at Lower Speeds:** The dynamic optimization of human data significantly improved tracking at lower speeds. The original human reference was a high-speed run, meaning its "style" was most accurate for faster speeds. The optimization allowed for better tracking across a broader velocity range by adapting the high-speed human motion effectively.
- **Conclusion for Q2 (fully):** Goal conditioning is crucial for improving velocity tracking, and its effectiveness is maximized when paired with a dynamic reference library and a CLF-RL tracking reward.

**C. Hardware Results, Integration with Autonomy Stack**
The developed policy was deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot in various real-world environments.

- **High-Speed and Endurance Running:** The robot achieved speeds of up to 3.3 m/s on an indoor treadmill, demonstrating its capability for dynamic running with a full flight phase. In outdoor tests, the robot successfully traversed distances exceeding 250 meters consecutively on sidewalks (Fig. 5). For these long-range experiments, the running controller operated within a layered control stack, receiving feedforward velocity commands and tracking global yaw angles and target positions (using lateral motion) via a P controller, with pose estimates from FastLio.
- **Autonomous Obstacle Avoidance:** The running controller was integrated into a full perception and planning autonomy stack for real-time obstacle avoidance (Fig. 6). A Model Predictive Control (MPC) algorithm, utilizing real-time lidar scans to build a local occupancy map and a Poisson safety function, generated velocity commands for the running controller. This MPC+CBF (Control Barrier Function) layer planned collision-free paths. The robot maintained forward speeds around 2 m/s while dynamically dodging detected obstacles in an outdoor environment.
- **Conclusion for Q3:** The hardware demonstrations confirm that the dynamic running controller is controllable and can be successfully integrated into a layered autonomy stack for tasks like long-range navigation and perceptive obstacle avoidance, thereby demonstrating its practical utility in real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a comprehensive pipeline for enabling performant and controllable humanoid running, addressing key limitations of existing RL-based locomotion methods. The significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Advancement in Reference Motion Generation:** The method of dynamically retargeting human data through constrained multiple-shooting optimization represents a notable step forward. By ensuring dynamic feasibility, periodicity, and generating a library of gaits from a single human demonstration, it provides higher-quality reference trajectories than purely kinematic retargeting or dynamic optimization without human inspiration. This approach can lead to more agile and robust robot behaviors that retain the natural characteristics of human movement while being physically realizable for the robot.
- **Enhanced Controllability through Reward Design:** The comparative study on reward structures demonstrates that control-guided RL (CLF-RL) combined with goal conditioning significantly improves both reference and velocity tracking performance. This finding emphasizes the importance of incorporating theoretical control principles into RL reward functions to achieve more stable and controllable dynamic behaviors, which is crucial for real-world deployment. The ability to accurately track commanded velocities (linear x, y, and yaw rate) transforms dynamic running from a mere demonstration into a usable skill for higher-level autonomy.
- **Practical Autonomous Locomotion:** The successful deployment of the policy on a Unitree G1 robot, achieving high speeds (up to 3.3 m/s), demonstrating endurance over hundreds of meters outdoors, and, critically, integrating into a real-time autonomy stack for obstacle avoidance, highlights the practical utility of this work. This is a significant step towards humanoids operating autonomously in complex, dynamic, and unstructured environments. The capability to perform collision avoidance while running at speed opens avenues for applications in dynamic inspection, logistics, search and rescue, or human-robot collaboration in challenging terrains.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The effective transfer of policies trained in simulation (IsaacLab/IsaacSim) to hardware, supported by robust domain randomization, contributes to the ongoing efforts in making simulation-trained policies viable for real robots.
- **Future Research Directions:** The pipeline provides a foundation for developing more versatile and robust humanoid skills. Future work could explore more complex motion libraries, adaptation to diverse terrains, or integration with manipulation tasks while maintaining dynamic running capabilities.

In summary, this research contributes to moving humanoid locomotion beyond pre-programmed or single-motion playback demonstrations towards truly autonomous, performant, and controllable operation in dynamic environments, facilitated by principled motion generation and reward design.
