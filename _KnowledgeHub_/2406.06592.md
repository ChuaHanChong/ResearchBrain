# Improve Mathematical Reasoning in Language Models by Automated Process Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> Luo et al. introduce OmegaPRM, a Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithm, to automate the generation of over 1.5 million per-step process supervision annotations for large language models. This method efficiently creates data for training Process Reward Models, leading to substantial improvements in LLM mathematical reasoning on benchmarks like MATH500 and GSM8K.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with complex, multi-step reasoning tasks like mathematics, where intermediate errors can propagate unnoticed.
- Process Reward Models (PRMs), which provide granular step-by-step feedback, require extensive and costly human-annotated process supervision data.
- Existing automated Monte Carlo methods for generating process supervision data are inefficient due to their brute-force approach of evaluating every intermediate step.

## Method

- Introduces ==OmegaPRM==, a divide-and-conquer ==Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)== algorithm designed to automate and efficiently generate process supervision data.
- Utilizes a ==binary-search-based Monte Carlo estimation== within rollouts to pinpoint the first error in a reasoning chain, significantly reducing the complexity of correctness evaluation.
- Constructs a state-action tree similar to MCTS to effectively reuse generated rollouts and strategically select valuable examples (supposed-to-be-correct wrong-answer rollouts) for ==PRM training==.

## Results

- OmegaPRM successfully generated over **1.5 million** per-step process supervision annotations without human intervention.
- The algorithm demonstrated a **75x** efficiency improvement over brute-force Monte Carlo methods for data generation, yielding **1.5 million** data points versus **200K** for the same computational budget.
- PRMs trained with OmegaPRM data boosted Gemini Pro's success rate on MATH500 from **51%** to **69.4%** and on GSM8K from **86.4%** to **93.6%**, with similar gains for Gemma2 27B.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Automated process supervision data generation using an MCTS-inspired algorithm, OmegaPRM, is highly efficient and scalable, enabling the creation of large datasets without human intervention.
> - Training Process Reward Models (PRMs) with these automatically generated, high-quality process supervision datasets substantially enhances large language models' mathematical reasoning abilities.
> - The pointwise soft label objective, which uses direct Monte Carlo estimation as the correctness score, proves effective for PRM training, yielding better classification accuracy than hard or pairwise labels.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has witnessed remarkable progress, yet complex multi-step reasoning tasks, such as solving mathematical problems or generating intricate code, remain a significant challenge. Traditional methods for enhancing LLM reasoning often involve Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022b), fine-tuning on question-CoT solution pairs (Ouyang et al., 2022), and Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022). While these techniques have improved performance, they often fall short for problems requiring lengthy or multi-hop reasoning chains, where intermediate errors can propagate unnoticed.

A crucial development in addressing this limitation is the use of reward models, which provide feedback to guide LLM behavior. Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) (Cobbe et al., 2021) offer feedback only on the final answer, making them less effective for multi-step tasks where the *process* of reasoning is critical. This led to the emergence of Process Reward Models (PRMs) (Li et al., 2023; Lightman et al., 2023), which provide granular, step-by-step feedback. PRMs have consistently shown superior effectiveness for complex reasoning by identifying errors precisely where they occur.

However, the primary bottleneck for widespread PRM adoption has been the exorbitant cost and labor intensity of obtaining process supervision signals. Historically, this has relied on extensive human annotation by domain experts (Lightman et al., 2023; Uesato et al., 2022). Recent attempts to automate this process, such as Math-Shepherd (Wang et al., 2024a) and MiPS (Wang et al., 2024b), utilized per-step Monte Carlo estimation. While promising, these methods still face efficiency limitations due to the vast search space and computational overhead of brute-force rollouts for every step.

This paper positions itself directly within this landscape by tackling the scalability and efficiency challenges of PRM data collection. It builds upon the concept of automated Monte Carlo estimation for process supervision but introduces a significant algorithmic innovation: a **divide-and-conquer Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm called OmegaPRM**. By drawing inspiration from the success of MCTS in reinforcement learning (e.g., AlphaGo Zero, Silver et al., 2017) and adapting it to the unique characteristics of LLMs (such as their ability to generate full rollouts), the authors aim to generate high-quality process supervision data much more efficiently and without human intervention. This work represents a crucial step towards making PRM training more practical and widely applicable, further advancing LLM capabilities in complex reasoning domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **significantly improve the mathematical reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models**. The authors identify the critical role of Process Reward Models (PRMs) in achieving this, as PRMs provide the necessary fine-grained, step-by-step feedback that Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) lack for complex, multi-step problems.

The core motivation driving this work stems from the current limitations in acquiring the essential training data for PRMs: **process supervision data**. Existing methods for collecting this data are either prohibitively expensive or inefficient:
- **Human Annotation:** Relying on human experts to annotate the correctness of each intermediate step in a solution is extremely costly, time-consuming, and difficult to scale to the large datasets required for training powerful LLMs. This bottleneck severely restricts the broad application of PRMs.
- **Automated Monte Carlo Estimation (Brute-Force):** While a step towards automation, current Monte Carlo methods for process supervision data generation (e.g., Math-Shepherd, MiPS) employ a brute-force approach. This involves performing numerous rollouts for *every* intermediate step in a solution to estimate its correctness, leading to high computational costs and inefficiency, especially for long reasoning chains.

In response to these challenges, the paper sets forth several key objectives:
1. **Develop a Novel, Efficient Algorithm for Automated Process Supervision Data Generation:** The primary objective is to propose and implement OmegaPRM, a divide-and-conquer style Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithm. This algorithm aims to efficiently identify the first error in a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and effectively reuse rollouts, thereby overcoming the scalability and efficiency limitations of prior automated methods.
2. **Generate a Large-Scale, High-Quality Process Supervision Dataset:** A direct objective is to use OmegaPRM to collect a substantial volume of process supervision annotations (over 1.5 million) without any human intervention, making the data collection process financially and computationally cost-effective.
3. **Train Effective Process Reward Models (PRMs):** Utilize the automatically generated process supervision data to train robust PRMs capable of accurately evaluating the correctness of intermediate reasoning steps.
4. **Demonstrate Enhanced LLM Mathematical Reasoning Performance:** Evaluate the efficacy of the trained PRMs, when combined with decoding strategies like weighted self-consistency, in boosting the success rates of state-of-the-art LLMs (Gemini Pro, Gemma2 27B) on challenging mathematical benchmarks like MATH500 and GSM8K.

Ultimately, the motivation is to unlock the full potential of process supervision for complex reasoning tasks by making its data acquisition process automated, scalable, and economically viable, thereby advancing the frontier of LLM capabilities in rigorous domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology revolves around **OmegaPRM**, a novel Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm designed for automated and efficient generation of process supervision data. The approach contrasts with traditional human annotation and brute-force Monte Carlo methods by employing a more strategic search and reuse of generated content.

#### 3.1. Process Supervision (Re-contextualization)

The authors first re-establish the distinction between Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) and Process Reward Models (PRMs). ORMs assess only the final answer's correctness, while PRMs evaluate each intermediate step of a solution ($x_t$). The challenge with PRMs lies in acquiring step-level correctness labels, historically necessitating expensive human annotation.

#### 3.2. Process Annotation with Monte Carlo Method: From Brute-Force to Binary Search

Building on prior work (Math-Shepherd, MiPS), the paper utilizes a "completer" policy (an LLM) to generate "rollouts"—full completions of a solution given a prefix. The correctness of these rollouts, compared to a golden answer, provides a Monte Carlo (MC) estimation of the prefix's correctness.
- **Brute-Force Limitation:** The traditional method involves sampling $k$ rollouts for *every* step, leading to an $O(kM)$ time complexity, where $M$ is the number of steps. This is inefficient.
- **Binary Search Optimization:** Recognizing that PRMs primarily need to identify the *first incorrect step*, the authors propose a binary-search-based MC estimation. Instead of evaluating every step, they repeatedly divide the solution at its midpoint. By performing rollouts from the midpoint, they determine if the error lies in the first or second half. This process continues iteratively, narrowing down the error location with $O(k \log M)$ complexity, a significant efficiency gain.

#### 3.3. Monte Carlo Tree Search (OmegaPRM)

While binary search improves single-solution annotation, collecting multiple PRM training examples (question, partial solution, correctness label triplets) for a single question still involves redundant effort. OmegaPRM addresses this by treating the process of collecting PRM training examples as constructing a state-action tree, similar to MCTS used in game-playing AI like AlphaGo.

- **Tree Structure:** Each node in the tree represents a state (question + prefix solution), and edges represent a reasoning step. Nodes store visit counts ($N(s)$), Monte Carlo estimation ($MC(s)$), and a state-rollout value function ($Q(s,r)$).
- **Adaptation for LLMs:** Traditional MCTS assumes a finite action space. For LLMs, which have infinite action spaces, OmegaPRM samples a fixed number ($k$) of completions using temperature sampling. Crucially, LLMs can sample a *full rollout* (to the final answer) from any state, which facilitates the binary search step.
- **Three Phases of OmegaPRM Iteration:**
 1. **Select:** A rollout $(s, r)$ is chosen from a pool of previously generated rollouts that have $0 < MC(s) < 1$. The selection strategy prioritizes "supposed-to-be-correct wrong-answer rollouts" using a heuristic $Q(s,r) = \alpha^{1-MC(s)} \cdot \beta^{\frac{\text{len}(r)}{L}}$, combined with a PUCT-like exploration term $U(s)$. The intuition is to surface examples where the model *should* have been correct but made a mistake, as these are valuable for PRM training.
 2. **Binary Search:** The selected rollout $(s, r)$ undergoes the binary search procedure (as described above) to pinpoint its first error location. This process generates new intermediate states and their corresponding MC estimations. All intermediate states with $0 < MC(s) < 1$ are added to the selection candidate pool for future iterations.
 3. **Maintain:** After the binary search, the tree statistics ($N(s), MC(s), Q(s,r)$) are updated for the involved nodes and newly sampled rollouts. This is akin to the backup phase in traditional MCTS.
- **Tree Construction:** These iterations continue until a predefined search limit is reached or no more rollout candidates are available. The resulting tree provides numerous (question, partial solution, correctness label) triplets.

#### 3.4. PRM Training

Each single-step edge $(s,a)$ extracted from the constructed state-action tree serves as a training example for the PRM. The PRM is trained to predict the correctness of step $a$ given prefix $s$.
- **Labeling Objectives:** The authors explore three loss functions for PRM training:
 - **Pointwise Soft Label:** Uses the Monte Carlo estimation $MC(s)$ directly as the correctness label ($\hat{y} = MC(s)$).
 - **Pointwise Hard Label:** Binarizes the MC estimation ($\hat{y} = 1$ if $MC(s)>0$, else $0$).
 - **Pairwise Loss:** Minimizes cross-entropy loss between PRM predictions and normalized pairwise preferences based on the Bradley-Terry model, as commonly used in RLHF.
The paper notes that pointwise soft label was used for the main results.

#### 3.5. Data Generation and Model Setup

Experiments were conducted on the MATH dataset (12K training, 500 test problems) and GSM8K. The search limit for OmegaPRM was set to 100 per question, generating over 1.5 million per-step annotations. Question filtering was applied to reduce noise from overly easy or hard problems. Proprietary Gemini Pro and open-source Gemma2 27B models were used as base LLMs, with PRMs trained from their pretrained checkpoints.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates that OmegaPRM effectively automates and scales the generation of high-quality process supervision data, leading to significant improvements in the mathematical reasoning abilities of LLMs.

1. **Efficient Process Supervision Data Generation:**
 - OmegaPRM successfully generated over **1.5 million** per-step process supervision annotations. This represents a substantial dataset size, larger than previously available, and was achieved entirely **without human intervention**.
 - **Algorithm Efficiency:** Quantitative evaluation showed that OmegaPRM offers a **75-times efficiency improvement** over brute-force Monte Carlo methods. For the same computational budget, OmegaPRM generated 1.5 million data points compared to 200K by brute-force. This validates its design for cost-effectiveness and scalability.

2. **Superior Performance of OmegaPRM-Trained PRMs:**
 - **Gemini Pro:** The instruction-tuned Gemini Pro model, when combined with a PRM trained on OmegaPRM data and weighted self-consistency decoding, showed remarkable improvements:
 - On **MATH500**, success rates increased from a base of **51% to 69.4%**, representing an absolute increase of 18.4 percentage points and a relative improvement of 36%.
 - On **GSM8K**, success rates improved from **86.4% to 93.6%**.
 - **Gemma2 27B:** Similar substantial gains were observed for the pretrained Gemma2 27B model:
 - On **MATH500**, success rates increased from **42.3% to 58.2%**.
 - On **GSM8K**, success rates improved from **74.0% to 92.2%**.
 - **Comparison to Baselines:** OmegaPRM consistently outperformed PRMs trained on other existing process supervision datasets, including human-annotated PRM800K and brute-force automated Math-Shepherd. Figure 3 visually confirms that while other PRMs' performance converges with majority voting at higher sample counts, the OmegaPRM-trained PRM maintains a clear accuracy margin.

3. **Flexible Step Division:**
 - The proposed flexible method for step division (treating any sequence of consecutive tokens as a step, with binary search terminating when a step is sufficiently short) produced a step length distribution similar to that of rule-based strategies. This suggests that semantic coherence in step splitting (e.g., by newlines) is not strictly necessary for effective PRM training, allowing for greater flexibility in data generation.

4. **Optimal PRM Training Objective:**
 - A comparison of different PRM training objectives (pointwise soft label, pointwise hard label, pairwise loss) revealed that the **pointwise soft label** (using the direct Monte Carlo estimation as the correctness score) yielded the best PRM classification accuracy at **70.1%**, indicating its effectiveness in capturing the nuanced correctness signals.

In summary, the main findings highlight OmegaPRM's ability to efficiently generate a large volume of high-quality process supervision data, which in turn enables the training of highly effective PRMs that significantly boost the mathematical reasoning performance of advanced LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of OmegaPRM and its demonstrated effectiveness carries significant implications for the future of Large Language Models, particularly in their ability to handle complex, multi-step reasoning tasks.

1. **Democratization of Process Supervision:** The most profound impact of this work is the **automation and scalability of process supervision data collection**. By eliminating the reliance on costly and slow human annotation, OmegaPRM makes high-quality, fine-grained supervision signals accessible to a much broader range of researchers and practitioners. This significantly reduces the financial and computational barriers to training powerful Process Reward Models, potentially democratizing access to this advanced technique.

2. **Enhanced LLM Reasoning Capabilities:** The substantial performance gains achieved by Gemini Pro and Gemma2 27B on challenging mathematical benchmarks (MATH500, GSM8K) directly demonstrate OmegaPRM's efficacy. This improvement is not just incremental; an 18.4% absolute increase on MATH500 for Gemini Pro signifies a qualitative leap in reasoning ability. This suggests that LLMs, when equipped with PRMs trained on such rich, automatically generated feedback, can become significantly more robust and reliable problem-solvers.

3. **Broader Applicability to Complex Reasoning:** While the paper focuses on mathematical reasoning, the principles of process supervision and efficient error identification through an MCTS-like approach are highly transferable. This methodology could be applied to other complex, multi-step reasoning domains where intermediate correctness is critical, such as:
 - **Code Generation and Debugging:** Identifying the first erroneous line or logical flaw in generated code.
 - **Scientific Discovery and Hypothesis Generation:** Evaluating the validity of intermediate steps in a scientific argument or experimental design.
 - **Robotics and Planning:** Providing feedback on sub-goals or actions in a long-horizon planning task.
 - **Medical Diagnosis:** Verifying the step-by-step reasoning in a diagnostic process.

4. **Advancement in Automated Feedback Mechanisms:** OmegaPRM contributes to the broader field of automated feedback and self-improvement for AI systems. By efficiently creating training signals from model rollouts and ground truth, it moves towards a more autonomous learning paradigm where AI systems can generate and learn from their own "experience," akin to how AlphaGo Zero learned from self-play.

5. **Future Research Directions:** The paper openly acknowledges limitations, such as the inherent noise in automated annotation and the continued need for a "golden answer" for ground truth verification. These limitations, however, open fertile ground for future research. Exploring methods to refine noise, integrate human feedback more selectively, or adapt the methodology for open-ended tasks where a single golden answer is not available will be crucial next steps, potentially leading to even more powerful and versatile reasoning AI.

In conclusion, OmegaPRM represents a significant algorithmic advancement that bridges the gap between the theoretical promise of process supervision and its practical, scalable implementation. By making high-quality process supervision data readily available, this work paves the way for more intelligent, reliable, and autonomously improving large language models across a wide spectrum of complex reasoning challenges.
