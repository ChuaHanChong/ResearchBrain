# Towards General Purpose Robots at Scale: Lifelong Learning and Learning to Use Memory

> [!summary] Summary
> This work by William H Yue introduces t-DGR and AttentionTuner, two methods addressing challenges in developing general-purpose robots capable of long-term autonomous operation. t-DGR enables robots to continuously acquire new skills without forgetting, achieving state-of-the-art success rates on continual learning benchmarks, while AttentionTuner allows explicit human guidance for memory utilization, leading to improved performance and efficiency in memory-intensive tasks.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with prolonged autonomous operation in unstructured environments due to limitations in continuous skill acquisition and effective memory recall.
- Existing lifelong learning methods suffer from catastrophic forgetting and either high sample complexity (i.i.d. state generation) or compounding errors (autoregressive trajectory generation).
- Learning to use memory in Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) is inefficient with standard imitation learning, as expert demonstrations do not explicitly capture memory strategies, leading to unstable optimization in Transformer models.

## Method

- **t-DGR** (trajectory-based Deep Generative Replay) is proposed for lifelong learning, generating state observations conditioned on their timestep within a trajectory to ensure uniform coverage and avoid compounding errors during replay.
- AttentionTuner addresses memory utilization by allowing human experts to provide 'memory dependency pairs' as annotations, explicitly guiding a ==Transformer=='s self-attention mechanism to recall relevant past observations.
- **t-DGR** utilizes a U-net ==diffusion model== as a generator and trains a policy with behavioral cloning, while AttentionTuner integrates a binary cross-entropy memory loss into a Transformer's first attention layer.

## Results

- t-DGR achieved state-of-the-art average success rates on Continual World benchmarks (**81.9%** on CW10 and **83.9%** on CW20), demonstrating robustness to blurry task boundaries with an **81.7%** success rate on the custom BB10 benchmark.
- AttentionTuner significantly improved success rates on memory-intensive tasks (e.g., **99.8%** on Mortar Mayhem compared to **20.8%** for a vanilla Transformer), exhibiting faster convergence and enhanced generalizability.
- Memory dependency annotations provided substantial human labor savings, with **0.1%** annotated trajectories yielding comparable performance to full annotation and an estimated 14-16 times reduction in expert effort compared to collecting additional full demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditioning state generation on timestep within a generative replay mechanism (t-DGR) is more effective for continual learning than i.i.d. or autoregressive methods, improving sample efficiency and mitigating error accumulation.
> - Explicitly teaching memory strategies via 'memory dependency pairs' (AttentionTuner) significantly enhances the stability, efficiency, and generalization capabilities of Transformer-based agents in POMDPs.
> - Diffusion models demonstrate superior performance as generators in pseudo-rehearsal for continual learning compared to GANs, especially for lower-dimensional sensor data, simplifying the overall learning framework.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Artificial intelligence (AI) has achieved considerable success in domains such as natural language processing and computer vision, largely due to the availability of extensive datasets and the development of large neural networks. However, the field of robotics has not yet realized a similar level of widespread deployment, with progress often constrained to controlled environments. This disparity is attributed to factors including the scarcity of large-scale training data relevant to robotics and the inherent complexity of real-world tasks that robots must address.

A long-term objective in robot learning research involves establishing a "data flywheel" through the widespread deployment of general-purpose robots. The premise is that increased robot usage would generate more diverse data, which in turn would facilitate the development of more capable models and robots, thereby attracting further users. Achieving this necessitates robots that can operate autonomously over long time horizons—ranging from hours to weeks—in unstructured environments such as homes and workplaces, rather than being limited to short-duration, pre-defined tasks.

Current robot learning systems are predominantly designed for short-horizon tasks and typically rely on immediate or very recent observations. These systems often employ fixed models that are unable to adapt or acquire new skills post-deployment as environments evolve. This research addresses two key challenges that impede robots from operating effectively over long time horizons:
1. **Lifelong Learning (or Continual Learning)**: The ability of a robot to continuously acquire new skills and adapt to changing environments without "catastrophic forgetting" of previously learned knowledge.
2. **Memory Utilization**: The capacity for a robot to effectively recall and leverage relevant past experiences for decision-making in partially observable environments.

The paper posits that overcoming these challenges requires fundamental advancements beyond simply scaling existing methods. It seeks to contribute to the development of adaptive lifelong learning algorithms and capable memory mechanisms essential for large-scale, real-world robot deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to contribute to the development of general-purpose robots capable of operating autonomously at scale in everyday, unstructured environments over extended time horizons. To achieve this, the work focuses on addressing two specific, interdependent challenges: lifelong learning and the effective utilization of memory.

**Motivation for Lifelong Learning:**
Current robot learning systems are not designed for continuous operation and adaptation after deployment. When confronted with new tasks or environmental changes, these systems often suffer from "catastrophic forgetting," where learning new information degrades or erases previously acquired skills. Deep Generative Replay (DGR) methods have shown promise in mitigating this issue by generating synthetic data from past tasks to reinforce prior knowledge. However, existing DGR approaches for decision-making tasks face limitations:
- **Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) state generation**: Methods that generate individual state observations i.i.d. without considering the temporal structure of trajectories suffer from high sample complexity. This means a disproportionately large number of generated samples are required to adequately cover all timesteps of a trajectory, especially when replay time is limited.
- **Autoregressive trajectory generation**: Methods that generate trajectories autoregressively (predicting the next state based on previous states) are susceptible to compounding errors. Small inaccuracies at earlier timesteps can accumulate, leading to increasingly unrealistic or incorrect generated trajectories over time.
The key objective for lifelong learning is to develop a DGR method that addresses these limitations, ensuring efficient sample coverage and mitigating error accumulation, thereby enabling robots to continually learn without forgetting.

**Motivation for Learning to Use Memory:**
In Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs), agents must integrate historical observations to make informed decisions due to incomplete current information. While humans inherently learn not only what actions to take but also which past events to recall for present decisions, standard imitation learning methods—which typically involve experts demonstrating observation-action pairs—do not explicitly capture these underlying memory processes. Although self-attention architectures like Transformers can process sequences and theoretically encode episodic memory, they often struggle with long-term credit assignment and can exhibit unstable optimization when learning complex memory strategies solely from observation-action data.
The key objective for memory utilization is to introduce a mechanism that allows human experts to explicitly teach agents *how* to use their memory. This aims to improve the efficiency, stability, and generalizability of learning memory mechanisms in POMDP tasks by directly guiding the agent's attention to relevant historical information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research proposes two distinct methods to address the identified challenges: **t-DGR** for lifelong learning and **AttentionTuner** for learning to use memory.

**A. t-DGR: Trajectory-based Deep Generative Replay for Lifelong Learning**

t-DGR is designed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual imitation learning tasks while addressing the limitations of existing deep generative replay (DGR) methods.

- **Core Idea**: Instead of generating individual state observations independently or generating full trajectories autoregressively, t-DGR trains a generative model to produce state observations conditioned on their specific timestep within a trajectory. This approach aims to ensure uniform coverage of all trajectory timesteps during replay, reducing sample complexity and avoiding compounding errors.
- **Generator Architecture**: A U-net architecture is employed as the generative diffusion model ($G_\gamma$). This model is trained to generate state observations. To condition the generation on the trajectory timestep, a sinusoidal positional encoder creates an embedding passed through linear layers and added to the convolutional layer output at each U-net level.
- **Learner Architecture**: A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) implements the policy ($\pi_\theta$). This MLP is trained using behavioral cloning to minimize the squared error between its predicted actions and expert actions. Task conditioning for the MLP is achieved by concatenating a one-hot vector representing the current task to the input state.
- **Replay Mechanism**:
 1. When a new task (T) is introduced, the system first collects real trajectories ($D_{real}$) for this task.
 2. Based on a predefined `replay ratio` (r), a number of synthetic trajectories (n) are determined to augment the training data.
 3. To generate each synthetic trajectory of a maximum length (L), t-DGR iterates through each timestep `j` from 1 to L.
 4. At each timestep `j`, the generator ($G_\gamma^{(t-1)}$), trained on all previously encountered tasks, generates a state observation ($S$) conditioned on `j`.
 5. The learner's policy ($\pi_\theta^{(t-1)}$), also trained on past tasks, then labels this generated state $S$ with a corresponding action $A$.
 6. The resulting $(S, A)$ pair is added to the synthetic trajectory. This process is repeated for all `L` timesteps to form a complete, albeit temporally incoherent, trajectory. Temporal incoherence is accepted because the learner is trained on individual state-action pairs, not full trajectories.
 7. The synthetic trajectories are combined with the current task's real data to form an augmented dataset.
 8. Both the generator ($G_\gamma$) and the learner ($\pi_\theta$) are updated using this augmented dataset, ensuring that knowledge from past tasks is reinforced while learning the new task.
- **Evaluation**: t-DGR was evaluated on the Continual World benchmarks (CW10 and CW20), which feature a sequence of Meta-World manipulation tasks. A custom benchmark, BB10 (Blurry Boundaries), was also used to assess performance in scenarios where task boundaries are less distinct. Proprioceptive observations were used for computational efficiency.

**B. AttentionTuner: Learning to Use Memory through Demonstrations**

AttentionTuner addresses the challenge of explicitly teaching agents memory utilization in Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs) using human demonstrations.

- **Core Concept**: The method introduces "memory dependency pairs" $(p, q)$ as a novel form of human annotation within expert trajectories. A pair $(p, q)$ signifies that the observation $o_p$ at timestep $p$ should be recalled for decision-making at timestep $q$ ($p < q$). These annotations are integrated into Transformer-based architectures.
- **Expert Self-Attention Matrix (E)**: For each expert trajectory, a binary matrix $E$ is constructed from the set of memory dependency pairs. $E_{ij} = 1$ if $(j, i)$ is a memory dependency pair (meaning observation $j$ is relevant for decision at time $i$), and $E_{ij} = 0$ otherwise. This matrix essentially represents the "expert's attention."
- **Memory Loss Function**: A binary cross-entropy loss $L(A, E)$ is computed between the expert self-attention matrix $E$ and the learner's self-attention matrix $A = \sigma(QK^\top)$ (where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, mapping attention scores to $$). This loss guides the learner's attention mechanism to align with the expert-defined memory dependencies.
- **Integration with Transformer Architecture**:
 - The memory loss is applied to a single attention head within the *first* Transformer layer. This placement is motivated by its proximity to the raw observation embeddings, allowing direct guidance on how to initially process and prioritize information for memory.
 - The overall training loss for AttentionTuner is a combination of the standard imitation learning loss ($L_{IL}$, a negative log-likelihood for action prediction) and the memory loss, weighted by a hyperparameter $\lambda$: $L = L_{IL} + \lambda L(A, E)$.
 - The agent's architecture consists of a causal Transformer decoder-only model. Observations are processed by a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) embedder, and actions by a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) embedder. Positional encodings are added to preserve temporal context.
- **Evaluation**: AttentionTuner was evaluated on Memory Gym and the newly developed Long-term Memory Benchmark (LTMB), both featuring procedurally generated POMDP tasks requiring specific memory recall patterns. Performance was compared against a vanilla Transformer (without the memory loss component).
- **Ablation Studies**: Experiments were conducted to assess the method's robustness to incomplete and imprecise memory annotations, as well as to quantify the human labor savings provided by memory dependency annotations compared to collecting additional full demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded distinct sets of findings for the two proposed methods.

**A. Main Findings for Lifelong Learning (t-DGR):**

- **Superior Performance on Continual World Benchmarks**: t-DGR demonstrated state-of-the-art average success rates on the Continual World 10 (CW10) and Continual World 20 (CW20) benchmarks. On CW10, t-DGR (diffusion) achieved an 81.9% average success rate, surpassing other continual learning methods including Finetune (16.4%), oEWC (18.6%), PackNet (81.4%), DGR (diffusion, 75.0%), and CRIL (diffusion, 28.4%). Similar performance advantages were observed on CW20, where t-DGR achieved 83.9%, outperforming DGR (74.1%) and CRIL (50.8%). A Welch t-test indicated statistically significant improvements over existing pseudo-rehearsal methods on CW20 (p-value = 0.005).
- **Robustness to Blurry Task Boundaries**: On the custom Blurry Boundaries 10 (BB10) benchmark, t-DGR maintained a high success rate (81.7%), demonstrating resilience to intermingled tasks without clear delineations. In contrast, PackNet's performance significantly decreased to 26.9%, illustrating its limitations in such dynamic scenarios.
- **Effectiveness of Diffusion Models as Generators**: The study found that diffusion models consistently outperformed Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) as generators within pseudo-rehearsal methods. When using GANs, CRIL showed better performance than DGR, which in turn outperformed t-DGR. However, with the more capable diffusion models, this ranking reversed, with t-DGR achieving the highest success rates, followed by DGR and then CRIL. This suggests that the benefits of offloading generation complexity to a dynamics model (as in CRIL) diminish when the primary generative model is highly capable.
- **Impact of Replay Ratio**: Increasing the replay ratio (the proportion of generated samples in the training data) for DGR improved its performance, reducing the performance gap with t-DGR. This observation aligns with theoretical expectations regarding sample complexity, where higher replay quantities can compensate for the less efficient i.i.d. sampling of DGR. However, t-DGR's approach offers higher efficiency when replay time is constrained.
- **Maintenance of Generative Quality**: Despite concerns about generation quality degradation in diffusion models during continual learning, t-DGR demonstrated that for lower-dimensional proprioceptive data, the diffusion model largely maintained its ability to generate high-quality samples from past tasks. While some fluctuations in average diffusion loss for past tasks were observed, this degradation did not significantly impact the learner's success rates.

**B. Main Findings for Learning to Use Memory (AttentionTuner):**

- **Significant Improvement in Success Rates**: AttentionTuner consistently achieved higher success rates on several memory-intensive tasks. On Mortar Mayhem, its success rate was 99.8% compared to the vanilla Transformer's 20.8%. Similar improvements were observed on Mystery Path (98.7% vs. 97.3%), Hallway (99.9% vs. 53.2%), and Ordering (99.9% vs. 59.4%). A Welch t-test indicated statistical significance for these improvements (p-values < 0.05).
- **Enhanced Optimization and Convergence**: AttentionTuner aided the optimizer in navigating the loss landscape, leading to more stable and efficient training. The vanilla Transformer often converged to suboptimal local optima, exhibiting high variability in performance across runs. AttentionTuner, by contrast, facilitated faster escape from these local optima (e.g., Mortar Mayhem: overcame two local optima in under 50 epochs, while vanilla Transformer took ~400 more epochs in its best run) and, in some cases, encountered fewer such optima (e.g., Ordering task).
- **Improved Generalizability**: Even when the training loss curves of AttentionTuner and the vanilla Transformer became nearly identical (e.g., Mystery Path after epoch 50), AttentionTuner still exhibited a higher success rate. This indicates that the memory loss objective helps in finding solutions with better generalization capabilities.
- **Limitations in Short-Term Memory Tasks**: AttentionTuner did not show significant improvements on the Searing Spotlights task, where the memory requirements are more akin to short-term, dense recall (attending to all previous actions). The method appears to be more effective for sparse, long-term episodic memory dependencies.
- **Efficiency of Memory Dependency Annotations (Ablations)**:
 - **Tolerance to Missing Annotations**: AttentionTuner maintained high performance even when only a small fraction of the training trajectories were annotated with memory dependency pairs. Annotating 10% of trajectories yielded results comparable to 100% annotation, and even with 0.1% annotation, AttentionTuner still outperformed the vanilla Transformer.
 - **Robustness to Imprecise Annotations**: The method exhibited robustness to small perturbations in the annotated timesteps for memory dependency pairs. Precision degradation was more pronounced when both the recalled timestep ($p$) and the recall timestep ($q$) were perturbed, compared to perturbing only $p$.
 - **Human Labor Savings**: Quantitative analysis suggested that annotating memory dependency pairs for a single demonstration could be equivalent to collecting approximately 40 additional full demonstrations for tasks like Mortar Mayhem and Hallway. Factoring in the time taken for annotation versus data collection, this translated to estimated human labor time savings factors of approximately 16 and 14 for Mortar Mayhem and Hallway, respectively.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the foundational capabilities required for the development and deployment of general-purpose robotic systems, addressing two critical aspects: lifelong learning and memory utilization. The advancements presented have several potential implications for the field of robotics and artificial intelligence.

**Advancing General-Purpose Robotics**: By addressing lifelong learning and memory in tandem, this work contributes directly to the long-term vision of robots operating autonomously in complex, unstructured human environments. Enabling robots to continuously learn new skills without forgetting and to effectively recall relevant past experiences are prerequisites for sustained operation at scale.

**Enhancing Lifelong Learning Capabilities**: The proposed t-DGR method offers a simple, scalable, and non-autoregressive approach to continual learning. Its demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on standard benchmarks and robustness to blurry task boundaries suggest a step towards more adaptable robotic agents. The ability to maintain generative quality even during continuous learning, particularly for lower-dimensional sensor data, indicates a practical path for developing robots that can accumulate knowledge over extended operational lifetimes, thereby accelerating the "data flywheel" of robot data collection and model improvement.

**Facilitating Human-Guided Memory Acquisition**: AttentionTuner introduces a novel paradigm for human-robot collaboration in which humans can explicitly teach robots *how* to use their memory. By leveraging memory dependency pairs as a form of expert demonstration, this method can guide a robot's internal attention mechanisms, leading to more efficient learning, improved optimization stability, and better generalization in memory-intensive tasks. This approach could simplify the process of imparting complex cognitive behaviors to robots, reducing the need for extensive trial-and-error reinforcement learning or purely data-driven discovery.

**Improving Learning Efficiency**: The ablation studies on AttentionTuner highlight the practical utility of memory dependency annotations. The finding that significant learning benefits can be achieved with a minimal percentage of annotated demonstrations, coupled with the calculated human labor time savings, suggests that this approach could offer a cost-effective alternative to collecting vast amounts of additional unannotated demonstration data. This efficiency can accelerate the development cycle for robotic behaviors that require sophisticated memory.

**Future Research Directions**: The work also identifies several avenues for future research, including the integration of t-DGR and AttentionTuner into a unified system, refining replay mechanisms in continual learning, enhancing AttentionTuner's performance for short-term memory tasks, and exploring the applicability of memory dependency pairs across diverse neural architectures beyond Transformers. These proposed directions indicate that the presented methods serve as building blocks for more advanced and integrated intelligent robotic systems.
