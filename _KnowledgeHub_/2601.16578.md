# Zero-Shot MARL Benchmark in the Cyber-Physical Mobility Lab

> [!summary] Summary
> A reproducible benchmark for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of multi-agent RL motion-planning policies for connected automated vehicles, spanning three realism levels: SigmaRL simulation, a grey-box digital twin, and the physical CPM Lab testbed with 1:18-scale vehicles. Deploying a MAPPO-trained SigmaRL policy without fine-tuning exposes two separable degradation sources: environment shift and control-architecture mismatch, with collision rate the most consistent indicator.

## Problem

- Most multi-agent RL motion-planning work for connected automated vehicles evaluates only in simulation, lacking physical deployment and reproducible protocols for measuring transfer.
- Existing sim-to-real remedies like domain randomization and system identification are validated ad hoc, without a shared platform enabling structured, repeatable cross-domain comparison of multi-agent policies.
- Prior multi-agent testbed transfer studies use simple two-lane maps, omitting intersections, highway loops, and ramps that stress coordination under realistic traffic topology.

## Method

- Three-tier evaluation stack: ==SigmaRL== ==kinematic-bicycle== simulation with direct noise-free actuation, a ==grey-box digital twin== identified from real logs, and the physical CPM Lab with overhead-camera localization.
- Interface layer mapping CPM Lab state vectors into SigmaRL's ego-centric observation format, denormalizing steering and reconstructing 2D velocity via kinematic bicycle slip-angle geometry.
- ==MPC==-style trajectory generation: policy actions rolled out over control horizon, then constant velocity with steering decayed to zero over the remaining prediction horizon, tracked by mid-level ==MPC==.

## Results

- Agent-agent collision rate rises monotonically across realism levels: **0.37** in simulation, **2.10** in digital twin, **4.49** events per **100 m** in the physical lab, over **27** runs each.
- Digital-twin-to-physical transfer drops average speed from **0.771** to **0.728 m/s**, with lane-violation distance rising from **38.80** to **41.09 m** per **100 m**.
- The mid-level MPC layer, not the policy, explains better lane keeping downstream: centerline deviation falls from **0.050 m** in simulation to **0.041 m** in the digital twin.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Discrete failure counts like collision rate track environment realism robustly, while continuous deviation metrics mostly reflect control-stack architecture rather than policy quality.
> - Adding a mid-level tracking controller at deployment can improve some metrics while worsening safety, so architectural mismatch must be separated from the true sim-to-real gap.
> - Structured, invariance-encoding observations such as boundary and centerline distances support zero-shot transfer and post-collision recovery without any fine-tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses a significant challenge within the field of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) for Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs): the sim-to-real gap. Reinforcement Learning (RL) and MARL have demonstrated capabilities in solving complex sequential decision-making problems and motion planning for CAVs in simulated environments. The decentralized nature of MARL is particularly appealing for real-world traffic scenarios due to its scalability and ability to coordinate multiple agents without complex centralized schemes.

However, policies trained in simulation often exhibit degraded performance when deployed on physical systems due to discrepancies between simulation and reality. These discrepancies can include sensor noise, actuation delays, model mismatches, and environmental variability, collectively referred to as the sim-to-real gap. Prior work has explored various techniques to bridge this gap, such as domain randomization (introducing variability in observations, physics, or dynamics during training), system identification (calibrating simulations with real-world data), and data augmentation (diversifying sensor inputs). While these methods have shown success in tasks like manipulation and autonomous racing, a common limitation is their reliance on simulation-based evaluation, with limited real-world deployments and a lack of reproducibility in physical experiments.

The broader landscape includes the development and use of small-scale testbeds, such as Duckietown, RoboRacer, and Robotarium, which offer cost-efficient platforms for validating RL and MARL algorithms in physical settings. These testbeds introduce uncertainties inherent in physical systems, providing a more faithful representation than purely computer simulations. The authors acknowledge their prior work, which analyzed the gap between small-scale and full-scale models, indicating that small-scale testbeds are effective for evaluating software components like motion planning but may not fully represent environmental interactions. This work is specifically related to studies that employ MARL for CAVs in small-scale environments, such as, which investigated domain randomization in Duckietown. The current paper differentiates itself by offering a reproducible benchmark platform for evaluating the sim-to-real gap, utilizing a more complex map with an eight-lane intersection and highway loops compared to the simpler two-lane map used in some related work.

Furthermore, the research builds upon the authors' previous contribution, SigmaRL, a sample-efficient and generalizable MARL framework for CAV motion planning. SigmaRL’s focus on structured observation design is relevant to the sim-to-real problem, as generalization from simulated to real environments is a key aspect of successful transfer. This paper positions itself as a tool to systematically quantify and understand the performance degradation that occurs during zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of MARL policies, thereby addressing the gap in reproducible, multi-fidelity evaluation platforms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged "sim-to-real gap" in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) for Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs). While MARL policies have demonstrated proficiency in simulated environments, their direct transfer to physical hardware often results in performance degradation. This gap is attributed to factors such as sensor noise, actuation delays, model inaccuracies, and environmental variabilities that are difficult to perfectly replicate in simulation. A significant limitation in current research is the scarcity of reproducible real-world deployments and systematic evaluation platforms for sim-to-real transfer, leading most studies to remain confined to simulation.

To address this limitation, the core objective of this work is to introduce a reproducible platform designed for the systematic evaluation of sim-to-real transfer for MARL-based motion planning policies. This platform aims to provide a structured environment for zero-shot deployment and direct comparison of policies across different levels of realism.

Specifically, the key objectives and contributions of this article are detailed as follows:
1. **Develop a Reproducible Platform:** To establish and present a platform capable of studying sim-to-real transfer in MARL-based motion planning. This platform integrates a simulation environment, a high-fidelity digital twin, and a physical testbed, the Cyber-Physical Mobility Lab (CPM Lab). The reproducibility aspect aims to enable other researchers to replicate experiments and validate findings.
2. **Provide Baseline Evaluation:** To conduct a baseline evaluation of a MARL policy, specifically one trained using the previously proposed SigmaRL framework, across these three levels of realism: simulation, digital twin, and physical deployment. This zero-shot deployment, without any additional fine-tuning, serves to demonstrate the platform's utility and quantify performance differences directly.
3. **Release Tools and Discuss Effects:** To release the necessary tools, configurations, and metrics required for replicating the experiments. Concurrently, a systematic discussion of the sim-to-real effects is provided, specifically focusing on performance degradation arising from domain shift (differences between training and evaluation scenarios) and increasing system realism (differences in vehicle dynamics, localization accuracy, actuation delays, and control execution infrastructure). The aim is to disentangle and understand these complementary sources of performance degradation.

The overall motivation is to move beyond purely simulated evaluations and provide a robust framework for validating MARL policies in conditions progressively closer to real-world deployment, thereby accelerating the development and deployment of safe and efficient CAV systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on a multi-fidelity evaluation platform that integrates simulation, a digital twin, and a physical testbed for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of MARL policies for CAVs. The core components include the MARL framework (SigmaRL), the Cyber-Physical Mobility Lab (CPM Lab) as the physical testbed, and a detailed experimental setup for evaluation.

#### 3.1. MARL Framework (SigmaRL)

The policies evaluated in this work are derived from SigmaRL, a framework that leverages Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO) with decentralized actors and a centralized critic. It models agents using a nonlinear kinematic bicycle model.

- **Observation Design:** SigmaRL employs an efficient, structured observation design that integrates domain knowledge. Observations are ego-centric and encode geometric and traffic-specific features, such as distances to road boundaries, lane centerlines, and states of surrounding agents. This design aims to incorporate task-relevant invariances, improving generalization and zero-shot transfer across diverse traffic scenarios. The necessary global states of all agents and High-Definition (HD) map data are assumed to be available to compute these ego-centric observations.
- **Reward Design:** The reward function for each agent comprises three components: `r_track` encourages following the reference path (road centerline), `r_speed` rewards maintaining higher forward speed for traffic efficiency, and `r_penalty` imposes penalties for unsafe behaviors, including collisions with other agents or road boundaries, and violations of safety distances.
- **Action Design:** Policies output a continuous action vector comprising commanded speed and steering angle. To ensure physical feasibility, a hyperbolic tangent (tanh) squashing function bounds the sampled actions, which are then rescaled to the actual control range. This approach maintains differentiability and produces smooth, bounded control commands.

#### 3.2. Cyber-Physical Mobility Lab (CPM Lab)

The CPM Lab serves as the physical testbed. It is a small-scale platform featuring 20 μCars (1:18 scale vehicles) and an overhead ceiling camera system for absolute position estimation of all agents. A synchronization layer orchestrates interactions between the motion planner and the lab.

- **Control Architecture:** The μCars have a three-level control architecture. The lowest level is the hardware controller for speed and steering. Above this, a vehicle controller determines the current speed and steering to follow a reference trajectory. The highest level is the offboard motion planner, which computes reference trajectories for all μCars, considering their positions and road boundary constraints.
- **Observation Mapping:** The CPM Lab provides a state list for each agent, including position, orientation (yaw), speed, and a normalized steering angle. To match SigmaRL's required state representation, a mapping function (`f`) is introduced. This involves denormalizing the steering angle to its physical range and converting the scalar speed into a 2D velocity vector using the kinematic bicycle model and the vehicle's yaw angle.
- **Action Transformation:** SigmaRL's continuous policy outputs (commanded speed and steering angle) are converted into a continuous reference trajectory for the CPM Lab's trajectory-following mode. This involves a control horizon ($H_c$) during which policy-generated actions dictate control inputs, and a prediction horizon ($H_p$) where a rules-based approach takes over. Within $H_c$, the kinematic bicycle model predicts subsequent states. After $H_c$, velocity is held constant based on the last policy action, while steering input is gradually reduced to zero over the remaining $H_p$. This adapted control schema integrates the RL policy outputs with the CPM Lab's trajectory-following capabilities.

#### 3.3. Training in SigmaRL

A SigmaRL policy was trained on an intersection segment of the map, designed to resemble part of the CPM Lab environment. The training used 𝑛_agent = 4 agents with randomly initialized states and reference paths. Upon collision or exiting the intersection, agents were reset.
- **Neural Network Architecture:** Both actor and critic used simple feedforward neural networks with three hidden layers of 256 neurons and tanh activation functions.
- **Model Size:** The observation size for the actor was 32, resulting in a lightweight model of approximately 140k trainable parameters, suitable for real-time deployment.
- **Training Process:** The policy was trained for 1000 iterations, collecting 2^12 samples per iteration (totaling about four million samples). Minibatch training (size 2^9) with 30 epochs per iteration was used, and the best-performing policy was retained.
- **Deployment:** The trained actor was directly transferred to physical vehicles in the CPM Lab without any additional fine-tuning, constituting a zero-shot deployment.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

The trained policy was evaluated across three environments:
1. **SigmaRL Simulation:** Uses a kinematic bicycle model with instantaneous, noise-free actuation, where policy actions are applied directly.
2. **High-Fidelity Digital Twin:** Employs a grey-box vehicle model identified from real-world logs, incorporating sensor and actuation imperfections. Policy outputs are converted to reference trajectories tracked by a mid-level Model Predictive Control (MPC) trajectory follower.
3. **Physical CPM Lab:** Uses scaled μCars with inherent motor delays, sensor noise, and imperfect localization from the overhead camera system with known latency. It utilizes the same mid-level MPC trajectory follower as the digital twin. The 1:18 scale testbed implies non-proportional dynamic scaling compared to full-scale vehicles.

- **Reproducibility and Statistics:** To ensure statistical robustness, 𝑛=27 runs were conducted per environment. This involved evaluating policies trained with three different seeds, across three different initial position configurations, with three repetitions per configuration. Initial positions in the simulation and digital twin were matched to those observed in physical runs for fair comparison.
- **Agents and Duration:** Evaluation used 𝑛_agent = 3 agents to ensure stable and reproducible trials, as preliminary tests with more agents led to frequent collisions. Each run lasted 18 seconds (180 steps at 0.1s timestep). In physical experiments, agents were manually reset near their last valid location after collisions.

#### 3.5. Performance Metrics

Four metrics were used to quantify safety and performance, normalized to the total distance traveled (per 100m) for fair comparison.
- **Agent–Agent Collision Rate (CRA-A):** Number of collision events (detected by overlapping geometries and a hysteresis filter) per 100m of aggregate agent travel.
- **Agent–Lane Collision Rate (CRA-L):** Sum of distances traveled while the agent's footprint was outside lane boundaries (beyond a 10% vehicle width slack), normalized per 100m.
- **Centerline Deviation (CD):** Mean lateral distance between the agent's center and its reference path (road centerline) over the entire run, reported in meters.
- **Average Speed (AS):** Mean forward speed (ℓ2 norm of velocity vector) of each agent, reported in m/s, reflecting traffic efficiency and hesitancy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation investigated the zero-shot sim-to-real transferability of SigmaRL-trained policies across three environments: a direct simulation, a high-fidelity digital twin, and the physical Cyber-Physical Mobility Lab (CPM Lab). The policies were trained on an intersection scenario and deployed to a more complex evaluation map, thus inherently incorporating a degree of domain shift from training to evaluation.

The performance metrics across the 27 runs per environment are summarized below (mean ± standard deviation, with Interquartile Mean (IQM) in parentheses):

| Environment | CRA-A ↓ [events/100 m] | CRA-L ↓ [m/100 m] | CD ↓ [m] | AS ↑ [m/s] |
| :-------------------------- | :--------------------- | :---------------- | :------------------- | :-------------------- |
| SigmaRL Simulation (no MPC) | 0.37 ± 1.33 (0.47) | 45.99 ± 6.69 (47.12) | 0.050 ± 0.009 (0.050) | 0.759 ± 0.024 (0.761) |
| Digital Twin (with MPC) | 2.10 ± 2.80 (1.47) | 38.80 ± 9.57 (38.40) | 0.041 ± 0.010 (0.039) | 0.771 ± 0.031 (0.777) |
| Physical Lab (with MPC) | 4.49 ± 6.26 (2.45) | 41.09 ± 7.21 (41.10) | 0.044 ± 0.010 (0.043) | 0.728 ± 0.035 (0.750) |

Two main forms of performance degradation or differences were identified:

1. **Sim-to-Real Degradation (Digital Twin to Physical Lab):**
 - **Collision Rate (CRA-A):** This metric consistently increased, rising from 2.10 events/100m in the digital twin to 4.49 events/100m in the physical lab. This indicates a decrease in safety as environmental realism increases.
 - **Average Speed (AS):** A modest drop was observed, from 0.771 m/s in the digital twin to 0.728 m/s in the physical lab. This suggests slight reductions in efficiency, possibly due to increased conservatism or reactions to disturbances.
 - **Lane Keeping (CRA-L) and Centerline Deviation (CD):** Both metrics showed a slight deterioration from the digital twin (38.80 m/100m and 0.041m, respectively) to the physical lab (41.09 m/100m and 0.044m).
 - **Interpretation:** These trends quantify a measurable sim-to-real gap, attributed to the increased environmental realism of the physical lab, including sensor noise, actuation delays, and communication latency. This degradation pattern provides a consistent measure of environment shift under an identical control architecture.

2. **Architectural Degradation (SigmaRL Simulation to Digital Twin/Physical Lab):**
 - **Collision Rate (CRA-A):** The SigmaRL simulation exhibited the lowest collision rate (0.37 events/100m) compared to both the digital twin and physical lab.
 - **Lane Keeping (CRA-L), Centerline Deviation (CD), and Average Speed (AS):** In contrast to the CRA-A, the SigmaRL simulation performed worse on these metrics. For example, CRA-L was 45.99 m/100m in simulation versus 38.80 m/100m in the digital twin, and CD was 0.050m versus 0.041m.
 - **Interpretation:** This counter-intuitive finding is attributed to architectural differences in control execution. In the SigmaRL simulation, policy control outputs are applied directly to the vehicle dynamics model. However, in the digital twin and physical lab, the planned trajectories are passed to a mid-level Model Predictive Control (MPC) trajectory follower. This MPC acts as an additional control layer, smoothing high-frequency control inputs and improving trajectory tracking. This results in fewer short-lived boundary crossings and reduced lateral oscillations, leading to better CRA-L, CD, and AS values in the digital twin and physical lab, despite their increased environmental complexities. The direct-control setup in the SigmaRL training environment differs from the hierarchical control used in the deployment environments.

**Overall Summary of Findings:**
The results reveal two distinct, yet complementary, effects. Firstly, a clear sim-to-real performance degradation is observed when transitioning from the digital twin to the physical lab, primarily reflected in the increased collision rate. Secondly, architectural differences in the control stack (direct control vs. MPC-based trajectory following) significantly impact performance metrics like lane keeping, trajectory smoothness, and speed. The collision rate (CRA-A) emerged as the most consistent indicator of overall robustness across all three environments, while continuous deviation metrics (CRA-L, CD, AS) were more sensitive to the control architecture and environmental fidelity. Despite these challenges, the policies demonstrated resilience, with agents recovering from collisions and disturbances, supporting the efficacy of SigmaRL's structured observation design for generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) for Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) by addressing the critical challenge of sim-to-real transfer through a systematically designed and reproducible evaluation platform. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Reproducible Benchmark for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The primary contribution is the introduction of a reproducible platform for evaluating the sim-to-real transferability of MARL policies. This platform, integrating simulation, a high-fidelity digital twin, and a physical small-scale testbed (CPM Lab), offers a structured environment for zero-shot policy deployment. This is significant because the lack of reproducible real-world evaluations has been a major impediment to validating MARL research beyond simulation, making the findings less tangible for real-world applications. By releasing tools, configurations, and metrics, the authors enable other researchers to replicate experiments, fostering transparency and accelerating progress in the field.

2. **Quantifying Performance Degradation:** The study provides a quantitative baseline evaluation using a SigmaRL-trained policy, explicitly measuring performance degradation across environments with increasing realism. This empirical data is crucial for understanding the practical challenges of deploying MARL policies. The identification of two complementary sources of degradation—the inherent sim-to-real gap due to environmental realism (sensor noise, actuation delays) and architectural differences in control execution (direct policy output vs. MPC-based trajectory following)—offers specific insights for future research.

3. **Robust Metric Identification:** The finding that the Agent-Agent Collision Rate (CRA-A) is the most consistent and robust indicator of overall performance across environments is valuable. This highlights a key metric for assessing the fundamental safety and robustness of MARL policies during sim-to-real transfer, especially compared to continuous deviation metrics that are more sensitive to specific control architectures. This insight can guide researchers in selecting appropriate evaluation criteria.

4. **Validation of Structured Observation Design:** The demonstration that policies trained with SigmaRL, which uses structured, semantically meaningful observations, exhibit strong resilience and recovery capabilities despite zero-shot transfer and environmental disturbances, validates the effectiveness of this design approach for generalization. This has implications for developing more robust and transferable MARL policies for complex real-world scenarios.

5. **Practical Value of Small-Scale Testbeds:** The work underscores the practical value of small-scale physical testbeds like the CPM Lab. While not a direct replacement for full-scale deployments, these testbeds provide a safe, scalable, and controllable intermediate platform for high-throughput sim-to-real validation. They bridge the gap between purely theoretical simulations and costly, complex full-scale experiments, enabling researchers to prototype, test, and refine MARL algorithms under realistic physical conditions with relative ease and lower cost.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The platform and its findings provide a foundation for future research. The stated plans to investigate partial observability, onboard perception, and communication-limited coordination leveraging this reproducible environment indicate its ongoing utility as a research tool. This systematic analysis of current limitations and future avenues can guide the development of more advanced and truly autonomous CAV systems.

In summary, this research provides a tangible, reproducible framework for advancing the understanding and practical implementation of MARL in CAVs. By rigorously evaluating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, it highlights critical factors influencing deployment success and offers a robust platform for continuous innovation in multi-agent control for autonomous mobility.
