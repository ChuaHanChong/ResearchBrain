# Iterative Closed-Loop Motion Synthesis for Scaling the Capabilities of Humanoid Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework called CLAIMS from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) and University of Oxford autonomously generates high-dynamic motion data and iteratively refines both the dataset and physics-based humanoid control policies. This approach reduces the average failure rate on complex test sets by 45%, enhancing controller generalization for professional-level skills.

## Problem

- Physics-based humanoid controllers are limited by the high cost and constrained scale of expert-driven motion capture (MoCap) datasets, which often lack diversity and high-difficulty actions.
- Current motion datasets exhibit a fixed difficulty distribution, preventing controllers from progressively mastering more complex skills beyond their initial training boundaries.
- Existing training pipelines lack mechanisms for dynamically adapting the difficulty of training data in response to the controller's evolving capabilities, hindering generalization to high-dynamic or diverse skill sets.

## Method

![[2602.21599-method.png]]
*Overview of the CLAIMS pipeline: a closed-loop system that refines prompts from a 5-domain library (martial arts, dance, combat, sports, gymnastics), synthesizes motions with MDM, filters them by physics and VLM checks, trains humanoid trackers with RL, and uses multimodal feedback to generate progressively harder tasks.*

- CLAIMS (==Closed-Loop Automated Iterative Motion== Synthesis) autonomously generates high-quality, high-dynamic motion data across various professional domains using a text-conditioned motion diffusion model guided by difficulty-aware language prompts.
- A competitive iterative curriculum co-evolves the synthetic motion dataset and the physics-based controller, with data difficulty escalating based on a multi-modal feedback loop.
- The feedback loop combines objective physical tracking metrics (e.g., MPJPE, velocity/acceleration discrepancies) with subjective evaluations from Vision and Language Models (VLMs) to guide subsequent prompt generation and training iterations.

## Results

- The iterative framework reduced the average failure rate of a PHC controller on out-of-distribution, high-difficulty test sets by **45%** (from **41.7%** to **23.1%**) compared to an AMASS baseline after six loops.
- The method demonstrated compatibility and consistent performance improvements across various high-difficulty benchmarks when applied to a MaskedMimic controller, outperforming its baseline on tasks like Kungfu (**65.8%** vs. **57.2%**) and AIST++ (**83.9%** vs. **68.9%**).
- Analysis confirmed that later iterative loops generated systematically harder motions, exhibiting progressively higher velocities and broader speed distributions, with VLM-assigned difficulty scores monotonically increasing with designed difficulty tiers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Text-conditioned motion diffusion models, when guided by expert-designed, difficulty-aware prompts, can synthesize out-of-distribution motions that represent professional and high-dynamic behaviors beyond their original training data manifold.
> - A multi-modal feedback system, integrating objective physics-based metrics and subjective VLM assessments, effectively drives a curriculum that allows physics-based controllers to progressively master increasingly challenging motion data.
> - The use of a structured variable template library for prompt generation, coupled with an LLM-guided policy, provides stability and domain fidelity to the iterative data synthesis process, leading to more targeted and diagnostically challenging motions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Physics-based humanoid control, which underpins dynamic locomotion, character animation, and virtual reality interactions, traditionally relies on imitation learning from large motion capture (MoCap) datasets. Significant progress has been made with methods such as DeepMimic, AMP, ASE, DReCon, and PHC, which have advanced skill learning, realism, reuse, and generalization. However, this field faces several inherent limitations.

One primary bottleneck is the reliance on costly, expert-driven MoCap systems, which constrain data acquisition in terms of scale, diversity, and the representation of high-difficulty actions. Existing datasets, such as AMASS, primarily consist of low-dynamic daily activities, making controllers trained on them struggle with acrobatic or professional motions. Domain-specific datasets like AIST++ and EMDB offer higher dynamism but are limited in scope and scale. Efforts to scale data collection through video mining (Humanoid-X, HuBE) or advanced annotation (Go to Zero, MotionLib, Motion-X++) have expanded coverage but often lack reliable semantic tags, difficulty stratification, or dynamic adaptability to controller capabilities.

In parallel, motion generation models, including diffusion models like MDM, MoFusion, MotionGPT, and T2M-X, have shown capabilities in text-conditioned motion synthesis. More recent advancements like Being-M0.5 leverage large corpora for unified vision, language, and motion control. However, these models typically inherit the static, low-dynamic distributions of their training data and lack mechanisms to adaptively generate or evaluate professional, high-difficulty actions in a controller-aware manner.

Furthermore, while closed-loop approaches have gained traction in various domains—from language modeling (SAI-DPO, SEAL) to robotics (RoboTwin 2.0) and character control (PARC)—they often suffer from limitations such as reliance on simplified evaluation criteria, lack of generalization across scenarios, or insufficient grounding in semantic and difficulty taxonomies. Integrations of diffusion and reinforcement learning for multi-task control (e.g., BeyondMimic, Closd) and language-based task specification (PADL, Humanoid-VLA) exist but generally operate on static corpora without dynamic difficulty adaptation.

This work addresses these gaps by proposing a closed-loop framework that co-evolves motion data synthesis and controller training. It aims to overcome the fixed difficulty distributions of existing datasets and the cost constraints of MoCap by autonomously generating professional, high-dynamic motion data and iteratively refining both the dataset and the control policy. The approach seeks to provide a general training framework for single-primitive trackers, facilitating adaptive difficulty escalation and enabling controllers to master complex skills beyond their initial training limitations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to address the limitations in training physics-based humanoid control policies, specifically concerning the quality, diversity, and scalability of motion data, and the static nature of dataset difficulty.

The motivations for this work stem from several challenges currently faced by the field:
1. **Fixed Difficulty Distribution of Motion Datasets:** Current motion datasets, while extensive, typically possess a fixed distribution of motion difficulties. Controllers trained on these datasets often achieve a performance ceiling determined by this distribution. For instance, controllers trained on datasets predominantly featuring low-dynamic activities, like the majority of AMASS, perform poorly on high-dynamic or acrobatic motions because the necessary features are underrepresented or entirely absent. This static nature prevents policies from progressively mastering more complex skills.
2. **High Cost and Scalability Issues of High-Quality Data Acquisition:** Obtaining professional-grade, high-dynamic motion data, particularly for specialized skills such as martial arts, dance, or gymnastics, requires expensive motion capture systems and expert performers. This high cost severely limits the ability to generate diverse and large-scale datasets, hindering the development of more capable humanoid controllers. Existing methods for scaling data, such as video mining, often compromise semantic reliability and difficulty stratification.
3. **Lack of Controller-Aware Difficulty Adaptation:** Most existing data generation and training pipelines lack a mechanism for dynamically adjusting the difficulty of training data in response to the controller's evolving capabilities. This means that even with large datasets, the training process may not efficiently push the controller beyond its current competence.
4. **Limited Generalization of Controllers:** Controllers trained on fixed, domain-specific, or low-difficulty datasets often exhibit weak generalization to unseen, high-dynamic, or diverse skill sets. The absence of a systematic way to introduce progressively challenging motions during training restricts their overall robustness and adaptability.

To address these issues, the research proposes a closed-loop automated framework, CLAIMS (Closed-Loop Automated Iterative Motion Synthesis), designed to:
- Generate high-quality, diverse motion data with rich action semantics (e.g., martial arts, dance, combat, sports, gymnastics) without relying on expensive MoCap.
- Enable difficulty iteration for both control policies and generated data through objective physical metrics and subjective evaluations, allowing trained trackers to exceed their initial performance limits.
- Reduce the average failure rate of trained controllers on complex test sets by iteratively increasing the difficulty and diversity of the training data.
- Establish a scalable, low-cost paradigm for co-evolving motion data and control policies, promoting enhanced generalization and robustness across various professional high-difficulty scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces CLAIMS (Closed-Loop Automated Iterative Motion Synthesis), a framework designed to co-evolve motion data synthesis and controller optimization. The methodology integrates several components in an iterative loop to achieve progressively harder, professional-level motion control.

#### 3.1. Defining High-Difficulty Motion Data

The framework formalizes motion professionalism and difficulty across five domains: sports, dance, combat, gymnastics, and martial arts, with an emphasis on high-dynamic motions. Difficulty is structured along four axes:
- **Base action:** Atomic skills (e.g., a specific jump or kick).
- **Combo action:** Compositional logic combining base actions.
- **Detail:** Technical cues (e.g., limb placement, specific postures).
- **Speed and rhythm:** Temporal structure and intensity.
These axes serve as templates for language prompts, enabling principled difficulty escalation and domain-faithful motion generation.

#### 3.2. Dataset Generation

Training data is synthesized using a pretrained text-conditioned motion diffusion model (MDM), specifically MDM-step50 with a DistilBERT text encoder.
1. **Prompt Generation:** In each iterative round, text prompts are sampled from a difficulty-aware variable bank, which is instantiated from the semantic taxonomy using an auxiliary Large Language Model (LLM). This template-based approach ensures stable, domain-faithful control over motion composition and difficulty.
2. **Motion Synthesis:** MDM generates motion trajectories based on the encoded prompts.
3. **Filtering:** Generated motions undergo a two-stage filtering process:
 - **Physics Validity Checks:** Root joint height bounds are applied to remove non-physical artifacts like floating, sinking, or interpenetration.
 - **Semantic Alignment Check:** A Vision and Language Model (VLM) assesses the semantic alignment between the prompt and the synthesized motion.
 Motions passing both filters are added to the synthetic training set. This pipeline is designed to be low-cost and repeatable, allowing the corpus to evolve towards high-dynamic professional behaviors.

#### 3.3. Training and Evaluation of Controller

The framework is designed to be controller-agnostic, demonstrating its compatibility with existing single-primitive trackers like PHC and MaskedMimic.
1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Setup:** The physics-based controller is trained using RL imitation with dense rewards covering pose, joint velocities, end effectors, and contact events. Sample filtering is employed for robust optimization. The original hyperparameters and training settings of the chosen tracker are maintained. The controller training is the only computationally intensive component of the framework.
2. **Evaluation Metrics:** After training, the controller's performance is evaluated using objective physical metrics:
 - **mpjpe-g:** Mean Per-Joint Position Error in the global (world) frame.
 - **mpjpe-l:** Mean Per-Joint Position Error in the root-relative frame.
 - **vel-dist:** Mean per-joint linear velocity discrepancy (reflects smoothness).
 - **accel-dist:** Mean per-joint acceleration discrepancy (exposes high-frequency jitter).
These metrics define the controller's capability frontier and provide feedback for the subsequent data generation loop.

#### 3.4. Competitive Iteration between Controller and Dataset

A competitive iterative curriculum is implemented to enhance controller performance.
1. **Difficulty Escalation:** After each training cycle, if the controller's objective metrics exceed predefined thresholds, indicating mastery of the current data distribution, the framework escalates the data difficulty.
2. **Multimodal Feedback:** A joint evaluation process combines objective physical tracking metrics from the controller with subjective visual judgments from VLMs. SMPL renderings of each training motion are evaluated by an ensemble of VLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Qwen-VL-MAX). VLMs provide a subjective difficulty score, descriptors (action sequence, technical complexity, intensity, balance, continuity), and a rationale.
3. **Semantic Observation Vector:** The physical metrics and VLM feedback are concatenated into a semantic observation vector, capturing both physical execution quality and perceived difficulty. This vector guides the next iteration.

#### 3.5. Automation of the Iterative Process

The entire closed-loop curriculum is automated by a central LLM policy (e.g., Gemini with chain-of-thought prompting).
1. **LLM Policy:** The LLM receives the semantic observation vector (tracker metrics + VLM feedback + previous action prompt).
2. **Prompt Selection:** Guided by its role prompt, the LLM selects the next action prompt from the difficulty-aware variable bank, grounding it in the professional templates.
3. **Environment Execution:** The environment then executes motion tracking training on the newly synthesized clips.
4. **Implicit Optimization:** The LLM implicitly optimizes by aiming to improve physical tracking scores while steadily increasing annotated difficulty, creating a self-reinforcing curriculum. This process iterates for a specified number of rounds (K), resulting in a multi-domain corpus with K difficulty tiers and an adapted physics-based controller capable of handling heterogeneous high-difficulty motions.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU, using the PHC single-primitive training configuration as the base RL setup. Performance was evaluated on six standard motion test sets: Kungfu, EMDB, AMASS, MDM, AIST++, and Video-Convert, reporting per-set results and an average calculated by clip.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments focused on verifying the framework's ability to generate professional, high-difficulty motions, generalize to unseen data, and improve controller stability through iterative feedback.

#### 4.1. Motion Professionalism Verification

- **Dataset Features and Domain Specificity:** T-SNE visualization of per-frame global joint rotation features showed that motions synthesized using expert martial arts prompts substantially overlapped the professional martial arts manifold, unlike motions generated from random prompts, which lay far away. This indicates that the prompt design effectively encodes domain priors.
- **Relationship with MDM Training Distribution:** A T-SNE analysis on clip-level pose embeddings revealed that expert-prompt synthesized samples largely resided outside the MDM’s training manifold (HumanML3D, AMASS, HumanAct12), while random-prompt samples clustered within it. This demonstrates that MDM, when guided by expert prompts, can produce out-of-distribution motions beyond its original training coverage.

#### 4.2. Generalization Research

- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Dataset Testing (PHC Tracker):**
 - The iterative framework was instantiated with the PHC single-primitive controller.
 - Loop0 (initial synthetic data) performed slightly worse than the AMASS baseline on average (55.9% vs. 58.3% success rate).
 - Loop1 surpassed the baseline (64.0% success rate).
 - Subsequent loops (L2-L6) continued to improve, with L6 achieving an average success rate of 76.9%. This represents a 45% reduction in average failure rate compared to the AMASS baseline (from 41.7% to 23.1%).
 - This demonstrated that the feedback-driven, prompt-conditioned curriculum is compatible with the PHC paradigm and outperforms one-shot training on large, generic corpora.
- **Compatibility with Different Trackers (MaskedMimic):**
 - The framework was applied to a DeepMimic-based MaskedMimic controller.
 - Loop0 matched the baseline MaskedMimic (trained on AMASS).
 - Loop1 showed consistent and significant improvements across various high-difficulty benchmarks (e.g., Kungfu: 65.8% vs. 57.2%; EMDB: 71.1% vs. 53.3%; AIST++: 83.9% vs. 68.9%; Video-Convert: 62.4% vs. 41.6%).
 - This indicates that the feedback-driven data and policy refinement transfers effectively to distinct imitation-based tracking frameworks.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **VLM Scoring Reliability Verification:**
 - Five tiers of compositional test prompts were created (Levels 1-5), and 1,000 motions were generated.
 - Qwen, a VLM blinded to the prompts, assigned difficulty scores (1-10).
 - Both the prompt-defined difficulty levels and the physical metric (mean velocity) exhibited a clear monotonic trend, with average velocity and Qwen's difficulty ratings increasing with each tier. This alignment supports the credibility of automated VLM scoring.
- **Observation Role Ablation:**
 - Four variants of the iterative feedback pipeline (full, w/o VLM, w/o physics metrics, w/o both) were tested using the PHC controller from loop0 to loop3.
 - Performance (average success rate) followed the order: no observations < no physics < no VLM < full.
 - This indicates that physics-based tracking metrics are the most diagnostic for targeted challenge design, while VLM feedback provides complementary subjective difficulty cues. Both signals contribute to performance gains, with physics being critical.
- **Variable Template Library Role Verification:**
 - An ablation without the variable library (Gemini-CoT proposing prompts directly) was compared to the configuration with the library.
 - The variant without the variable library consistently underperformed across all third-party benchmarks (e.g., on Kungfu L3: 59.9% vs. 59.1% with library; on AIST++ L3: 76.5% vs. 82.1% with library), even exhibiting performance degradation in some later loops.
 - This suggests the variable library provides a structured prior that stabilizes prompt generation, leading to more targeted and diagnostically challenging motions.
- **Feedback Iteration Role Verification:**
 - A size-matched, non-iterative baseline (randomly sampled data equal to cumulative loop0-loop6 data) was compared to the iterative regimen.
 - The feedback-iterative regimen (loop0→loop6) consistently outperformed the one-shot baseline across multiple large-scale test suites (e.g., Kungfu: 60.3% vs. 58.7%; AIST++: 88.1% vs. 85.3%).
 - This confirms that gains are attributable to iterative feedback and curriculum effects rather than merely increasing data volume.
- **Dataset Difficulty Variation Verification:**
 - Inference with the PHC+ composition controller on each loop's motion set showed a monotonic drop in tracking success with increasing loop index (e.g., L0: 75.3% SR; L6: 53.6% SR). This indicates that the feedback-driven curriculum generated systematically harder motions.
 - Velocity analyses (global root-velocity distributions and per-clip mean inter-frame speed) demonstrated that AMASS is predominantly low-dynamics, whereas synthesized data from later loops exhibited progressively higher velocities, broader tails, and higher peak speeds. These trends confirmed the intrinsic higher dynamics and difficulty of later-loop datasets.

#### 4.4. Limitations

The authors acknowledge two main limitations:
1. **Generative Model Capacity:** The quality of motion synthesis is bounded by the generative model's (MDM) capacity for extreme high-dynamics.
2. **Manually Curated Variable Library:** The manually curated variable library lacks objective calibration and comprehensive coverage.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses fundamental challenges in physics-based humanoid control, offering significant implications for the development of more capable and generalized autonomous agents in simulated environments.

The primary significance lies in the introduction of a **low-cost, adaptive, closed-loop pipeline for motion data generation and iterative controller optimization.** This approach directly tackles the bottlenecks of data scarcity, the expense of high-quality MoCap, and the static difficulty distribution of existing datasets. By autonomously generating diverse, high-dynamic motion data across professional domains (martial arts, dance, combat, sports, gymnastics), the framework mitigates the reliance on expensive manual data collection.

The **iterative co-evolution of data and controller policies** is a key contribution. Unlike traditional methods where controllers are trained on fixed datasets, CLAIMS enables the control policy to break through its initial performance ceiling by progressively adapting the difficulty of the training data. This is achieved through a multi-modal feedback loop that combines objective physical tracking metrics with subjective VLM evaluations, creating a self-reinforcing curriculum. This dynamic adaptation fosters continuous improvement and mastery of increasingly complex skills.

The demonstrated **generalization capabilities** are notable. The framework significantly reduced the average failure rate on out-of-distribution, high-difficulty test sets (e.g., 45% reduction for PHC tracker compared to AMASS baseline) and showed compatibility with different underlying control architectures (PHC and MaskedMimic). This suggests a path towards developing more robust and versatile humanoid controllers capable of performing a wider range of actions beyond their initial training distributions.

Furthermore, the research establishes a **semantic definition and normalization for action data**, coupled with a difficulty-aware variable library. This structured approach to prompt generation enhances the specificity and controllability of synthesized motions, ensuring domain fidelity and targeted difficulty escalation. The verification of VLM scoring reliability and the ablation studies on observation roles and the variable library underscore the rationality and effectiveness of the proposed design choices.

Potential impacts of this work include:
- **Accelerated Development of Humanoid Controllers:** By automating data generation and curriculum learning, the framework can expedite the training of controllers for complex tasks, reducing development cycles and resource requirements.
- **Enhanced Realism in Character Animation and VR:** More capable physics-based controllers can lead to more realistic and dynamic character behaviors in games, film, and virtual reality applications.
- **Advancements in Robotics and Embodied AI:** The principles of adaptive, difficulty-escalating curricula and autonomous data generation could be extended to real-world humanoid robots, enabling them to learn and adapt to a broader range of physical tasks.
- **Foundational Research for Future Generative Models:** The framework's modularity, allowing integration of future generative models, ensures its long-term relevance and potential to benefit from continued advancements in motion synthesis.

In conclusion, this research provides a comprehensive, empirically validated framework for advancing physics-based humanoid control towards high dynamics and diverse scenarios by addressing critical limitations in data acquisition, quality, and curriculum design.
