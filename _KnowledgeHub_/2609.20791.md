# StageGuard: Learning Stage Transitions for Long-Horizon Robot Tasks via Agentic Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> StageGuard is an agentic distillation framework. It trains a lightweight student VLM to decide when a robot should continue, advance, or skip subtasks in hierarchical long-horizon control. A large teacher VLM produces multi-layer reasoning grounded in annotated demonstrations. The student rewrites that reasoning into compact self-explanations for weighted fine-tuning. It reaches 90.18% transition completion on BEHAVIOR-1K, versus under 4% for VLM baselines.

## Problem

- Hierarchical planners rely on predefined skill termination conditions or privileged simulator signals, which are unavailable in real-world execution and hard to specify for learned policies.
- Large VLMs used as execution monitors have decision boundaries not aligned with subtask completion criteria, so plausible scene interpretations still produce wrong transition judgments.
- Lengthy cloud-hosted VLM reasoning limits monitoring frequency, and demonstration transition labels alone do not explain the visual evidence or task dependencies behind each switch decision.

## Method

- ==Multi-layer agentic reasoning==: a Qwen3.5-397B teacher runs ==state-layer agents== (history, robot state, object relations), ==task-layer agents== that infer a decision without labels, and ==decision-layer agents== that contrast it with ground truth.
- **Learning by explanation**: the Qwen3.5-0.8B student rewrites teacher traces into compact ==self-explanations==, generating three parallel candidates, and the teacher acts as judge to select the best-grounded one.
- ==Weighted SFT objective==: the autoregressive loss weights compact reasoning tokens at λ=0.5 and structured-output tokens (continue/advance/skip decision, next subtask, updated semantic history) at 1.0.

## Results

- On BEHAVIOR-1K, StageGuard reaches **90.18%** transition completion and **57.23%** next-subtask accuracy at **1.74 Hz**, versus GPT-5.6 Luna's **1.96%** and **0.24%** at **0.59 Hz**.
- Ablations on BEHAVIOR-1K: training on full teacher CoT collapses to **0.00%** transition completion and decision-only supervision reaches **37.72%**. On simpler LIBERO, both variants nearly match StageGuard's **96.23%**.
- Closed-loop BEHAVIOR-1K success rises to **0.53**, versus **0.35** decision-only and **0.30** end-to-end, narrowing the oracle gap from **0.28** to **0.10**. Real robots succeed **18/20** on UR5e and **16/20** on Piper.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reasoning supervision should match student capacity: a small model restating teacher reasoning in its own words beats imitating long teacher traces, and the margin grows with task complexity.
> - General-purpose VLM reasoning, even from frontier proprietary models, does not yield task-aligned completion boundaries. A small demonstration-grounded fine-tuned model beats scale for execution monitoring.
> - In hierarchical control, transition monitoring is a first-order bottleneck: bad switching decisions erode planner and skill gains, so decision-only monitors barely improve over end-to-end policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Imagine a robot arm tasked with opening a drawer, picking up a plate from a rack, placing it inside a cabinet, and then closing the drawer. A planning system knows the sequence, and a separate controller knows how to move the arm for each step. But something still has to watch the camera feed and decide: has the plate actually landed in the cabinet, or is it still mid-air? Switch too soon and the drawer closes on nothing; wait too long and the arm may nudge the plate back out. StageGuard is a learned monitor that makes those handoff decisions by studying demonstration recordings of the same task, guided by a large teacher model that explains which visual evidence justifies each switch. On two real robot arms, StageGuard completed the assigned tasks in 34 of 40 trials, with all monitoring-attributed failures traceable to partial observability rather than systematically wrong decisions.

The UR5e (top) and Piper arm (bottom left) each face a multi-step plate task. The pie charts distinguish control failures from monitoring failures: all four Piper failures were unsuccessful grasps of the thin plate edge, not missed transitions. This separation is the key diagnostic: the monitor's contribution can be assessed independently of the arm's mechanical limits.

### 2. Why handoff timing is its own problem

Each skill in a hierarchical robot plan has a precondition the previous skill must satisfy. The monitor watching these transitions must choose among three outputs: keep running the current skill, advance to the next, or skip ahead after an unrecoverable failure. Advancing prematurely leaves prerequisites unsatisfied; skipping is reserved for situations where the current subtask cannot be recovered. Making that choice requires reading the current camera frame alongside a history of what has already happened, the overall task plan, and the specific completion conditions for the current step.

Existing systems often rely on hand-coded termination checks or access to simulator state signals, neither of which transfers to real-world learned policies. Large vision-language models (VLMs)—models that take both images and text as input and produce text reasoning—can interpret scenes flexibly, but they were not trained to align their outputs with the precise boundaries of each subtask. Prompting a general-purpose model to judge completion tends to produce plausible-sounding but mistimed decisions, and querying a cloud-hosted model introduces delays that limit how often the monitor can check. The BEHAVIOR-1K evaluation covers six household mobile-manipulation tasks, with 10 training demonstrations per task and remaining demonstrations held for evaluation.

### 3. Teaching a small model with compact explanations

The teacher's reasoning flows from physical observation upward through task structure to a final comparison with the annotated decision (top). The student then rewrites that reasoning in a compact form it can reliably reproduce, and the best rewrite becomes the training signal (bottom). Ground-truth annotations are used only while building the training corpus.

StageGuard's training pipeline has two stages. In the first, a 397-billion-parameter teacher model works through each moment in a demonstration recording using three nested layers of reasoning. The state layer has four specialized agents—each instantiated through a role-specific prompt to the teacher—that extract what the robot and its surroundings looked like, what has happened previously, and which objects are in contact with the gripper. The task layer connects those observations to the current subtask's purpose and completion conditions. The decision layer then compares an initial judgment with the annotated progress marker, the intended next step, and the ground-truth transition label, explaining discrepancies when the initial reasoning was wrong.

The resulting reasoning traces are long and detailed. Directly training a compact 0.8-billion-parameter student to reproduce them turns out to be counterproductive on complex tasks. Instead, the student reads the full teacher trace as background context and rewrites the key decision logic in its own shorter form. Three candidate rewrites are generated for each training moment, and the teacher model selects the one that is best grounded in the observation, preserves decision-relevant reasoning, and correctly justifies the annotated outcome. The selected compact explanations, paired with the correct decisions, form the fine-tuning dataset. At deployment, the student runs alone, producing both a brief explanation and a structured output—continue, advance, or skip, plus the name of the next subtask when applicable.

### 4. Where supervision form matters most

On BEHAVIOR-1K's longer mobile-manipulation sequences, compact student explanations are the only supervision form that retains high transition accuracy. Directly imitating full teacher reasoning collapses to zero, while omitting explanations entirely yields 37.72%—well below the 90.18% of the full system. Inference speed favors the decision-only variant by roughly 20×, a genuine cost of adding explanations.

Transition completion measures the fraction of required switches detected within ±3 seconds of the annotated moment. On BEHAVIOR-1K, StageGuard reached 90.18% transition completion even on evaluation trajectories with subtask orderings not seen during training. The two ablations illuminate what drives this. Training the student to copy the teacher's full long reasoning ("w/o explain") scored 0% on all accuracy metrics: the traces appear too complex for a 0.8B model to reliably reproduce and apply. Training only on the correct labels without any explanation ("Decision-Only") reached 37.72%—better, but far short of 90.18%. The gain from compact self-explanation is largest on BEHAVIOR-1K, which features changing viewpoints, navigation between rooms, and varied task contexts; on the simpler constrained tabletop tasks in LIBERO, all three variants approached 96% transition completion, suggesting that explanation form matters mainly when scenes are complex and varied.

The comparison with [ROVER](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2508.01943)—a method that uses recursive subtask decomposition and sliding visual context to reason about progress without task-specific fine-tuning—adds context: adapted ROVER with a 4B model reached only 3.92% transition completion on BEHAVIOR-1K, suggesting that structured inference-time reasoning helps on simpler tasks but remains limited by the backbone model on more complex sequences. That said, StageGuard receives annotated boundary data that ROVER does not, so the comparison is not purely algorithmic.

### 5. Handoff accuracy transfers to task success

Closed-loop simulation on BEHAVIOR-1K shows the benefit of accurate switching. StageGuard narrows the gap to the ground-truth-transition oracle substantially; decision-only monitoring adds only a modest gain over an end-to-end policy with no hierarchical structure at all. The oracle's remaining failures reflect low-level execution limits that no transition monitor can fix.

In simulation, hierarchical control with ground-truth transition annotations achieved a success rate of 0.63 (defined as the fraction of trials completing the full task) and a progress score of 0.88 (fraction of required subgoals achieved). An end-to-end policy without hierarchical structure reached 0.30 success and 0.43 progress; StageGuard reached 0.53 and 0.76, while decision-only monitoring reached only 0.35 and 0.54. The oracle's failures confirm that even perfect switching cannot compensate for low-level controller errors.

### 6. Compact reasoning, aligned decisions

What the evidence collectively shows is that hierarchical control delivers its intended advantage only when the monitor's judgments are calibrated to the specific moment a skill finishes—not to a general-purpose understanding of scenes. Label-only training provides some alignment but misses the visual reasoning needed to handle varied, long-horizon sequences. Copying a much larger model's lengthy reasoning overwhelms a small model. Compact student-generated explanations, selected for quality by the teacher, appear to transfer the relevant decision logic in a form the small model can actually use, bridging the gap between a teacher that reasons well and a student that can monitor in real time.
