# Dynamic Neural Koopman Distillation for Real-Time Robot Control Using Diffusion Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel Dynamic Neural Koopman Distillation framework transforms iterative diffusion model inference into a single forward pass, enabling real-time, low-latency robot control while preserving multimodal trajectory generation. This approach achieved up to 172x speedup in simulation and 37x on a Kinova manipulator, maintaining strong control performance across locomotion and obstacle avoidance tasks.

## Problem

- Diffusion models generate expressive, multimodal robot trajectories but are too slow for real-time, high-frequency closed-loop control due to iterative denoising.
- Current fast sampling or one-step distillation methods often compromise either speed, multimodal expressivity, or ability to handle state-dependent nonlinear robot dynamics.
- Static Koopman distillation approaches lack the flexibility to model the state-dependent, dynamic nature of robot motion generation effectively.

## Method

- Developed the Dynamic Neural ==Koopman== (DNK) Distillation framework to compress the iterative diffusion process into a single, fast forward pass.
- Integrated a Factorized Dynamic Koopman (FDK) layer to enable state-dependent latent dynamics, adapting the linear transition based on the robot's current condition.
- Employed a comprehensive training objective with teacher-fidelity, control-oriented, and structural regularization terms to preserve multimodality and ensure training stability.

## Results

- Demonstrated substantial reduction in inference latency, achieving up to **172x** speedup in simulated D4RL tasks and **37x** speedup (to **4.08** ms) on a physical Kinova manipulator.
- Maintained or improved control performance compared to the original diffusion teacher, achieving normalized returns of **0.8885** on HalfCheetah and **1.0973** on Walker2d, and **100%** success rate on a real-world obstacle avoidance task.
- Showcased enhanced robustness and multimodal trajectory generation, with lower intra-run variability and effective candidate diversity, outperforming existing one-step distillation methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling the latent denoising process with state-dependent dynamics, rather than a static global operator, is essential for effective diffusion distillation in robotics.
> - Factorizing the Koopman latent transition using modal projection/reconstruction matrices and sample-dependent modal gains provides a flexible and efficient parameterization for complex robot behaviors.
> - Decoupling multimodal trajectory generation from immediate action selection via an external critic enables real-time control while preserving the teacher's distributional diversity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic motion generation and control has seen the emergence of diffusion models as a promising approach. These models offer advantages over traditional unimodal policies by generating diverse and context-appropriate plans through stochastic trajectory generation. This capability makes them suitable for robots operating in dynamic environments and for tasks requiring multimodal futures, long horizons, or ambiguous feasible behaviors, as demonstrated in applications such as locomotion, manipulation, motion planning, and exploration.

However, a significant challenge for deploying diffusion policies on physical robotic systems is their inherent inference latency. The generative process of diffusion models involves multiple iterative reverse denoising steps to transform an initial noise sample into a task-conditioned trajectory. This sequential computation can result in inference times of hundreds of milliseconds per decision in high-dimensional control scenarios, which is often incompatible with the low-latency updates required for agile, high-frequency closed-loop control.

To address this latency, two main directions have been explored in the broader research community:
1. **Fast Samplers:** These methods, such as Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM), DPM-Solver, and EDM-style sampling, aim to reduce the number of denoising evaluations while retaining the original diffusion model. While they improve efficiency, they remain iterative and may not achieve the speed necessary for real-time, high-frequency closed-loop control.
2. **Distillation-Based Generators:** These frameworks train a "student" model to replicate the "teacher" diffusion model's behavior in fewer denoising steps or a single forward pass. Examples include Progressive Distillation and Consistency Models (Consistency Training and Consistency Distillation), which aim for substantial reductions in inference cost. While successful in reducing inference time, many existing one-step distillation approaches learn a direct mapping from noise to action or trajectory without explicitly modeling the structured evolution of denoising trajectories. This can lead to a loss of multimodality or temporal inconsistencies, particularly in robotic tasks characterized by strong nonlinearities, rapid behavioral changes, or varying state-dependent requirements, such as agile locomotion.

Koopman operator theory offers a principled method to introduce dynamical structure by lifting nonlinear dynamics into a globally linear latent space. Deep Koopman models have been applied in robotics for control and dynamics learning, but they often still rely on iterative upstream diffusion planners. Recent advancements in image synthesis, such as the Koopman Distillation Model (KDM), have proposed using a fixed global latent transition to compress diffusion inference into a single step. However, directly applying this static distillation framework to robot control is challenging because robotic trajectories exhibit strong state dependence, contact changes, and varying motion regimes. A single global latent transition may be too restrictive to represent the diverse denoising behaviors across different trajectory modes, potentially degrading control performance.

This work addresses the identified gap by developing a distillation framework that combines the expressivity of diffusion models with the real-time speed requirements of robot control. It integrates Koopman-inspired latent dynamics with a state-dependent, factorized transition mechanism, specifically designed to handle the complexities of robotic trajectories that static Koopman models might not capture. The aim is to achieve millisecond-level inference latency while preserving the multimodal expressivity characteristic of diffusion models, thereby enabling their practical deployment in real-time, closed-loop robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the disparity between the high expressive capability of diffusion models for robot trajectory generation and their computational cost, which currently prevents their widespread application in real-time, closed-loop control scenarios. Diffusion models excel at generating diverse and multimodal trajectories, offering robust solutions for complex robotic tasks by sampling from conditional distributions rather than producing deterministic outputs. This makes them well-suited for situations with inherent ambiguity, long planning horizons, or multiple plausible future states. However, their iterative denoising process requires multiple sequential network evaluations, resulting in inference latencies that are often too high for the millisecond-level update rates needed in agile physical robot control.

Existing attempts to accelerate diffusion model inference, such as faster sampling techniques or one-step distillation methods (e.g., Consistency Models), have partially mitigated this issue. However, these solutions face their own limitations in the context of robotics. Fast samplers remain iterative, which might not be sufficient for the most demanding real-time applications. One-step distillation methods, while reducing inference time, often learn direct mappings from noise to action without explicitly modeling the underlying structured evolution of the denoising process. This can lead to a loss of multimodal expressivity or temporal consistency, rendering them suboptimal for systems with highly nonlinear dynamics or scenarios requiring state-dependent adaptations in behavior.

Furthermore, while Koopman operator theory offers a framework to linearize nonlinear dynamics in a lifted latent space, and Koopman Distillation Models (KDM) have shown promise in compressing diffusion inference for image generation, these models typically rely on a static global operator. This static nature is a significant impediment for robotic control, where optimal trajectories are highly state-dependent and can vary substantially based on current observations, contact conditions, and desired motion regimes. A fixed global latent transition cannot adequately capture the dynamic and conditional nature of robot trajectory generation, potentially leading to degraded control performance.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a one-step diffusion distillation framework for robot control:** The core objective is to approximate the iterative reverse denoising process of a diffusion model into a single forward pass. This aims to drastically reduce inference cost, thereby enabling high-frequency closed-loop control for nonlinear robotic systems.
2. **Retain multimodal expressivity:** The framework intends to preserve the ability of the teacher diffusion model to generate diverse, context-appropriate trajectories, which is a key advantage of diffusion models over unimodal policies.
3. **Introduce a Factorized Dynamic Koopman (FDK) layer:** This layer is designed to model the latent linear transition in a state-dependent manner, addressing the limitations of static global operators. It achieves this through a product of learned matrices and state-dependent modal gains, offering a more flexible parameterization of denoising dynamics tailored for robotic control.
4. **Incorporate structural regularization:** The FDK layer is to be regularized with modal-gain and inverse-consistency terms to improve conditioning, ensure stability during training, and support multimodal candidate generation effectively.
5. **Evaluate performance on standard benchmarks and a physical robot:** The method is to be empirically validated on established D4RL MuJoCo locomotion tasks and a real-world Kinova manipulator obstacle-aware control task to demonstrate its efficacy in terms of control performance, inference latency reduction, and real-time operational capability compared to existing one-step distillation baselines and the original multistep diffusion teacher.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Dynamic Neural Koopman (DNK) Distillation framework is designed to bridge the gap between the expressive power of diffusion models and the low-latency requirements of real-time robot control. The methodology involves an offline distillation process followed by online deployment in a receding-horizon control loop.

#### 3.1. Offline Teacher-Target Pair Generation

The initial step involves creating an offline dataset for distillation. A pre-trained Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) serves as the "teacher" policy. Conditioning variables (e.g., current observation, history, task specification) are sampled from the dataset used to train the teacher. For each context `c`, a trajectory-shaped Gaussian noise tensor `τ_N` is generated, where task-relevant entries are replaced with values from `c` to form a conditioned prior `τ_c_prior`. The teacher diffusion policy is then queried with this `τ_c_prior` to generate a denoised trajectory target `˜τ_0`. This process creates a dataset of `(τ_c_prior, ˜τ_0)` pairs, where the task conditioning `c` is implicitly embedded in the fixed entries of `τ_c_prior`.

#### 3.2. State-Dependent Lifted Dynamics via the Factorized Dynamic Koopman (FDK) Layer

The core of the student model is the FDK layer, which models the one-step denoising process as a state-dependent transition in a learned latent space.
1. **Encoding:** The conditioned prior `τ_c_prior` is first mapped to a lifted latent state `z^(1) ∈ R^L` using an encoder network `E_ϕ`. The same encoder is used for both conditioned priors and denoised teacher targets, ensuring that denoising and reconstruction occur within a shared latent space.
2. **Factorized Dynamic Koopman Layer:** Unlike static Koopman distillation models that apply a single global linear operator, the FDK layer introduces state dependence. It replaces a fixed global transition with a factorized and state-dependent latent map. The transition is defined as `z^(0) = P(Qz^(1) ⊙ γ_ω(z^(1)))`.
 - `Q, P ∈ R^(L×L)` are learned modal projection and reconstruction matrices, respectively.
 - `γ_ω: R^L → R^L` is a modal-gain network with parameters `ω`, which predicts sample-dependent modal gains. Since `z^(1)` is derived from `τ_c_prior`, these gains `γ_ω(z^(1))` implicitly depend on the current condition `c`, allowing the latent transition to adapt to different observations and task specifications.
 - This formulation effectively defines a state-dependent factorized latent transition `K_ω(z) = P diag(γ_ω(z)) Q`, which provides a more flexible parameterization of denoising dynamics compared to a static operator, while maintaining lower parameter and computational overhead than a fully sample-specific dense operator.
3. **Decoding:** After the FDK transition, the predicted denoised latent state `z^(0)` is transformed back into a trajectory `ˆτ_0` using a decoder network `D_ψ`.

#### 3.3. Training Objective

The student model is trained using a comprehensive objective function that combines teacher-fidelity losses, control-oriented supervision, and structural regularization, weighted by teacher-quality scores.

1. **Teacher-Fidelity Losses:**
 - `L_rec`: Reconstruction loss `∥D_ψ(E_ϕ(˜τ_0)) - ˜τ_0∥_2^2`. This term regularizes the encoder-decoder pair, ensuring that denoised teacher trajectories are accurately represented and reconstructed within the shared latent space.
 - `L_lat`: Latent alignment loss `∥z^(0) - sg(E_ϕ(˜τ_0))∥_2^2`. This aligns the FDK-evolved latent state `z^(0)` with the encoded teacher target `E_ϕ(˜τ_0)`. The stop-gradient operator `sg(·)` ensures that `E_ϕ(˜τ_0)` acts as a fixed target, facilitating latent alignment while `E_ϕ` and `D_ψ` learn representations.
 - `L_pred`: Trajectory prediction loss `∥ˆτ_0 - ˜τ_0∥_2^2`. This directly supervises the decoded student trajectory to match the teacher's denoised target trajectory.

2. **Control-Oriented Supervision:**
 - `L_act`: Weighted action-sequence loss `∥Π_a(ˆτ_0) - Π_a(˜τ_0)∥_W_a^2`. Given that only the first action is executed in receding-horizon control, this term applies a diagonal weighting matrix `W_a` to emphasize the accuracy of immediate actions while maintaining consistency across the entire predicted action sequence.

3. **Structural Regularization:**
 - `L_spec`: Spectral regularization `(1/L) Σ max(0, |γ_ω,ℓ(z)| - 1)`. This term discourages excessive amplification in the lifted transition, promoting stable training.
 - `L_inv`: Inverse-consistency regularization `∥PQ - I∥_F^2`. This term encourages the modal projection and reconstruction matrices `P` and `Q` to behave as approximate inverses, improving the conditioning of the factorized latent transition.

4. **Total Objective:** The total loss `L` is a weighted sum of these terms:
 `L = α_rec L_rec + α_lat L_lat + α_pred L_pred + α_act L_act + α_spec L_spec + α_inv L_inv`.
 Data-fitting losses (L_rec, L_lat, L_pred, L_act) are weighted by `w^(i)`, a quality score derived from the teacher's cumulative-return classifier, emphasizing higher-quality teacher trajectories during training. Structural regularizers are not sample-weighted.

#### 3.4. Inference: Batched One-Step Receding-Horizon Control

During deployment, the trained student model operates in a receding-horizon control loop.
1. At each control step, a conditioned Gaussian prior trajectory `τ_prior` is constructed based on the current robot state and task context.
2. `N_cand` independent noise seeds are sampled, and the student model evaluates the resulting batch of conditioned priors in parallel to generate `N_cand` candidate trajectories.
3. These candidates are then ranked by an external selector, such as an Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) critic or a teacher-side classifier, which assigns a task-dependent control score to each.
4. The controller executes only the first action of the highest-scoring candidate.
5. The process repeats at the next control step with updated observations, allowing for continuous replanning. This separation of multimodal candidate generation from control-oriented selection enables the student to retain distributional diversity while ensuring action quality at execution time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the DNK distillation framework was evaluated through simulations on D4RL MuJoCo locomotion benchmarks and real-world experiments on a Kinova Gen3 manipulator.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (D4RL MuJoCo Locomotion)

The proposed method was tested on `halfcheetah-medium-expert-v2` and `walker2d-medium-expert-v2` tasks, with comparisons against the multistep diffusion teacher and various one-step baselines (Consistency Distillation (CD), Consistency Training (CT), Koopman Distillation Model (KDM), and Factorized KDM (KDM-F)). All one-step student methods were evaluated using 64 candidate trajectories and IQL-based candidate scoring.

**Overall Control Performance:**
- The DNK method achieved the highest normalized returns on both tasks: 0.8885 ± 0.0042 on HalfCheetah and 1.0973 ± 0.0002 on Walker2d. These returns were comparable to or, in some cases, higher than those of the multistep diffusion teacher (HalfCheetah: 0.8518 ± 0.0032; Walker2d: 1.0295 ± 0.0051), which the authors attribute partly to the teacher-weighted offline distillation strategy focusing on higher-quality teacher trajectories.
- The method also yielded the highest worst-case returns, suggesting enhanced performance consistency across different experimental seeds.
- In contrast, consistency-based baselines (CD, CT) and static Koopman baselines (KDM, KDM-F) performed substantially worse than both the teacher and the proposed DNK method. For example, KDM on Walker2d exhibited unstable gait in rollouts, suggesting limitations of a fixed global latent transition for state-dependent robotic dynamics.
- Comparing IQL-based scoring with classifier-based scoring (Ours (classifier)), IQL-based selection generally resulted in slightly higher returns, indicating its suitability for emphasizing immediate action quality in receding-horizon control.

**Real-Time Performance and Pareto Frontier:**
- A significant reduction in decision latency was observed. The diffusion teacher, requiring 20 inference steps, had latencies of 116.51 ms on HalfCheetah and 301.22 ms on Walker2d. The DNK method, as a one-step student, reduced these to 0.82 ms and 1.75 ms, respectively. This corresponds to approximately 142x and 172x speedups.
- The method demonstrated a favorable latency–return trade-off, achieving teacher-level performance at significantly lower latencies, which is critical for high-frequency closed-loop control.

**Variability and Multimodal Candidate Generation:**
- The structural regularization (spectral and inverse-consistency) contributed to low intra-run variability (σ_ep). On Walker2d, DNK achieved the lowest intra-run variability (0.0004) among all one-step baselines.
- Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projections indicated that the candidate trajectories generated by DNK occupied a broad region, aligning well with teacher-generated candidates, which suggests that the method retained multimodal diversity while improving stability.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments (Kinova Gen3 Manipulator)

The DNK method was deployed on a physical Kinova Gen3 manipulator for an obstacle-aware reconfiguration task, moving the robot from an initial to a goal configuration while avoiding static obstacles. The student was trained by distilling a custom B-spline planner-generated teacher policy.

**Hardware Performance:**
- **Inference Latency:** Mean inference latency was reduced from 151.00 ms for the teacher to 4.08 ms for DNK, an approximate 37.0x reduction. The 95th-percentile (P95) latency for DNK was 4.73 ms, staying well within the 50 ms command period, unlike the teacher which exceeded it.
- **Task Completion Time:** The reduction in latency translated to a decrease in task completion time, from 314.95 s for the teacher to 85.43 s for DNK.
- **Task Success and Accuracy:** Both methods achieved a 100.0% success rate over 50 hardware trials. Terminal error (end-effector distance to goal) was comparable (DNK: 3.46 ± 1.75 mm; Teacher: 3.09 ± 1.42 mm).
- **Obstacle Avoidance:** DNK achieved a slightly larger mean obstacle clearance (48.95 ± 12.12 mm) compared to the teacher (46.29 ± 12.91 mm), indicating no compromise in obstacle-avoidance behavior due to faster inference.
- **Qualitative Behavior:** The teacher policy exhibited slower corrective motions and visible hesitation near obstacles, while the DNK student replanned more promptly, resulting in smoother obstacle-avoidance behavior and faster goal reaching.

These results collectively demonstrate that the DNK distillation framework successfully enables real-time robot control with diffusion models by substantially reducing inference latency while maintaining or improving control performance, robustness, and multimodal expressivity across both simulated and physical robotic platforms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in enabling the real-time deployment of diffusion models for robotic control. The core significance lies in effectively addressing the primary bottleneck of diffusion policies—their high inference latency—without sacrificing their core advantages of expressivity and multimodality.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

1. **Bridging the gap between expressive planning and real-time control:** Diffusion models offer robust planning capabilities for complex, multimodal robotic tasks. By distilling their multistep inference into a single forward pass, this work makes these powerful generative models practically applicable to dynamic, closed-loop robotic systems operating under stringent real-time constraints. This enables robots to execute agile and precise maneuvers that were previously computationally prohibitive for diffusion-based policies.
2. **Novel Koopman-inspired architecture:** The introduction of the Factorized Dynamic Koopman (FDK) layer provides a principled way to model the state-dependent evolution of denoising trajectories. Unlike static Koopman approaches, the FDK layer adapts to varying robot states, contact conditions, and motion regimes through learned modal gains. This structural design is crucial for capturing the complexities of robot dynamics and retaining the multimodality of the teacher policy, which is a limitation of many existing one-step distillation methods.
3. **Demonstrated performance on diverse robotic tasks:** The method's effectiveness is validated across both simulated D4RL locomotion benchmarks and a real-world Kinova manipulator task. The significant speedups (up to 172x in simulation, 37x on hardware) coupled with maintained or improved control performance (higher returns, comparable terminal error, enhanced obstacle clearance) and robustness (low variability, high worst-case returns, 100% success rate on hardware) underscore its generalizability and practical utility.
4. **Enabling new capabilities for robotic systems:** Achieving millisecond-level inference latencies allows for higher control frequencies, leading to smoother, more responsive, and safer robot operation. For instance, the Kinova manipulator's reduced task completion time and smoother obstacle avoidance behavior highlight the direct benefits of faster replanning in dynamic environments. This can open doors for more sophisticated human-robot interaction scenarios, where reactive and adaptive robot behavior is paramount.
5. **Foundation for future research:** The framework provides a strong foundation for future work in several directions. The ability to generate high-quality, diverse trajectories in real-time could be leveraged for advanced safety-critical control algorithms, where rapid decision-making and consideration of multiple future possibilities are essential. Furthermore, extending this approach to more complex, unstructured environments and richer interaction settings could significantly expand the applicability of diffusion models in robotics.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art by transforming high-latency, multimodal diffusion planners into real-time, deployable policies for physical robots. It provides a methodological blueprint for leveraging the generative power of diffusion models in demanding real-world robotic applications.
