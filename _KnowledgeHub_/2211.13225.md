# Learning to Imitate Object Interactions from Internet Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> RHOV reconstructs 4D hand-object trajectories from unconstrained Internet videos by optimizing hand and object poses jointly with 2D cues (masks, keypoints, monocular depth, optical flow) plus temporal smoothness. The recovered object trajectory then defines a dense two-phase reward for PPO, training a Franka arm with a parallel jaw gripper to imitate the interaction in simulation, reaching 79.3% success.

## Problem

- Prior visual-demonstration robotics requires humans acting deliberately for the robot, usually in instrumented lab settings with depth cameras, which does not scale to everyday video.
- Existing hand-object reconstruction targets lab videos or single in-the-wild images, and per-frame methods flip object orientation under symmetry, yielding temporally invalid trajectories.
- Retargeting and teleoperation work usually ignores the manipulated object and assumes human-like end-effectors, so recovered motion transfers poorly to grippers or other morphologies.

## Method

- **RHOV** initializes hands via ==Frank MoCap== ==MANO== fits and bidirectional box tracking, then fits object pose per frame by ==differentiable rendering== under mask and normalized monocular-depth losses.
- A joint temporal stage optimizes hand articulation, object pose and shared scale under mask, penetration/interaction, occlusion, ==optical-flow==, ==GrabNet== grasp prior, MANO norm and exponentially-weighted smoothness losses.
- Object-centric imitation frames the 4D trajectory as an RL goal: a two-phase dense reward (pick-up then pose/velocity matching with bonuses) trains a 3-layer policy with ==PPO== in ==IsaacGym==.

## Results

- On HO3D, RHOV reaches **10.1** hand error and **9.6** object error, cutting the per-frame RHO object error of **19.9** roughly in half while staying competitive with video baselines.
- Ablations show temporal optimization drops object error **14.3** to **13.0** and temporal distance **87.3** to **4.1**; joint hand-object optimization improves object error from **14.6** to **13.0**; oracle masks reach **9.3**.
- Imitation with the full reward attains **79.3%** success (**0.039** position, **0.289** rotation distance); dropping velocity, frames-matched, or rotation terms falls to **46.6%**, **30.9%**, and **0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reconstructing the hand helps even when only the object trajectory is needed, since contact and occlusion constraints disambiguate object pose under mutual occlusion.
> - Temporal smoothness is not cosmetic: it resolves symmetry-induced orientation flips that make per-frame reconstructions unusable as physical goal trajectories.
> - Object-centric goals decouple imitation from morphology, letting non-anthropomorphic embodiments reproduce human interactions; gains are bounded by 2D segmentation and object-model quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields: **computer vision, imitation learning, and robot manipulation**. Its primary goal is to address a critical bottleneck in robot learning: the scarcity of high-quality, diverse, and scalable demonstration data.

Traditionally, learning robot skills through imitation often relies on carefully curated data collected in controlled laboratory environments. This involves using specialized sensors like depth cameras, motion capture systems, or human operators performing actions specifically for the robot. While effective, this approach is resource-intensive, time-consuming, and often results in datasets that lack the diversity and realism of everyday human activity. The "Learning from visual demonstrations" paradigm, as highlighted in the introduction, is popular but predominantly confined to these constrained settings.

The emergence of vast amounts of **Internet video data** (e.g., YouTube) and large-scale vision datasets (like EPIC-Kitchens, Ego4D, 100 Days of Hands) presents an opportunity to overcome this data limitation. These videos depict a rich tapestry of human actions in unconstrained, everyday settings, offering an unprecedented scale of realistic interaction data. However, leveraging this data for robot learning introduces significant challenges:

1. **Unconstrained Environments:** Internet videos vary widely in lighting, camera angles, backgrounds, and subject matter, making robust analysis difficult.
2. **4D Reconstruction:** Understanding dynamic human-object interactions requires reconstructing not just 3D poses but their evolution over time (4D trajectories) – a challenging task due to factors like mutual occlusions (e.g., hand obscuring the object). Existing methods for hand-object reconstruction often focus on static images or highly controlled lab videos.
3. **Transfer to Robotics:** Reconstructing human motion is one thing; making it imitable by a robot with different kinematics, dynamics, and morphology (e.g., a parallel jaw gripper vs. a human hand) is another. Many previous works in teleoperation or learning from demonstrations tend to focus on human-like end-effectors or require direct human control interfaces.

This paper directly addresses these challenges by proposing a novel pipeline that bridges the gap between raw, unconstrained Internet video and actionable robot control policies. It extends the state-of-the-art in 4D hand-object reconstruction to handle the complexities of "in-the-wild" videos and demonstrates a robust framework for translating these reconstructions into robot skills through reinforcement learning. By adopting an "object-centric" approach, the work also moves beyond the limitations of human-like end-effectors, making the learned skills transferable to a broader range of robotic systems.

In essence, this research represents a significant step towards democratizing robot learning data, potentially unlocking a scalable and diverse source of realistic demonstrations that can accelerate the development of more capable and general-purpose robotic agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable robots to **learn to imitate object interactions directly from readily available Internet videos of human activity.** This ambitious goal is driven by a clear motivation to overcome the limitations of traditional, lab-centric data collection for robot learning.

The **key motivations** can be summarized as:

1. **Scalability and Abundance:** Internet videos offer an immense and ever-growing reservoir of human interaction data. Leveraging this data could provide a scalable and cost-effective alternative to expensive and time-consuming in-lab data collection, which is a major bottleneck for developing diverse robot skills.
2. **Realism and Diversity:** Videos of "normal human activity" provide a more realistic and diverse set of interactions than those typically captured in controlled lab settings. This exposure to varied contexts and interaction styles can potentially lead to more robust and generalizable robot behaviors.

To achieve this overarching goal, the authors identify and aim to address several **specific challenges**, leading to their explicit key objectives:

1. **Robust 4D Hand-Object Reconstruction from Unconstrained Video:** The primary technical objective is to develop a computer vision technique capable of accurately recovering the 4D (3D spatial + time) trajectories of both the human hand and the manipulated object from noisy, unconstrained monocular Internet videos. This is challenging due to inherent ambiguities, mutual occlusions between the hand and object, and the variability of "in-the-wild" visual data. This leads to the first main contribution:
 - **Objective 1: Develop RHOV (Reconstructing Hands and Objects from Videos), a novel reconstruction technique.** This technique must leverage 2D image cues (like keypoints, masks, depth) and temporal smoothness constraints to infer 3D hand and object poses over time.

2. **Imitating Reconstructed Object Interactions in a Physics Simulator:** Once the 4D trajectories are reconstructed, the next challenge is to translate this kinematic information into dynamic, physically plausible robot actions. This requires a system that can effectively use the reconstructed trajectories as goals for robot control, even when the robot's morphology differs from the human hand. This forms the basis of their second main contribution:
 - **Objective 2: Develop a system for imitating object interactions in a physics simulator using reinforcement learning.** The system should use the recovered trajectories to define a dense reward function and train a neural network policy to control a robot arm (e.g., with a parallel jaw gripper) to reproduce the object's motion. A key aspect here is the "object-centric" approach, making it morphology-agnostic.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to pave the way for a new paradigm in robot learning, where robots can autonomously acquire complex manipulation skills by observing human behavior in the real world, as captured in readily available video content.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology presented in this paper is a two-stage pipeline: first, reconstructing 4D hand-object trajectories from Internet videos, and second, imitating these trajectories with a robotic arm in a physics simulator using reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Stage 1: Reconstructing Hands and Objects from Videos (RHOV)

RHOV is an optimization-based technique designed to reconstruct 4D hand and object poses from a monocular RGB video sequence and a 3D object model. It generates hand poses in MANO format (articulation parameters, global translation) and object poses as global rotation, translation, and scale parameters.

1. **Initial Hand Pose Estimation:**
 - Uses Frank MoCap (a regression-based method for 3D hand pose) to get initial hand articulation parameters for each frame.
 - To maintain a consistent right-hand track across the video, a tracking mechanism initializes the hand bounding box in consecutive frames, helping resolve ambiguities between multiple hands or left/right hands.

2. **Initial Object Pose Estimation:**
 - **Object Mask Tracking:** The hand-object detector from provides bounding boxes for the manipulated object. PointRend, an instance segmentation model, then generates 2D masks for all objects. The mask with the highest Intersection-over-Union (IoU) with the detected object bounding box is selected as the object's 2D mask track.
 - **3D Pose Estimation:** For each frame, a gradient-based optimization with a differentiable renderer is used to fit a 3D object pose to the 2D mask track and depth cues.
 - **Mask Loss (L_proj):** Compares the rendered object mask to the 2D object segmentation mask. Crucially, this loss is only computed for regions *not* occluded by the initial hand mask, mitigating ambiguity from hand-object occlusion.
 - **Depth Loss (L_depth):** Leverages monocular depth predictions from MiDaS, normalizing both rendered and predicted depth to handle scale ambiguities inherent in monocular RGB.
 - The optimization problem minimizes a weighted sum of L_proj and L_depth. Initializing the object pose from the prior frame's optimized result encourages temporal smoothness.

3. **Temporal Optimization (Joint Hand-Object):**
 - Given the initial hand and object poses, RHOV performs a joint optimization over *all frames* to refine both hand and object parameters for consistency. This step is critical for generating smooth, physically plausible 4D trajectories.
 - **Optimization Variables:** Hand translation (T_h), rotation (R_h), finger articulation (A_h), and object rotation (R_o), translation (T_o), and a globally shared object scale (s_o).
 - **Loss Functions:** A comprehensive set of losses ensures spatial and temporal consistency, physical plausibility, and visual alignment:
 - **Mask Loss:** Compares rendered hand/object masks to input segmentation masks.
 - **Interaction Losses (L_p, L_inter):** Penalizes hand-object mesh penetration and encourages contact between fingertips and the object.
 - **Depth Loss (L_depth):** Re-applied for refined poses.
 - **Temporal Loss (L_temporal):** Penalizes changes in hand/object vertices, translations, and MANO parameters between consecutive frames, encouraging smoothness over a temporal window.
 - **Optical Flow Loss (L_of):** Compares the projected 2D motion of object vertices to optical flow predictions from RAFT between frames, ensuring consistency with apparent motion.
 - **Occlusion Loss (L_h_occ, L_o_occ):** Enforces visibility constraints, penalizing situations where, e.g., the hand is rendered in front of the object but its 2D mask suggests the object is visible.
 - **MANO Loss (L_mpn):** Regularizes hand articulation parameters to prevent unnatural poses.
 - **GrabNet Loss (L_gn):** Encourages realistic grasps by penalizing deviation from grasp parameters predicted by GrabNet, applied when the hand is near the object.
 - **Projection Loss (L_iop, L_ihp):** Stabilizes optimization by minimizing deviation from the initial 2D projections of hand and object, preventing large drifts.
 - **Two-Stage Optimization:** The joint optimization proceeds in two phases:
 - Phase 1: Optimizes only object translation and scale, encouraging the object to move closer to the (fixed) hand while preserving its initial 2D projection.
 - Phase 2: Optimizes all parameters with all loss functions. GrabNet loss is introduced midway when hand poses are more stable. Adam optimizer is used for both stages.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Imitating Object Interactions

This stage uses the 4D object trajectories recovered by RHOV as goal specifications for a reinforcement learning agent controlling a robot in a physics simulator.

1. **Task Setup:**
 - **Simulation Environment:** NVIDIA IsaacGym with PixMC benchmark suite.
 - **Robot:** 7 DoF Franka robotic arm with a parallel jaw gripper.
 - **Objects:** 3D models imported, manually estimated physical properties (e.g., mass).
 - **Task Phases:**
 - **Phase 1 (Initial Pickup):** Robot picks up the object from a starting position on the table and moves it to the initial pose of the reconstructed trajectory.
 - **Phase 2 (Imitation):** Robot imitates the remainder of the trajectory, matching the object's 6 DoF pose (position and rotation) at each timestep.

2. **Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
 - **Object-Centric Perspective:** The RL focuses solely on imitating the *object* trajectory, making the approach morphology-agnostic (not limited to human-like end-effectors).
 - **Goal Specification:** The recovered 4D object trajectory (sequence of position and rotation pairs) serves as the dense, step-by-step goal.
 - **Actions:** The policy predicts delta joint positions for the robot arm at 60 Hz.
 - **Observations:** Include robot joint positions/velocities, distance from gripper to object, and the position/rotation distance to the *current* and *next* target goal poses from the trajectory.
 - **Reward Function:** A dense, two-phase reward function guides the learning:
 - **Initial Reward (r_initial):** Incentivizes gripper fingers to close on the object, lifting it above the table, and matching its position/rotation to the first target frame of the trajectory. Bonuses are awarded for meeting position/rotation thresholds.
 - **Imitation Reward (r_imitation):** Applied after the initial frame is reached. It strongly incentivizes matching the object's position (r_d), orientation (r_g), linear velocity (r_d_dot), and angular velocity (r_g_dot) to the target trajectory. Exponential terms ensure smooth gradients. Bonuses are also given for reaching position/rotation/velocity thresholds, and a term (f_t) rewards the percentage of matched frames in the trajectory.
 - **Training:** A neural network policy (3 hidden layers) is trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, with a separate policy trained for each video.

This comprehensive methodology allows for the extraction of rich interaction data from unstructured videos and its subsequent conversion into practical robot manipulation skills, representing a significant advancement in learning from human demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents both qualitative and quantitative results for its RHOV reconstruction technique and demonstrates the successful imitation of object interactions in a physics simulator.

#### 4.1. RHOV Reconstruction Performance

- **Qualitative Results on Internet Videos:**
 - RHOV was applied to 100 YouTube videos from the 100 Days of Hands dataset. The qualitative examples (Figure 4) showcase RHOV's ability to reconstruct diverse 4D hand-object trajectories involving various interactions and objects.
 - A visual comparison (Figure 7) with the state-of-the-art HOman method suggests that RHOV produces reconstructions of better quality on Internet videos, particularly in terms of visual consistency and plausibility.
 - The authors committed to releasing all 100 "4Dfied" video sequences, which is a valuable contribution to the research community.

- **Quantitative Results on In-Lab Datasets (with Ground Truth):**
 - **HO3D Dataset:** When evaluated on HO3D, a dataset with 3D ground truth for hands and objects, RHOV performs comparably to state-of-the-art techniques (Table 1). It significantly outperforms a per-frame baseline (RHO), highlighting the benefit of its temporal optimization. RHOV achieves competitive hand (MPJPE) and object (Chamfer distance) errors against Photo-consist and HOman.
 - **DexYCB Dataset:** On DexYCB, a large dataset with ground truth from depth cameras, RHOV demonstrates "respectable performance" (Tables 2, 3) despite not being specifically trained on this dataset. It achieves competitive Mean Per Joint Position Error (MPJPE) for hand poses and average recall for object poses compared to highly tuned, state-of-the-art methods presented in the DexYCB paper.

- **Ablation Studies on RHOV Components (Table 4):**
 - **Temporal Smoothness:** Temporal optimization is shown to be crucial. Removing it (i.e., "per frame" optimization) leads to significantly higher object error and drastically higher "Temporal Distance" (87.3 vs 4.1), indicating severe jitter and orientation flips, which would render trajectories unusable for imitation.
 - **2D Mask Quality:** Using "oracle masks" (ground truth 2D object masks) instead of predicted masks significantly improves 3D object reconstruction performance (object error drops from 13.0 to 9.3). This suggests that the 3D reconstruction quality is highly dependent on the quality of initial 2D segmentation and could improve with advancements in 2D perception models.
 - **Impact of Hand Reconstruction on Object:** Jointly optimizing both hand and object ("joint optim.") yields better object reconstructions compared to independent optimization of the object ("independent optim."). This indicates that reconstructing the interacting hand provides valuable context and constraints that benefit object pose estimation, even if the hand's trajectory isn't directly used for robot imitation.

#### 4.2. Imitation Learning Performance

- **Successful Imitation in Physics Simulator:** The paper demonstrates that the reconstructed 4D object trajectories can be successfully imitated by a 7 DoF Franka robotic arm with a parallel jaw gripper in a physics simulator (Figures 1, 6). The object-centric approach allows for effective control despite the morphological difference between the human hand and the robot gripper.

- **Ablation Studies on Imitation Rewards (Table 5):**
 - The composition of the dense reward function is critical for successful imitation. Removing any of the key reward components leads to a substantial drop in performance:
 - **Without velocity terms:** Position distance increased by 2.5x, rotation distance by 2.3x, and success rate dropped from 79.3% to 46.6%. This indicates that matching velocity is essential for dynamic, smooth imitation.
 - **Without number of matched frames (progress reward):** Position distance increased by 2.5x, rotation distance by 2.5x, and success rate dropped dramatically to 30.9%. This highlights the importance of providing a reward signal for overall task progress.
 - **Without rotation reward:** While position distance increased less (0.039 to 0.070), rotation distance soared by over 6x (0.289 to 1.782), and the success rate plummeted to 0.00%. This emphasizes that accurately matching orientation is non-negotiable for successful object interaction, indicating that a partially matched state is not considered successful.

#### 4.3. Failure Cases

- The authors candidly acknowledge failure cases (Figure 8). RHOV struggles when there are **poor 2D masks**, **incorrect or articulated object models** (i.e., using a static 3D model for an object that changes shape), or **severe hand-object occlusions**. These limitations point to areas for future improvement in the foundational computer vision components.

In summary, the main findings confirm the feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed pipeline. RHOV successfully extracts coherent 4D interaction trajectories from challenging Internet videos, and these trajectories are robust enough to serve as dense goals for training a robot to imitate complex object manipulation skills via reinforcement learning. The ablation studies rigorously support the design choices for both reconstruction and imitation stages.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Learning to Imitate Object Interactions from Internet Videos" carries significant implications for the fields of robotics, computer vision, and artificial intelligence, potentially transforming how robots learn complex manipulation skills.

1. **Democratization and Scalability of Robot Learning Data:** The most profound impact of this research lies in its ability to unlock the vast, untapped potential of **Internet videos as a scalable and realistic data source for robot learning**. Currently, data collection for robot manipulation is a costly, time-consuming, and often specialized endeavor. By demonstrating a viable pipeline to extract actionable information from readily available, diverse, and natural human activity videos, this work moves away from expensive lab-based setups. This could democratize access to training data, enabling researchers and practitioners to train robots for a much wider array of tasks and environments, accelerating progress in general-purpose robotic manipulation.

2. **Advancement in 4D Hand-Object Reconstruction "in the Wild":** The RHOV technique itself is a significant contribution to computer vision. Reconstructing 4D (3D spatial + time) hand-object interactions from monocular, unconstrained videos is an extremely challenging problem due to occlusions, diverse backgrounds, and inherent ambiguities. RHOV's optimization-based approach, leveraging a rich set of 2D image cues and, crucially, temporal smoothness and physical interaction constraints, represents a robust solution. The quantitative and qualitative results confirm its effectiveness and superiority over per-frame methods, pushing the state-of-the-art in this critical area. The commitment to release the 100 "4Dfied" trajectories will also provide a valuable benchmark for future research.

3. **Morphology-Agnostic Imitation and Generalization:** By adopting an "object-centric" perspective for imitation learning, the approach is not limited to robots with human-like hands. Instead, it focuses on reproducing the *object's* trajectory, allowing a standard robot arm with a parallel jaw gripper to learn the skill. This is a crucial step towards generalization, as it means learned skills are transferable across different robot embodiments, making the approach more practical and widely applicable in industrial and domestic settings where specialized grippers might not be feasible.

4. **Bridging Kinematic Observation and Dynamic Robot Control:** The methodology effectively bridges the gap between observed human kinematics (reconstructed 4D trajectories) and physically plausible robot dynamics. Using reinforcement learning within a physics simulator to match these kinematic trajectories allows the robot to infer the necessary forces and controls to execute the motion robustly. This can be viewed as a form of "dynamic 4D reconstruction" or "kinematic-to-dynamic transfer," improving upon mere kinematic reproduction by embedding physical realism.

5. **Foundation for More Complex Robot Learning:** The robust 4D trajectories generated by RHOV could serve as foundational data for more advanced robot learning paradigms. They could be used as demonstrations for skill learning, task-and-motion planning, or even for understanding human intent and interaction patterns at a deeper level. This could lead to robots that are not only capable of mimicking actions but also understanding the *purpose* behind those actions.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The identified failure cases (poor 2D masks, incorrect object models, articulated objects, severe occlusions) provide clear avenues for future research, particularly in improving foundational 2D perception models and enhancing 3D object representation for more complex scenarios. The success of the current approach provides strong motivation for continued investment in these areas.

In conclusion, this paper presents a compelling vision for scalable robot learning, taking a significant step towards enabling robots to learn autonomously from the wealth of human activity available online. Its dual contributions in advanced 4D reconstruction and effective imitation learning promise to accelerate the development of more intelligent, versatile, and robust robotic systems.
