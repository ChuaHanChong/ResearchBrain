# FabriVLA: A Lightweight Vision-Language-Action Model for Precise Multi-Task Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> FabriVLA is a lightweight Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed for precise multi-task robotic manipulation, achieving a 90.0% tier-average success rate on the Meta-World MT50 benchmark with approximately 0.89 billion parameters. This model integrates architectural innovations like gated self-attention and shallow VLM layer fusion to enhance performance while maintaining computational efficiency.

## Problem

- Existing large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are computationally intensive, with billions of parameters, leading to high inference latency.
- The high resource demands of these models restrict their practical use in real-time robotic control and deployment in environments with limited computational resources.
- There is a need for lightweight VLA architectures that can balance competitive manipulation performance with improved efficiency and a reduced parameter footprint.

## Method

- Develop **FabriVLA**, a lightweight Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model built on an InternVL3.5-1B backbone and a specialized ==flow-matching action head==.
- Integrate architectural innovations, including ==gated self-attention== for learning inter-step action dependencies and ==shallow VLM layer fusion== for enriched spatial context.
- Employ a ==single-stage joint optimization== strategy where the entire model, including the pre-trained VLM backbone and randomly initialized action head, is trained end-to-end.

## Results

- FabriVLA achieved a **90.0%** tier-average success rate and a **92.0%** overall episode-level success rate on the Meta-World MT50 benchmark.
- With **0.89 billion** parameters, the model surpassed other lightweight VLA models on MT50, including LA4VLA (**87.5%**) and Evo-Depth (**84.4%**), without requiring extensive robotic data pretraining.
- Ablation studies showed that shallow VLM layer fusion boosted the tier-average success rate by **7.1 percentage points**, and gated self-attention contributed a **9.2 percentage point** improvement (from **48.5%** to **57.7%** in a specific ablation setting).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Competitive multi-task manipulation capabilities can be achieved by VLA models with a compact parameter footprint, demonstrating that efficient architectures can balance performance and resource demands.
> - Gated self-attention in the action head provides a mechanism for gradually learning inter-step dependencies, leading to more stable optimization and better temporal action understanding.
> - Fusing features from shallow and deep VLM layers enriches the spatial context available to the action head, which is crucial for precise object localization and fine-grained manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represent an emerging paradigm in robotic manipulation, designed to enable general-purpose robots to understand natural language instructions, interpret visual observations, and execute corresponding action sequences. Existing large-scale VLA models, such as RT-2, OpenVLA, and $\pi_0$, have demonstrated capabilities in leveraging extensive pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) and diverse robotic datasets. However, these models typically involve tens of billions of parameters, leading to substantial computational costs and significant inference latency. Such resource requirements can pose practical challenges for real-time robotic control and deployment in environments with limited computational resources.

This research addresses the existing gap between high-performance, large-scale VLAs and the need for more efficient, deployment-friendly solutions. It positions itself within the growing effort to develop lightweight VLA architectures that can achieve competitive manipulation performance while maintaining a significantly reduced parameter footprint and improved efficiency. FabriVLA builds upon recent advancements in lightweight VLA design, exemplified by models like Evo-1, which also focus on achieving strong performance without relying on multi-billion parameter backbones or extensive robotic data pretraining. By proposing architectural modifications and a targeted training strategy, FabriVLA aims to demonstrate that a compact VLA can effectively balance performance with efficiency, thereby expanding the practical applicability of VLA models in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop FabriVLA, a lightweight Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model designed for precise multi-task manipulation. This overarching goal is driven by several specific objectives and motivations:

- **Addressing Computational Constraints of Large VLAs**: The development of FabriVLA is motivated by the computational intensity and high inference latency associated with large-scale VLA models. While these large models (often with tens of billions of parameters) achieve notable manipulation capabilities, their resource demands restrict their use in real-time robotic control and practical applications. The authors aim to demonstrate that a more compact model can achieve comparable performance, thereby offering a more viable solution for deployment.

- **Achieving Competitive Performance with a Modest Parameter Footprint**: A key objective is to build a VLA model with a significantly smaller parameter count (approximately 1 billion parameters) that can still achieve high success rates on challenging multi-task manipulation benchmarks. The Meta-World MT50 benchmark, encompassing 50 diverse manipulation tasks, serves as the primary evaluation environment to validate FabriVLA's performance across various difficulty levels and manipulation demands.

- **Exploring Architectural Innovations for Efficiency and Precision**: The research seeks to integrate specific architectural designs within a lightweight framework to enhance performance. This includes:
 - **Gated Self-Attention**: To enable the action head to learn inter-step dependencies within predicted action sequences more effectively and gradually, providing a stable optimization path.
 - **Shallow VLM Layer Fusion**: To enrich the spatial context available to the action head by combining high-level semantic features from deep VLM layers with lower-level spatial details from shallow VLM layers. This is particularly motivated by the need for precise object localization and contact-rich manipulation tasks.

- **Demonstrating Single-Stage Joint Optimization Effectiveness**: The authors aim to validate the efficacy of a single-stage joint optimization strategy, where a pre-trained VLM backbone and a randomly initialized action head are trained together from the outset, with all parameters unfrozen. This approach seeks to efficiently adapt the VLM's knowledge to the robotic manipulation task without complex multi-stage training pipelines or extensive robotic data pretraining.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between the theoretical capabilities of large VLA models and the practical requirements for real-world robotic deployment by developing an efficient, high-performing, and precisely controlled manipulation agent within a lightweight framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FabriVLA integrates an InternVL3.5 vision-language backbone with a specialized flow-matching action head, incorporating gated self-attention and shallow VLM layer fusion. The model undergoes a single-stage joint optimization process.

#### 3.1. Model Architecture

FabriVLA comprises three main components: a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone, state and action encoders, and a flow-matching action head.

#### 3.1.1. Vision-Language Backbone

- **Model**: InternVL3.5-1B (Wang et al. 2025) is utilized as the VLM backbone.
- **Input Processing**: Input RGB images are resized to 448 × 448 pixels and fed into the vision encoder, generating a sequence of visual tokens. A natural language task prompt (e.g., “Dunk the basketball into the basket”) is tokenized by a language tokenizer and concatenated with the visual tokens.
- **Multimodal Processing**: The VLM processes this fused multimodal sequence through 14 retained transformer layers, producing contextualized tokens that capture both visual semantics and language grounding.

#### 3.1.2. State and Action Encoding

- **State Encoding**: The robot state $s \in \mathbb{R}^{24}$ (encompassing end-effector pose, gripper state, and object positions) is processed by a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with ReLU activation. This generates a state token $z_s \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}$, which is then prepended to the VLM context tokens.
- **Action Encoding**: Noisy action chunks $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 24}$ (representing a 50-step prediction horizon, with each step having a 24-dimensional action) are encoded by a MultiEmbodimentActionEncoder. Each action step is individually projected through a shared MLP (24 dimensions to 1024 dimensions, with two intermediate 1024-dimensional layers and ReLU). The projected tokens are then augmented with a horizon positional encoding to maintain temporal order, resulting in a sequence of action tokens $Z_a \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 1024}$.

#### 3.1.3. Action-Head Modules

The action head, termed the "action expert," consists of a stack of 8 FabriVLA TransformerBlock layers. Each block processes action tokens $A \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 1024}$ and context tokens $C \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1024}$.

- **Gated Self-Attention Transformer Block**:
 1. **Gated Self-Attention**: Action tokens $A$ first undergo multi-head self-attention, modulated by a learnable gate parameter $g \in \mathbb{R}$. This gate is initialized to zero, effectively suppressing self-attention at the start of training. As training progresses, the gate parameter learns to open, allowing for the gradual integration of inter-step dependencies among action tokens.
 2. **Cross-Attention to Context**: The (potentially self-attended) action tokens then perform cross-attention with the VLM context tokens, integrating multimodal information.
 3. **Feed-Forward Network with Time Conditioning**: A sinusoidal time embedding (TimeEmb($t$)) corresponding to the flow-matching timestep $t$ is added to the tokens before they pass through a feed-forward network (FFN). The FFN expands tokens to 4096 dimensions, applies GELU activation, and projects them back to 1024 dimensions.
 The output of the final transformer block is layer-normalized, flattened, projected via a learned pooling projection, and decoded by a two-layer MLP to produce the predicted velocity field $v \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 24}$.

- **Shallow VLM Layer Fusion**: To provide the action head with both high-level semantic context and lower-level spatial detail crucial for precise manipulation, FabriVLA fuses features from two VLM layers.
 - The final retained VLM layer (layer 14) provides deep semantic information.
 - An intermediate VLM layer (layer 6) is chosen empirically to provide richer spatial detail.
 - Fusion is implemented via a "concat\_proj" strategy: the deep and shallow token sequences are concatenated along the feature dimension and then projected back to the original embedding size (1024 dimensions) using a learned linear layer. This linear layer is initialized such that at the start of training, the fused context is identical to using only the deep layer, allowing the model to learn to utilize the shallow features. This fusion mechanism adds approximately 2.1 million parameters.

#### 3.2. Flow Matching Training

FabriVLA uses flow matching (Lipman et al. 2023) to generate action sequences. This generative framework learns a time-dependent vector field that transforms samples from a simple base distribution (uniform noise) to the target data distribution (expert action chunks).
- **Flow Interpolation**: For a ground-truth action chunk $a$ and a random noise sample $\epsilon$ (uniform noise in normalized action space), an interpolated sample $x_t = (1 - t) \epsilon + t a$ is created, where $t \in$ is the flow time. During training, $t$ is sampled from a Beta(2, 2) distribution, which concentrates samples in the intermediate denoising regime.
- **Velocity Field Prediction**: The model learns a velocity field $v_\theta(x_t, t | s, \text{image}, \text{prompt})$ that predicts the instantaneous direction of probability transport. The training objective minimizes the squared error between the predicted velocity $v_\theta$ and the target velocity $(a - \epsilon)$.
- **Inference**: To generate actions, the model starts with uniform random noise $x_0$ and numerically integrates the learned velocity field over 50 equally spaced timesteps from $t=0$ to $t=1$. The final sample $x_1$ is the predicted normalized action chunk, which is then denormalized and executed in a closed-loop receding horizon fashion.

#### 3.3. Training Procedure

FabriVLA is trained in a single stage using the openly released Evo-1 Meta-World demonstration dataset, which consists of 50 trajectories per MT50 task, totaling 2,500 trajectories.
- **Initialization**: The action head is randomly initialized, while the InternVL3.5 backbone is loaded from a publicly released pre-trained checkpoint.
- **Optimization**: The entire model, including both the VLM backbone and the action head, is jointly optimized from the start, with all parameters unfrozen throughout training.
- **Hyperparameters**: Training is conducted for 100,000 steps with a batch size of 40 across 5 NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPUs. The learning rate is $2.0 \times 10^{-5}$, employing a linear warmup over 1,000 steps followed by cosine decay. Gradient clipping is applied at a norm of 1.0, and a weight decay of $10^{-4}$ is used.
- **Mixed Precision**: BF16 mixed precision is used in conjunction with DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 and FP32 master weights. This configuration is critical to ensure that the VLM backbone receives meaningful gradient updates, preventing small weight updates from being lost due to BF16 quantization noise during joint training. The AdamW optimizer is employed, and data augmentation techniques (random cropping, rotation, and color jitter) are applied. Training orchestration is managed via HuggingFace Accelerate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FabriVLA was evaluated on the Meta-World MT50 benchmark, which consists of 50 distinct robotic manipulation tasks categorized into four difficulty tiers: easy, medium, hard, and very hard.

#### 4.1. Meta-World MT50 Performance

FabriVLA demonstrated the following success rates:
- **Tier-average success rate**: 90.0%
- **Overall episode-level success rate**: 92.0%
- **Per-tier success rates**:
 - Easy: 95.0%
 - Medium: 88.2%
 - Hard: 86.7%
 - Very Hard: 90.0%

#### 4.2. Comparison with Recent VLA Models

When compared against recent VLA models on Meta-World MT50, FabriVLA, with 0.89 billion parameters and no robotic data pretraining, achieved the highest tier-average success rate among the evaluated methods.
- FabriVLA's 90.0% tier-average surpassed LA4VLA (87.5%) and Evo-Depth (84.4%).
- It demonstrated the strongest performance on the easy tier (95.0%) and was the second strongest on the medium (88.2%), hard (86.7%), and very hard (90.0%) tiers, with LA4VLA reporting higher scores in medium and very hard tiers.
- FabriVLA achieved these results with a relatively compact model size compared to some larger VLA architectures that were also evaluated.

#### 4.3. Task Demand Results

An analysis of FabriVLA's performance across different task groupings provided insights into its strengths and weaknesses:
- **Strengths**: FabriVLA performed strongly on tasks involving planar precision and sliding (95.7%), articulated/contact actuation (95.7%), and insertion/extraction/assembly (93.3%). It achieved 94.0% on "Precision contact actuation" and 92.0% on "Tight insertion & assembly."
- **Weaknesses**: The model exhibited lower success rates on tasks involving tool-mediated manipulation (83.3%), coarse reaching and transport (85.0%), and grasp and place (86.0%). This suggests that tasks requiring broad transport capabilities or precise, timed tool usage presented greater challenges compared to local spatial alignment tasks.

#### 4.4. Training Dynamics

The study of training dynamics revealed that FabriVLA's performance improved substantially as single-stage joint optimization progressed. The 93,000-step checkpoint yielded the best tier-average and overall episode-level scores. Performance showed a mild degradation after this peak, specifically at the 100,000-step mark, indicating that the optimal checkpoint for evaluation was identified prior to the completion of the full training schedule.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study: Shallow VLM Layer Fusion

An ablation study on shallow VLM layer fusion demonstrated its positive contribution to performance.
- The inclusion of layer 6 fusion improved the deep-only baseline (using only layer 14 features) across all MT50 tiers.
- This fusion mechanism increased the tier-average success rate by 7.1 percentage points (from 82.9% to 90.0%).
- The overall episode-level success rate also improved by 5.2 percentage points (from 86.8% to 92.0%).
- These results indicate that the policy successfully leverages the additional shallow features for enhanced spatial precision, which is particularly relevant for manipulation tasks.

#### 4.6. Ablation Study: Action Head Modules

An ablation study investigating the individual contributions of three optional action head modules (gated self-attention (SA), token-residual head (TR), and depthwise temporal convolution (TC)) confirmed the significance of gated self-attention.
- **Gated Self-Attention (SA)**: Adding gated self-attention to the base cross-attention-only head resulted in the most substantial performance gain. It increased the tier-average success rate from 48.5% to 57.7% and the overall episode-level success rate from 55.4% to 66.9%. This supports its role in effectively modeling inter-step dependencies across the action horizon.
- **Other Modules**: The token-residual head (TR) and depthwise temporal convolution (TC) did not further improve performance in this ablation setting (which used a frozen VLM and lacked DeepSpeed FP32 masters for efficiency). In fact, adding TR led to a decrease in metrics (51.2% tier-average, 57.6% overall), and TC (51.4% tier-average, 59.4% overall) did not surpass the performance achieved by gated self-attention alone. Consequently, only gated self-attention was enabled in the final FabriVLA model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FabriVLA represents a contribution to the field of generalist robot manipulation by demonstrating that a lightweight Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model can achieve high-performance multi-task capabilities.
The work's significance lies in several aspects:

- **Bridging Efficiency and Performance**: FabriVLA successfully addresses the trade-off between model size and performance in VLA systems. By achieving a 90.0% tier-average success rate on Meta-World MT50 with approximately 0.89 billion parameters and without reliance on multi-billion parameter backbones or extensive robotic data pretraining, it illustrates that competitive capabilities are attainable within a more compact and computationally efficient framework. This finding challenges the presumption that only extremely large models can deliver robust generalist manipulation.

- **Architectural Innovations for Enhanced Control**: The introduction of gated self-attention within the flow-matching action head provides a mechanism for gradually learning inter-step dependencies in action sequences. This design choice contributes to a stable optimization process and allows for a nuanced capture of temporal action structures, which is critical for complex manipulation. Furthermore, the shallow VLM layer fusion technique effectively combines semantic context with spatial details, enhancing the model's ability for precise object localization and fine-grained interaction. These architectural elements contribute to the model's overall performance.

- **Practical Implications for Robotic Deployment**: The reduced computational requirements and potentially lower inference latency associated with a lightweight model like FabriVLA enhance its suitability for real-time robotic control and deployment in diverse practical environments, including those with limited computational resources. This could broaden the accessibility and applicability of VLA models in various robotic domains beyond research laboratories.

- **Advancing VLA Research Directions**: FabriVLA's strong performance on a widely recognized benchmark encourages further research into efficient VLA architectures and optimized training strategies. It provides a baseline for future work focused on balancing model complexity with robotic capabilities, potentially accelerating the development of more practical and widely deployable generalist robotic systems.
