# Mirror Learning: A Unifying Framework of Policy Optimisation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Oxford introduce Mirror Learning, a new theoretical framework that unifies diverse policy optimization algorithms, including deep reinforcement learning methods like PPO. The framework provides formal guarantees for monotonic policy improvement and convergence to optimal policies, effectively bridging the gap between theoretical understanding and the empirical success of modern RL algorithms.

## Problem

- A significant gap exists between the empirical success of deep reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, particularly PPO, and the lack of robust theoretical guarantees for their practical implementations.
- Existing theoretical frameworks like Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI) and Trust-Region Learning (TRL) are too restrictive or computationally intractable for scalable deep RL applications.
- Widely used algorithms like PPO, despite their effectiveness, were often justified by 'approximate analogies' to TRL, with recent analyses revealing fundamental violations of trust-region constraints.

## Method

- Mirror Learning defines policy optimization through three core components: a flexible =='drift functional'== (quantifying policy update cost), a =='neighborhood operator'== (constraining update size), and a =='mirror operator'== (penalizing changes based on drift).
- It establishes a general update rule, formulated as ==maximizing a mirror operator within the defined neighborhood==, which inherently guarantees monotonic policy improvement and convergence.
- The framework demonstrates that existing algorithms like GPI, TRL, TRPO, ==PPO==, and MDPO can be rigorously instantiated as specific cases of Mirror Learning by defining their respective drift functionals and neighborhood operators.

## Results

- The 'Fundamental Theorem of Mirror Learning' proves that any algorithm instantiated within the framework guarantees strict monotonic improvement in expected return and convergence to optimal policies.
- The framework formally unifies and provides theoretical guarantees for state-of-the-art algorithms including TRPO, PPO, PPO-KL (with fixed
τ), and MDPO, for the first time in their practical formulations.
- Numerical experiments on simple environments confirm that Mirror Learning instances consistently exhibit monotonic improvement in return and convergence to optimal returns, with cumulative drift remaining bounded as theoretically predicted.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The clipping objective in PPO is mathematically equivalent to maximizing an objective with an intrinsic 'drift functional' and a trivial neighborhood, thereby providing direct theoretical justification for its success.
> - Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI) and Trust-Region Learning (TRL) are revealed as pathologically restrictive corner cases within the broader Mirror Learning framework.
> - Policy optimization can be interpreted as a search on a directed acyclic graph (Policy DAG), where the total 'weight' (sum of drifts) along any path from an initial to an optimal policy is bounded by the optimality gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has seen immense empirical success, particularly with the advent of deep learning. However, this success has often outpaced theoretical understanding. Historically, two foundational theoretical frameworks guided the design of RL algorithms:
1. **Generalised Policy Iteration (GPI):** This framework (Sutton & Barto, 2018) involves iteratively evaluating a policy and then improving it. It guarantees monotonic improvement in value and convergence to optimal policies for small, discrete Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). However, applying GPI with function approximation and sample-based learning in large state spaces has proven unstable (Mnih et al., 2015), necessitating heuristics without strong theoretical backing.
2. **Trust-Region Learning (TRL):** Introduced by Schulman et al. (2015), TRL aims to improve stability by optimizing a surrogate objective that restricts policy update sizes, ensuring monotonic improvement. It uses distance measures like KL-divergence to define a "trust region." While theoretically sound, exact TRL is often intractable for large MDPs due to the computational cost of these distance measures.

The current landscape is dominated by algorithms like **Trust-Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)** (Schulman et al., 2015) and **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** (Schulman et al., 2017). These are highly scalable and have achieved state-of-the-art performance across diverse tasks, from robotics to game-playing (Berner et al., 2019). Crucially, TRPO and PPO were motivated as *heuristic approximations* of TRL. Their empirical success was often justified by "proof by analogy" – that they roughly behave like TRL.

However, recent studies (Wang et al., 2020; Engstrom et al., 2020) have revealed a significant **chasm between theory and practice**: PPO, despite its success, often fundamentally violates the trust-region constraints it was supposed to approximate. This exposes a concerning lack of theoretical understanding for one of the most widely used RL algorithms.

"Mirror Learning" directly addresses this chasm. Instead of trying to force successful algorithms into existing, restrictive frameworks, it proposes a *new, more general theoretical framework* that naturally encompasses and provides guarantees for these modern, scalable methods in their practical forms. It aims to unify the design space of theoretically sound policy optimization algorithms, vastly expanding beyond the traditional GPI and TRL paradigms. This work positions itself as a fundamental re-evaluation of policy optimization theory, aiming to provide a robust foundation for current and future RL algorithm development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind introducing the Mirror Learning framework are:

- **Reconcile Theory and Practice in Deep RL:** The most pressing motivation is to bridge the significant gap between the empirical success of state-of-the-art deep RL algorithms, particularly PPO, and the lack of robust theoretical guarantees for their practical implementations. Existing theoretical frameworks (GPI, TRL) have proven too restrictive or intractable for large-scale applications, leading to algorithms whose success is attributed to "approximate analogies" rather than sound theory.
- **Provide Theoretical Guarantees for Scalable Algorithms:** The authors aim to introduce a novel theoretical framework that can provide rigorous monotonic improvement and optimal-policy convergence guarantees to a broad class of algorithms, including TRPO and PPO, in their *current, practical formulations*. This contrasts with previous attempts that tried to "shoe-horn" these algorithms into existing, narrower frameworks.
- **Unify Diverse Policy Optimization Approaches:** Mirror Learning seeks to be a unifying framework, demonstrating that GPI and TRL are merely "pathologically restrictive corner cases" within its broader scope. This unification reveals deeper connections and principles underlying various successful RL methods.
- **Empower the Design of New, Theoretically Sound Algorithms:** By establishing a general framework with inherent theoretical guarantees, Mirror Learning aims to "set us free to boldly explore novel, theoretically sound RL algorithms, a thus far uncharted wonderland." It provides a template for RL algorithm designers, allowing them to construct new methods with built-in convergence properties.
- **Explain Unexplained Empirical Phenomena:** The framework is also designed to offer explanations for previously puzzling observations regarding the performance and design choices of other algorithms, such as the observed failure of certain PPO-KL variants compared to others.

In essence, the motivation is to move beyond heuristic approximations and provide a principled, robust theoretical foundation for the field of policy optimization in reinforcement learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Mirror Learning framework is built upon three core conceptual components: the **drift functional**, the **neighborhood operator**, and the **mirror operator**, which together define an **update rule** that guarantees desirable theoretical properties.

- **Preliminaries:** The paper first establishes the standard Markov Decision Process (MDP) formulation, defining key concepts like state-action value function ($Q^\pi$), state value function ($V^\pi$), advantage function ($A^\pi$), and expected return ($\eta(\pi)$). It then briefly reviews GPI and TRL, highlighting their strengths (monotonic improvement, convergence) and weaknesses (scalability, exactness issues), setting the stage for Mirror Learning.

- **Drift Functional (D):** This is a central innovation. A drift functional $D_{\pi}(\bar{\pi}|s)$ quantifies the "cost" or "distance" of updating a policy $\pi$ to a new policy $\bar{\pi}$ at a given state $s$. It must satisfy two key conditions:
 1. **Nonnegativity:** $D_{\pi}(\bar{\pi}|s) \geq D_{\pi}(\pi|s) = 0$. The cost is non-negative and zero if the policy doesn't change.
 2. **Zero Gradient:** Its Gâteaux derivatives (a generalization of gradients for functional spaces) with respect to $\bar{\pi}$ must be zero when evaluated at $\bar{\pi} = \pi$. This ensures that at the current policy, small changes do not incur an immediate "drift" penalty.
 The total drift $D^\nu_\pi(\bar{\pi})$ is an expectation of the state-conditional drift over a state distribution $\nu_{\bar{\pi}}^\pi(s)$. Importantly, unlike Bregman distances used in mirror descent, the drift functional is not required to be convex or everywhere differentiable, offering greater flexibility.

- **Neighborhood Operator (N):** This operator $N(\pi)$ defines a compact set of permissible new policies $\bar{\pi}$ that are "close" to the current policy $\pi$. It must be a continuous map, return a compact set, and contain a closed ball around $\pi$ under some metric $\chi$. This concept allows for explicit update-size constraints, reflecting risk aversion or algorithmic design choices (e.g., learning rates). The trivial neighborhood operator is $N = \Pi$ (the entire policy space).

- **Mirror Operator (M):** Combining the value function and the drift, the mirror operator is defined as:
 $M^{\bar{\pi}}_D V^{\pi_{old}}(s) = E_{a \sim \pi_{new}}[Q^{\pi_{old}}(s, a)] - D^{\pi_{old}}(\pi_{new}|s)$.
 This operator effectively transforms the value function into a functional of the new policy $\bar{\pi}$, penalizing large changes as defined by the drift.

- **Mirror Learning Update Rule:** The core of the framework is defined by the update rule:
 $\pi_{new} = \arg \max_{\bar{\pi} \in N(\pi_{old})} E_{s \sim \beta_\pi}[M^{\bar{\pi}}_D V^{\pi_{old}}(s)]$.
 Here, $\beta_\pi$ is a sampling distribution, allowing for both on-policy and off-policy learning. This objective is designed to be optimizable by Monte-Carlo estimation and gradient ascent for parameterized policies.

- **Theoretical Guarantees (The Fundamental Theorem of Mirror Learning):** The paper provides rigorous proofs (detailed in the appendix) for the following properties under general conditions:
 - **Monotonic Improvement (Lemma 3.3, 3.5):** If the mirror operator of the new policy is greater than or equal to that of the old policy for all states, then the new policy's value function and expected return are monotonically improved. The update rule in Definition 3.4 is explicitly shown to satisfy this condition.
 - **Convergence to Optimal Policies (Theorem 3.6):** A sequence of policies generated by Mirror Learning:
 1. Attains strict monotonic improvement in expected return.
 2. Its value functions converge to the optimal one ($V^*$).
 3. Its expected returns converge to the optimal return ($\eta^*$).
 4. Its $\omega$-limit set (the set of accumulation points of the sequence) consists of optimal policies.
 The proof strategy involves showing the convergence of value functions, characterizing policy limit points as fixed points of the mirror learning update, and then demonstrating that these fixed points are also fixed points of GPI, hence optimal.

- **Unification and Explanation:** A crucial part of the methodology is demonstrating how existing, successful RL algorithms fit within this framework by appropriately defining their drift functional, neighborhood operator, and sampling distribution:
 - **GPI:** Trivial drift and trivial neighborhood.
 - **TRL:** Scaled KL-divergence as drift, trivial neighborhood, specific state distribution.
 - **TRPO:** Trivial drift, with the average-KL ball as the neighborhood operator.
 - **PPO:** The clipping objective $L_{clip}$ is mathematically transformed to reveal an inherent drift functional (a ReLU-based term) and a trivial neighborhood operator. This is a key insight.
 - **PPO-KL:** Variants with fixed $\tau$ are shown to be instances of Mirror Learning, explaining their empirical success.
 - **MDPO:** Also fits by using KL-divergence with a specific scaling factor as drift.

- **Graph-Theoretical Interpretation:** Mirror Learning is shown to cast policy optimization as a search on a directed acyclic graph (Policy DAG) where policies are vertices and edges represent improvements. The total path-weight (sum of drifts) is bounded by the optimality gap, offering a novel perspective and a way to constrain cumulative "costs" during training.

- **Numerical Experiments:** Proof-of-concept experiments on simple environments (Single-step Game, Tabular Game, GridWorld) are conducted. These are not for SOTA performance but to verify that algorithms instantiated within Mirror Learning (using various drift functionals and neighborhood operators) indeed exhibit monotonic improvement and convergence to optimal returns, and that the total drift remains bounded, validating Theorem 3.6 and Inequality (9).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper's main findings and results are primarily theoretical, establishing Mirror Learning as a powerful and unifying framework for policy optimization:

- **Formal Unification of Modern RL Algorithms:** Mirror Learning successfully unifies a diverse set of prominent reinforcement learning algorithms under a single theoretical umbrella. This includes foundational methods like Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI) and Trust-Region Learning (TRL), as well as scalable, state-of-the-art algorithms such as Trust-Region Policy Optimization (TRPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), PPO-KL (with fixed $\tau$), and Mirror Descent Policy Optimization (MDPO). For PPO, the authors rigorously derive its clipping objective as being equivalent to maximizing an objective with an intrinsic "drift functional" and a trivial neighborhood operator, thereby providing a direct theoretical justification for its success.

- **The Fundamental Theorem of Mirror Learning (Theorem 3.6):** This is the core theoretical result, proving that any algorithm instantiated within the Mirror Learning framework (i.e., defined by a valid drift functional, neighborhood operator, and sampling distribution) possesses the following critical properties:
 1. **Strict Monotonic Improvement:** The expected return of the policy monotonically improves at each step.
 2. **Convergence of Value Functions:** The sequence of learned value functions converges to the optimal value function ($V^*$).
 3. **Convergence of Expected Returns:** The sequence of expected returns converges to the optimal expected return ($\eta^*$).
 4. **Optimality of Limit Policies:** The $\omega$-limit set of the learned policies consists exclusively of optimal policies.
 These guarantees resolve the long-standing issue of theoretical soundness for algorithms like PPO, whose empirical success was previously explained only by heuristic approximations.

- **Explanation of Empirical Phenomena:** The framework provides clear theoretical explanations for previously puzzling empirical observations. For instance, it clarifies why PPO-KL with a fixed $\tau$ (which allows the KL-penalty to be a continuous drift) is empirically successful, while versions with a dynamically adjusted $\tau$ (which cause discontinuities and violate drift conditions) tend to fail.

- **Graph-Theoretical Interpretation of Policy Optimization:** A novel interpretation is introduced, viewing policy optimization as a search problem on a directed acyclic graph (Policy DAG). The total "weight" (sum of drifts) along any optimization path from an initial policy to an optimal policy is shown to be upper-bounded by the optimality gap divided by a factor related to the sampling distribution. This provides a new conceptual tool for analyzing policy paths and potentially for designing algorithms that control cumulative costs.

- **Experimental Validation on Toy Environments:** Numerical experiments on simple discrete environments (Single-step Game, Tabular Game, GridWorld) confirm the theoretical predictions. These experiments demonstrate that Mirror Learning instances, utilizing various drift functionals (e.g., KL-divergence, L2 distance, total variation distance, trivial drift) and neighborhood operators (e.g., expected drift ball, KL ball), consistently exhibit monotonic improvement in return and convergence to optimal returns. Furthermore, the experiments visually confirm that the cumulative "total drift" remains bounded, as predicted by the graph-theoretical interpretation.

In summary, the paper successfully proposes a new foundational theory that not only validates existing deep RL successes but also provides a principled blueprint for future algorithm development, moving the field towards a more unified and theoretically rigorous understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Mirror Learning carries profound significance and potential impact for the field of reinforcement learning:

- **Restoring Theoretical Rigor to Deep RL:** The most immediate and significant impact is the resolution of the "theory-practice chasm" for widely used and empirically successful algorithms like PPO. By formally proving that PPO is a rigorous instance of Mirror Learning and thus enjoys monotonic improvement and convergence guarantees, the framework provides a strong theoretical foundation that was previously lacking. This restores confidence in these algorithms and shifts their justification from "approximate analogies" to direct theoretical properties.

- **Guidance for Future Algorithm Design:** Mirror Learning offers a **powerful template** for designing new RL algorithms. Researchers can now systematically explore novel combinations of drift functionals, neighborhood operators, and sampling distributions, knowing that any valid instantiation within the framework will inherit strong theoretical guarantees. This opens up an "uncharted wonderland" of theoretically sound algorithms, potentially accelerating innovation by providing a principled design methodology. For example, the paper suggests defining drifts based on "risk" or "memory expense," allowing for algorithms tailored to specific practical constraints while maintaining guarantees.

- **Deeper Understanding of Existing Algorithms:** The framework provides a fresh lens through which to understand the mechanics and success of existing algorithms. The re-interpretation of PPO's clipping objective as an intrinsic drift is a key example, offering insights into why certain design choices (like fixed vs. dynamic $\tau$ in PPO-KL) lead to different outcomes. This deeper understanding can inform refinements and improvements to current methods.

- **Bridging RL and Other Fields:** The novel graph-theoretical interpretation connects policy optimization to graph search problems, offering a new perspective and potential avenues for cross-disciplinary research. The concept of bounded total path-weight, related to the optimality gap, could inspire new analysis tools and optimization strategies from graph theory.

- **Increased Reproducibility and Robustness:** By grounding algorithms in robust theory, Mirror Learning can contribute to more reproducible and robust RL research. Algorithms developed within a theoretically sound framework are less likely to rely on brittle heuristics or extensive hyperparameter tuning for their performance, potentially reducing the "implementation matters" problem often observed in deep RL.

- **Foundation for Extensions:** The generality of Mirror Learning suggests its potential for extension to more complex RL settings, such as multi-agent reinforcement learning, constrained RL, or risk-sensitive RL, where defining appropriate "drift" or "cost" functions could be particularly valuable. The authors' background in multi-agent RL (Kuba et al., 2021) hints at this future potential.

In conclusion, Mirror Learning represents a fundamental advancement in the theoretical understanding of policy optimization. It not only validates the empirical successes of modern deep RL but also provides a principled, unifying framework that promises to guide the systematic development of new, theoretically sound, and robust reinforcement learning algorithms for years to come.
