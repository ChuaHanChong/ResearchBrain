# DARO: Difficulty-Aware Reweighting Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> DARO introduces an adaptive reweighting algorithm for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) that dynamically adjusts loss contributions based on sample difficulty, achieving state-of-the-art performance and faster convergence in large language model mathematical reasoning tasks. It resolves the "loss scale issue" found in previous GRPO-based methods by employing learnable weights to balance training signals across different problem difficulties.

## Problem

- Existing Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) algorithms, particularly GRPO-based methods, rely on static or overly simplistic weighting schemes for training samples.
- This leads to a "loss scale issue" where the magnitude of the loss varies dramatically across different problem difficulty levels, causing unbalanced training.
- Such imbalances hinder efficient learning, slow convergence, and lead to suboptimal performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) in complex reasoning tasks.

## Method

- ==DARO== provides a unified mathematical framework for GRPO-based RLVR algorithms, revealing their underlying static weighting mechanisms.
- It introduces ==dynamic, learnable weights== for different difficulty groups (defined by empirical pass rates), which are optimized jointly with the LLM's policy parameters.
- The loss function is designed with a ==regularization term== to ensure weights are inversely proportional to their group's loss, thereby balancing training signals across difficulty levels.

## Results

- DARO consistently achieves the highest average accuracy across **three** base LLMs and **six** diverse math reasoning benchmarks, with peak performance of **50.8%** for Qwen2.5-Math-7B.
- The algorithm demonstrates significantly faster convergence rates and reaches higher final performance levels that are often unattainable by other static-weighting methods.
- Ablation studies confirm the crucial role of DARO's dynamic weights, showing substantial performance gains (e.g., **+2.7** over GRPO baseline for Llama3.1-8B) compared to static or simplistic weighting strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prior GRPO-based RLVR methods (GRPO, DAPO, LIPO, Dr. GRPO) can be unified and mathematically shown to employ static or predefined weighting schemes tied to sample difficulty.
> - The "loss scale issue" formally demonstrates that loss magnitude in RLVR is inherently imbalanced across different empirical pass rates (difficulty levels), impeding balanced learning.
> - Dynamically adapting loss weights based on the inverse of their group's loss magnitude effectively mitigates this imbalance, promoting more stable and efficient training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "DARO: Difficulty-Aware Reweighting Policy Optimization" is situated within the rapidly evolving field of enhancing Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically focusing on their reasoning capabilities. While LLMs excel at many natural language tasks, improving their ability to perform complex, multi-step reasoning, such as in mathematics, remains a significant challenge and a critical frontier in AI research.

The leading paradigm for this enhancement is Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). This approach allows LLMs to learn from binary feedback on generated solutions, enabling them to explore vast solution spaces and refine their reasoning without relying solely on static, human-annotated datasets. RLVR has gained prominence through its success in models like DeepSeek-R1 and the "o-series" by OpenAI.

Within RLVR, algorithms derived from Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and, more specifically, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), have become "de facto" standards. GRPO is particularly influential because it estimates advantages directly from reward scores of multiple sampled solutions, bypassing the need for a separate value model. This has led to several notable variants, including DAPO, Dr. GRPO, and Lite PPO (LIPO), all of which have achieved significant improvements in mathematical benchmarks.

DARO builds directly upon this lineage of GRPO-based RLVR algorithms. The authors identify a fundamental, previously unaddressed limitation inherent in these methods: the "loss scale issue." Their work provides a unified mathematical framework that reveals existing variants as essentially weighted variations of GRPO, employing static or overly simplistic weighting schemes tied to sample difficulty. This static weighting fails to adapt to the LLM's evolving capabilities, leading to disproportionate training focus on certain difficulty levels at the expense of others.

By introducing a dynamic, difficulty-aware reweighting mechanism, DARO directly addresses this core limitation. It pushes the boundaries of RLVR by moving beyond fixed heuristics to a more intelligent, adaptive learning strategy. This represents a significant advancement in policy optimization for LLM reasoning, potentially leading to more robust, efficient, and higher-performing models. The work not only refines existing methodologies but also offers a novel perspective on managing sample difficulty in reinforcement learning for complex cognitive tasks. Its contribution is crucial for enabling LLMs to learn more effectively from their own generated solutions, thereby advancing the state of the art in reasoning capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an adaptive algorithm, Difficulty-Aware Reweighting Policy Optimization (DARO), that dynamically modulates the loss contribution of each difficulty group during Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) training. This adaptation aims to resolve a critical limitation—termed the "loss scale issue"—observed in current state-of-the-art RLVR algorithms, ultimately leading to more stable, efficient, and higher-performing LLMs in complex reasoning tasks.

The motivation for DARO stems from a rigorous mathematical analysis of existing GRPO-based RLVR algorithms, including GRPO, DAPO, Dr. GRPO, and LIPO. The authors identify several key shortcomings:

1. **Static/Simplistic Weighting Schemes:** Existing methods inherently rely on weighting schemes that are either static or overly simplistic. These weights are typically tied to the "empirical pass rate" (a proxy for sample difficulty) and do not evolve with the model's learning progress or adapt to different model types.
2. **The "Loss Scale Issue":** The static nature of these weighting schemes leads to a significant imbalance in the training objective. The magnitude of the loss varies dramatically across different difficulty levels (empirical pass rates), causing the training process to disproportionately focus on certain difficulty levels while neglecting others. This creates an unstable learning environment where the model might "rote-learn" existing knowledge or become overly fixated on extremely difficult problems without proper balance.
3. **Hindered Performance and Efficiency:** This imbalance disrupts the crucial balance between exploration and exploitation during training. It impedes both the efficiency of the learning process (slower convergence) and the final effectiveness (suboptimal ultimate performance) of the LLM on reasoning benchmarks. Specifically, methods that prioritize medium-difficulty samples risk "catastrophic forgetting" of simpler tasks, while those that don't adapt miss opportunities to efficiently learn from hard examples as the model improves.
4. **Lack of Adaptability:** Current methods lack the ability to adapt their focus based on the model's current capabilities, which inherently change throughout training. A "one-size-fits-all" weighting strategy is suboptimal for a dynamic learning process.

In light of these motivations, DARO's core objective is to introduce a learnable, dynamic weighting mechanism that intelligently recalibrates difficulty weights. This aims to ensure that the model can both consolidate existing knowledge on easier tasks and efficiently explore and master new, challenging problems, thereby fostering a more balanced, stable, and ultimately superior learning trajectory for LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of DARO unfolds in three main stages: a theoretical unification and analysis of existing RLVR methods, the identification and formalization of the "loss scale issue," and finally, the proposal and implementation of the Difficulty-Aware Reweighting Policy Optimization (DARO) algorithm.

#### 3.1. Unified Loss Formulation and Analysis of Existing Methods

The authors begin by providing a unified loss formulation that encompasses several prominent GRPO-based RLVR algorithms. They simplify the original GRPO loss function for binary rewards, noting that the KL divergence term is often non-essential and adopting a generalized token-mean loss aggregation. This leads to a base loss `L_BASE` where a variable weight `w_i` is introduced for each response `o_i`.

Through this unified lens, they analyze GRPO, DAPO, LIPO, and Dr. GRPO:
- **GRPO:** Implicitly uses a constant weight `w_i = 1`.
- **DAPO:** Uses a binary weight `w_i = I(0 < μ < 1)`, meaning it considers all samples with an empirical pass rate `μ` between 0 and 1 identically, ignoring samples with `μ=0` or `μ=1`.
- **LIPO and Dr. GRPO:** Their analysis shows that these methods' effective weights are proportional to `sqrt(μ(1 - μ))`. This function peaks at `μ = 0.5` and approaches zero as `μ` nears 0 or 1, indicating a prioritization of medium-difficulty samples and a downweighting of both very easy and extremely difficult ones.

This unification reveals that all these methods, despite their variations, rely on static or predefined weighting schemes that do not adapt to the model's learning state.

#### 3.2. Identification and Formalization of the Loss Scale Issue

To understand the impact of these static weights, the authors reframe the RL process as a multitask learning problem. Each empirical pass rate `μ = k/K` (where `k` is the number of correct responses out of `K` samples for a given prompt) is treated as a distinct "task" or difficulty group (`G_μ`).

They then approximate the base loss for each group `L_BASE(G_μ)`. Using Hoeffding's inequality, they show that `L_BASE(G_μ)` is approximately proportional to `(P_pos(|o|) - P_neg(|o|)) / (L * sqrt(μ(1 - μ)))`. This theoretical finding implies that the magnitude of the loss is strongly linked to the sample's empirical pass rate `μ`. Furthermore, they empirically confirm that response length is correlated with difficulty, further accentuating the loss magnitude disparities.

Empirical experiments across different base models (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Llama3.1-8B) track the mean loss for sub-batches with varying `μ`. The results clearly validate the "loss scale issue": the loss magnitude varies significantly across difficulty levels and throughout training, with certain `μ` values consistently yielding much higher losses than others. This forces the model to disproportionately focus on specific difficulty levels, hindering balanced learning.

#### 3.3. Difficulty-Aware Reweighting Policy Optimization (DARO)

Motivated by these findings, DARO is designed to dynamically adapt loss contributions.
- **Adaptive Weights:** DARO treats the weights `w_μ` for each difficulty group `G_μ` (where `μ = k/K` and `k` is not 0 or `K`, as these samples have zero advantage) as *learnable parameters*.
- **Optimization Principle:** The goal is to optimize these weights such that they become inversely proportional to the original loss of their respective groups (`w_μ ∝ L_μ^-1`). This ensures that the weighted loss `w_μ L_μ` is approximately equal across all difficulty groups, balancing the training signal.
- **Regularization:** To achieve this inverse proportionality during optimization and prevent weights from collapsing to trivial solutions (e.g., zero), a regularization term `N(w_μ)` is introduced. By setting the derivative of the regularizer `N'(w_μ)` to `-C/w_μ` (where `C` is a constant, set to 1 for simplicity), the integration yields `N(w_μ) = -C ln w_μ`.
- **Final Loss Function:** The overall loss for DARO is formulated as `L = Sum_{μ≠0,1} (w_μ L_μ - ln w_μ)`.
- **Joint Optimization:** The learnable weights `w_μ` are optimized jointly with the LLM's policy parameters `θ` during training using standard gradient-based optimization (AdamW).
- **Implementation Details:** Consistent with DAPO, samples with empirical pass rates of 0 or 1 are excluded from the loss calculation as they provide no policy gradient signal. DARO also utilizes token-mean loss aggregation and a clip-higher strategy, consistent with effective practices in RLVR.

This adaptive weighting mechanism allows DARO to intelligently recalibrate its focus as the model learns, mitigating the loss scale issue and promoting a more stable and effective learning trajectory.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on three base models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B) across six diverse math reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, AMC23, AIME24, AIME25, Olympiad, Minerva, GSM8K) demonstrate the significant superiority of DARO over four leading baselines: GRPO, LIPO, Dr. GRPO, and DAPO.

#### 4.1. Superior Overall Performance

- **Consistent Outperformance:** DARO consistently achieves the highest average accuracy across all three base models: 21.4% for Llama-3.1-8B, 40.6% for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, and 50.8% for Qwen2.5-Math-7B. This robust performance underscores the effectiveness of its difficulty-aware reweighting strategy.
- **Benchmark Dominance:** On individual benchmarks, DARO secures the top score in the majority of test cases. For instance, it outperforms other baselines on 5 out of 7 benchmarks for Llama-3.1-8B. Even when not the absolute best, its performance remains highly competitive, often being the second-best result, indicating strong overall generalization.

#### 4.2. Faster Convergence and Higher Final Performance

- **Enhanced Training Efficiency:** The training dynamics show that DARO achieves significantly faster convergence rates compared to all baselines. For Llama3.1-8B, DARO reached a 20% pass rate in roughly half the training steps required by DAPO.
- **Unattainable Performance Levels:** DARO converges to superior final performance levels that are often unattainable by other algorithms, including those with static weighting strategies like LIPO and Dr. GRPO. This highlights the critical advantage of dynamically adapting loss weights to the model's evolving state.
- **Sustained Performance Gains:** Validation accuracy reported at steps 100, 200, and 300 further corroborates that DARO not only starts strong but continues to improve and maintain its lead throughout the training process across various difficulty levels and model sizes.

#### 4.3. Validation of Dynamic Weights Through Ablation Study

- **Crucial Role of Dynamic Weights:** An ablation study explicitly confirms that the "Dynamic Weights" strategy is the core innovation driving DARO's success. When this component is incorporated, the full DARO model consistently and substantially outperforms all other variants.
- **Significant Gains:** DARO achieves performance gains of +1.4, +1.0, and +2.7 over the GRPO baseline on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, and Llama3.1-8B, respectively.
- **Limitations of Static/Simplistic Weighting:** The study also reveals the shortcomings of alternative approaches:
 - **Dynamic Sampling (DAPO):** While a step forward, merely incorporating dynamic sampling results in modest and inconsistent gains, indicating its insufficiency on its own.
 - **Predefined Static Weights (Dr. GRPO, LIPO):** Methods employing fixed weighting schemes (e.g., proportional to `sqrt(μ(1 - μ))`) often fail to generalize effectively and can even be detrimental, with Dr. GRPO lagging behind the baseline on all models and LIPO showing a notable performance drop on Llama3.1-8B. This underscores the suboptimality of fixed heuristics that cannot account for model-specific differences or dynamic learning progression.

#### 4.4. Training Dynamics Insights

- **Higher Policy Entropy:** DARO maintains a consistently higher policy entropy throughout training compared to baselines. This indicates that the adaptive weighting mechanism successfully mitigates premature convergence, fostering more robust and continuous exploration of the solution space.
- **Increasing Response Lengths:** The length of generated responses steadily increases with DARO. This suggests that the model not only improves in accuracy but also develops more complex, multi-step thought processes necessary for advanced reasoning.
- **Average Pass Rate:** DARO consistently achieves the highest average pass rate during training, demonstrating its overall efficiency and effectiveness in mastering mathematical reasoning tasks.

In summary, the main findings unequivocally establish DARO as a state-of-the-art method for enhancing LLM mathematical reasoning, primarily due to its novel and effective dynamic, difficulty-aware reweighting mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Difficulty-Aware Reweighting Policy Optimization (DARO) carries significant implications for the field of large language models and reinforcement learning, with broad potential impacts:

1. **Resolution of a Fundamental Flaw in RLVR:** DARO addresses and effectively resolves the "loss scale issue," a critical, inherent limitation identified in prevalent GRPO-based RLVR algorithms. This is a significant theoretical and practical contribution, as it unifies existing methods and exposes a common bottleneck that hindered effective training. By rectifying this, DARO paves the way for more robust and reliable fine-tuning of LLMs.

2. **Significant Advancement in LLM Reasoning Capabilities:** By enabling LLMs to learn more efficiently and effectively from diverse difficulty levels, DARO pushes the state-of-the-art in mathematical reasoning. Superior performance and faster convergence translate directly into more capable and intelligent LLMs, which can tackle increasingly complex problems that require multi-step logical inference.

3. **Enhanced Training Efficiency and Resource Optimization:** The demonstrated faster convergence rates of DARO mean that high-performing reasoning models can be trained with fewer computational resources and in less time. This reduces the barriers to entry for researchers and organizations, making advanced LLM fine-tuning more accessible, sustainable, and democratized.

4. **A Generalizable Learning Principle:** The core idea of dynamically adapting loss weights based on sample difficulty is a fundamental learning principle that extends beyond LLM reasoning. It could potentially be applied to other reinforcement learning tasks, curriculum learning strategies, or even other machine learning paradigms where dealing with imbalanced or dynamically changing sample difficulty is crucial. This could inspire new research directions in adaptive learning algorithms.

5. **Foundation for Future AI Development:** Improved reasoning abilities are a cornerstone for developing more sophisticated AI systems, including truly intelligent agents. By enhancing the ability of LLMs to engage in complex, verifiable reasoning, DARO contributes directly to the long-term goal of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). Future work involving multi-turn interactions, tool use, and more general reasoning domains will benefit from the robust learning foundation established by DARO.

6. **Practical Tool for Researchers:** DARO is designed to be straightforward to implement, offering a readily adoptable improvement for anyone working with RLVR for LLM fine-tuning. This practicality ensures its rapid integration into existing research pipelines and accelerates further innovation in the field.

In essence, DARO not only provides a technically sound solution to a crucial problem but also offers a more intelligent and adaptive framework for training LLMs. Its impact is likely to be seen in the development of more powerful, efficient, and versatile AI models capable of tackling the next generation of complex reasoning challenges.
