# Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Contact-Rich Assembly via Proprioception-Anchored Cross-Modal Pretraining

> [!summary] Summary
> PACE is a proprioception-anchored cross-modal encoder for zero-shot sim-to-real contact-rich assembly. Learnable zero-sum temporal kernels turn wrist-camera and force/torque streams into motion codes supervised to predict proprioceptive state transitions via cycle consistency, plus spatial-alignment, engagement, and cross-modal masked reconstruction losses. A frozen encoder feeds an asymmetric actor-critic policy, reaching 93.3% real success with a 2.7-point sim-to-real drop.

## Problem

- Privileged-state simulation policies deploy through external pose estimators, so millimeter pose errors from lighting or calibration drift break submillimeter-clearance assembly at the perception-control interface.
- Domain randomization leaves residual gaps large relative to assembly tolerances, domain adaptation needs real-robot data, and system identification is costly and task-specific.
- Prior multimodal dynamics pretraining uses shared transformers or temporal differences without grounding them in a modality that is consistent across simulation and hardware.

## Method

- ==Proprioceptive anchoring==: a shared dynamics head predicts the proprioceptive innovation from visual and force motion codes, with a backward branch from negated differences enforcing ==cycle consistency==.
- Per-modality learnable depthwise ==temporal kernels==, mean-subtracted each forward pass to be exactly zero-sum, strip static appearance and force-sensor bias while keeping contact transients.
- Auxiliary supervision: a ==spatial-alignment head== regressing planar direction and yaw error, an ==engagement classifier==, and ==stop-gradient cross-modal masked latent reconstruction== over half the fused dimensions.

## Results

- PACE reaches **93.3%** average real-robot success over four tasks with a **2.7-point** sim-to-real drop, versus **18.9** for pose-based PPO, **18.4** for AugInsert, and **43.7** for no-pretrain RL.
- Dropping the dynamics loss raises the average gap from **2.7** to **25.5 points** while costing only **4.7 points** in simulation; removing the visual stream collapses simulated success to **5-9%**.
- Under GearMesh perturbations PACE holds **90-100%**, while pose-based PPO falls from **90%** to **10%** under a 2-cm camera shift and AugInsert from **90%** to **35%** under a 2-N force bias.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Supervise fragile modalities with a stable one: a signal sharing a coordinate frame across domains can act as a free cross-domain anchor requiring no real data.
> - Predict state innovations rather than absolute states, otherwise the model copies the prior observation and leaves the learned motion codes unconstrained.
> - Enforcing zero-sum temporal filters is a cheap architectural prior that removes static nuisance offsets like sensor bias and lighting before they reach the policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the persistent challenge of enabling robots to perform contact-rich assembly tasks in real-world environments, particularly focusing on the "sim-to-real" gap that often hinders the direct transfer of policies trained in simulation.

**Current Landscape of Robotic Assembly:**
Classical approaches to contact-rich assembly often rely on meticulously engineered compliance, hybrid force-position control, or impedance control. These methods typically necessitate task- and geometry-specific designs. More recently, simulation-based reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a scalable paradigm for acquiring such skills, allowing for training in a controlled environment where privileged information (e.g., ground-truth poses, contact states) is available. However, a significant hurdle in this approach is the discrepancy between simulation and reality, affecting visual observations, contact dynamics, and force/torque (F/T) measurements. This "sim-to-real gap" can lead to policies that perform well in simulation but fail when deployed on physical robots.

**Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap:**
Existing strategies to bridge this gap include:
- **Domain Randomization:** Varying visual and physical parameters during training to encourage policy robustness to environmental variations. While effective, residual discrepancies can still be significant for tasks requiring submillimeter precision.
- **Domain Adaptation:** Aligning feature distributions between simulation and reality, but this typically requires real-robot data for adaptation, which can be costly and time-consuming.
- **System Identification:** Calibrating simulation parameters to match hardware behavior, a process often costly and task-specific.
- **Asymmetric Training:** Training a critic with privileged simulation information while the actor uses only deployable observations, aiming to learn policies that generalize.

**Role of Representation Learning:**
General-purpose visual encoders (e.g., R3M, VIP, VC-1, DINOv2) provide foundational visual features, but they are not specifically designed for the fine-grained spatial and contact reasoning required in submillimeter assembly. Multimodal policies frequently combine visual and force/tactile information, often through feature concatenation or cross-attention mechanisms. Dynamics-aware pretraining, such as DynaMo and AFRO, uses forward and inverse dynamics objectives but primarily focuses on visual or 3D point cloud data. MSDP is a closely related prior work that jointly pretrains visual, F/T, and proprioceptive representations using masked autoencoding and cross-sensor prediction.

**The Niche of this Work:**
This paper positions itself within the context of sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich assembly by introducing a novel approach that leverages the comparative consistency of proprioceptive measurements across simulation and hardware. It diverges from prior multimodal pretraining methods by using proprioceptive state-transition prediction as a direct supervisory signal to filter domain-specific information from visual and F/T representations. This aims to create representations that are invariant to domain shifts without requiring real-robot training data or external perception modules for object pose tracking. The method also introduces specific architectural choices, including separate temporal kernels, structured auxiliary supervision, and cross-modal masked reconstruction, to enhance representation learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to achieve robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich assembly tasks. This goal is motivated by several identified challenges and observations:

1. **Challenges of Contact-Rich Assembly:** Assembly tasks often involve submillimeter clearances, necessitating high spatial accuracy and reliable interpretation of contact forces. Vision alone is frequently insufficient, as small misalignments can lead to jamming or failure, and successful compliant alignment requires fine-grained spatial information combined with accurate force interpretation.

2. **Limitations of Simulation-Based RL for Real-World Deployment:** While simulation offers a scalable training environment, directly deploying policies trained in simulation to real robots is hindered by the sim-to-real gap. This gap manifests in three key areas:
 - **Visual Observation Gap:** Differences between rendered and real camera images (e.g., lighting, texture, appearance).
 - **F/T Sensing Gap:** Discrepancies between simulated and real F/T sensor characteristics (e.g., noise, bias, dynamics).
 - **Physical Interaction Gap:** Differences in contact mechanics and robot-environment interactions between simulation and reality.
 Domain randomization attempts to mitigate the physical interaction gap, but the visual and F/T sensing gaps often remain substantial relative to assembly tolerances.

3. **Brittleness of Decoupled Perception-Control Systems:** A common deployment paradigm involves training a privileged-state policy in simulation and then using external perception modules (e.g., Mask R-CNN, AprilTags) to estimate object poses for the real robot. This decoupled design creates a fragile interface where even millimeter-scale pose errors from perception can cause control failures, from which the policy may not recover. The goal is to move towards pixel-to-action transfer without such external pose estimators.

4. **Observation of Proprioceptive Consistency:** The central motivating observation is that proprioceptive measurements (joint positions and velocities) are significantly more consistent across simulation and hardware than visual or F/T measurements. This consistency arises because proprioception is typically represented in a common joint coordinate system and joint velocities are computed similarly in both domains. This makes proprioception a potentially stable "cross-domain anchor."

**Specific Objectives of PACE:**
Based on these motivations, the research aims to:
- Develop a novel encoder architecture (PACE) that uses proprioceptive state transitions as a supervisory signal to guide the learning of visual and F/T representations.
- Enable the encoder to suppress static, domain-specific nuisance factors (e.g., lighting, texture, sensor bias) from visual and F/T data, while preserving task-relevant motion cues.
- Achieve robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich assembly tasks, meaning policies trained on the encoder's features in simulation can be directly deployed on hardware without any real-world fine-tuning or adaptation.
- Demonstrate high real-world success rates and minimal sim-to-real performance degradation across a variety of challenging assembly scenarios.
- Evaluate the robustness of the proposed method against various real-world perturbations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PACE (Proprioception-Anchored Cross-Modal Encoder) pipeline involves two main stages: pretraining a multimodal encoder and then training an RL policy on the frozen features for zero-shot deployment.

**Problem Formulation:**
The framework considers quasi-static contact-rich assembly tasks where a robot, given a grasped object and a target receptacle, must complete the assembly within a submillimeter positional tolerance (0.1–1.0 mm). At each timestep, observations include images from two wrist-mounted cameras (I_L, I_R), a six-axis F/T measurement (F_t), joint positions (q_t), joint velocities (˙q_t), and the previous action (a_t-1). The action (a_t) is a 6-dimensional end-effector pose increment (∆x, ∆y, ∆z, ∆θ_roll, ∆θ_pitch, ∆θ_yaw) under impedance control, effectively a 4-DoF action space (∆x, ∆y, ∆z, ∆θ_yaw) as roll and pitch are fixed. Proprioception (p_t) is defined as [q_t; ˙q_t]. The goal is to learn an encoder ϕ that produces a 256-dimensional latent feature vector z_t = [z_v_t; z_f_t] from (I_L_t, I_R_t, F_t, p_t) that is insensitive to domain-specific factors, allowing a policy π(a_t | z_t, p_t, a_t-1) trained in simulation to transfer directly to hardware.

**A. Expert Demonstrations:**
For each assembly task, a privileged-state PPO policy is trained in Isaac Lab, a GPU-accelerated simulation framework, using ground-truth poses, velocities, and contact states. This expert policy is trained with default Factory domain randomization settings until it achieves high success rates. Subsequently, 300 trajectories per task are collected by rolling out this stochastic expert. The collected data includes images from both wrist cameras, a history of wrist F/T measurements, joint positions and velocities, the executed action, and a binary engagement label derived from simulated contact detection. This dataset is then split 80:20 for training and validation.

**B. Encoder Architecture:**
The PACE encoder features modality-specific streams (visual and F/T) that follow a temporal-differencing pipeline.
1. **Per-Frame Encoding:**
 - **Visual Stream:** A frozen DINOv2 vision transformer backbone encodes images from both wrist cameras. Learned attention pooling and cross-view fusion then produce a compact per-frame visual feature (v_t).
 - **Force Stream:** A multilayer perceptron (MLP) encodes a recent window of six-axis F/T measurements into a per-frame force feature (f_t).
2. **Temporal Differencing and Motion Codes:** For each stream, a buffer stores the preceding T features. A learnable, per-dimension depthwise 1-D convolution, initialized to approximate a first-order difference (e.g., [−1, 1, 0...]), is applied to these buffered features to extract a "motion code." A key constraint is enforcing an exactly zero-sum kernel by subtracting the per-channel mean of its weights. This design choice aims to suppress static offsets (e.g., constant appearance components, sensor bias) and capture temporal changes in the signals. This yields a forward motion code (α_v+ for visual, α_f+ for force).
3. **Feature Fusion:**
 - The visual stream output (z_v_t) combines v_t, α_v+ t, and proprioception p_t.
 - The force stream output (z_f_t) combines f_t, α_f+ t, and p_t.
 - The final latent representation (z_t) for the RL policy is the concatenation of z_v_t and z_f_t.

**C. Multi-Objective Dynamics Pretraining:**
The encoder is pretrained on the collected expert demonstrations using three distinct, complementary objectives, while the visual backbone remains frozen. All losses operate on the latent features.
1. **Proprioceptive Anchoring (L_dyn):** This objective is designed to promote cross-domain invariance. A lightweight dynamics head takes the concatenated motion codes (α_v+ t, α_f+ t) and the proprioceptive state from K steps earlier (p_t-K) as input. Its task is to predict the proprioceptive innovation ∆p_t = p_t - p_t-K. Predicting the innovation rather than the absolute state prevents the trivial solution of simply copying p_t-K, forcing the motion codes to encode relevant dynamics. Cycle consistency is imposed by also predicting the earlier state (p_t-K) from the estimated current state and backward motion codes (α_v- t, α_f- t, derived from negated temporal differences). The loss is a smooth-L1 error in both forward and backward directions, sharing a single prediction head. Gradients through the forward prediction are stopped before the backward prediction to ensure consistency. This objective encourages the temporal representations to encode physical motion, as domain-specific static factors offer little information about proprioceptive transitions.
2. **Task-Related Supervision (L_align, L_eng):**
 - **Spatial Alignment (L_align):** A head conditioned on the visual representation and previous action predicts the normalized planar vector from the end-effector to a target keypoint. For tasks like JointAssemble, it also regresses yaw error. A cosine loss is used for planar direction and a smooth-L1 loss for yaw.
 - **Engagement (L_eng):** A head taking both stream representations as input predicts a binary label indicating whether the held part is engaged with the receptacle, using a binary cross-entropy loss. These provide task-specific guidance for spatial positioning and contact.
3. **Cross-Modal Reconstruction (L_recon):** To encourage complementary information encoding across modalities and discourage redundancy, a masked autoencoding objective is applied in the feature space. Half of the dimensions in the concatenated vector [z_v_t, z_f_t, p_t] are uniformly masked. A shallow MLP attempts to reconstruct the complete vector. The reconstruction loss is applied to the entire vector, and the target is detached from the computation graph. This setup ensures gradients pass to the visual and force pathways to learn to reconstruct missing information, often by inferring across modalities.

**Pretraining Procedure:** The total pretraining loss is a weighted sum of these three objectives. Data augmentation (color jitter, blur, cropping) is applied to images, and annealed Gaussian noise is added to F/T measurements to simulate real-world sensor noise. The encoder is trained with early stopping for up to 100 epochs.

**D. Policy Training and Deployment:**
After pretraining, the encoder's parameters are frozen. Its output features (z_t) are then used as observations for training an RL policy.
- **Asymmetric Actor-Critic:** An asymmetric actor-critic architecture is employed. The actor receives only the latent features (z_t), proprioception (p_t), and the previous action (a_t-1). The critic, however, has access to privileged simulation state for more accurate value estimation. This ensures the actor relies solely on deployable observations.
- **RL Training:** All RL policies are trained for 200 iterations in Isaac Lab using PPO, with consistent policy architecture, hyperparameters, and domain-randomization settings to isolate the effect of the observation representation.
- **Zero-Shot Deployment:** The frozen encoder and trained policy are directly transferred to a real Franka FR3 manipulator without any real-world parameter updates or training. Camera images and F/T measurements undergo the same preprocessing as in simulation. The policy commands incremental end-effector poses via Cartesian impedance control. A one-time hardware configuration (F/T sensor zeroing, camera settings, end-effector frame offset, impedance gains) is performed, but no policy or encoder parameters are adapted on real data.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on four contact-rich assembly tasks: PegInsert, GearMesh (from Factory benchmark), JointAssemble, and KnobTighten (from AutoMate dataset), covering a range of geometric and contact-sensing challenges. Evaluation involves 100 simulated episodes (3 seeds) and 30 real-robot trials (one checkpoint per method/task). Baselines include a Pose-Based PPO policy (using external perception on hardware), No-Pretrain RL (using generic DINOv2 features and raw F/T), and AugInsert (behavior cloning + cross-attention). Ablation studies examine the contributions of proprioceptive anchoring, force stream, visual stream, and masked reconstruction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation provides a detailed assessment of PACE's performance in zero-shot sim-to-real transfer and its robustness to real-world perturbations, comparing it against several baselines and through ablation studies.

**A. Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer (Table I):**
- **PACE (Full):** Demonstrated strong performance in real-world deployment. It achieved an average real-world success rate of 93.3% across the four contact-rich assembly tasks. The average sim-to-real performance drop (∆) was 2.7 percentage points (pp). Specifically, on PegInsert, the drop was 0.7 pp (97.3% sim, 96.7% real); on GearMesh, 2.3 pp (95.7% sim, 93.3% real); on JointAssemble, 5.3 pp (95.3% sim, 90.0% real); and on KnobTighten, 2.3 pp (95.7% sim, 93.3% real).
- **Baselines:**
 - **Pose-Based PPO:** Achieved 77.5% average real-world success, but with a significantly larger average sim-to-real drop of 18.9 pp. This performance degradation highlights the sensitivity of pose-based methods to errors introduced by external perception modules. For instance, on JointAssemble, the drop was 22.0 pp, and on KnobTighten, 22.7 pp.
 - **AugInsert:** Exhibited an average real-world success rate of 68.3% and an average sim-to-real drop of 18.4 pp. This suggests that behavior-cloning initialization combined with cross-attention-based multimodal fusion did not provide the same level of domain invariance as PACE.
 - **No-Pretrain RL:** Showed the lowest real-world success rate, averaging 7.5%, and the largest average sim-to-real drop of 43.7 pp. This indicates that relying on generic visual features and raw multimodal observations without task-specific encoder pretraining is insufficient for learning transferable assembly policies.

**Cross-Domain Feature Alignment (Fig. 4):**
Analysis of matched simulation and hardware trajectories revealed that PACE's visual and force features exhibited higher cosine similarity across domains compared to unadapted representations. Specifically, within the contact window, PACE visual features had a mean cosine similarity of 0.91 ± 0.08, and PACE force features had 0.64 ± 0.23. In contrast, frozen DINOv2 features showed 0.69 ± 0.08, and unprocessed F/T measurements 0.23 ± 0.20. This indicates that PACE representations are more aligned between simulation and reality.

**Ablation Studies (Table I):**
- **Without Proprioceptive Anchoring (w/o L_dyn):** This ablation resulted in the most significant degradation in transfer performance. The average sim-to-real drop increased from 2.7 pp to 25.5 pp, despite only a modest 4.7 pp decrease in simulated success. This finding strongly suggests that proprioceptive anchoring is a critical component for achieving robust cross-domain transfer.
- **Without the Force Stream:** Removing the force pathway led to the largest decrease in simulated success (11.3 pp reduction from full PACE), impacting tasks like GearMesh, JointAssemble, and KnobTighten most significantly. The average real-world success rate dropped to 75.8%, and the average sim-to-real drop increased to 8.8 pp. This highlights the importance of F/T sensing for resolving ambiguities in meshing, rotational alignment, and detecting surface engagement that are difficult to infer from vision alone.
- **Without the Visual Stream:** This variant achieved very low simulated success rates (5–9%), confirming that visual information is essential for initial alignment in these tasks. This ablation was not evaluated on hardware due to its poor simulated performance.
- **Without Masked Reconstruction (w/o L_recon):** Removing the cross-modal masked reconstruction objective led to a 4.1 pp decrease in simulated success and an increase in the average sim-to-real drop from 2.7 pp to 6.1 pp. This indicates that masked reconstruction contributes to both nominal transfer performance and representation robustness.

**B. Real-World Robustness Under Perturbations (Fig. 5):**
PACE demonstrated high robustness to various real-world perturbations on the GearMesh task:
- **Reference Condition:** 100% success.
- **Lighting Change:** 100% success.
- **Camera Shift (2-cm lateral):** 90% success.
- **F/T Bias (2-N static):** 90% success.
- **Background Change:** 100% success.
In contrast, Pose-Based PPO's success rate dropped from 90% to 10% under a camera shift. AugInsert's success rate decreased from 90% to 35% under an F/T bias. The ablation without masked reconstruction showed reduced performance under all perturbations, with the largest drops observed under lighting and background changes. These results indicate that both proprioceptive anchoring and masked reconstruction contribute to the method's resilience against visual and sensor disturbances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in addressing the sim-to-real gap for contact-rich robotic assembly, a domain critical for many industrial and advanced manufacturing applications. The significance and potential impact of PACE can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The primary impact is the demonstration of robust zero-shot transfer for complex contact-rich assembly tasks. By achieving an average real-world success rate of 93.3% with a minimal 2.7 pp sim-to-real performance drop, PACE reduces the need for expensive and time-consuming real-world data collection and fine-tuning, which are major bottlenecks in robot learning.

2. **Novelty of Proprioceptive Anchoring:** The introduction of proprioceptive anchoring as a supervisory signal for learning domain-invariant representations is a key conceptual contribution. The insight that proprioception offers a comparatively stable cross-domain reference (due to shared coordinate systems and consistent computation) is leveraged effectively. This approach offers a distinct alternative to conventional domain adaptation or randomization techniques by directly guiding feature learning to suppress domain-specific noise.

3. **Improved Robustness to Real-World Variability:** PACE demonstrates high resilience to common real-world perturbations, including changes in lighting, camera position, F/T sensor bias, and background appearance. This robustness is crucial for deploying robotic systems in unstructured or varying industrial environments, where such factors are common. The performance of PACE under perturbations significantly surpasses that of pose-based and learned-fusion baselines, indicating a more stable and generalizable control policy.

4. **Reduced Reliance on External Perception:** By enabling pixel-to-action control without external object pose estimators or trackers, PACE simplifies the system architecture and eliminates a major source of sim-to-real errors—the fragile perception-control interface. This streamlines deployment and reduces calibration overhead.

5. **Complementary Multimodal Representation Learning:** The method effectively integrates visual and force/torque information, highlighting their complementary roles. Vision is shown to be essential for initial spatial alignment, while F/T sensing is critical for resolving contact-sensitive interactions, meshing, and detecting engagement. The modular design, including learnable temporal differencing and cross-modal masked reconstruction, contributes to learning compact and complementary representations, which could inform future multimodal sensor fusion strategies.

6. **Broader Applicability of the Proprioceptive Anchoring Principle:** The core idea of using a stable sensor modality (proprioception) to supervise representations learned from modalities prone to larger domain shifts (vision, F/T) has potential implications beyond contact-rich assembly. This principle could be applied to other robotic manipulation or locomotion tasks where a reliable cross-domain anchor exists, fostering more generalizable robot learning paradigms.

In summary, this work contributes a practical and theoretically grounded approach to zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for contact-rich assembly, offering both high performance and robustness. It paves the way for more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems by reducing the real-world data burden and enhancing policy generalization.
