# Vision-Language Models as Success Detectors

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from DeepMind and UC Berkeley developed SuccessVQA, a framework that reframes success detection as a Visual Question Answering task for intelligent agents. By finetuning a pretrained Vision-Language Model, Flamingo, the method achieves enhanced generalization to novel language specifications and visual variations, outperforming bespoke models in out-of-distribution scenarios across simulated environments, real-world robotics, and human egocentric videos.

## Problem

- Traditional success detectors and reward models require extensive, labor-intensive data annotation and retraining for each new task or environmental variation.
- Existing methods struggle to generalize effectively to unseen language instructions, new visual conditions (e.g., camera viewpoints, backgrounds), or the presence of distractor objects.
- Developing accurate and scalable reward signals for complex, real-world scenarios remains challenging due to the ambiguity of successful states and difficulties in environment state identification.

## Method

- The work introduces **SuccessVQA**, a unified framework that reconfigures success detection as a ==Visual Question Answering (VQA)== task.
- A pretrained generative ==Vision-Language Model (VLM)==, specifically **Flamingo (3B)**, is finetuned as a multi-task success detector across diverse domains, keeping its large language model backbone frozen.
- SuccessVQA datasets are constructed from human-annotated trajectories, where success states are transformed into ==yes/no answers== to templated or rephrased questions.

## Results

- FT Flamingo 3B achieved **59.3%** balanced accuracy on unseen language tasks in a simulated environment, significantly outperforming a bespoke model's **49.9%** and demonstrating strong language generalization.
- The VLM-based detector showed greater robustness to visual variations in robotic manipulation, with less than a **10 percentage point** accuracy drop under viewpoint changes, compared to **10-50 percentage point** drops for bespoke models.
- In the complex 'in-the-wild' Ego4D domain, FT Flamingo 3B reached **62%** balanced accuracy on held-out videos after finetuning, outperforming zero-shot and 4-shot Flamingo models (**50%** and **52%** respectively).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging large pretrained VLMs enables a unified approach to success detection, transferring semantic understanding from web-scale pretraining to domain-specific tasks.
> - Finetuning only the vision and cross-attention layers of a VLM while keeping the language model frozen allows for robust generalization to novel language specifications.
> - The VLM-based approach demonstrates superior robustness to visual variations (e.g., camera angles, distractor objects) compared to domain-specific models, suggesting a more general understanding of task completion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Detecting successful behavior is a foundational requirement for training intelligent agents, serving as a reward signal for policy learning or as an evaluation metric. While success detectors can be manually engineered for constrained environments like games or specific control tasks, defining them for complex, real-world scenarios presents challenges related to environment state identification and the inherent ambiguity of successful states (e.g., subjective goals).

Traditional approaches to developing success detectors often involve reward modeling from human preference data or expert demonstrations, commonly through techniques like Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) or comparative preference learning. However, these methods typically yield models that are accurate only for the specific tasks and environment conditions observed in the training data. This limitation necessitates extensive, labor-intensive data annotation and model retraining for each new task or environmental variation, posing a significant bottleneck for scalable agent development. The requirement for success detectors to generalize to unseen language specifications (e.g., different phrasings of the same task) and visual changes (e.g., altered backgrounds, new camera angles, distractor objects) remains an open challenge.

Recent advancements in large-scale pretraining have led to the development of powerful foundation models, including large language models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs). In language modeling, reward models are commonly trained by finetuning LLMs with human preferences to improve generation quality. For embodied agents, large datasets of human videos have been used to train reward models. More directly related to this work, some prior research has explored using contrastive VLMs like CLIP to generate task rewards from text-based goals and pixel observations in simulated robot domains, or specialized video CLIP models for detecting alignment with text descriptions in environments like Minecraft.

This research distinguishes itself by leveraging a *generative* VLM, specifically Flamingo, which is built upon a frozen large language model. The authors hypothesize that this architecture, combined with web-scale pretraining, may enable superior language generalization. Furthermore, the work frames success detection as a Visual Question Answering (VQA) problem, termed SuccessVQA, and applies this unified framework across three distinct domains: a simulated interactive environment, real-world robotic manipulation, and "in-the-wild" human egocentric videos. This broad application, particularly to real-world scenarios where ground-truth rewards are difficult to obtain, differentiates it from prior VLM-based reward modeling efforts predominantly focused on simulated or highly structured environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop accurate and generalizable success detectors for intelligent agents. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and specific aims:

1. **Addressing Generalization Limitations:** Current reward models and success detectors often exhibit limited generalization. They typically perform well only for the exact tasks and environmental conditions encountered during training. This necessitates re-collecting and re-annotating data, followed by retraining, whenever a task's language specification changes or visual conditions (e.g., camera viewpoint, background, presence of distractor objects) are altered. The work aims to overcome this limitation by developing detectors that can generalize robustly across such variations.
2. **Leveraging Pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs):** The authors hypothesize that large pretrained VLMs, specifically Flamingo, can provide a foundation for more robust success detectors. This is based on the premise that Flamingo's pretraining on vast and diverse multimodal data (language and vision) imbues it with a semantic understanding that can transfer to success detection tasks, enabling better generalization.
3. **Unifying Success Detection across Domains:** A core motivation is to establish a universal framework for success detection. By reframing success detection as a Visual Question Answering (VQA) task, termed SuccessVQA, the research seeks to use a single architecture and training scheme applicable across vastly different domains, from simulated environments to real-world robotics and human videos. This unification aims to simplify the development of success detectors and make them more broadly applicable.
4. **Demonstrating Robustness to Language Variations:** A specific objective is to evaluate whether a VLM-based success detector can accurately identify success for novel tasks specified with language not encountered during training. This involves testing semantic understanding and generalization beyond superficial linguistic changes.
5. **Demonstrating Robustness to Visual Variations:** Another key aim is to assess the detector's ability to maintain accuracy in the presence of naturalistic visual perturbations. This includes scenarios where the camera viewpoint changes or new distractor objects are introduced into the scene, challenging the model's ability to identify task success independently of superficial visual changes.
6. **Exploring Real-World Applicability:** The research endeavors to demonstrate the potential of VLM-based success detectors in challenging, unstructured real-world settings, specifically using "in-the-wild" human egocentric videos. This domain serves as a testbed for the method's capabilities under highly diverse and unpredictable conditions, where ground truth rewards are only obtainable through human annotation.
7. **Outperforming Bespoke Models:** The work aims to show that the proposed Flamingo-based method can outperform bespoke, domain-specific reward models in out-of-distribution test scenarios involving language or visual variations, while potentially maintaining comparable performance in in-distribution tasks without requiring domain-specific architectural modifications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodological contribution of this paper is SuccessVQA, a framework that reconfigures success detection as a Visual Question Answering (VQA) task. This allows for the direct application and finetuning of large, pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs), specifically Flamingo (3B), as multi-task success detectors across diverse domains.

**Model Architecture and Training:**
The Flamingo (3B) model serves as the backbone. Flamingo integrates a pretrained vision encoder with a frozen large language model, connected via a Perceiver Resampler and interleaved cross-attention layers. During finetuning for SuccessVQA, all vision layers (vision encoder, perceiver, and cross-attention layers) are updated, while the language layers are kept frozen. This finetuned model is referred to as "FT Flamingo 3B."

**SuccessVQA Dataset Creation:**
To create SuccessVQA datasets for finetuning, existing human-annotated behavior trajectories are leveraged. The process involves:
1. **Annotation Processing:** Human raters indicate whether a task is successful within a trajectory and, if so, the precise frame where success occurs. For disagreements, a majority vote determines success, and the median of the first annotated success frame is used. Subsequent frames are considered successful unless the task is reversed.
2. **Trajectory Segmentation:** Each annotated trajectory is divided into non-overlapping subsequences. These clips are standardized to a length of 8 frames, achieved by creating 211-frame subsequences from 30 FPS video and then downsampling to 1 FPS.
3. **Question Generation:**
 - For tasks with explicit language instructions (e.g., simulated household, robotics), a template is used: "Did the {robot/agent/person} successfully {task}?".
 - For tasks derived from narrations (e.g., Ego4D), a frozen Flamingo model is used to rephrase the narration into a question (e.g., "The person is scooping the ice cream" becomes "Did the person successfully scoop the ice cream?").
4. **Answer Generation:** The answer is "yes" if the subsequence ends in a successful state according to human annotations, and "no" otherwise.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
Evaluation is performed using both clip-level and episode-level balanced accuracy on held-out test sets. Balanced accuracy accounts for potential class imbalance between successful and failed episodes/clips, with a random model yielding 50%. Episode-level accuracy, crucial for comparison with bespoke baselines, is consolidated from clip-level predictions:
- For state-defined success tasks (robotics), an episode is classified as successful if its initial clip is a failure and its final clip is a success.
- For behavior-defined success tasks (simulated household), an episode is deemed successful if *any* of its constituent subsequences is classified as successful.

**Experimental Domains and Baselines:**

1. **Language Robustness with Interactive Agents (IA Playroom):**
 - **Domain:** A simulated 3D household environment designed for language-conditioned interactive agents, featuring diverse rooms, objects, and tasks. Challenges include large language variations and a multi-task nature.
 - **Training Data:** 546,887 trajectories (1,421,111 clips) from the Standardized Test Suite (STS) and human interaction data, focusing on movement-based tasks.
 - **Baseline:** A "bespoke SD" model, specifically designed for STS. This model processes 32 downsampled frames via a ResNet-101, embeds input/output text, concatenates embeddings, and feeds them into a transformer with an MLP head predicting success likelihood. An auxiliary instruction-matching contrastive loss is also applied. This whole-episode model (outperforming an autoregressive variant) serves as the primary comparison.
 - **Evaluation Tests:**
 - **Test 1 (Unseen Episodes):** In-distribution hold-out (10% of training data).
 - **Test 2 (Unseen Behavior):** Trajectories from novel agents performing known tasks.
 - **Test 3 (Unseen Tasks):** Trajectories from novel agents performing entirely new tasks, requiring semantic understanding beyond training data.

2. **Visual Robustness with Robotic Manipulation:**
 - **Domain:** Real-world robotic manipulation tasks involving a Panda robot arm inserting or removing small, medium, or large gears into a basket, observed from a basket-mounted camera.
 - **Training Data:** 101,789 human demonstrations for 6 distinct gear tasks. Each episode includes 6 reward annotations (one per task), converted to SuccessVQA examples. A single FT Flamingo 3B model is trained for all tasks.
 - **Baseline:** A "bespoke SD" model using a ResNet-18 pretrained on ImageNet, finetuned with a binary classification layer *separately* for each of the 6 tasks, with image augmentations.
 - **Evaluation Tests:**
 - **Test 1 (In-domain):** Episodes from the same visual conditions as training.
 - **Test 2 (Viewpoint Variation):** Episodes recorded from a different (back) camera angle.
 - **Test 3 (Distractor Objects):** Episodes featuring task-irrelevant distractor objects (pegs, board) in the basket.
 - **Auxiliary Experiment:** Policy training using reward-filtered behavior cloning, where FT Flamingo 3B classifies demonstration episodes for training.

3. **Real-World Success Detection with Ego4D:**
 - **Domain:** "In-the-wild" egocentric videos of humans performing common tasks, sourced from the Ego4D dataset. This unstructured domain features high visual and linguistic diversity.
 - **Dataset Construction:** Utilizes annotations from the Ego4D Forecasting + Hands & Objects (FHO) dataset, particularly critical state change frames (PRE, Point of No Return (PNR), POST). The PNR frame signifies success, and frames prior to PRE serve as negative examples. Flamingo is used to rephrase narrations into questions.
 - **Evaluation:** Clip-level balanced accuracy on held-out videos. Compared against zero-shot and 4-shot Flamingo models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the performance and generalization capabilities of Flamingo-based success detectors (FT Flamingo 3B) across the three investigated domains.

**IA Playroom (Language Robustness):**
- **In-Distribution Performance:** FT Flamingo 3B achieved a balanced accuracy of 83.4% on "Test 1: unseen episodes" (in-distribution), which is comparable to the bespoke SD model's 80.6%.
- **Unseen Behavior:** On "Test 2: unseen behavior" (new agents, known tasks), FT Flamingo 3B obtained 85.0% balanced accuracy, matching the bespoke SD's 85.4%.
- **Unseen Tasks (Language Generalization):** Critically, on "Test 3: unseen tasks" (new agents, novel tasks), FT Flamingo 3B demonstrated significantly better generalization, achieving 59.3% balanced accuracy. This contrasts with the bespoke SD model, which performed close to random chance at 49.9%. This result suggests that the Flamingo-based model possesses a degree of semantic understanding necessary for generalizing to novel language instructions, attributed to its large language model backbone and web-scale pretraining.
- **Initial State:** Flamingo without finetuning performed at random chance (50%) in this domain, indicating the necessity of finetuning on domain-specific data.

**Robotic Manipulation (Visual Robustness):**
- **In-Domain Performance:** On "Test 1: in-domain episodes," the bespoke SD models consistently outperformed FT Flamingo 3B. For insertion tasks, FT Flamingo 3B's accuracy was comparable (e.g., 95.0% vs. 99.1% for Insert Large), but for removal tasks, its performance was lower (e.g., 82.1% vs. 97.3% for Remove Small). This discrepancy in removal tasks is hypothesized to be due to an imbalance in training data, with insertion tasks having significantly more examples.
- **Policy Training:** FT Flamingo 3B served as a reward model for filtered behavior cloning, yielding policies with success rates ranging from 50% to 75% across various tasks on manual resets. In a more challenging automated reset scenario (Insert Large), FT Flamingo 3B achieved a 30% success rate, comparable to the bespoke SD's 33%. These results indicate that the FT Flamingo 3B's reward signal is sufficient for some level of policy learning.
- **Viewpoint Variation (Visual Generalization):** On "Test 2: viewpoint variation" (different camera angle), FT Flamingo 3B exhibited substantially greater robustness. Its accuracy decreased by less than 10 percentage points across all tasks (e.g., Insert Medium: 94.4% to 89.8%), while the bespoke SD models saw drastic drops of 10-50 percentage points, sometimes approaching random guessing levels (e.g., Insert Medium: 98.4% to 53.1%). FT Flamingo 3B became the best-performing model in 5 out of 6 tasks under this condition.
- **Distractor Objects (Visual Generalization):** For "Test 3: distractor objects," FT Flamingo 3B's accuracy remained largely stable, with a maximum drop of 4.5 percentage points (and slight increases in some cases). In contrast, the bespoke SD models experienced accuracy drops of 4-30 percentage points.
- **Data Efficiency (Appendix):** When trained with significantly less data (100-200 episodes vs. tens of thousands), FT Flamingo 3B was less affected by the reduced dataset size compared to the bespoke SD model in 5 out of 6 tasks, suggesting better data efficiency.

**Real-World Success Detection with Ego4D:**
- **Performance:** FT Flamingo 3B, after finetuning on the Ego4D SuccessVQA dataset, achieved 99% balanced accuracy on the training set and 62% on the test set.
- **Necessity of Finetuning:** Zero-shot and 4-shot Flamingo models achieved only 50% and 52% accuracy, respectively, highlighting that finetuning on in-domain data is crucial for performance in this complex setting.
- **Generalization Gap:** The substantial difference between training and test accuracy (99% vs. 62%) indicates that generalizing to completely unseen videos and language tasks in this unstructured, "in-the-wild" domain remains a significant challenge, despite promising initial finetuned results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces SuccessVQA as a generalized framework for success detection, leveraging pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs). This approach unifies the problem of success detection across diverse domains, including simulated environments, real-world robotics, and human egocentric videos, by formulating it as a visual question answering task.

A key significance of this work lies in its demonstration of enhanced generalization capabilities. The Flamingo-based success detectors exhibit increased robustness to out-of-distribution language and visual changes compared to task-specific bespoke reward models. Specifically, FT Flamingo 3B showed a superior ability to generalize to unseen language instructions in a simulated household environment and demonstrated greater stability under variations in camera viewpoint and the presence of distractor objects in real-world robotic manipulation tasks. This robustness is attributed to the extensive, diverse pretraining of the Flamingo VLM, which is hypothesized to foster a deeper semantic understanding of tasks and scenes.

This improved generalization holds significant implications for the training of intelligent agents. By providing more generalizable reward models, the proposed method can reduce the need for labor-intensive, domain-specific data annotation and model retraining for every new task variant or environmental condition. Such robust success detectors are a prerequisite for agents that can learn to generalize their behavior effectively. Preliminary results from policy training through reward-filtered behavior cloning further indicate that the Flamingo-based reward signals are adequate for initiating policy learning. The authors also highlight that VLMs as reward models focus on "what to do" rather than "how to do it," suggesting they might transfer more readily across scenarios where a task can be accomplished in multiple ways, without requiring fine visual details for motor control.

While acknowledging limitations such as potential performance gaps compared to highly optimized bespoke models in some in-distribution scenarios, the computational cost of inference with large VLMs, and the need for sufficient in-domain finetuning data, the work paves the way for several impactful future research directions. These include exploring larger VLM scales for further robustness, investigating data efficiency for few-shot generalization to novel tasks within domains, and combining finetuning with few-shot prompting strategies. The shared SuccessVQA format could also facilitate cross-dataset finetuning, enhancing transfer learning. Ultimately, this research encourages further development in real-world success detection and reward modeling, which are crucial components for building more capable and adaptable intelligent agents.
