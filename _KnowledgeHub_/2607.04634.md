# Governed Caste Reassignment in Heterogeneous Swarms: An Asymmetric-Trust Protocol with Audited Operator Countersignature

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents an asymmetric-trust protocol that governs caste reassignment in heterogeneous robot swarms, providing external authorization for privilege-elevating changes and creating cryptographically verifiable audit trails. It demonstrates effective resistance against various attack patterns while maintaining efficient operation, with auto-tighten transitions occurring in milliseconds and 70-72% of bounded-relax requests handled without live operator interaction.

## Problem

- Existing multi-agent systems treat robot role reassignment as internal algorithmic decisions, lacking external authorization and auditability required by regulated environments.
- Critical governance events, such as privilege-elevating caste changes, require auditability, external authorization, and per-axis bounded relaxation, which current systems do not jointly provide.
- The absence of cryptographically anchored, offline-verifiable audit trails prevents external verification of role changes without trusting the internal state of the swarm.

## Method

- A ==privilege lattice== and a three-tiered taxonomy are introduced to classify caste reassignments, enabling an **asymmetric-trust protocol** with distinct authorization paths for permissionless tightening and gated privilege relaxation.
- A "==cause-chain==" mechanism cryptographically documents each reassignment with signed records and triggering observations, integrated into a hash-chained fleet-level audit log for tamper evidence.
- A distributed audit layer ensures a consistent, fork-free total order of caste changes across the swarm through a ==Byzantine quorum mechanism==, preventing equivocation and guaranteeing agreement.

## Results

- Auto-tighten transitions exhibited median latencies of **6.0 ms** (N=**10**) to **17.7 ms** (N=**100**), and **70-72%** of bounded-relax requests were handled via a short-circuit path, avoiding live operator interaction.
- The governed protocol successfully resisted all **four** defined attack patterns (caste laundering, repeated-relaxation privilege escalation, operator impersonation, cause-chain forgery), showing **zero** successful attacks across **7500** pooled trials per pattern.
- The distributed audit layer achieved **100%** agreement among honest replicas, detected all Byzantine equivocations, and prevented any forked records from being committed, ensuring a single, ordered, and verifiable log.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - External governance for robot swarms can be achieved by structurally differentiating between privilege-tightening (permissionless) and privilege-relaxing (gated) caste changes using an asymmetric-trust protocol.
> - Cryptographic primitives, when integrated with a distributed quorum-based commit mechanism, can provide robust, offline-verifiable auditability and strong resistance to privilege escalation and forgery attacks in a multi-robot context.
> - Operator workload for governance can be efficiently managed through pre-committed budgets for bounded privilege relaxation, enabling automated 'short-circuit' approvals for routine changes while reserving live operator attention for critical events.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a specific challenge within multi-agent systems, particularly concerning heterogeneous robot swarms operating in regulated environments. Existing approaches to robot role or "caste" reassignment primarily treat this as an internal algorithmic decision, such as auctions, consensus bundles, or behavior trees. These methods focus on optimizing internal objectives like throughput or energy efficiency and typically do not expose reassignment events to external authority.

This work posits that in regulated deployments, a caste change that increases a robot's privilege requires external authorization and auditability, classifying it as a "governance event." The paper identifies three specific properties that existing constructions generally do not jointly provide in this context:
1. **Privilege monotonicity as a structural invariant:** Safer (tightening) changes are permissionless, while privilege-elevating (relaxation) changes are gated.
2. **Cryptographic, offline-verifiable auditability:** Every transition must produce an audit trail that can be verified externally without relying on the internal state of the swarm.
3. **Per-axis bounded budget on relaxation:** An external regulator should be able to inspect a predefined budget for privilege relaxation without needing to trust internal allocation algorithms.

The paper situates its contributions relative to several established areas of multi-agent systems research:
- **Multi-Robot Task Allocation (MRTA):** While MRTA frameworks (e.g., Gerkey & Matarić, Korsah et al., coalition formation, market-based coordination) focus on algorithmic optimization for task assignment, they do not incorporate external governance or cryptographic audit for role changes. The proposed protocol is designed to operate above these allocators, governing the role change itself rather than the coalition choice.
- **Role Allocation and Reallocation:** Work by Nair et al. and Campbell & Wu formalizes role reallocation, sometimes involving human approval (e.g., Al-Hussaini et al.). However, these approaches typically lack the cryptographic provenance or asymmetric gating mechanisms emphasized in this paper.
- **Organisational MAS and Reorganisation:** Models like MOISE+ and surveys by Horling & Lesser define permissions and obligations associated with roles. This paper's privilege lattice can be seen as a runtime, cryptographically enforced implementation of such permissions. While reorganisation frameworks exist (e.g., Hübner et al.), they do not specify the asymmetric authorization gates and cryptographic audit records introduced here.
- **Adjustable Autonomy and Human Supervisory Control:** This domain (e.g., Scerri et al., Chen et al.) explores how agents transfer control to human operators. The operator countersignature in the proposed protocol aligns conceptually with this, but the paper differentiates itself by adding an offline-verifiable cause-chain and a per-axis budget mechanism.
- **Normative MAS and Electronic Institutions:** These fields (e.g., Boella et al., Esteva et al.) involve norms, monitoring, and sanctioning in multi-agent interactions. The operator countersignature on caste reassignment functions as an instance of a regulative norm, with the cause-chain serving as the cryptographic monitoring and evidence record.
- **Multi-Robot Authorisation Infrastructure and Blockchain Swarms:** Existing solutions like SROS2 offer per-node certification and attribute-based access control. Blockchain swarms (e.g., Strobel et al., Dorigo et al.) address inter-robot trust but often lack an operator-anchored authorization for privilege changes. This work builds upon the concept of a privilege lattice and operates within a framework that prioritizes operator authority for specific caste changes.
- **Biological Caste Plasticity:** The paper adopts the "caste" terminology from eusocial insect colonies (e.g., Wilson, Robinson), acknowledging the biological precedent for frequent role reassignment, but the authorization mechanism is an engineered construct.

This work specifically extends previous research by some of the authors on single-agent persona-mutation governance and fleet-level federation, applying the same runtime governance posture established in to heterogeneous multi-robot fleets. The novelty lies in the specific combination of privilege monotonicity, cryptographic auditability, and per-axis bounded relaxation within a swarm context, rather than in the individual cryptographic or MAS primitives themselves.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the operational requirements of heterogeneous robot swarms deployed in environments subject to external regulation. Such environments include hospital wards, factory floors, or defense installations. In these settings, robots frequently need to change their operational roles or "castes" due to various runtime events, such as battery depletion, changes in payload, or shifts in mission priorities. For example, a logistics robot might transition to a lower-power patrol caste, or a heavy-lift robot might be reassigned to an observation caste.

The central problem identified is that current multi-agent systems approaches for role allocation primarily treat these reassignments as internal decisions. They prioritize internal system optimization (e.g., efficiency, robustness) and do not typically expose caste changes to external scrutiny or authorization. However, for regulated applications, any caste change that elevates a robot's operational privileges (e.g., allowing access to sensitive areas, handling dangerous payloads, or performing critical functions) constitutes a critical governance event. Such an event, from a regulatory standpoint, requires:
1. **Auditability:** A clear, immutable record of what happened and why.
2. **External Authorization:** A mechanism for an external authority (e.g., a human operator or regulator) to approve or deny privilege-escalating changes.

The paper argues that existing methods do not collectively provide three necessary properties:
- **Privilege Monotonicity:** A system should inherently distinguish between privilege-tightening (safer) and privilege-relaxing (potentially riskier) transitions, allowing the former to be permissionless and the latter to be gated.
- **Cryptographic, Offline-Verifiable Auditability:** Every single caste transition should generate a cryptographically secured log entry that can be verified offline by an external auditor without needing to trust the robots' internal states.
- **Per-Axis Bounded Relaxation:** There should be a transparent and inspectable mechanism to set limits on how much a robot's privilege can be elevated along specific axes (e.g., battery consumption, payload capacity), independent of the internal allocation algorithms.

To address these motivations and gaps, the key objectives of this research are:
- To develop a comprehensive taxonomy for classifying caste reassignments based on their privilege implications.
- To design an asymmetric-trust protocol that implements distinct authorization paths for different classes of caste transitions, ensuring that privilege-elevating changes are explicitly governed.
- To construct a "cause-chain" mechanism that cryptographically documents the provenance of each reassignment, enabling forensic reconstruction and audit.
- To evaluate the protocol's performance, overhead, and resilience against specific attack patterns using a reference implementation with real cryptographic primitives in a simulated environment.
- To establish and evaluate a distributed audit layer that ensures agreement on the audit log and detects attempts at equivocation, thereby providing a fork-free total order of caste changes across the swarm.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on designing and evaluating an asymmetric-trust protocol for governing caste reassignment in heterogeneous robot swarms. The approach combines principles from access control, cryptography, and distributed systems.

#### 3.1. Foundational Concepts

- **Privilege Lattice:** The concept of a privilege lattice (Definition 1) is introduced, comprising a set of castes ($P$), a partial order ($\preceq$) defining privilege relationships, and functions mapping castes to sets of allowed actions. Crucially, it defines magnitude functions ($\Delta_\alpha$) for specified relaxation axes (e.g., battery budget, payload weight). An external operator declares an envelope ($E$) of relaxable axes and per-axis bounds ($B_\alpha$), forming the authorization state. The charge for relaxation is measured from a fixed operator-reset baseline caste ($c^*$), preventing "caste laundering."
- **Caste Reassignment Taxonomy:** A three-tiered classification (Definition 2) is established:
 - **Operator-only:** For highly sensitive castes explicitly reserved for direct operator authorization.
 - **Auto-tighten:** For transitions where a robot moves to a strictly lower-privilege caste, admitted permissionlessly.
 - **Bounded-relax:** For transitions that increase privilege within operator-pre-committed bounds along specific axes.
 - A default "Operator-only" category captures all other transitions, including those between incomparable castes or relaxations outside defined envelopes, requiring explicit operator intervention. This classification is mechanical and locally computable by fleet members.

#### 3.2. Asymmetric-Trust Protocol

The protocol implements distinct authorization paths for each class of caste reassignment:
- **Tighten Path:** A robot proposes an auto-tighten transition by signing a request. Fleet members verify the transition against the privilege lattice and, upon successful verification, append the record to their local audit chain and send an acknowledgment. The proposing robot adopts the new caste after receiving a quorum of acknowledgments (one round trip). No operator involvement is required.
- **Bounded-Relax Path:** A robot proposes a bounded-relax transition with a signed triggering observation. Fleet members verify that the request is within the operator-pre-committed envelope, meaning the target caste's privilege elevation from the operator-reset baseline is within the per-axis bound ($B_\alpha$). The operator then countersigns the record. This can occur either via a "short-circuit" path, if a pre-issued ratification token (representing depleting headroom) covers the request, or via a "live path" requiring an interactive operator signature if the token is exhausted. After countersignature, fleet members append the record, and the robot adopts the new caste (two round trips).
- **Operator-Only Path:** For transitions into operator-only castes, the robot sends a request to the operator. The operator inspects and either issues a fresh, operator-signed caste binding (a countersignature over the record) or denies the request. The signed binding is then recorded in the audit chain, and the robot adopts the caste. This is an explicit certificate issuance.

#### 3.3. Cause-Chain Construction

Every caste reassignment generates a Cause-Chain Record (CCR), which is a signed tuple containing the triggering observation (obs), the proposing robot's identity hash ($h_i$), source and target castes ($c, c'$), timestamp ($t$), class of transition, robot's signature ($\sigma_i$), and the operator's countersignature ($\sigma^?_O$) if applicable. A crucial aspect is that the triggering observation itself must carry a valid upstream signature from an authorized sensor or actor, preventing cause-chain forgery by the proposing robot. These records are integrated into a fleet-level audit chain, which is hash-chained for tamper evidence and committed per epoch to an operator-signed Merkle root, enabling forensic reconstruction by an external auditor (Proposition 5).

#### 3.4. Fleet and Threat Model

- **Fleet Model:** A fleet of $N$ robots with at most $f = \lfloor(N-1)/3\rfloor$ Byzantine members, communicating over authenticated channels (e.g., SROS2). An external operator holds a signing key, known to all members, for countersignatures and certificates.
- **Threat Model:** Explicitly addresses four attack patterns:
 1. **Caste laundering:** An adversary attempts to tighten then relax to exceed bounds while appearing neutral.
 2. **Repeated-relaxation privilege escalation:** Adversary repeatedly takes small relaxations to exceed cumulative bounds.
 3. **Operator impersonation:** Adversary forges operator countersignatures.
 4. **Cause-chain forgery:** Adversary submits a false triggering observation.
 The protocol's construction aims to resist these attacks (Corollary 1).

#### 3.5. Distributed Audit and Fork Exclusion

To ensure a consistent, ordered history of caste reassignments across the fleet, a distributed audit layer is implemented. Each fleet member maintains a local, hash-chained replica of the audit log. When a record is proposed, honest members accepting it sign a position-attestation binding the record's hash to its sequence position, with a strict "one-signature-per-position" invariant. A record commits at a given position only when a quorum ($q = \lceil(N+f+1)/2\rceil$) of distinct member attestations for the same hash is collected. This guarantees agreement on a single, fork-free total order and enables detection of equivocating Byzantine members (Proposition 6).

#### 3.6. Reference Implementation and Evaluation

The methodology includes developing a genuine reference implementation using standard cryptographic primitives (Ed25519 keys, SHA-256 hash-chained Merkle logs). This implementation is evaluated through discrete-event network simulations for latency and against the defined attack patterns, confirming resistance by cryptographic verification and envelope checks. A "robot-class timing point" provides realistic parameters for slower crypto and wireless variance. The distributed audit layer is evaluated both in simulation and as a real multi-process deployment over TCP sockets, including Byzantine equivocator processes, to confirm agreement and fork exclusion properties on a physically distributed system.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the asymmetric-trust protocol and its reference implementation focused on five key properties: tightening latency, bounded-relax gating behavior, resistance to attack patterns, quorum admission and offline audit, and distributed audit with fork exclusion.

#### 4.1. Tightening Latency (RQ1)

- **Latency:** Auto-tighten transitions, which require one round trip for quorum acknowledgment, exhibited median latencies in the range of single-digit to low-double-digit milliseconds. Specifically, at fleet sizes ($N$) of 10, 50, and 100 robots, the median latencies were 6.0 ms, 11.3 ms, and 17.7 ms, respectively. This growth in latency is attributed to increasing quorum size, shared-medium contention, and the proposer's verification cost for multiple acknowledgements.
- **Governance Overhead:** Compared to an ungoverned reassignment (immediate adoption without audit), the governance gate introduced an overhead of 3.8 ms (N=10), 7.9 ms (N=50), and 12.9 ms (N=100). These figures remained significantly below the target 2-second inter-event budget for typical deployments.
- **Robot-Class Timing:** A specific timing point modeling a 10-robot wireless deployment with slower cryptographic operations yielded a median latency of 12.5 ms, with a 99th percentile (p99) of 17.3 ms, indicating that the protocol remains within practical operational bounds.

#### 4.2. Bounded-Relax Gating (RQ2)

- **Short-Circuit vs. Live Path:** For bounded-relax transitions, 70-72% of requests were handled via the short-circuit path, utilizing a pre-issued ratification token and thus avoiding an interactive operator round trip. The remaining 28-30% required a live operator countersignature, incurring a modeled median latency of 85 ms for operator interaction. This demonstrates the protocol’s ability to manage the majority of relaxations efficiently while reserving live operator attention for situations requiring explicit confirmation, all while the security envelope remains enforced regardless of the token's state.

#### 4.3. Attack Pattern Resistance (RQ3)

- **Governed Protocol's Efficacy:** The governed protocol demonstrated effective resistance to all four explicit attack patterns defined in the threat model (caste laundering, repeated-relaxation privilege escalation, operator impersonation, and cause-chain forgery). Across 7500 pooled trials for each pattern, the protocol admitted zero successful attacks (Wilson 95% upper bound < 0.001).
- **Ablation Study:**
 - An "ungoverned" or "reallocation" baseline, lacking any governance, admitted every attack (100% admission rate).
 - An "authorization-only" baseline, which required an operator countersignature but omitted the per-axis envelope and observation checks, successfully refused operator impersonation (0% admission) but admitted caste laundering, repeated-relaxation escalation, and cause-chain forgery (100% admission). This empirically isolated the specific gates responsible for stopping each attack: operator signature verification for impersonation, per-axis envelope for laundering/escalation, and upstream observation signature for forgery.
- **Randomized Fuzzing:** A randomized adversary, mutating otherwise valid records, found no new vulnerabilities; the protocol admitted zero unauthorized mutations and every legitimate control, confirming robustness beyond predefined attack scenarios.
- **Decision Cost:** The measured wall-clock cost for the governed protocol's decisive `propose()` decision (including signature and envelope checks) was between 0.19 and 0.30 ms per attempt.

#### 4.4. Quorum and Offline Audit (RQ4)

- **Tighten Quorum Enforcement:** The Byzantine quorum mechanism for tighten admissions was robust. Honest tighten requests consistently achieved quorum (1.00 success rate). Conversely, forged tighten requests (e.g., with a bad robot signature) backed by up to the maximum tolerated number of Byzantine members ($f$) never reached a valid quorum (0.00 success rate), confirming that a rogue coalition cannot unilaterally force a tighten.
- **Offline Verifiability:** The offline auditor successfully reconstructed and verified the entire audit log solely using the operator's public key, the operator-signed identity manifest, and the operator-signed epoch root. It consistently detected any tampering with log entries, re-verified all embedded signatures (robot, operator, upstream observation), checked quorum certificates for tighten records, and validated per-axis bounds for bounded-relax records, thereby preventing unauthorized or out-of-envelope changes from being accepted offline.

#### 4.5. Distributed Audit and Fork Exclusion (RQ5)

- **Simulated Replication:** In simulation, a distributed audit layer with N per-member replicas consistently achieved agreement (1.00) among all honest replicas, committing an identical ordered log. Distributed-commit latency was in the single-digit to low-double-digit millisecond range (5.5 ms at N=10, 10.7 ms at N=50, 17.0 ms at N=100).
- **Byzantine Equivocation:** Across 50 equivocation trials per fleet size, every honest replica detected Byzantine equivocation (1.00 detection rate), and no forked record was ever committed (0.00 fork rate).
- **Real Multi-Process Deployment:** The safety properties were confirmed in a real-world, multi-process deployment on a single host. All honest processes agreed on a single ordered log, detected the Byzantine equivocator, and no forked record committed. End-to-end commit latency over real TCP sockets ranged from 4.1 ms (N=10) to 110.4 ms (N=100), reflecting the overhead of a reference implementation on a single-host loopback network. This demonstrated that the one-signature-per-position invariant holds true in a practical distributed setting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a fundamental requirement for the safe and compliant operation of heterogeneous robot swarms in regulated environments. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Enabling Regulated Deployments:** By providing a mechanism for auditable and externally authorized caste reassignment, the protocol enables robot swarms to operate in sensitive sectors such as healthcare, advanced manufacturing, and defense, where regulatory compliance, accountability, and human oversight are paramount. This capability is crucial for expanding the deployment of autonomous systems into environments that require stringent governance.
- **Enhanced Trust and Accountability:** The cryptographically anchored audit log and forensic reconstruction capabilities establish a high level of trust and accountability. External regulators or auditors can independently verify every caste transition, its justification, and the authorization path taken, reducing the need to trust the internal mechanisms or self-reported states of the swarm. This transparency can facilitate regulatory approval and public acceptance of autonomous systems.
- **Robust Security Against Specific Attacks:** The protocol is designed to resist critical attack patterns, including caste laundering, repeated-relaxation privilege escalation, operator impersonation, and cause-chain forgery. This by-construction security, validated through empirical testing, contributes to the overall resilience of multi-robot systems against malicious actors or malfunctions that attempt to subvert privilege boundaries.
- **Efficient Operator Engagement:** The asymmetric-trust model, with its distinction between permissionless tightening and gated relaxation, significantly optimizes operator workload. The short-circuit path for bounded relaxation, facilitated by pre-issued ratification tokens, allows the majority of privilege-elevating changes to occur without immediate human intervention, reserving operator attention for only the most critical or boundary-exceeding events.
- **Foundation for Future Governance Systems:** The work generalizes a single-agent governance pattern to a swarm context, offering a reusable primitive for runtime governance in multi-agent systems. It lays a groundwork for future research into more complex governance structures, such as multi-operator federations, hierarchical certificate authorities, and formal specification of authorization envelopes.
- **Practical Implementability:** The development and release of a genuine reference implementation, employing standard cryptographic primitives and evaluated in both simulation and real multi-process deployments, demonstrate the practical viability of the proposed concepts. This artifact supports reproducibility and provides a concrete basis for further research and industrial adoption.

In summary, this research contributes to the field of multi-agent systems by moving beyond internal algorithmic optimization to address the external governance requirements of robot swarms. It offers a structured, secure, and auditable approach to managing robot roles, which is essential for the responsible and safe integration of autonomous systems into human-centric and regulated environments.
