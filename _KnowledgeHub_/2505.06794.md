# Dynamic Safety in Complex Environments: Synthesizing Safety Filters with Poisson's Equation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Caltech developed a method for synthesizing smooth, differentiable safety functions from real-time perception data using Poisson's equation, addressing a long-standing challenge in Control Barrier Function (CBF) based control. This approach provides functions with guaranteed differentiability, enabling high-order safety control and demonstrating real-time collision avoidance on quadruped and humanoid robots.

## Problem

- Ensuring collision avoidance and overall safety for autonomous robotic systems operating in complex, dynamic, and unknown real-world environments.
- Existing rigorous methods like Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis are often too computationally intensive for real-time application in high-dimensional systems.
- Traditional safety function designs, such as Signed Distance Functions (SDFs), suffer from gradient discontinuities, leading to chattering or suboptimal performance in Control Barrier Function (CBF) based controllers.

## Method

- A framework is introduced to synthesize smooth safety functions by solving ==Poisson's equation== from environmental perception data with Dirichlet boundary conditions on obstacle surfaces.
- Instead of direct assignment, a variational approach uses the divergence of a pre-designed smooth =='guidance vector field'== as the forcing function for Poisson's equation, allowing precise control over safety gradients.
- The synthesized safety function is integrated with ==Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)== and ==CBF backstepping== to guarantee forward invariance of safe sets for both first-order and high-order robotic systems.

## Results

- Simulations showed Poisson safety functions produced smoother robot trajectories compared to Signed Distance Functions, which led to oscillatory control behavior due to gradient discontinuities.
- The Poisson safety function was updated online at approximately **10 Hz** with solving times of **0.2-0.3 ms** on a GPU for a **120x120** grid, demonstrating real-time computational efficiency for dynamic environments.
- Hardware demonstrations on a Unitree Go2 quadruped and G1 humanoid robot showed successful navigation and collision avoidance in complex static and dynamic environments using the CBF-based safety filter.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Solving Poisson's equation with a Holder continuous and strictly negative forcing function yields a safety function with guaranteed high-order differentiability, crucial for robust control.
> - The method establishes that if the forcing function is sufficiently smooth, the r-th derivatives of the resulting safety function are Lipschitz continuous, supporting the design of high-order Control Barrier Functions.
> - A variational approach allows for shaping the safety function's gradient field by designing a smooth 'guidance vector field', offering flexible control over repulsive forces from obstacles.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of autonomous robotic systems in real-world, complex, and dynamic environments presents a fundamental challenge: ensuring collision avoidance and overall safety. This paper directly addresses the critical need for quantifiable and constructive methods to define and enforce safety constraints in such scenarios.

Historically, various approaches have been explored for dynamic safety. **Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis** provides rigorous safety guarantees by computing the "reachable set" of unsafe states, but its computational complexity often limits real-time application to low-dimensional systems or offline analysis. **Model Predictive Control (MPC)** can incorporate safety constraints directly into optimization problems, offering predictive capabilities, but its guarantees are often tied to the prediction horizon and perfect model knowledge, and it can be computationally intensive for high-frequency control. **Artificial Potential Fields (APFs)** offer an intuitive heuristic for navigation and obstacle avoidance by creating repulsive forces around obstacles and attractive forces towards goals. While simple and effective for many tasks, APFs typically assume kinematic models (single integrator dynamics), often couple safety and stability objectives (leading to potential "deadlocks"), and struggle with complex geometries due to gradient discontinuities.

The advent of **Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)**, a field where Professor Ames's group has been instrumental, marked a significant advancement. CBFs provide a formal framework to enforce safety for general nonlinear systems by defining a safe set as a superlevel set of a function and ensuring its forward invariance through control inputs. A key advantage of CBFs is their ability to decouple safety from stability objectives, often implemented via quadratic programs (QPs) that minimally modify a nominal controller to ensure safety. However, a persistent challenge in CBF-based control, especially for collision avoidance in complex environments, has been the constructive design of the safety function itself. Traditional methods often rely on simple analytical expressions (e.g., signed distance functions for spheres), which are either overly conservative or non-constructive for arbitrary geometries and multiple obstacles. Signed Distance Functions (SDFs), while widely used, suffer from gradient discontinuities at "ridges" or "corners," which can lead to undesirable chattering in controllers when used directly as CBFs.

This paper positions itself precisely at this intersection. It proposes a novel approach to *synthesize* the required smooth, differentiable safety functions from perception data, overcoming the limitations of traditional SDFs and heuristic methods. By leveraging elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs), specifically Poisson's equation, the authors provide a principled way to generate these safety functions for arbitrary and dynamically changing geometries. This work significantly advances the practicality and rigor of CBF-based safety filters, making them more applicable to real-world robotic systems operating in unstructured, obstacle-rich environments. It connects the geometrical representation of safety (via PDEs) with the formal enforcement of safety (via CBFs), offering a comprehensive solution to a critical problem in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a systematic and computationally efficient algorithm for generating *safe sets* and their corresponding *safety functions* from real-time perception data in complex, dynamically changing environments. This capability is crucial for enabling the construction of robust safety filters, particularly those employing Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), that can guarantee collision-free operation for robotic systems.

The motivation stems from several key challenges in current safety-critical control:
- **Complexity of Real-World Environments:** Robotic systems need to operate autonomously amidst multiple obstacles of arbitrary and often dynamically changing geometries, which is difficult for traditional safety function synthesis methods.
- **Limitations of Existing Safety Functions:** Heuristic approaches are not generalizable, while methods like Signed Distance Functions (SDFs) suffer from gradient discontinuities that complicate their direct use in smooth control designs, leading to potential chattering or sub-optimal performance.
- **Decoupling Safety from Control Objectives:** While CBFs offer this decoupling, the *construction* of the underlying safety function itself for complex scenarios remains a significant hurdle. There is a need for a constructive method that provides functions with desirable differentiability properties.
- **Real-time Applicability:** For dynamic environments, the safety function must be synthesizable in real-time, adapting to changes in the environment, a requirement that often rules out computationally intensive methods like full Hamilton-Jacobi reachability.

By addressing these motivations, the paper aims to fuse the synthesis of robust safety constraints with the generation of safe controllers, creating a holistic framework for dynamic safety. The specific goal is to demonstrate that Poisson's equation can effectively serve as the mathematical tool for this synthesis, yielding safety functions that are inherently smooth and well-suited for integration with CBF-based safety filters for high-performance robotic platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology presented in this paper revolves around leveraging elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs), specifically Poisson’s equation, to synthesize smooth safety functions from environmental perception data.

#### 3.1. Safety Function Definition and Problem Formulation

The authors define a "safety function" $h: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ where its 0-superlevel set, $C = \{y \in \Omega | h(y) \ge 0\}$, characterizes the safe region in the spatial domain $\Omega$. The boundary $\partial\Omega$ represents obstacle surfaces. The key idea is to solve Poisson's equation for $h$:
$\Delta h(y) = f(y)$ in $\Omega$
$h(y) = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$ (Dirichlet boundary condition)
where $\Delta$ is the Laplacian operator, and $f(y)$ is a "forcing function." A crucial theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that if $f(y)$ is H¨older continuous and strictly negative ($f(y) < 0$) in $\Omega$, then the solution $h$ is a safety function that is twice continuously differentiable ($h \in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})$) and characterizes a compact safe set. This differentiability is critical for control design, especially for higher-order systems.

#### 3.2. Forcing Function Construction

The design of the forcing function $f(y)$ is central to shaping the safety function $h$ and ensuring its desirable properties:
- **Direct Assignment:**
 - **Distance Metric:** $f(y) = - (\text{dist}(y, \partial\Omega) / \|\text{dist}(y, \partial\Omega)\|_{\infty})^\alpha$. This ensures $f$ is negative and H¨older continuous, yielding $h \in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})$. However, it may lack higher-order differentiability if $\alpha < 1$, making it less suitable for high-order CBFs.
 - **Constant Value:** $f = \text{constant} < 0$. This guarantees $h \in C^\infty(\bar{\Omega})$, providing maximum smoothness. To relate $f$ to the desired "repulsive" gradients on obstacle surfaces, Gauss's divergence theorem is used to show how $f$ can be set to enforce a desired *average flux* on $\partial\Omega$.
- **Indirect Assignment (Variational Approach via Guidance Field):** This is the paper's novel approach.
 - **Concept:** Instead of directly specifying $f$, the authors design a smooth "guidance vector field" $\vec{v}: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^3$ that encodes desired gradient information (e.g., pointing away from obstacles). The safety function $h$ is then found as the unique minimizer of a variational problem: $h = \arg \min_h \int_\Omega \frac{1}{2} |Dh(y) - \vec{v}(y)|^2 dV$.
 - **Euler-Lagrange Equation:** The Euler-Lagrange equation for this variational problem is $\Delta h(y) = \nabla \cdot \vec{v}(y)$, meaning the forcing function $f(y) = \nabla \cdot \vec{v}(y)$ (the divergence of the guidance field).
 - **Guidance Field Synthesis:** The smooth guidance field $\vec{v}$ itself is generated by solving Laplace's equation for each of its components, subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions that specify the desired outward normal flux $\vec{v}(y) = b(y)\hat{n}(y)$ on $\partial\Omega$, where $b(y) < 0$ is a scalar function. This allows fine-grained control over the "repulsive" gradients near obstacles.
 - **Ensuring Negativity of $f$:** Since $\nabla \cdot \vec{v}(y)$ is not guaranteed to be strictly negative, a softplus-like transformation is applied: $f(y) = - \frac{1}{\beta} \ln(1 + e^{-\nabla \cdot \vec{v}(y)\beta})$. This ensures $f(y) \in C^\infty(\bar{\Omega}; \mathbb{R}_{<0})$, guaranteeing $h \in C^\infty(\bar{\Omega})$. This sophisticated approach ensures both smoothness and control over the shape of the safety function.

#### 3.3. Integration with Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)

The synthesized safety function $h$ (or a modified version of it) is then used to construct a CBF to ensure forward invariance of the safe set $C$.
- **First-Order Systems (Relative Degree 1):** For systems like $\dot{y} = w$, $h$ directly serves as a CBF (Proposition 1), ensuring $C$ is forward invariant. A standard QP-based safety filter can be formulated to minimally adjust nominal control inputs.
- **High-Order Systems (Relative Degree $r \ge 2$):** For systems with higher-order dynamics (e.g., double integrators), the authors leverage **CBF backstepping**. This involves constructing an auxiliary CBF candidate $h_B$ (Eq. 36) that incorporates not just position but also velocity information. Crucially, this requires the *r*-th partial derivatives of $h$ to be Lipschitz continuous. Lemma 1 shows that if $f \in C^{k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})$, then $h \in C^{2+k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})$, which implies D$^r h$ is Lipschitz continuous if $r \le k+1$. Since the variational approach yields $f \in C^\infty(\bar{\Omega})$, this condition is readily met for any relative degree. This allows for the construction of a locally Lipschitz continuous controller that renders a shrunken safe set $C_B \subset C$ forward invariant.

#### 3.4. Real-time Implementation

The methodology emphasizes real-time utility. Poisson's equation is solved numerically on a discrete spatial grid using a finite difference scheme (Successive Overrelaxation, SOR). For dynamic environments, warm-starting the solver with the previous time step's solution significantly speeds up computation, allowing for online updates (e.g., at 10 Hz) from perception data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling theoretical results and validates them through extensive simulations and hardware demonstrations.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Poisson Safety Function Guarantee (Theorem 1):** The central theoretical contribution is the proof that solving Poisson’s equation with a H¨older continuous and strictly negative forcing function ($f \in C^{k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}; \mathbb{R}_{<0})$) and Dirichlet boundary conditions ($h=0$ on $\partial\Omega$) yields a safety function $h \in C^{2+k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}; \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0})$. This function inherently defines a safe set and possesses the necessary differentiability properties (smoothness).
- **Lipschitz Regularity of Higher Derivatives (Lemma 1):** This lemma is crucial for high-order CBF design. It states that if $f \in C^{k,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})$, then the $r$-th derivatives of $h$ ($D^r h$) are Lipschitz continuous for $r \le k+1$. Since the proposed guidance-field based forcing function is $C^\infty$, this condition holds for any relative degree $r$, guaranteeing the existence of locally Lipschitz continuous controllers.
- **CBF Applicability (Proposition 1 & Theorem 6):** The paper formally establishes that the constructed Poisson safety functions can serve as CBFs (or part of a backstepping-based CBF) for both first-order and higher-order systems, guaranteeing the forward invariance of the defined safe sets (or subsets thereof).

#### 4.2. Simulation Results (Double Integrator)

- **Comparison with SDFs:** Simulations with a 2D double integrator model clearly demonstrate the advantages of Poisson safety functions over traditional Signed Distance Functions (SDFs). While SDFs produce "flat" surfaces with sharp ridges (gradient discontinuities) leading to oscillatory or chattering control behavior, Poisson safety functions are inherently smooth, resulting in smoother trajectories.
- **Gradient Manipulation:** The indirect assignment method (guidance field) allows for specifying non-uniform boundary fluxes, effectively enabling the manipulation of gradients around different obstacles. This flexibility is shown to be beneficial in avoiding undesired equilibria (deadlocks), a common issue with simpler potential field methods.

#### 4.3. Hardware Demonstrations

The practical utility and real-time efficiency are highlighted through experiments on Unitree's Go2 quadruped and G1 humanoid robots, using a reduced-order model (ROM) framework.
- **Real-time Computation:** The Poisson safety function is updated online at approximately 10 Hz (solving time of 0.2-0.3 ms on a GPU for a 120x120 grid), demonstrating the method's computational efficiency suitable for dynamic environments.
- **Complex Static Environment (Quadruped):** The quadruped successfully navigated a static obstacle-filled environment, with the CBF-based safety filter ensuring collision avoidance while minimally deviating from nominal goal-reaching objectives. The safety function value remained positive, confirming safety adherence.
- **Dynamic Environment (Quadruped):** The quadruped effectively avoided dynamically rearranging obstacles (chair and box) while tracking a nominal sinusoidal reference. The safety function value remained positive, showcasing robust real-time safety.
- **Dynamic Environment (Humanoid):** A similar experiment with the humanoid robot showed generally good collision avoidance with a moving obstacle. However, minor safety violations (brief negative $h$ values) occurred. This was attributed to model mismatch: the single-integrator ROM was less accurate for the humanoid, which struggled to track velocity commands precisely, highlighting the importance of accurate ROMs in safety-critical control.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of safe autonomous robotics, particularly for systems operating in complex, unknown, and dynamic environments. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Constructive Synthesis of Smooth Safety Functions:** The paper provides a principled, constructive method for generating differentiable safety functions from raw perception data. This addresses a long-standing challenge in safety-critical control, offering a powerful alternative to heuristic or discontinuous methods like traditional Signed Distance Functions (SDFs). The inherent smoothness of Poisson safety functions is crucial for synthesizing high-quality, non-chattering control policies.
- **Enhanced Real-time Capability:** By demonstrating real-time computation of these safety functions from live perception data, the work makes CBF-based safety filters practical for dynamic, unstructured environments. The warm-starting technique for the PDE solver is a key enabler for this efficiency.
- **Improved Control Design for Complex Systems:** The rigorous mathematical properties, especially the guaranteed regularity (differentiability), of Poisson safety functions are directly beneficial for advanced control techniques, such as high-order CBFs and CBF backstepping. This allows for safe control of systems with complex dynamics (e.g., higher relative degrees) without sacrificing theoretical guarantees.
- **Decoupled and Flexible Gradient Control:** The variational approach with guidance fields allows designers to precisely sculpt the "repulsive" gradients around obstacles by choosing appropriate boundary conditions. This flexibility can help mitigate common problems like "deadlocks" and customize safety responses based on obstacle properties, going beyond the fixed gradient magnitudes of traditional SDFs.
- **Broad Applicability in Robotics:** The hardware demonstrations on both quadruped and humanoid robots underscore the versatility and practical impact of the proposed algorithm. It can be applied to a wide range of robotic platforms requiring robust collision avoidance, from mobile robots to manipulators.
- **Bridging Theoretical Gaps and Practical Implementation:** The work effectively bridges theoretical concepts from partial differential equations and control barrier functions with practical considerations of real-time robotic perception and control. This contributes to closing the loop from sensing to safe action for autonomous systems.

**Potential Impacts:** This methodology could pave the way for more widespread and reliable deployment of autonomous robots in human-centric or unpredictable environments, such as smart factories, logistics centers, or even personal robotics. By providing robust safety guarantees in the face of complex dynamics and environmental uncertainties, it increases trust and reduces risk in autonomous operations. It also opens new avenues for research at the intersection of geometric modeling, numerical PDE solvers, and advanced control theory for robotics. While the paper acknowledges limitations such as potential for undesired equilibria (deadlocks) and smoothness across safe/unsafe region boundaries, it provides a strong foundation for future work addressing these challenges.
