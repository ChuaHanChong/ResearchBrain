# DART: Noise Injection for Robust Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> DART is an off-policy imitation learning method that injects optimized noise into a supervisor's control stream during demonstration collection, forcing the supervisor to show error recovery. It iteratively fits the noise covariance by maximum likelihood of the current robot's controls, then scales it to the anticipated final policy error. DART matches DAgger on MuJoCo locomotion at 3x less computation and improves grasping in clutter by 62% over Behavior Cloning.

## Problem

- Behavior Cloning is off-policy, so covariate shift makes robot errors compound as it drifts into states absent from the supervisor's demonstration distribution.
- On-policy correction methods are tedious for human supervisors, and force the robot to execute suboptimal policies that visit dangerous states during training.
- On-policy methods retrain the policy from scratch after every round of corrections, and cheaper local gradient updates degrade final performance.

## Method

- **DART**'s ==noise-injected off-policy data collection==: sample demonstrations from a ==stochastic supervisor policy==, so corrective actions near the ideal policy boundary enter the training set.
- ==Covariate-shift bound via Pinsker's inequality== reduces shift minimization to minimizing ==negative log-likelihood== of the robot's control under the noise-injected supervisor, giving a ==closed-form covariance estimate==.
- ==Shrinkage-style scaling== of that covariance by a factor set from a target error level alpha, iterated over rounds since the final robot distribution is unknown in advance.

## Results

- On Walker, Hopper, Half-Cheetah and Humanoid, DART reaches parity with DAgger, while Behavior Cloning and the intermittently retrained DAgger variant fall short on Walker and Humanoid.
- On Humanoid, DART trains **3x** faster and loses only **5%** of supervisor cumulative reward during collection, versus over **80%** reward loss for the on-policy baseline.
- With four human supervisors on Toyota HSR grasping in clutter, DART at alpha=3 hit **79%** success versus **49%** for Behavior Cloning; alpha=6 gave **72%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulating a learner's future errors during data collection can substitute for querying the learner's own state distribution, keeping training safe and cheap.
> - Noise magnitude matters more than noise itself: isotropic or badly scaled noise degrades policies, while randomly drawn covariances matching the right simulated error work as well as optimized ones.
> - Noise injection only helps when the learner cannot perfectly represent the supervisor, and excessive noise can degrade human demonstration quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated within the field of Imitation Learning (IL), a paradigm where a robot learns a policy by observing a supervisor's demonstrations rather than through trial-and-error reinforcement learning. IL is particularly appealing for robotic tasks due to its ability to circumvent the need for manually crafting reward functions and to potentially reduce the vast sample complexity often associated with deep reinforcement learning.

The landscape of Imitation Learning is broadly divided into two categories:
- **Off-Policy Methods:** The most common approach is **Behavior Cloning (BC)**, where the robot passively observes the supervisor's actions and learns a direct mapping from states to actions through supervised learning. While simple and data-efficient in terms of supervisor effort, BC suffers from a critical limitation known as **covariate shift**. As the robot executes its learned policy, it inevitably makes small errors, drifting into states not covered by the supervisor's demonstrations. In these unseen states, the robot's policy can degrade rapidly, leading to compounding errors and potentially unsafe or catastrophic failures. Pioneering work by Pomerleau et al. (1989) on self-driving cars highlighted this issue, and Ross and Bagnell (2010) later provided a theoretical explanation based on covariate shift.
- **On-Policy Methods:** To address covariate shift, **DAgger (Dataset Aggregation)**, proposed by Ross et al. (2010, 2014), emerged as a leading on-policy solution. DAgger iteratively rolls out the robot's current policy, queries the supervisor for corrective actions in states the robot visits (which might be off the original demonstration trajectory), aggregates these new (state, action) pairs into the dataset, and retrains the policy. This process effectively trains the robot to recover from its own errors, leading to more robust policies. However, DAgger introduces its own set of limitations for practical robotic applications:
 - **Supervisor Tedium:** Human supervisors find it challenging and tiresome to constantly provide corrective actions as the robot explores suboptimal or dangerous states.
 - **Safety Concerns:** Allowing a physical robot to explore potentially dangerous or highly suboptimal states during training poses significant safety risks and can lead to hardware damage.
 - **Computational Expense:** Iteratively retraining a deep learning policy from scratch after each round of data collection is computationally demanding, especially for large neural networks and complex robot dynamics.

This paper, DART, proposes an **off-policy alternative that aims to mitigate covariate shift while addressing the limitations of DAgger**. It draws inspiration from robust control theory, specifically the concept of "persistence of excitation," which emphasizes the need for rich training data to learn robust system models. Just as injecting noise can improve model identification robustness, DART posits that injecting noise into the supervisor's *demonstrations* can achieve similar benefits in IL by forcing the supervisor to implicitly demonstrate recovery behaviors from simulated errors. This positions DART as a novel approach that seeks to bridge the gap between the simplicity of Behavior Cloning and the robustness of DAgger, making Imitation Learning more feasible and safer for real-world robotic deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind DART is to overcome the fundamental challenges in Imitation Learning, particularly the issue of **covariate shift** in Behavior Cloning, while simultaneously addressing the practical limitations of state-of-the-art on-policy methods like DAgger.

The key objectives can be summarized as follows:

- **Objective 1: Mitigate Covariate Shift in an Off-Policy Manner.** The central problem DART aims to solve is the compounding error phenomenon in Behavior Cloning. When a robot deviates from the supervisor's demonstrated trajectory, it encounters states for which its policy was not sufficiently trained, leading to performance degradation. DART seeks to provide the robot with "corrective examples" without requiring the robot to actually visit off-distribution states or requiring the human to constantly intervene during robot execution.
- **Objective 2: Reduce Supervisor Burden.** DAgger, while effective, demands continuous and often tedious corrective feedback from human supervisors. DART aims to shift this burden from reactive correction *during robot execution* to proactive demonstration *during data collection*. By injecting noise into the supervisor's actions, the supervisor is subtly forced to demonstrate how to recover from small, simulated errors, which is hypothesized to be less cognitively demanding and more natural than correcting a physically misbehaving robot.
- **Objective 3: Enhance Training Safety and Efficiency.** On-policy methods like DAgger can lead robots into dangerous or highly suboptimal states during exploration and policy rollouts. DART, being off-policy, keeps the robot (or supervisor) primarily within the bounds of successful demonstration trajectories, albeit with injected disturbances. This minimizes exposure to risky states. Furthermore, DAgger's requirement for frequent policy retraining is computationally expensive. DART aims for greater computational efficiency by allowing for less frequent policy updates, as the "correction" is baked into the data collection process itself.
- **Objective 4: Develop an Algorithm for Optimal Noise Injection.** The paper recognizes that simply injecting arbitrary noise is insufficient. The noise should ideally approximate the type and magnitude of errors the robot's *trained policy* is likely to make. Thus, a key objective is to propose a method, DART, that iteratively optimizes the level and characteristics of the injected noise to effectively simulate these errors, thereby maximizing the informativeness of the collected demonstrations.
- **Objective 5: Validate Effectiveness Across Diverse Robotic Tasks.** To demonstrate its generality and practical utility, DART aims to be evaluated in both high-dimensional simulated continuous control tasks (MuJoCo locomotion) and complex real-world manipulation tasks involving human supervisors (grasping in clutter), comparing its performance against Behavior Cloning and DAgger.

In essence, DART is motivated by the desire to make robust imitation learning both practical and scalable for real-world robotics, by making the data collection process more informative, safer, and less burdensome for human supervisors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DART operates on the core insight that by injecting controlled noise into the supervisor's policy during demonstration, the supervisor is implicitly prompted to exhibit recovery behaviors. These recovery behaviors serve as crucial "corrective examples" that train the robot to handle deviations from the ideal trajectory, thereby mitigating covariate shift without the drawbacks of on-policy methods like DAgger.

### 4. Problem Formulation and Theoretical Foundation

The paper frames Imitation Learning as minimizing the expected surrogate loss between the robot's policy ($\pi_\theta$) and the supervisor's policy ($\pi_{\theta^*}$) along trajectories induced by the robot's own policy. This objective, $\min_\theta E_{p(\xi|\pi_\theta)} J(\theta, \theta^*|\xi)$, highlights the challenge of covariate shift, where the training distribution ($p(\xi|\pi_{\theta^*})$ for Behavior Cloning) differs from the execution distribution ($p(\xi|\pi_\theta)$).

DART introduces noise into the supervisor's policy, denoted $p(\xi|\pi_{\theta^*}, \psi)$, where $\psi$ parameterizes the noise distribution. The theoretical basis for DART relies on bounding the covariate shift using KL-divergence. Lemma 4.1 shows that the difference between the expected loss on the noise-injected supervisor's distribution and the robot's distribution is bounded by $T\sqrt{\frac{1}{2} D_{KL}(p(\xi|\pi_\theta), p(\xi|\pi_{\theta^*}, \psi))}$. The goal, therefore, is to minimize this KL-divergence with respect to $\psi$.

Minimizing the KL-divergence effectively translates to maximizing the likelihood of the noise-injected supervisor producing the actions that the *robot's current policy* would take in given states. This creates a "chicken and egg" problem: the optimal noise depends on the final robot policy, which is not yet known.

### 5. The DART Algorithm (Disturbances for Augmenting Robot Trajectories)

DART addresses the "chicken and egg" problem using an iterative approach:

1. **Initialization:** Start with an initial noise parameter (e.g., no noise, $\psi_0$).
2. **Iterative Data Collection and Policy Learning:** The algorithm proceeds for *K* iterations:
 - **Collect Demonstrations:** Collect *N* demonstrations from the supervisor, with noise parameterized by the current $\psi_k$ injected into the supervisor's control stream. The supervisor reacts to these disturbances, implicitly providing recovery trajectories.
 - **Train Robot Policy:** Train a robot policy ($\hat{\pi}_\theta$) via empirical risk minimization on the *aggregated dataset* collected so far (from all previous iterations).
 - **Optimize Noise Parameter (MLE-based):** The core of DART is to update the noise parameter $\psi$. The algorithm aims to find $\hat{\psi}_{k+1}$ that minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the supervisor's noise-injected policy taking the *current robot's actions* ($\hat{\pi}_\theta$) in the states visited during the *noise-injected demonstrations*. This objective, $\min_\psi E_{p(\xi|\pi_{\theta^*},\psi_k)} -\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \log [\pi_{\theta^*}(\hat{\pi}_\theta(x_t)|x_t, \psi)]$, effectively estimates the noise characteristics that best "explain" the current robot's behavior if the supervisor were to account for it.
 - For **Gaussian noise** with covariance matrix $\Sigma$, the optimal $\hat{\Sigma}$ is derived as the empirical covariance of the difference between the robot's action and the supervisor's action: $\hat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{T} E_{p(\xi|\pi_{\theta^*})} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (\hat{\pi}_\theta(x_t) - \pi_{\theta^*}(x_t))(\hat{\pi}_\theta(x_t) - \pi_{\theta^*}(x_t))^T$. This means the noise simulates the variance of the robot's deviation from the supervisor.
 - For **epsilon-greedy noise** in discrete action spaces, a similar closed-form solution for $\epsilon$ is derived based on the frequency of disagreement between robot and supervisor actions.
 - **Scale Noise Parameter (Shrinkage Estimation):** To prevent overfitting the noise parameter to the current, possibly poor, robot policy, DART introduces a scaling step (inspired by shrinkage estimation). The optimized $\hat{\psi}_{k+1}$ is scaled by a factor that aims to align the *expected simulated error* (e.g., trace of the covariance matrix for Gaussian noise) with a predefined target error $\alpha$, representing an estimated guess of the final robot's error: $\psi^{\alpha}_{k+1} = \hat{\psi}_{k+1} \cdot \text{argmin}_{\beta \ge 0} |\alpha - E_{p(\xi|\pi_{\theta^*},\beta\cdot \hat{\psi}_{k+1})} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} l(u_t, \pi_{\theta^*}(x_t))|$. For Gaussian noise, this simplifies to $\Sigma^{\alpha}_{k+1} = \frac{\alpha}{T \text{tr}(\hat{\Sigma}_{k+1})} \hat{\Sigma}_{k+1}$.
 - The updated $\psi^{\alpha}_{k+1}$ is then used for the next iteration's data collection.

### 6. Theoretical Analysis (Proposition 4.1)

The paper provides a theoretical proposition stating that if the robot policy has non-zero error, injecting Gaussian noise into the supervisor's policy (thereby ensuring finite probability for all controls) reduces the KL-divergence between the robot's trajectory distribution and the supervisor's noise-injected distribution compared to Behavior Cloning (which has infinite KL-divergence in regions where the robot deviates). This supports the claim that DART effectively reduces covariate shift.

### 7. Experimental Design

- **Simulated Experiments (MuJoCo):**
 - **Environments:** Walker, Hopper, Half-Cheetah, Humanoid (locomotion tasks). These offer continuous, high-dimensional control spaces.
 - **Supervisor:** Algorithmic supervisor (TRPO-trained neural network).
 - **Baselines:** Behavior Cloning (BC), DAgger, DAgger-B (DAgger with intermittent retraining), Isotropic-Gaussian (fixed non-optimized noise).
 - **Metrics:** Normalized cumulative reward (performance), total computation time, cumulative reward obtained *during training* (reflecting supervisor burden/safety).
- **Real-World Experiments (Toyota HSR Robot):**
 - **Task:** Grasping in clutter (robot clears occluding objects to reach a target).
 - **Supervisors:** Four human supervisors with robotics experience.
 - **Baselines:** Behavior Cloning, DART with two different $\alpha$ values (alpha=3 and alpha=6, reflecting different levels of anticipated error).
 - **Metrics:** Success rate (identifying goal object, clear path).

This methodical approach combines theoretical grounding with comprehensive empirical validation across different domains and types of supervisors, aiming to demonstrate DART's practical advantages.

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted in both simulated MuJoCo environments and a real-world robotic grasping task demonstrate DART's effectiveness in reducing covariate shift, improving performance, and enhancing practicality compared to existing imitation learning methods.

### 9. MuJoCo Locomotion Environments (Algorithmic Supervisor)

- **Performance Parity with DAgger:** DART consistently achieved performance levels (normalized cumulative reward) comparable to, or at parity with, DAgger across all four high-dimensional continuous control tasks (Walker, Hopper, Half-Cheetah, Humanoid). This indicates that DART successfully addresses covariate shift to a similar degree as DAgger.
- **Significant Computational Speedup:** For high-dimensional tasks like Humanoid, DART was **3x faster in computation time** than DAgger. This is a crucial finding, as DAgger's computational cost due to frequent retraining is a major bottleneck. DART avoids this by shifting the "correction" into the data collection phase, allowing for less frequent policy updates.
- **Reduced Supervisor Burden/Safer Training:** During training, DART's policies only decreased the supervisor's cumulative reward by **5%** for Humanoid, meaning the noise-injected demonstrations stayed very close to optimal trajectories. In contrast, DAgger executed policies that had **80% less cumulative reward** than the supervisor, indicating frequent visits to highly sub-optimal and potentially dangerous states. This suggests DART is far less taxing on supervisors and safer for physical robots.
- **Ineffectiveness of Naive Noise Injection:** The "Isotropic-Gaussian" baseline (fixed, non-optimized noise) performed poorly, highlighting that simply adding noise is not sufficient; the noise level and characteristics must be optimized, as DART does.
- **Importance of Iterative Updates:** DAgger-B (DAgger with intermittent retraining to reduce computation) showed significantly deteriorated performance, especially in Humanoid. This underscores that while frequent updates are necessary for on-policy methods, DART achieves comparable robustness *without* the costly retraining at each step, instead optimizing the data collection process itself.
- **Covariate Shift Reduction:** Figure 4 (in the appendix) visually confirms that DART, like DAgger, effectively reduces the disparity between the loss on the supervisor's distribution and the robot's distribution, indicating a successful reduction in covariate shift.
- **Optimal Noise Level:** Figure 5 (in the appendix) suggests that while certain randomly chosen noise levels might perform well, DART's optimization process is necessary to reliably determine the appropriate noise level, which is usually unknown *a priori*.

### 10. Robotic Grasping in Clutter (Human Supervisors)

- **Significant Performance Increase over Behavior Cloning:** On the challenging real-world grasping in clutter task, DART demonstrated a substantial improvement over traditional Behavior Cloning when trained by human supervisors.
 - DART with $\alpha=3$ achieved a **79% success rate**, representing a **62% performance increase** compared to Behavior Cloning's 49% success rate.
 - DART with $\alpha=6$ also outperformed BC with a 72% success rate, but slightly less than $\alpha=3$. This intriguing result suggests that there might be an optimal level of noise for human supervisors, and excessive noise (even if theoretically optimal for the robot) might hinder the human's ability to provide high-quality demonstrations. It points to a delicate balance between simulating robot errors and maintaining a manageable demonstration environment for humans.

Overall, the findings strongly support DART as a robust, efficient, and user-friendly alternative for imitation learning, particularly beneficial in scenarios involving high-dimensional continuous control and human supervisors.

### 11. Significance and Potential Impact

The DART algorithm represents a significant contribution to the field of Imitation Learning and robotics, offering a practical and theoretically sound solution to long-standing challenges. Its potential impact spans several key areas:

- **Bridging the Gap in Imitation Learning:** DART successfully navigates the trade-off between the simplicity of off-policy Behavior Cloning and the robustness of on-policy DAgger. By injecting noise into supervisor demonstrations, DART effectively addresses covariate shift – the Achilles' heel of Behavior Cloning – without inheriting the main drawbacks of DAgger, such as supervisor tedium, computational expense, and the risk of visiting dangerous states. This makes robust imitation learning more accessible and viable for a wider range of real-world robotic applications.
- **Enhancing Practicality for Human-Robot Collaboration:** A major bottleneck in applying advanced robot learning techniques is the human effort required for data collection. DART's design minimizes the cognitive load on human supervisors. Instead of reacting to a robot's gross errors in real-time, supervisors are prompted to demonstrate recovery from subtle, simulated disturbances. This more natural interaction paradigm makes learning from human demonstrations more scalable and less burdensome, which is crucial for deploying robots in complex, unstructured environments where human expertise is indispensable.
- **Improving Safety and Efficiency of Robot Training:** By keeping the training trajectories close to the supervisor's optimal path, even with injected noise, DART significantly reduces the likelihood of a physical robot exploring unsafe or highly suboptimal states during training. This is a critical safety advantage over on-policy methods. Furthermore, the substantial reduction in computational time (e.g., 3x faster for Humanoid) makes the training process more efficient, allowing for faster iteration cycles in development and deployment.
- **Generalizability and Future Research Directions:** The core concept of intelligently perturbing supervisor demonstrations to elicit robust recovery behaviors is broadly applicable. DART provides a concrete algorithmic framework for optimizing this perturbation. This opens avenues for future research into adaptive noise models, task-specific noise injection strategies, and integrating DART within hierarchical imitation learning or even hybrid reinforcement learning systems. The empirical success on diverse tasks (locomotion and manipulation) suggests its potential across various robotic domains.
- **Towards Deployable Robot Learning:** By making imitation learning more robust, efficient, and human-friendly, DART brings robot learning closer to real-world deployment. The ability to learn complex manipulation skills like "grasping in clutter" from relatively few human demonstrations, with a significant performance boost over traditional BC, highlights its practical utility in scenarios like logistics, healthcare, or personal robotics.

In conclusion, DART offers a compelling solution to a fundamental problem in robot learning, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with imitation learning by balancing theoretical rigor with practical considerations for human-robot interaction and computational efficiency.
